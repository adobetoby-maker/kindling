# Chapter 17 — Hers

"Has it got a name?" said Milo.

"Has what got a name."

"The thing you teach us. The room. Going all the way through." He had his knees up and his arms round them and the stove going orange on the side of his face. "You told me it hasn't. You said the names are all on the doors and you'd rather not hand us one more word to be right about. But she'd have had one, wouldn't she. Two hundred words nobody else has."

Rook was quiet for a while.

"Satori," he said.

It was an ordinary sound. Toren had expected, without knowing he expected it, that it would be a large one.

"What does it mean?"

"It means you saw it. Not that you're strong and not that you've learned anything — that you looked at the thing everybody's been standing next to their whole life and you actually saw it, and it went all the way in." Rook turned the Ember over. "It's not one of the words her clan invented after the Fall. It's old. They carried it over with the rest. She said it was a word about a moment, and that the moment isn't long, and that everything before it is preparation and everything after it is housekeeping."

"Is that what you did? In the field. For four years."

"That's what she did in a field for two years, and then she told me how, and then it took me another two, and I have never once managed to say it to anybody in a way that made it happen." He put his hands flat on the Ember and stopped moving them. "I've tried it on you four different ways in a month. I'll keep trying."

"Why don't you use the word?"

"Because it's hers," said Rook, "and because I've watched what happens to a true thing when it gets a name people can be right about. That's all of it." He looked down the step. "Voss has had it once. Don't repeat it, and that goes for the four of you and it goes double for him."

Toren felt the whole step change.

Dessa did not turn her head. Milo did, and opened his mouth, and Wyck said "Leave it" from the ballast, without any heat in it at all, and Milo left it.

"I won't," said Toren.

"I know you won't," Rook said, and then, in exactly the same voice: "They came in the afternoon."

---

"It was the sixteenth of the eighth month, nine years ago, at about half past three. Everybody was up. It wasn't dark and nobody was asleep and there wasn't any of that." Rook's hands were still on the Ember. "I was in the water room. There was a valve on the feed off the hill that had been dripping for a fortnight and I'd said I'd look at it twice and not done it, and that morning she'd said, out loud, at breakfast, in front of eleven people, that I was the only man she'd ever met who could hold a spear together with his mind and could not tighten a nut.

"So I was under the tank with a wrench.

"That's where I was. That's the whole reason I'm sitting here. There is no other reason. If she hadn't said that at breakfast I'd have been in the east building at half past three with everybody else."

Nobody on the step moved.

"They came over the ridge on the west side and down through the gate, and they didn't come quietly and they didn't need to. There were about thirty of them. They had rifles, which almost nobody has and almost nobody has ammunition for, and they had four people who could work, and one of those was better than anybody we had.

"It took about forty minutes. I'm telling you that because everybody always thinks it takes longer.

"I was outside. I came up out of the water room into the yard about two minutes in, and I got as far as the top of the steps, and after that I was in it, and I can't give you the order of it. I've tried. I've had nine years and I can't. I've got about eleven things and none of them join up."

"You don't have to—" said Milo.

"I'm not going to," said Rook. "You don't need them and I'm not handing them to anybody sitting on this brick tonight."

---

"She was in the instrument room," he said. "Under the east building. That's where she'd have been at that hour, and she was, and I couldn't get down there for a long time, and by the time I could it had been a long time.

"She'd have gone down instead of out. I want you to understand why, because it's the only part of this I'd want anybody to know about her. There were nine thousand pictures in that room and eleven years of paper with them and she was the one who'd read all of it, and when the shooting started she went *down*.

"She wasn't trying to save herself and she wasn't trying to save anybody else. She'd made a judgment about which of the two things in that hill was harder to replace and she went and stood in front of it."

The wind came round the corner of the brick.

Senna said, "How long did she have to make it in?"

"About four seconds."

"Mm," said Senna, and put her chin back down on the blanket, and did not say anything else for two hours.

"Did she—" said Milo.

"She was already gone when I got there," Rook said. "That's all of that you're getting and it's all there is. I sat down on the floor for a while. I don't know how long, and there wasn't anybody left to ask."

---

"They burned the paper."

He said it in the same voice, and it took a moment to hear that something in it had changed.

"That's the part I'd get wrong if I let myself. They didn't take it. If they'd taken it, somebody would have it, and paper in somebody's hands is paper that can be got at again. They pulled it off the shelves and stacked it in the corridor and burned it in the corridor, which is a thing you do when you have been told to.

"Eleven years. Nine thousand pictures and the notes that went with them and four people's whole working lives. The only proof anybody has ever assembled that the two halves of this world are the same thing wearing two coats, and it went up in a passage under a hill, and there has not been one page of it anywhere on this continent since the sixteenth of the eighth month, nine years ago."

"Who," said Wyck.

"I didn't know for four years."

"But you know now."

"I know now," said Rook.

He did not say it.

He turned the Ember over in his hands once, and looked out at the pale line of the left-hand bank going away north-west into nothing, and Toren felt the whole of the last three weeks come round and click into place like a wheel dropping into a rut: a burned wayhouse, an empty rack of cradles, three graves dug properly by people with all afternoon, and a man reading dates off a slate in a voice with nothing whatsoever in it.

He had his mouth open to say it when Wyck said it.

"Cinder Company."

"Yes."

"Right," said Wyck.

He did not sound like a boy who had guessed something. He sounded like a man putting a tool down at the end of a job.

"I've had that off a wall for ten days," he said. "I was four feet from you when you burned the board and I read it on the way past, and I've had it in my head every hour since and never asked you one question about it. I want that said with everybody sitting here, because in about a week somebody's going to tell me I was good about it.

"I didn't ask because I worked out you'd say no, and then I'd have had the no as well as the not knowing. That's the whole of it."

---

"Tanner," said Rook. "I'm going to say a thing to you in front of them and you won't enjoy it.

"I put a boy of thirteen at point over you on purpose and did not tell you why, and I've watched you decide about nine separate times that it was because he's better than you or because I like him more. Both of those are wrong. I let you keep them because correcting you would have cost me something.

"And you've carried it better than I'd have carried it at fifteen, and I've sat on that for a month because saying it would have made me feel a great deal better and done nothing whatever for you."

"Don't," said Wyck.

"All right."

"No — " Wyck shifted the leg with both hands and got his back against the brick. "I mean don't hand me that tonight as though it settles anything. You've told us a thing that happened to you nine years ago and it's the worst thing I've ever heard, and it doesn't get you the last five weeks. Those are separate and I'd rather have them separate.

"I'm not angry with you for having a dead woman. I'm angry with you for the fifth of the month and the loop road and every morning since, and I'm going to go on being angry about it for a while yet, and I'd like to do that without anybody telling me it's been settled."

"Nothing's been settled," said Rook. "You've been right the whole month and I've been managing you."

"Yes," said Wyck.

And that was the whole of it, and Toren, sitting three feet away with his arms round his knees, understood that something had gone on between those two that had nothing at all to do with agreement.

---

"There's a second half of it and it's worse, and I'm going to give it to you plainly and then we're not going to discuss it.

"I got out of Homura with about nine other people and we scattered, because that's what you do. I went home. It took me five weeks. My village was still standing and the charter still had two years on it, and the physician still came round, which I have never worked out, because there was nobody left to send him." Rook's voice was perfectly level. "I stayed two years. I was no use to anybody and they put up with me. And in the second winter a rift-tear opened about nine miles up the valley and a hunting company came through on contract to close it, and the fighting came down into the fields below the houses, and some of it came into the houses.

"They didn't mean it. That's not me being generous, it's a fact — it was a bad night in bad ground and they were shooting at things that fold up and come at whatever's nearest and there were people where nobody thought there'd be people. I was there. I saw the whole of it and there was nothing in it that anybody did on purpose.

"And it killed my mother and my brother and my brother's two children."

Toren's hands had gone numb.

"And I did not find out for another two years," Rook said, "that it was the same company."

---

He stopped.

The stove ticked against the wall of the brick box. Milo was crying, without any noise at all and without appearing to have noticed, and Dessa reached over and took the can out of his hands before he dropped it and put it down on the ballast, and that was all anybody did about it.

"I buried my own people not knowing," Rook said. "I stood in that valley and I was grateful to them. Four of them helped dig. I have shaken hands with a man who was there at Homura and I did not know it, and he did not know me, and I have thought about which of those two things is worse and I have never got an answer."

"How did you find out?" said Toren.

"A tally-board," said Rook. "In a trading post, about two hundred miles from here, five years ago. Somebody had written down what they'd sold and who'd bought it and on what day, because that is what people do, and the day was the sixteenth of the eighth month, and the place was eleven miles from a bowl of hills."

The wind moved.

"I've been on that line ever since. And I have never once found out who paid them, and I've stopped expecting to. Somebody wanted eleven years of paper in a corridor. Somebody worked out what that paper was going to do to the way this world holds itself apart, and decided against it, and had the money to decide. I don't know their name. I'm not going to."

---

"If you catch them," Toren said.

"Go on."

"If you get all the way down that line and you catch them and it goes the way you want it to. Does that — "

He stopped, because he could not find the end of the sentence.

"Does it what," said Rook.

"I don't know. Does it fix any of it?"

Rook looked out at the left-hand bank for a long time, and every one of them sat and let him do it.

"They took forty-one people out of a hill," he said. "Four of them are mine, with the valley, and it's five if I'm honest about her, and there is a version of that which I can answer. Men do it. It's an old debt and everybody agrees what it costs and there's a road for it and I've been on that road for five years and I know exactly how to walk it.

"And then there's the other thing they took, which was eleven years of paper in a corridor, and I could kill every man in that company tomorrow with my own hands and it would not put back one page."

---

"Can I see it?" said Toren.

"See what?"

"Your Ember."

Rook's hands stopped.

He had been holding the thing the entire night. Toren had watched him do it since the brick step — turning it over, holding it still, putting his palms flat on it, and Toren had thought, for about three hours, that it was what a man does with his hands when he cannot put them anywhere.

Rook held it out.

Toren took it.

It was heavier than his father's. That was the first thing. It was shorter and heavier and the taper at the ends was sharper, and there was a wrap of leather round the grip, dark and gone soft with handling, and it was worn through in one place near the base — one place, about the width of a thumb, down at the bottom of the grip where no part of a hand naturally goes.

Toren turned it over.

He had a family Ember in his own coat pocket that was worn glass-smooth in a band around the middle, where four generations of Voss thumbs had sat in exactly the same place, and he had had that explained to him at a fire under a broken overpass on the last good night of his life.

He put his own thumb where the leather was gone.

It did not reach. He had to turn his wrist to do it and the whole grip went wrong in his hand.

"That's not where you hold it," he said.

"No."

"That's not where *anybody* holds it." Toren's voice had stopped working properly. "You'd have to be — your hand would have to be smaller, or you'd have to hold it right down at the—"

"She was about as tall as Cole," said Rook.

---

Toren sat with the thing in both hands.

Then he leaned along the step and held it out to Wyck, and did not say anything, because there was no way to say it.

Wyck took it.

He had seen it come down a hillside as seven feet of spear. He had had it laid flat along the whole length of his own opened leg for eleven minutes while a boy with a useless light held his hand steady above it, and Toren had been that boy, and neither of them had ever said one word to the other about that afternoon.

Wyck turned it over on his palm exactly once and found the worn place without being shown where it was.

"Eleven minutes," he said.

"Yes."

"On my leg."

"Yes."

"And flat across his torn hands in the road, and in the barrow on her, more times than any of us have counted." Wyck was not looking at Rook. He was looking at the thing on his palm in the orange light off the stove. "Every single thing you've done for any of us. Five weeks of it. With hers."

"Yes," said Rook.

Wyck put it back into the man's hand, grip first, and took his own hand away.

"You'd better not lose it," he said.

---

Dessa said, "I'm going to ask for something, and I'll say it badly, so let me get to the end."

"Go on."

"Nobody in any of those nine thousand pictures was doing anything."

Rook's head came round.

"You said it about five times tonight without hearing yourself. You lie on your back in a tube for an hour and it's very loud and very cold and *nothing hurts and nothing happens*, and then you get up and go and have your breakfast, and on the other side of a wall there's a picture with a place lit in it." Her hands were out of her pockets and flat on her knees. "Eleven years of the only proof anybody's ever had, and not one page of it is a person doing a thing.

"I do nothing. That's my whole trade. I sit in the wet and don't move and nothing comes out of me and there's nothing to look at, and every day somebody calls a distance I gave them and then goes off and does the interesting part. I've spent five weeks deciding that what I've got is a chore you invented so I'd have something to be, because if it were anything it would make a noise." Her voice did not change at all. "That's gone. It went about an hour ago and it isn't coming back.

"And here's the ask. Sixty people in a hole in a hill agreed with a picture for eleven years and one of them went and tried the other door. One. And you've told us it's the thing you've thought about more than anything else in your life, and then you've sat on this brick for four hours and not asked a single one of us whether we'd like to try it."

Nobody said anything at all.

"So I'd like to try it. Tonight. I want an Ember in my hand and I want to be shown the first thing, and I'm not asking whether I'm allowed and I'm not asking whether it'll work. I've watched you teach Toren one thing four different ways in a month. I'd like my four ways."

Rook sat with his forearms on his knees and the Ember in both hands and did not answer for what felt to Toren like a very long time.

"I haven't got one to give you," he said.

"You've got one in your hands."

"Yes," said Rook. "And no."

"All right," said Dessa, and Toren heard exactly what it cost her to say it in that voice and say nothing after it.

There was a sound from the ballast at the end of the step, which was Wyck getting himself up in two parts with the stick, and then a second sound, which was Wyck's own Ember coming out of his coat.

He came along the step and put it in Dessa's hand and closed her fingers over it with his own hand, the bad one, which took him a moment.

"After dark," he said. "Every night. I don't use it after dark and I won't want it back before breakfast, and if you're still holding it at breakfast I'll wait."

"Wyck—"

"Don't make a speech. I've had enough speeches tonight and half of them were mine." He got his stick under him. "And this isn't about him. I want that clear in front of everybody, because he'll have told himself it is by tomorrow afternoon. It's about you. You've been calling distances off a bank in the wet for five weeks so the rest of us could walk about being interesting, and I have said thank you for that exactly never."

He went back to the ballast and sat down, in two parts.

---

"Faust," said Rook.

Milo's head came up.

"Get the stone hot."

"It's four in the morning."

"I know what time it is. Get it hot and make it out of what you've got and don't count it tonight."

Milo looked at him for a second and then got up and did it, and Toren watched the whole of him come back into his own body while he did it.

He got the stone hot and mixed the meal and put the first flat cake down and turned it when it needed turning, and when it came off he broke it in two and then broke a piece off the corner of one half.

Then he stopped, with the piece in his hand.

He held it out to Rook.

"It's yours," he said. "The whole time it's been yours and I've been doing it without knowing, and I'd rather you had it back now I do."

Rook looked at the piece of bread in the boy's hand for about three seconds, which was exactly as long as he had looked at it on a stone by a fire every night for five weeks.

"No," he said. "You do it."

"I don't know what it's for."

"Neither did she," said Rook. "Neither did her grandmother. That's not a gap in it. That's the shape of it."

Milo stood there a moment longer.

Then he turned round and put the piece down on a flat stone at the edge of the coals, the way he had put it down for ten nights, and set it aside.

"Vera," he said, to the stone, in a perfectly ordinary voice, because nobody had told him not to.

And he handed the rest of it round in his order. Senna. Wyck, because of the leg. Whoever was on watch. Everybody else.

Rook took his and ate it and did not say anything, and did not look away.

---

"Right," he said, standing up. "We'll want water in the morning. There's a ditch behind the hedge back at the camp and it wants boiling twice, not once, and I'll do it, and the rest of you will sleep till somebody kicks you."

"It's done," said Wyck.

Rook stopped.

"I did it yesterday afternoon between the standing holds," said Wyck. "Two cans, boiled twice, on the tailboard since about five o'clock. You walked past them four times." He was not looking up. "And I'm not doing it because we're square. We're not square. I'm doing it because you haven't slept in two days and if you go down out here we're four children and a barrow, and I'd rather nobody called that affection."

Rook stood in the middle of the triangle with his hands in his pockets.

Then he sat back down, which Toren had not expected. At the end of a day, every day for five weeks, that man had gone up a bank and sat with his forearms on his knees facing the way they had come.

He sat down on the brick between Milo and the barrow and put his boots out and did not go anywhere.

---

Wyck went off to the top of the bank with the stick.

Dessa sat against the brick with a borrowed Ember in both hands and her eyes shut and her face doing nothing at all. Milo was asleep inside ten minutes with his coat over his head and the stone still warm beside him. Senna had been asleep for two hours and had not been asleep for the four before it.

Toren went round to the other side of the brick box, where the wind was, because there was nowhere else on that triangle to go, and after a while the man came round after him.

"There's one more," Rook said. "It's yours and it isn't theirs, and I'd have skipped it altogether if I could, because I don't have a way of saying it that doesn't sound like a man selling something at a market."

"Say it."

"About a year after Homura I started having the same dream."

Toren waited.

"I put it down to grief. Everybody does, and everybody's mostly right, and for two years I was right." Rook had his hands empty and open. "It didn't stop. It's not a picture and it's never been a picture. I've never seen a face in it and I've never seen a place. It's the way it is when you wake up at somebody's house and for one second you can't think where you are, except the other way about — I'd wake up knowing exactly where I was and knowing there was a person somewhere who hadn't been born yet or hadn't been found yet, and that they were carrying a flat dark thing with cuts across the face of it, and that when they turned up it was going to be mine to answer for.

"I've had it for eight years. Not every night. Often enough."

He said the next part very plainly.

"I did not want it. I want you to have that, because in about a week you're going to start wondering whether any of this was ever about you, and I'd rather you had the true version than a kind one. For eight years I have been walking a line with a company at the end of it, and about twice a season I've woken up in the dark with somebody else's obligation sitting on my chest, and I have resented it the way you'd resent a debt you didn't take out."

"And then the sluice," said Toren.

"And then the sluice."

---

"You came up the hill with your hand in your pocket," Rook said, "and you put your hand in your pocket and there was a hole in it, and you went straight back down into a flooded building on the worst day of that boy's life, alone, four days after I'd told all four of you that nobody goes anywhere alone. And I went after you to haul you out by the collar.

"And I came up out of that sluice with it on my hand and I couldn't make my hands stop, and I'd have told you it was the water and the cold and eleven minutes inside a leg, and you were three feet away and you'd been watching me for four days and you knew it wasn't."

"I knew it wasn't."

"I know." Rook looked at him for the first time in some while. "I'd like one thing on the record, because you asked me a question on that bank tonight and I gave them the short answer and I owe you the long one.

"I put that pen down at Tallow Gate two days before I ever saw what you had in your pocket. I didn't know. There was nothing in that office except a woman being fair and four children in a row, and I had the cleanest way out of my life in my hand and I put it down on the desk, and nobody made me. That one's mine. It is not yours and you don't get to take it off me." He put his hands back on his knees. "So: no. It isn't your fault. The ledger is the part that isn't, and you can have it, and I'd like you to actually keep it."

Toren's eyes had gone and he did not try to do anything about them.

"And point?"

"Point's yours," said Rook. "Point was the day after the sluice."

---

"Why wouldn't you tell me?"

"Because I didn't know what you were," Rook said. "I've had eight years of a thing on my chest with no face on it, and then there's a boy of thirteen in a road with a torn pocket, and I can tell you the exact shape of what I felt and I could not tell you one useful thing about what it meant. Whether you were the whole of it or a piece of it. Whether I was supposed to teach you or get you to Meridian or put you somewhere with a wall round you and walk away.

"So I did the only thing I could stand behind, which was to teach you as hard and as fast as I could get away with, and keep my mouth shut about the reason, and see what turned up."

"They've got the rest of it now."

"They've got the rest of it now, and an old woman in a barrow had to stand me in front of it." He was quiet a moment. "Which is yours as well, and I'd rather you had it tonight than worked it out in the spring. I wasn't going to bring them. I was going to walk one of you out here and go on managing the other three for another month, and I had the whole of it arranged and it was tidy, and none of you would ever have known there'd been a choice."

The wind dropped, the way it does an hour or two before light, and the flat went quiet.

---

Rook went back round the brick box after a while and sat down on the step with the others.

Toren stayed where he was.

He sat on a fall of broken brick in the last black hour of the night, in the middle of a flat triangle of ground that six hundred people had built by hand before the Fall, with the disk warm in a pocket sewn with two rows of stitches, and looked at the left-hand bank going away north-west, and at the right-hand bank curving east out of sight behind the shoulder of the hill.

He did not know which one they were taking.

Every one of them knew now what was down the left, and what had been done in a corridor under a hill on the sixteenth of the eighth month, and whose piece of bread went on the stone.

And there was a flat dark thing with cuts across the face of it in his coat, and a river at the bottom of the right-hand line, and one person on that triangle had put those two things in the same sentence out loud — on a bank, at dusk, to a man who had not answered.
