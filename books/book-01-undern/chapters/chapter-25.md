# Chapter 25 — Standing Ash

By the third night Toren could hold Stride standing still for five seconds, and could not take one step in it.

That was the shape of the problem and it did not get any better for saying it out loud. The frame came up his legs the colour of a cold morning, hip to heel, and it held him — he could feel the road through it the way you feel the floor through a chair — and the instant he asked either foot to leave the ground the whole thing went off like a lamp in a draught and he went down.

"You're telling your legs to run," said Rook, from the verge.

"I'm telling my legs to walk."

"You're telling them. That's the whole of it." He had his coat collar up and had not moved in an hour. "Do it again and this time don't decide which foot."

Toren did it again and went down on his shoulder, which by now he could do without thinking about it, and lay in the grass looking up at a sky the colour of a cook pot.

"That was worse," Rook said.

"I know it was worse."

"Good. Again."

---

On the fourth morning they met the haulers coming north.

There were nine of them with three carts and two dogs, and they had the carts loaded wrong — heavy at the back, tools on top where they should have been under, rope thrown on in a heap instead of coiled. Toren registered all of that before he registered a single face, and understood about a half-second later that he had registered it because Rook had spent seven weeks teaching him to look at a load and tell how much time the people packing it had thought they had.

They came up the crown of the road at a pace that was faster than good for the dogs.

The woman in front was about fifty, broad, grey-haired, with a hauler's shoulders and a strip of cloth bound round one hand.

"Rell," she said, which Toren took a moment to understand was her name.

"Marsh."

"You're going south."

"We are."

"Don't."

Rook set the shafts down.

"Say it out," he said, "and I'll hear the whole of it, and then I'll tell you what I'm going to do, and neither of us has to argue about it."

---

Rell put her bound hand on the side of her cart.

"We had a paper on the Drownings," she said. "Old quarry, nine mile short of the lamps, flooded these thirty years, and there's a tear in the lip of it that's been open since the spring and the Register put it out to contract in the sixth month and we took it. Eleven of us. We've been in that ground since the eighth month and we've had a good season off it — eight casks up the road in five loads and every one of them weighed honest at the gate.

"Four days ago Corb and Idony went down into the workings after a pair of Flask and didn't come up. We went down after them the next morning, all of us, in daylight, which I want said, because I've had it said to me twice already this week that we went at night."

"Did you find them?"

"We found where they went to ash," said Rell. "Both of them. On the floor of the workings, side by side, in the open. And I want you to hear the part that I've not been able to get anybody at the lamps to hear yet, because it's the only part that matters and it sounds like nothing.

"There wasn't enough of it."

Rook's face did not change.

"Say that again."

"There wasn't enough of it," said Rell. "Two grown people go to ash and you get what you get, and I've been at this twenty-two years and I know what a person leaves on the ground. There was about a hand of it each. Grey, cold, sat up on the surface like flour on a table. And it hadn't gone in. It sat there for as long as we stood and looked at it and it never went in."

"Ash goes in," said Dessa.

"Ash goes in," Rell agreed. "It goes in in about four minutes and it goes in on stone and it goes in on wet and I have watched it go into the deck of a boat. That did not go in." She took her hand off the cart. "So we came up, and we came off the contract, and we've left eight days' work and a good rope in the bottom of that hole, and I'll be paying the Register back for the rest of my life, and Mother's mercy, I'd do it again tomorrow."

---

"What's the gate like?" said Rook.

It came out flat and short and in the middle of nothing, and Toren watched Rell answer it without ever once noticing she had been asked anything.

"Tight. Tighter than the spring. They've had two hundred come up that road since the harvest and they're taking about one in three and they want everything written down twice." She jerked her chin at the barrow. "You've got a sick one. That'll get you a hearing and it won't get you a bed, so don't walk up there thinking it will."

"And if we go round the workings?"

"Two days east and you'll be in the fen and you'll want four days, and there's nothing in the fen but the fen." She looked at him for a second longer than she needed to. "You're not going round."

"No."

"No," said Rell. "I could see it off you." She got her hand back on the cart and put her weight into it. "The tip road across the middle's good stone and it's twelve foot wide and it's eighty yard end to end. If it were me I'd be on it at noon with the light on top of me and I'd not stop in the middle for anything on this earth. That's all I've got and I've given you the whole of it."

"You have," said Rook. "Thank you."

She got four paces and stopped and looked back.

"There were eleven of us," she said.

---

They went south.

Nobody said anything about it for about a mile. Then Wyck said, "Two days round," and Rook said, "And four in the fen, and she's got eleven days in her and I'm not spending six of them in standing water," and that was the whole of the conversation and everybody had known how it was going to go before it started.

Senna slept most of that afternoon, which she had begun to do.

---

The country went bad the way weather comes on.

Toren could not put a milepost on it afterward and neither could anybody else. There was no line to cross. It was simply that at some point on the fifth day he noticed the road had gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sound, and then noticed that he had been noticing it for an hour.

The trees along the verge stopped. Not all at once and not in a line — they thinned, and then there were only the small hard ones, and then there were none of those either and the road ran out across open ground with nothing on it but grass the colour of a dirty sheep.

The grass was wrong too. It grew in patches with bare ground between, and the bare ground was not earth. It was pale grey and very fine and it took a bootprint and kept it.

"Don't kick it up," said Rook.

"What is it?"

"It's four years of a hunting ground," he said. "This is the north lip of the workings and everything anybody's killed up here since the tear opened has gone into this ground. It's full. That's all that is. A ground gets to a point where it won't take any more and then it sits on top."

"It's not *doing* anything," said Wyck.

"No. It's ash, and ash is the leftovers of a thing with nobody home, and it does not do anything at all." Rook kept walking. "It's a nuisance, is what it is. Keep out of it and don't breathe it and don't harvest off this ground, because whatever you pull up here will be four years old and mixed with dirt and it'll weigh dirty at the gate."

Dessa had stopped.

---

She was standing in the middle of the road with her chin up and her eyes open on nothing at all, and she stayed that way for long enough that Wyck went back for her.

"Cole."

"Give me a minute."

"Have you got something?"

"No," said Dessa. "That's what I'm telling you. I haven't got *anything* and I can't work out whether that's because there's nothing or because there's too much." Her hands had come up, and she was holding them out from her sides a little, the way a person does on ice. "It's like — right. All right. You know when a room's got a lot of people talking in it, and you can't pick out one voice, and you'd say the room was loud. This isn't loud. This is a room where everybody's saying the same word."

"Range?"

"I've got eighty feet," she said. "Eighty. I had three hundred at the weir."

Rook set the shafts down.

"Say that again."

"Eighty feet and it isn't dropping off with distance, it's just stopping. I put it out and it goes eighty feet and it hits the same thing everywhere at once." She brought her hands down. "I'm not broken. I want that said before anybody says it kindly at me. The thing I do still works. There's nothing left in this country for it to work *on*."

"You're not broken," said Rook. "You're standing in a field full of shouting and I should have told you it was coming and I didn't, because I've never been out here with a sensor and I didn't know." He said it flatly and immediately, without any apparatus round it, which Toren had noticed he did about once a month and which was worth more than an apology. "Right. New rule, and it's the whole of the next three days.

"Everything goes in your pockets. Embers away. Nobody lights anything for any reason. Nobody drills, nobody practises, nobody puts up so much as an inch of it to see whether they still can. Cole keeps her eighty feet and that's the only worked power on this road until I say different, and I'm allowing it because she can't put it down any more than she can stop hearing."

"And if something comes?" said Toren.

"Then it comes at the nearest one, and the nearest one will be her, and that is exactly why she walks in the middle from now on and none of you argue with me about it once."

---

Toren put his father's Ember in his coat at about four in the afternoon on the fifth day and did not take it out again.

He had not been without it in his hand for any waking hour in three weeks. He had drilled while walking, while eating, while arguing, while pulling a shaft; two feet of white standing out of his fist had become as much a part of the road as the road. His hand felt like somebody else's for the rest of that day.

He understood, at about dusk, that he was frightened in a way he had not been since the pumphouse, and that the reason was not the country. It was that Rook had taken the tool away.

---

They found the Flask on the morning of the sixth day.

It was lying half off the crown of the road with its limbs folded under it in the wrong number of places, already gone, and the shape of it was still there in the grey on the ground the way the shape of a burned beam stays in a fire.

Rook crouched down beside it for some time.

"How long?" said Wyck.

"Day. Day and a half." He put a finger into it and took it out and looked at what was on the finger. "That's a Flask. That's a good clean Flask and a fair one, and somebody put it down properly with one stroke from the left side."

"Then why's it still here?"

"Because whoever did it didn't take the ash," said Rook.

He stood up and looked north along the road, and then south, and then out across the grey ground.

"And there's not a Flask's worth on this road," he said. "There's about a Handful. So they took some and left the rest, which is the single stupidest thing I have ever heard of anybody doing in this country, and there is no version of a hunter who kills a Flask with one stroke and then harvests it like a child picking blackberries."

"Rell's people?" said Dessa.

"Rell's people went north four days ago with three carts and they'd never have left it." He was still looking out at the grey. "Somebody's out here working poorly. That's what it is. Somebody's out here who's frightened, and frightened people harvest poorly, and I'd rather it were that than anything else I can think of."

Toren, standing eleven feet away with his hands in his coat, looked at the ground where the ash had not gone in.

Then he looked at the ground beside it.

There was a patch there, about four feet across, where the grey was gone. Not scuffed and not blown — gone, clean down to wet dark earth with grass starting in it, and it had a soft edge all the way round like a puddle drying.

"Rook."

"Yes."

"It's gone in there."

Rook came over and looked at it and put the flat of his hand down on the dark ground and held it there, and after a moment he took it off and wiped it on his coat.

"Good," he said. "That's the ground still working. That's what I wanted to see."

They went on south.

Toren looked back once, from about two hundred paces, because something about the shape of it had not sat right, and from two hundred paces he could see four more patches of dark ground between the Flask and the lip of the workings, all about the same size, all clean down to the earth.

They were in a line.

He thought about mentioning it and did not, because he could not have said what he would be mentioning, and because he had been wrong out loud twice that week already.

He was never afterward able to decide whether it would have made any difference.

---

They camped that night in the angle of a concrete wall that had been a weighbridge house, with no fire, eating cold, with the barrow backed into the corner and the four of them lying across the open side of it.

Senna was awake.

"Say it," she said, to the dark.

"Say what."

"Whatever it is you've been keeping behind your teeth since noon, Elias. You've done nine miles with a jaw like a gate bolt."

Rook was quiet for a moment.

"There's a thing in the Drownings," he said. "I've thought so since the road and I've been certain since the Flask, and I've spent the afternoon trying to find a way that I'm wrong, and I can't.

"Two people who knew their work went down in daylight and left a hand of ash each. There's a Flask on the road with one stroke through it and most of it gone. There's a ground full to the top for four years and then five clean patches in a line running down to the lip. And there's a woman who's been at this twenty-two years who walked off the best contract of her life and is going to spend the rest of it paying the Register back, and who looked at me on that road and told me not to go, and I have met exactly four people in my life who would have done that."

"And?"

"And it's not a Flask and it's not a lot of Flask," said Rook. "That's a Barrel."

Nobody moved.

Toren lay on his side in the dark and found that the word had gone into him in a different place from where words usually went. He had heard it every week since Tallow Gate. It was what a warden said over a scale. It was a grade and a price and a thing men argued about in yards, and in seven weeks he had never once heard it used about something that was still alive.

"You've seen one," said Wyck.

"I've seen two," said Rook. "One at nineteen, from about a mile off, going away, and I have never been so glad of anything. And one at thirty-four, with eleven other people, and four of them were better at this than I am and two of them didn't walk out."

"What is it?"

"I don't know," said Rook. "That's the honest answer and you're all going to get the honest answer tonight because tomorrow you'll get orders instead. Nobody knows what any of them are. Homura had eleven years and nine thousand pictures and the best it ever got to was a guess, and the guess was that the things that come through aren't creatures at all — that they're the stuff itself, the same stuff that's in your hands and in her hands and in that ground, come through a hole with nothing to hold it in a shape and nobody home inside it.

"And the guess went on: that the different kinds are different kinds because power does different things when people work it, and these are those same things gone loose with nobody doing them." He shifted against the wall. "Which means every one of them is a thing somebody, somewhere, does on purpose. And when you meet a new one, the useful question is never what is it. It's *what does it look like a person doing*."

"And that one?"

"I'll tell you tomorrow," said Rook, "when I've seen it."

---

At first light he stood up on the wall of the weighbridge house and looked south, and the rest of them came up after him.

The road ran on for two miles across the grey ground and then went over a lip and down.

Past the lip there was a great open bite out of the country, a mile and a half across, with sheer grey faces stepping down into it and standing water in the bottom of it the colour of the sky. A spine of stone ran straight out across the middle of the water from the near side to the far side, pale and narrow and perfectly level, with the flooded workings forty feet deep on both sides of it.

And nine miles beyond the far lip, small and low and quite clear in the flat morning air, there was a ring of lights.

They were not in a wall. That was the thing Toren could not make sit right, and he stood on the concrete for a long time working at it. They were out in the open ground, on poles, in a circle perhaps half a mile across with nothing inside it and nothing beside it, and they had been burning all night in empty country with nobody anywhere near them.

"What are those?" said Dessa.

"Those are Meridian," said Rook.
