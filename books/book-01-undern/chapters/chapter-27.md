# Chapter 27 — Intake

They took about half a cask off the tip road and left the rest.

It was all they could carry. They had one canvas and two empty supply bundles and a barrow with a woman in it, and they filled what they had by scraping the drifts off the quarry block with a tin plate and the flat of a boot sole, and it took forty minutes, and Rook sat on the parapet the whole time and gave instructions and did not lift anything.

"There's a cask and a half on that road," said Wyck.

"There is."

"Somebody'll come and take it."

"Somebody will," said Rook. "Good luck to them. I've had enough off it."

---

They walked all night, because Rook would not stop.

He set the pace himself for the first two miles and then stopped setting it and let Dessa set it, which he had never done, and nobody said anything about that either. Toren rode the tailboard for four miles and then got off it and walked, poorly, and was allowed to.

At about three in the morning Rook fell back and walked beside him for a while.

"Voss."

"Yes."

"You had both of them on that ramp."

Toren did not say anything. He had known it since about two seconds after it stopped, and he had been carrying it beside him for eleven hours the way you carry a thing you cannot look at while you are moving.

"Nine seconds," said Rook. "Maybe eleven. You came forty yards of stone with a loaded barrow and you took a Flask on the way past and you did it poorly, and the frame was up your legs the whole of it and there were two feet of white in your hand the whole of it, and I watched you do it from sixty yards with something on top of me and I want you to know that I saw it, because I'd have been sorry all my life if I hadn't."

"What is it?"

"What's what?"

"What's it called," said Toren.

"It isn't called anything," said Rook.

They walked about thirty paces.

"People have got a word for what it looks like from the outside," he said. "I'm not giving it to you and I'll tell you exactly why, and then you can be angry about it for a week like you were about the other one. It's because the word describes what somebody watching you can see, and you'd start trying to be it. You'd go out on a verge every night for a year trying to get back to a thing you did once when you had no choice, and you'd get nothing, and you'd decide you'd lost it.

"So here's what you're getting instead, and it's true and it's the whole of it.

"You're Flame. You were Flame at that weir and you're Flame now and nothing that happened on that road moved you a rung. What you did wasn't a bigger amount of what you had. It was a different thing, and it isn't on the ladder anywhere, and you may not do it again for a year and you may do it on Thursday, and either of those is normal."

"Then what was it?"

"It was you stopping," said Rook.

"Stopping what?"

"Picking." He had his hands in his pockets, which he could only do with one of them. "You've been told since you were six that it's one at a time. Your father was told. I was told. Everybody who has ever held one of those things has been told, and it is a perfectly good rule for anybody who believes the thing in their hand is doing the work, because a stick can only be one shape at once.

"You didn't stop believing it tonight. You stopped believing it about five weeks ago under a stone arch in the rain and you've agreed with me about it every day since. Tonight was the first time in your life you were too busy to remember."

---

The lamp line came up on them at about half past four.

Toren had been looking at it for three hours as a low ring of small orange sparks on a black plain, and he had built a whole idea of it in his head on the walk — poles and oil and somebody with a ladder — and the idea did not survive the first one they came within fifty feet of.

It was a post of welded pipe about nine feet high with a flat iron plate bolted across the top of it, and standing on the plate, unsupported, needing nothing, was a light.

It was about the size of a fist. It was pale and steady and it did not gutter and it did not drift and there was nothing under it and nothing in it, and it sat on a cold iron plate nine feet in the air in the middle of empty ground at half past four in the morning, and it did not need anybody at all.

Toren stopped walking.

"Voss," said Rook, from ahead.

He could not make his legs do anything.

There were two hundred of them. He could see the whole ring from here, a circle of small pale lights half a mile across with a black wall inside it and nothing between, and every single one of them was sitting on a plate on a post out in the open country where anything that came looking for worked power would find it first.

"They're all his," he said.

"They're not his."

"They're *his*." His voice had gone. "That's the — he did that on a stone above a winding-house. He did it on a fence rail on the grade and he did it on the arch and everybody in Coldwater told him for two years it was the most useless thing anybody had ever come out of a Kindling with, and it's—"

"I know what it is," said Rook.

"There's two hundred of them."

"There's two hundred and forty and there's a trade for it and they've had it here forty years, and they send people out at dusk to set them and out at dawn to bring the posts in and look at the ground, and it is the single most dangerous job on this continent and every one of the people doing it is thirteen to nineteen years old and can do one thing." He came back and stood next to Toren in the dark. "Nobody ever told him. There wasn't anybody in Coldwater to tell him. That's all that is and it's not any smaller for being ordinary."

"Would they have taken him?"

"Yes," said Rook.

Toren stood in the grass and looked at two hundred and forty lights burning in empty country.

"Don't," said Rook.

"I'm not."

"You are. You're standing there doing the arithmetic where he's alive and it's four months from now and he's out here on a post line with a bag over his shoulder being pleased with himself, and I'd like you to put it down, because I have done that arithmetic about a woman four thousand times and it has never once come out." He put his good hand on Toren's shoulder, which he had done perhaps twice in eight weeks. "Walk. There's a gate."

---

The gate was a cut through a berm eleven feet high faced with broken concrete, with a ditch in front of it and two sets of doors, and between the sets of doors there was a yard about the size of a mill yard, roofed, with lamps in it that were only lamps.

They were put in the yard and the outer doors were shut behind them and they stood there for twenty minutes.

Nobody was rude. That was the thing Toren had not expected and could not get his hands round. A young man brought out a bench and set it against the wall for Senna and did not say anything to anybody; a woman came and looked at Rook's side through his coat without touching it and went away again at a run; somebody brought water in a bucket with a tin dipper, and the water was clean and cold and there was no limit on it.

And nothing happened, and nothing went on happening, and the inner doors stayed shut.

"They're looking at us," said Dessa.

"They're looking at us."

"From where?"

"From about four places," said Rook, "and I'd leave off finding out which."

---

The proctor's name was Nye and he came out at ten past five with three ledgers and a clerk and a folding table.

He was about forty, narrow, with reading glasses on a cord and ink on the side of his hand, and he set the table up himself and squared it to the wall before he sat down at it, and Toren, watching him do it, had the sudden violent sensation of standing in a tunnel under a fallen fuel tank with his father four days dead.

"Right," said Proctor Nye. "This takes about an hour and I'm going to do the whole of it even though one of you is bleeding, and I'll tell you why now so we don't have to argue about it in twenty minutes. The physician is standing on the other side of that door. She does not come through it until this is done. That is not me being hard, it's the rule, and the rule exists because the last time somebody made an exception at this gate, forty people died inside a fortnight." He opened the first ledger. "Names."

They gave their names.

He wrote down Coldwater and the date and the milepost. He wrote down Senna Voss, eighty-one; Toren Voss, thirteen; Dessa Cole, fifteen; Wyck Tanner, fifteen. He asked Dessa how to spell Cole, and Dessa said, "C, O, L, E," in a voice that did something at the end of it, and Nye looked up once and then went back to writing.

"And you."

"Elias Marsh."

"Marsh." The pen went along. "Age."

"Forty-one."

"Origin."

"A village called Ferrow, about four hundred and ten miles north. It's on your charter list and it's been lapsed nine years."

Nye wrote that down too.

---

"Kindled status, each of you, and I'll want it plain. Door and stage."

"Ember, Flame," said Wyck.

"Magic side," said Dessa. "Sensing mostly. Some closing. Flame."

"Ember, Flame," said Toren.

"Nothing," said Senna Voss, from the bench, "and I'd like it noted that I've had eighty-one years of people finding that disappointing."

"Noted," said Nye, and wrote it, and then looked at Rook.

"Ember and magic," said Rook. "Both."

The clerk's pen stopped. Nye's did not.

"Stage."

"Further up."

"I need a word, Mr Marsh."

"Then put down that I wouldn't give you one," said Rook, "and put down that I've been asked at this gate and asked properly, and that I've had nine years of not answering it and I'm not going to start at five in the morning with a hole in me. You'll get a better answer off your own physician in about an hour than you'll get off me."

Nye looked at him for two seconds and then wrote, in a small neat hand, *declined*, and went on.

---

"Devices."

"Three Embers," said Rook.

"Put them on the table."

Wyck put his on the table. Toren put his father's on the table, the grey rod the length of his forearm, worn glass-smooth in a band round the middle where four generations of Voss thumbs had sat in exactly the same place, and it made a small sound on the wood.

Rook took Vera's out of his coat and put it down beside them.

"They go into the gate store and they're tagged and they stay there," said Nye. "You get a receipt. You get them back when you leave, or on a call-out, and not otherwise, and there is no version of this where anybody works power inside that wall. I'll say the reason once, because everybody wants it and nobody believes it until they're told. There are four thousand people behind that berm and one of the reasons they're alive is that from out on that plain, this place is dark. The lights are out there. We put them out there. We spend seven or eight young people a year keeping them out there." He wrote the three Embers into the ledger. "You light anything inside the wall and you are a lamp in the middle of four thousand people. That's all it is. It isn't about what you are."

"It's a bit about what we are," said Wyck.

"Yes," said Proctor Nye. "It is a bit."

---

"Last item, and then the physician. Anything else on any of you that's Kindled work, rift-made, pre-Fall, or that you can't account for. Tools, objects, anything you're carrying that you can't explain."

Nobody said anything.

Toren stood in a roofed yard at twenty past five in the morning with his hand flat over two rows of stitches and understood, with complete clarity, that he could say no.

He could say no and the man would write nothing and they would open the door and his grandmother would go in. Nobody in that yard would ever know. Rook would know and Rook would not say one word about it ever, because Rook had put the whole of it in his hands at a junction and told him it was his.

He thought about his father's hands closing his fingers over it in wet grass, one at a time.

*You put it in a river and you don't tell anyone where.*

"I've got something," he said.

---

He put it on the table.

It was about the size of the round bottom of a cup and it was heavier than it had any business being, and it was some dark metal that was not quite any colour, and nine narrow channels ran across the face of it in a pattern that would not resolve.

Proctor Nye looked at it for some time without touching it.

"What is it?"

"I don't know."

"What does it do?"

"Nothing," said Toren. "It's never done anything. It's been in my family five generations and not one of us has ever got a single useful thing out of it."

"Where's it from?"

"I don't know that either."

Nye took his glasses off.

"Son," he said, "I've had thirty-one people through this gate in the last four days and eleven of them told me they were carrying a thing that would change the world, and every one of those eleven is now sitting in the outer sheds waiting on a hearing, because I write down what they tell me and then somebody senior comes and is disappointed. You've told me you've got a lump of metal that does nothing and you don't know where it's from. That's a worse answer and it's going to cost you a great deal more, and I'd like to know whether you understand that before I put the pen on the page."

"Yes," said Toren.

"Why are you telling me?"

Toren thought about it properly, because the man had asked properly.

"Because there's a ledger at Tallow Gate with my father's name in it," he said, "and the woman who wrote it told us it comes here twice a year and doesn't get lost. And I've spent eight weeks finding out what it costs to be the only person who knows something."

Nye put his glasses back on.

He wrote for about a minute and a half. Then he set the pen down, and picked up a small brass bell off the corner of the table, and rang it twice.

---

The inner doors opened at half past five and after that it went very fast.

The physician was a broad short woman of about fifty-five called Kell, and she went to Rook first because Rook was the one bleeding, and Rook said, "The old woman," and Kell said, "The old woman has had twenty years of it and you've had eleven hours, don't be a child," and got his coat open.

Senna went in on a wheeled chair with two people pushing it.

Toren went as far as the inner doors with her and was stopped there, and his grandmother reached out and got hold of his sleeve and pulled him down and said, into his ear, very clearly:

"Straighten up. Walk like people who've been somewhere."

And then she was gone down a corridor with light in it, electric light, steady and white and coming out of the ceiling, more light than Toren Voss had seen in the whole of his life put together.

---

Registrar Helen Quist came into the yard at six.

She was about sixty, tall, in a grey coat, with a face that did not do anything, and she had Nye's page in her hand and she read it once more standing up before she said a word to anybody.

"Cole," she said. "Tanner. Voss. Marsh."

"That's us," said Wyck.

"I know it's you." She folded the page. "I'm going to say three things and I'd like them heard in order.

"First. The woman is admitted. That's done and it isn't a favour and it isn't reversible and nobody in this yard has to be pleasant to me about it. She'll be seen properly this morning and somebody will come out and tell you what they've found, and it will be the truth, and you will not like all of it.

"Second." She looked at the four of them. "I've had two hundred and eleven people at that gate since the harvest. Nine days ago I had a family off the fen, eleven of them, grandmother down to an infant, and when my proctor told them to stand apart and put their hands where he could see them it took four minutes and they got it wrong twice and one of them cried.

"My people put you in that yard at four in the morning and shut a door on you. You went to the four corners of it in about a second and a half. The girl took the side with no light on it. The lame one put himself between the bench and the doors. Nobody looked at anybody and nobody said one word, and then you all stood still for twenty minutes like people who have stood still before."

"We're not a company," said Rook.

"No," said Registrar Quist. "You're worse than a company. A company I can write to."

---

"Third," she said.

She held up the folded page.

"You came up the south haul road last night out of a country that put eleven experienced haulers to flight inside a week, with two casualties, one non-ambulant, and half a cask of Barrel-grade on a handcart. I've had a runner down to the Drownings since first light and he came back an hour ago and told me there's a cask and a half of it lying on the tip road in drifts and eleven separate places on eighty yards of stone where something enormous went down and got up again.

"So I have four Kindled, one of them a man who won't give me a stage, a child of thirteen, and a dead Barrel, and I have to decide by tonight what the five of you are. And I want you to understand that I am not frightened of you and I am not suspicious of you and I have not got one single thing against any of you personally." She put the page in her coat. "I've got a wall with four thousand people behind it, and my whole job is knowing what's inside it. That's the entire matter and there is nothing underneath it."

She turned to go, and then stopped, the way people do.

"The object," she said.

Toren's stomach went.

"It's yours and it stays yours and nobody in this enclave is going to take it off a child at a gate," said Quist. "But it's in my intake now, in my proctor's hand, with a date on it. And I'm going to go and find out whether anybody has ever written it down here before."

"Has anybody?"

"I've no idea," said Registrar Helen Quist. "That's what a register's for."
