# Chapter 14 — The Split

It was Senna who saw it, because Senna had nothing else to do.

She had been in a box since the fifth of the month and she had said so twice a week for five weeks, and what nobody had worked out until that morning was that a woman lying on her back in a barrow for five weeks with her head at the level of the tailboard sees a great deal of country and sees all of it slowly.

"There's two of them," she said.

They had been walking an hour. The grade had come up off the flat onto a low swell of ground and the whole country ahead had opened out: fields gone to rough pasture, a line of willow where water was, and the embankment running away north-west through the middle of it in the same dead-straight line it had held for two hundred miles.

"Two of what?" said Milo.

"Two lines." Senna lifted her chin off the blanket. "Look at the far side of the willows and stop looking at the near side. There's the bank we're on, and there's another one."

Toren looked.

It took him a while, and then he could not stop seeing it. Past the willows the ground rose again, and on the rise there were two banks, not one — the one they were walking on, holding its north-west line, and a second one peeling away from it in a long shallow curve, bending east, holding its own level across the slope until it went behind a shoulder of hill and was gone.

"That's a fork," said Dessa.

"It's a junction," Wyck said. "It's where the line split."

He had stopped in the middle of the grade with his stick planted. He was looking at it the way he looked at things: for longer than anyone else, with his head slightly down, as though the country were a page of writing that could be gotten wrong.

"What's at the bottom of it," he said.

Nobody answered him. Toren turned around, which was what he had already done with the front half of himself, and Rook was thirty paces back with the shafts of the barrow on his shoulders and his eyes on the willows, and he had stopped walking.

---

He started again about four seconds later and nobody but Toren saw it, and Toren was not sure afterward whether it had been four seconds or one, only that it had been a pause and that in a month of walking behind the man he had never seen him make one.

They went down through the willows and came up the other side and the junction was a mile and a half in front of them by noon.

It was a considerable piece of work.

That was the first thing about it, and it was the thing Toren had not expected. He had been thinking of a fork in the way you think of a fork in a road — a place where the ruts divide and somebody has scratched an arrow on a stone. This was a shape somebody had built. The single bank came up onto a broad flat and opened out into a triangle of level ground the size of the whole of Coldwater, all of it raised, all of it hand-laid, and at the far corners of the triangle two banks went out — the left one holding north-west, the right one swinging east in a curve so long and so gradual that you could stand in the middle of it and believe you were still going straight.

In the middle of the triangle, there was a brick box with no roof on it.

Along the east side there was a round stone pad thirty feet across with four bolt-stumps standing out of it, and a shallow basin in the ground beside that, filled with water and green weed, that had been something before it was a pond.

There was not one piece of iron left anywhere on any of it.

"Mother's mercy," said Senna, from the barrow, in the tone of a woman at a market. "Look at the *size* of that."

---

They did not go to it.

Rook took the barrow off the crown of the grade a mile short, on the north side, where a hedge line came up to the bank and made a corner out of the wind, and he put the shafts down in the grass and said, "We'll stop here."

It was one o'clock in the afternoon.

Milo said, "It's one o'clock in the afternoon."

"It is."

"We've done four hours. We do nine."

"We'll do four." Rook went round the barrow and started on the wheel chock without being asked to by anybody, including the wheel. "Faust, get the stove out. Cole, water — there's a ditch behind the hedge and it'll want boiling. Tanner, sit down and get the boot off that leg before I tell you twice."

Toren stood on the grade and looked at the ground Rook had picked.

He had been on that grade for a month. He had watched a man choose a camp every single night of it, and had had the reasons explained to him exactly once and had worked out the rest by watching: high enough to see, low enough not to be seen, water inside two hundred paces, a back you did not have to hold, and never, ever a place where the only way out was the way in.

This was a corner between a hedge and a bank. There was one way out of it and it was along the grade.

Nobody else said anything about it. Dessa went for the water and Milo got the stove out and Wyck sat down on the grass and started on his bootlace, and Toren stood where he was for another moment, and then went and helped with the canvas.

---

Rook walked out to the junction at five in the afternoon.

He did not say he was going. He put his coat on, which he had taken off, and went up onto the crown of the grade and started along it, and he was four hundred paces out before Milo looked up and said, "Where's he gone?"

"He's walking," said Dessa.

"He's not walking, he's—"

"He's walking, Milo."

Toren went up the bank and sat on the crown of it with his arms round his knees, and watched.

He could see the whole of it from there. He watched the small grey figure go away down the line and get smaller and arrive, after a long time, at the near corner of the triangle, and stop.

And then stand.

Toren had watched Rook stand still on twenty nights. He had watched him stand still on the bank at the wayhouse and in the yard at Tallow Gate and at the mouth of the arch in the rain, and every single one of those times the stillness had been a man looking at something. There was a quality to it that Toren had never had a word for and had absolutely no trouble recognizing: the head moving in small increments, the weight going from one foot to the other, the sense of a thing being read.

This was not that.

The figure at the corner of the junction stood with its hands in its pockets and did not move its head at all. It did that for a length of time Toren could not have estimated afterward and which was long enough for the light to change, and then it walked out into the middle of the triangle to the brick box with no roof, and stood there, and then it came back.

---

He got in at dusk and ate what Milo gave him and said nothing about any of it.

"Is it a good line?" Milo asked. "The other one. Is it—"

"They're both good lines."

"Where do they go?"

Rook ate.

Milo waited for four or five seconds, which for Milo Faust was a period of tremendous self-discipline, and then said, "Right," and got up and went to see to the stove, and Toren watched Dessa watch him go.

---

"He's not going to sleep," Wyck said.

It was full dark and the fire was down and the two of them were sitting against the bank with the hedge behind them taking the wind off. Wyck had his bad leg out straight and his hands laced on his stomach and his head back against the earth.

"He does sleep," Toren said.

"He sleeps like a man on a watch. Three hours, on his back, against a wheel, boots on. He's done it every night since the Gate." Wyck did not open his eyes. "He's not doing it tonight. He's been up on that bank since he came in and he's been standing in the same place, and I've been lying here not looking at him for about an hour, and I'd like somebody else to have noticed so I can stop feeling like I'm the mad one."

"I've noticed."

"Good."

The fire ticked. Out past it, the barrow was a black shape with Senna's breathing going in and out of it, and Milo was flat on his back with an arm over his face, and Dessa was up at the top of the camp with her boots on, holding.

"I'll tell you what I keep coming back to," Wyck said. "On the loop road he walked off the crown at a place with no turning. There was no mark. There was nothing. He came off a road at a spot in the grass like a man going through a door in his own house, and he's been on this line ever since, and he's never once looked at it like he wasn't sure."

"I know."

"He looked at it today."

Toren did not say anything.

"That's all," said Wyck. "I'm not building anything out of it. I'm just telling you what I saw, because I'd rather two of us saw it." He shifted the leg with both hands. "The other thing I'd say is that there's a fork out there, and a fork's a choice, and a man who's walked two hundred miles on purpose to get to a choice has already made it. So he isn't standing up there deciding."

"Then what's he doing?"

"I don't know," said Wyck Tanner. "That's the part I don't like."

---

Toren went up at some point after the middle of the night, because he could not sleep either, and because he had worked out about an hour before that he had not spoken to the man since noon.

Rook was on the crown of the grade with his back to the camp.

He did not turn round. Toren stopped six feet short of him and stood there, and the wind came across the flat, and the two banks out at the junction were faintly visible in the dark as two lines of nothing where the grass did not catch what light there was.

"Go to sleep," Rook said.

"Is something coming?"

"No."

"Then—"

"Voss." The man's voice did not change at all. "There's nothing out there and nothing's coming and nobody's in any danger tonight. Go and lie down."

Toren stood there a moment longer.

He had known this man for thirty-four days. In that time, he had seen him refuse a warden, dig three graves, carry an old woman out of thornbrush, put his hands inside a boy's leg for eleven minutes, cross a flooded building faster than a thought, burn a piece of slate that would not burn, and say *I don't know* twice, both times about things that mattered.

He had never once seen him look like a man with nowhere to put his hands.

"All right," Toren said, and went back down the bank, and lay on his side in the grass with his coat over him, and did not sleep for a long time either.

When he did sleep, the man was still up there.

When he woke at first light, he was still up there, and the fire was cold, and the barrow had not been loaded, and nobody was going anywhere.
