# Chapter 26 — The Drownings

"Say the failure," Rook said.

"Anybody stops on the stone," said Toren.

He said it before anyone else had drawn breath and he said it flat, and Rook looked at him for about a second and a half and then went on as if he had not.

"That's it. That's the whole of it and there's nothing underneath it.

"That road out there is eighty yards long and twelve feet wide and it is the only clean ground within four miles. Everything on this side of it is ash to a depth of about a foot, and everything on the far side is the same, and between the two of them there is a spine of quarry stone with forty feet of water on either hand and nothing in it and nothing under it and nothing for anything to come up out of.

"We go over that at noon in the middle of the day with the light straight down. We do not go fast, because going fast on that road with a barrow puts a wheel over the edge and then we have lost this before anything has even happened. We go at a walk, and we go the whole eighty yards, and nobody stops."

He put a finger up.

"And on the way down to it, and on the way off the far end, nobody lights anything and nobody kills anything. If a Flask comes at you, you give it ground. If it follows you, you give it more ground. If it puts a hand on you, you go down and you cover your head and you let it, and one of us will come and take it off you, and it will hurt and you will keep your arm."

"That's not—" Wyck stopped.

"Say it."

"That's not a thing you can promise a person they'll do."

"No," said Rook. "It isn't."

---

They came down off the lip at nine in the morning.

The haul road went down the side of the workings in four long slants with a turn at each end, and it had been cut for carts, and forty years of nothing had not done much to it. The grey came down with them. It lay in the corners of the turns in drifts a foot deep and it lay thin across the open faces, and where a wheel went through it, it did not puff up and hang the way dust does. It sat where the wheel put it.

Toren walked at the near shaft with both hands on the wood and his father's Ember in his coat pocket, and made himself look at the country.

That was Rook's rule from the second week and it had never once been relaxed: you look at ground before you are standing on it, because on it you will only ever see the four feet in front of you. So he looked. He made himself take the whole of it, the way he had taken a walkway and a weir.

The floor of the workings was a flat grey pan a quarter of a mile across with standing water over most of it and bare stone in patches where the water had gone. The tip road came off the near side at the bottom of the last slant and went out across it dead straight, built up eight or ten feet above the water on a spine of stacked block, pale and hard-edged, with no rail of any kind.

At the far end it ran up onto the south bank and there was a second haul road going up and out, and past that, over the lip, nine miles of open country and a ring of lights that were not burning now because it was morning.

And on the near side, between the bottom of the slant and the start of the stone, there was about a hundred and eighty yards of open pan.

Grey. Unbroken. Four years deep.

"There's the hundred and eighty," said Rook, without being asked. "That's the whole of it. Everything before that and everything after is somebody else's problem. That hundred and eighty yards is ours."

---

They waited at the bottom of the last slant for two hours, in the shadow of the face, and did not talk much.

Senna slept for some of it and woke and did not ask anything. Wyck sat with his bad leg out and worked the knee with both thumbs, the way he did before anything, which he had started doing at Tallow Gate and had never once been teased about by anybody.

Dessa stood.

"Anything?"

"Eighty feet of the same word," she said. "I'll tell you the second it changes."

At about half past eleven Toren said, quietly, to nobody in particular: "We'd have sent Milo up the far bank."

Wyck's thumbs stopped.

"With the light," Toren said. "On a stone at the top of the south lip, a quarter mile off the road, where anything coming out of that water would have it in front of it the whole way. And we'd have walked across this in a queue with our hands in our pockets and never seen a thing."

"Yes," said Wyck. "We would."

Neither of them said anything else about it. Rook, eleven feet away with his back against the stone face and his hat over his eyes, did not move at all, and Toren could not afterward say whether he had been listening.

---

At noon Rook stood up and picked up the shafts and they went out onto the pan.

It was better than Toren had let himself expect for about ninety seconds.

The grey was firm underfoot, packed hard by four years of carts, and the barrow ran on it; and the sun was straight down out of a white sky and there were no shadows anywhere at all; and the stone of the tip road was a clean pale line in front of them getting closer at the rate a person walks.

At a hundred and ten yards Dessa said, "Six."

She said it in the flat voice.

"Where."

"North-west. Out of the water at the edge of the pan, where it's shallow." Her chin had come up. "They're not coming at us. They're going up the slant."

"Behind us?"

"Behind us."

"Then we keep walking," said Rook, "and nobody looks round."

Toren kept walking and did not look round and discovered that not looking round took about as much of him as everything else he had ever been asked to do.

At eighty yards Dessa said, "Two of them have turned."

"Distance."

"Two hundred. Coming on the pan." A pause of three seconds. "They're faster on this than they were at the weir."

"Of course they are," said Rook. "It's a floor."

---

At fifty yards the first of them came up on the right side.

It was a Flask. Toren had its whole shape at once — slate-grey and wet-looking with the light going off it wrong, every limb with one joint too many so that it did not step but folded and unfolded forward, and the seam down its chest lit up dull like a coal under ash.

It came in at the barrow's right rear quarter, which is to say at Wyck, who was on the off shaft with his stick hung on the board because both his hands were on the wood.

He gave it ground. He did it exactly as he had been told, without one instant of argument: he let go of the shaft and went four steps left behind the barrow and put the whole width of it between them, and the thing folded round the back of the barrow after him.

And then it went past him.

Toren saw it happen from eleven feet away with the shafts in his hands. The thing came round the tailboard and it did not follow Wyck at all, it went in over the tailboard, and there was an old woman in a barrow with a splinted leg and no way of getting anywhere, and she was the nearest thing in the world with nothing in front of her.

Wyck Tanner put three feet of white light through it from below the ribs.

It came apart. The ash went up in a soft grey wash over the tailboard and over Senna's blanket and over Wyck's forearms, and he stood there with his weight all on the good leg and his mouth open and his Edge still out, and it did not go down. It hung. It sat on the air for a moment like flour thrown in a kitchen and then it settled onto the grey that was already there and lay on top of it.

"I had to," Wyck said.

"You had to," said Rook.

"She was—"

"You had to. Put it out and get back on the shaft and walk."

Wyck put it out. He got back on the shaft. They walked.

Forty yards.

---

At twenty-five yards the ash went sideways.

That was all it did. There was no sound and nothing moved on the pan and the light did not change; the grey wash lying on the ground behind the barrow simply drew off to the left, all of it, at about the speed water goes down a drain, in a thin line running north-west across the pan and away.

"Rook," said Dessa.

"I see it."

"I've lost the word."

"What?"

"The word. The everywhere one." Her hands had come up from her sides. "It's gone. There's nothing in eighty feet. There's nothing in eighty feet in any direction and there hasn't been for four seconds and that is not—"

The pan opened.

---

It did not come up the way the others came up.

The others came out of water or out of cover, folding, one part of them at a time, and you could watch it happen and know what you were looking at before it finished. This did not arrive. Sixty feet off the left side of the barrow a circle of the grey floor about four yards across went soft, and sank, and then stood up.

It stood up grey-white, the colour of the ground, and it kept standing up, and by the time it had stopped it was higher than the barrow was long.

It had the joints. That was the only thing about it Toren recognized and it was the worst thing about it, because it had them everywhere — not one extra to a limb but a dozen, twenty, more than his eye could hold, so that nothing on it folded and unfolded and the whole of it flowed instead, like a sack of water with too many corners. It had no seam down the chest. It was lit all through, dull and even, the way a bank of coals is lit under its own ash, and the light moved inside it slowly and did not come to the surface anywhere.

It had no eyes. It put itself round to face them without turning.

"Down," said Rook.

"What is it—"

"*Down.*"

They went down. Toren went flat on the grey with his cheek in it and the shaft across his back and his father's Ember in his coat under his own chest, and Dessa went down beside him with her arm over her head, and Wyck did not get down because Wyck could not get down fast, and stood at the tailboard with his hands on the wood and his face perfectly white.

Rook put the shafts down and walked out from the barrow at an angle, eleven paces, twelve, and stopped.

"Right," he said, in an entirely ordinary voice. "Now I know what it looks like a person doing."

---

Toren, with his face in the ash, heard him say it, and it took until the end of that day for him to work out what he had heard.

He had watched a man do it in a road on the fifth of the month, in the worst hour of his life, kneeling over a woman with a broken leg: a flat palm laid down on the ground with the grey still crawling in it, and the ground giving it back up, and the light coming off the earth into a hand.

He had watched him do it eleven times since.

Every kill they had ever made, every Flask and every Handful, had gone into whatever ground it fell on and stayed there. Four years of a hunting ground. Two people in the bottom of a working, with a hand of ash left where there should have been a great deal more.

There was something out here doing the only trick Rook had that the rest of them could not learn, and it had been doing it on this pan since the spring, and it had no hands and nobody home.

---

The next part took perhaps forty seconds and Toren got it in pieces.

Rook went at it. He went at it from the left with the frame coming up around his legs so fast that the grey went out from under him in a sheet, and the spear came out of Vera's Ember at half length because he was inside four feet of it before it had finished turning, and he put the whole of it through the thing at the height of a man's chest and took it out the other side.

The Breach came apart.

It came apart the way they all came apart — the shape let go all at once and the whole of it went down into grey, four yards of it, a fall of ash like a wall of grain going over, and Toren's chest did something violent and stupid that was almost relief.

And then the grey on the floor of the pan drew in, and it stood up again, fifteen feet to the left, and it was the same size it had been.

Rook was already moving. He did it again, from the other side, and it came apart again, and it stood up again eleven feet further on toward the tip road, and this time it was very slightly smaller and Toren did not notice that at all.

"It doesn't—" Dessa's voice had gone up. "It doesn't *die*—"

"It dies," said Rook. He had come back to the barrow at a flat run and got the shafts up in one motion. "It dies exactly the way everything else out here dies. It just doesn't stay down while it's standing in a field of itself."

"Then—"

"Then we do the thing we were already doing," said Rook, "faster."

---

Twenty-five yards of grey to the stone, and the thing was between them and it.

That was the arithmetic and everybody standing on that pan had it at the same moment. It had come apart twice and each time it had stood up nearer the tip road than it had gone down, and it had not done that on purpose because it could not do anything on purpose, and it did not matter in the least that it could not.

"Cole," said Rook. "Take the near shaft. Tanner, the off. Voss, on the tailboard and push and do not stop pushing whatever you hear.

"You are going for the stone. You are going for the stone if I go down and you are going for the stone if somebody screams and you are going for the stone if the whole of this pan comes up behind you, and when your feet are on it you keep walking, because this is only the first half."

"What's the second half?" said Wyck.

"The second half is eighty yards," said Rook, and went.

---

Toren pushed.

He had the tailboard in both hands and his boots in four years of ash and his grandmother eighteen inches in front of his face, facing backward, watching the whole of it happen over his shoulder, and he pushed, and the wheels turned, and it was the slowest thing he had ever been part of.

Behind him and to the left there was a man doing something enormous.

He could not see it. He got it in noise and in what the light did on the grey in front of him — a wash of white going up and going out, and going up again, and the flat hard sound Rook's spear made going into something, and the long soft roar of four yards of ash falling over. Three times. Four.

Each time the roar came, Toren pushed harder, because each time the roar came it meant the thing was down for about two seconds.

On the fourth one he heard Rook shout something that was not a word.

"He's put it in the water," Senna said.

"What?"

"He's driven it off the west side into the water and it's gone in and it hasn't come out." His grandmother had both hands on the front board and she was up off the blankets on her elbows, eighty-one years old, looking backward down a quarter mile of open pan. "Don't look round. Push."

Toren pushed.

The wheels went from grey onto stone with a change of sound like a door shutting, and all four of them were on the tip road.

---

Rook came on last, backward, at a walk, with the frame still burning round his legs and the spear at half length in his right hand.

"It'll come," he said. "It's not done. Keep walking."

"Why did you—"

"Because it can't do that on stone." He was breathing like a man who has been carrying something up a hill. "It goes down into a ground and it comes up out of a ground and both of those need a ground. There isn't one out here. Eighty yards of block and water on both hands and it has got nothing to go into and nothing to come out of, and if it wants us it has to walk, and it is enormous, and it will be slow." He put a hand flat on the parapet block for a second and took it off again. "That's what the road's for. That's what it's been for since I saw it off the weighbridge yesterday morning."

"Is that certain?" said Dessa.

"No," said Rook. "It's the best I've got and I've had a day to make it better and I couldn't."

They walked.

---

Twenty yards out along the stone, Toren looked down over the edge.

He had not meant to. The water was eight or ten feet below the road and it went down from there into a green-black nothing and there was old machinery in it — the top of a gantry standing up out of the surface about forty feet off, red-brown, with a bird on it.

There was ash on the water.

It lay on the surface in a wide grey film, not sinking, spreading slowly out from a place near the west bank and moving toward the road, and Toren watched it for two whole seconds before anything in him understood what he was looking at.

"Rook—"

The stone came up.

It came up out of the road itself, out of the packed grey and grit that forty years of carts had ground into the joints between the blocks, eleven feet ahead of the barrow — not four yards of it this time but half that, thin and wrong-shaped and already sliding apart at the edges, and it did not matter, because it was directly in front of the near wheel and there was water on both sides.

Dessa went off the road.

She did not fall. She was on the near shaft and it swung, and she let go of it because holding it would have taken the barrow with her, and she went over the edge sideways into forty feet of water with her arms out, and the last thing Toren saw of her was her face, which had nothing on it but a decision.

---

"Go round it!" Rook was coming up the road at a run. "Voss — the shaft — *go round*—"

There was no round. There was twelve feet of stone and a thing standing in nine of it.

Wyck Tanner came off the off shaft and forward and put three feet of white light into the side of it, and it came apart, and there was grey all over the road, and it started to draw in again.

"Don't let it lie!" Rook shouted. "Kick it in the water — kick it *off*—"

Wyck kicked it off. He did it on one leg, holding the parapet block with both hands, sweeping the grey off the edge of the road with the side of his boot in long flat strokes, and where the grey went into the water it went out flat and thin and stopped being anything.

And then he did it again, further up, moving backward along the stone on the stick with three feet of light still standing out of his fist — moving, and holding it, which he had never in his life been able to do and did not notice he was doing until four days later.

---

Behind them, at the near end of the road, the pan stood up.

Toren had the shafts. He had both of them, alone, because Dessa was in the water and Wyck was eleven feet ahead of him sweeping ash off stone with his boot, and he had the barrow moving at the pace one thirteen-year-old boy can move a loaded barrow on the flat, which was a walk, which was not enough.

At the near end of the tip road the whole of it came up together, all of it at once, taller than it had been on the pan because it had taken back everything it had lost — and it came onto the stone.

It came on at about the pace of a man walking quickly.

Rook turned round on the road and went back at it and Toren heard him go, and that was the last help there was going to be, because sixty yards behind him one man was standing in twelve feet of gap with an old woman's Ember, and forty yards in front of him, where the tip road ran up onto the south bank and the second haul road began, a Flask came up out of the shallows and folded itself into the only way off.

---

Nine seconds. He worked it out afterward and it was probably eleven.

He had the shafts in both hands. He had two feet of Edge in his coat pocket, four inches of reach away, and the thing on the bank in front of him was an ordinary Flask, a plain one, the kind he had put down twice at the weir and once in the birch, and he could have gone through it on any other afternoon of his life.

To do that he had to let go of the shafts.

If he let go of the shafts the barrow stopped, and if the barrow stopped he was standing still on eighty yards of stone with a thing behind him that was coming at the speed of a man walking quickly.

To not let go of the shafts he had to move faster than he could move.

He had Stride. He had six nights of it and five seconds of it standing still and not one single step, and it was in his legs, and his hands were full, and the two of them lived in the same place and you got one.

*You pick*, his father had said, in the wet grass under a broken overpass on the last good night of his life, with the fire behind him and the whole valley going dark blue. *One at a time. Never both. You pick, and you live with what you picked until you can put it down and pick again.*

*Why never both?*

*Because that's not how it works.*

And then the other thing. The thing Garrick Voss had said next, across the top of the fire's heat, in a voice Toren had not understood at the time and had carried in his coat for eight weeks without ever once taking it out and looking at it.

*That's what I was told, too.*

---

He did not reach and he did not ask and he did not decide which foot.

He stopped picking.

The white in his fist did not go out, because it was not in his fist, and it never had been. The frame came up his legs hip to heel and it did not take anything with it on the way. The shafts went light in his hands the way a full bucket goes light when somebody else takes the handle.

He was never afterward able to tell anybody what it was like, because from the inside it was not like anything at all. There was a very large quiet room at the back of a house he had been living in for thirteen years, and he had been standing in the doorway of it since the fifth of the month, and he walked in.

Then he was doing forty yards of stone with a loaded barrow behind him and his grandmother shouting something he could not hear.

---

He took the Flask on the run and he took it wrong.

That was the honest account of it and it was the one he gave, every time he was ever asked, for the rest of his life. He did not have a stroke ready and he did not pick a side and he went in too high, and two feet of white light went through the shoulder of the thing instead of the middle of it, and it came apart anyway because they come apart, and the ash went up and over him and into his mouth and his eyes, and he did not stop and could not have.

The wheels came off the stone onto the ramp of the south haul road.

He got four more paces up the slope and the frame went out.

It did not fade. It went out the way a lamp goes out, all of it at once, and his legs were legs again and they had nothing whatsoever in them, and he went down on the ramp between the shafts with the barrow running back on him and did not have one thing left to do about it.

The barrow stopped.

Wyck Tanner had come up the ramp behind him on a stick and got both hands on the tailboard and put his whole weight into it, standing on one leg, and he held it, and he was shouting, and Toren could not hear any of that either.

---

The last of it happened sixty yards away and Toren watched it lying on his side on a quarry ramp with his cheek on the stone.

The Breach came up the tip road and Rook did not let it past.

That was all it was. A man on twelve feet of pale block, going backward, and a thing the height of a house coming on at the speed of a man walking quickly, and the man putting white light into it every time it got inside four feet — and every time, the thing coming apart, and going down, and having nothing to go down into.

It came apart eleven times on that road.

It stood up eleven times, and each time it stood up smaller, because each time a quarter of what it was went off the edges of eighty yards of stone into forty feet of standing water, and water is not a ground, and there is nothing in it to come back out of.

On the eleventh it stood up about the size of a dog.

Rook put the spear through it and it went to ash on the blocks and it lay there, warm and grey, with the light crawling in it, and it did not go anywhere, because there was nowhere for it to go.

Then Rook put a hand on the parapet and sat down.

---

Dessa came up out of the water on the east side of the south bank at about twenty past one, with her coat gone and one boot gone and a cut across the back of her head, having swum sixty yards in her clothes and climbed a quarry face, and the first thing she did on getting to the top of it was look for the barrow, and the second thing she did was start running.

Rook was still sitting on the parapet block when she got to him. He had his left arm across his front and his hand inside his coat, and the coat was wet through from the bottom of the ribs down on that side, and it had gone through the coat and out onto the stone.

"Move your hand," said Dessa Cole.

He moved his hand.

She worked for a long time. Toren, who by then had been got up the ramp and propped against a wheel and could see out of about half of one eye, watched her do it and could not have said one useful thing about it. What he did see, and remembered, was the moment about four minutes in when she stopped and sat back on her heels and put both palms flat on her own knees and breathed twice, and then went back in — and did not go and lie down afterward, because that had stopped being something that happened to her a month ago on a riverbank.

"That's the ends together," she said at last.

"That'll do."

"That is not the ends together. That is the ends nearly together and the rest of it wants somebody with a table and a light." Her voice was completely level and her hands were shaking. "Why can't you close it?"

"Because there isn't any left," said Rook.

"There's—" She looked round at the road. There was a great deal of ash on it. There was more ash on eighty yards of quarry block than any of them had ever seen in one place in their lives, more than Braid had had in her whole tunnel, lying in drifts against the parapet and refusing, still, to go anywhere.

"There it is. It's right there. Take it up, you've done it a hundred—"

"No," said Rook.

"Why?"

"Because that is exactly what it did," he said, "and I have spent the last hour watching what happens to something that stands on a road and draws ash up out of the ground with nobody home inside it, and I am going to sit here and bleed instead."

---

Nobody moved for a while after that.

Senna Voss said, from the barrow, "Elias."

"I'm here."

"Grade it."

"What?"

"Grade it," said his grandmother. "You've been grading things at me since the fifth of the month. Somebody in this family is going to stand on that road and say the word out loud and it is not going to be one of the children."

Rook turned his head and looked at eighty yards of stone with the grey lying on it in drifts.

"That's a Barrel," he said.

"Say the rest."

"That's two casks. It's two casks and it's clean and it's the best weighing anybody will see out of this country in ten years, and it's nine miles from a gate that buys." He put his head back against the parapet. "And I'd give the whole of it to have a table and a light."

---

They came up onto the south lip at about five in the afternoon: an old woman in a barrow, a boy of fifteen on a stick pulling it with a girl who had lost a boot, a man walking behind holding his own side, and Toren Voss riding on the tailboard because he could not be made to stand up.

The sun went down in front of them, out across nine miles of flat open country.

And while they watched, in a wide ring half a mile across, out in the empty ground with nobody near them and nothing inside them, one at a time and then all together, the lamps came on.
