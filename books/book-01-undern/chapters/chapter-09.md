# Chapter 9 — The Same Room

It rained from before light and it did not look like stopping, and at eight in the morning Rook put the cart under the arch where the grade crossed a stream and said they were not walking today.

The arch was a stone bridge with the railway on top of it and the water going through underneath, and there was a dry shelf of gravel along one side of the channel wide enough for six people and a cart if nobody wanted to be comfortable. The rain came down past both ends of it in two grey sheets. Inside, it was dim and loud and almost warm.

Milo got the stove going. Senna had a blanket up to her chin and her leg out along the boards and said the arch was the best room she had been in since the fifth of the month, which was a joke and was also true.

Rook sat on a stone with his back to the wall and let them get settled, and then he said:

"Voss. On the first night at the Gate you asked me a question and I told you to go to sleep."

Toren looked up.

"Ask it again."

---

"Why do they come at the light?"

"That's not the question. That's the question you asked. Ask the real one."

Toren sat with his hands around a tin cup and thought about it, and the rain came down at both ends of the arch, and he found that he was frightened, which made no sense at all.

"Why do they come at worked power," he said. "Ember or magic. No difference. That's what you said at Tallow Gate — you said *Ember, magic, doesn't matter which*, and nobody asked you about it, because it's a rule and rules are just true." He turned the cup around. "But it shouldn't work. Wyck's Ember is a piece of metal his aunt bought off a hunter. Dessa hasn't got anything. Milo's light comes out of his hand. Those are three completely different things and something with no eyes is coming at all three of them like they're the same thing."

Nobody moved.

"So the question is why they can't tell the difference," Toren said.

"No," said Rook. "That's still not it. That's the last easy one, though, so I'll give it to you for free." He leaned his head back against the stone. "They can tell the difference between a fire and a fox. They can tell the difference between a cart and a man. There's nothing wrong with what they use for seeing. The question is *what's actually the same* about the three of you that something with no eyes can pick out at eighty feet in the dark."

---

"Faust."

"Yes."

"Make your light."

Milo put out his hand and the little pale plum of it came up in his palm, steady as a held stone, and sat there.

"Now tell me what you do to make it come."

"I just—" Milo stopped. He looked at his own hand with an expression Toren had never seen on him. "That's a horrible question."

"It is. Take your time."

Milo took his time. The rain went on. Dessa had pulled her knees up and was watching him the way you watch someone carry something heavy across a room.

"All right," Milo said at last. "You know how you can make your ears go? Not wiggle them. The thing *behind* your ears, when you're listening hard on purpose and you sort of — open up sideways. There's a muscle that isn't a muscle."

"Go on."

"It's that. I do that, except further back. And then—" He frowned at his own palm. "I want to say I let it out. That's what it feels like from the outside, like pouring. But that's not it. It's more like I stop being in the way of it. I get out of the road and it's already there and it comes up." He looked up. "That's the stupidest thing I've ever said out loud."

"It's the best answer anybody's given me in nine years," said Rook. "Put it out. Cole."

Dessa said, "I'm not going to say anything different from him."

"Say it anyway."

"It's the same." She had gone very still. "It's the exact same. Last night. I stopped doing something and it was already on. I said that to Toren by the fire and I thought I was describing something about *me*, about being tired, and—" She stopped. She said, in a smaller voice, "It's the same thing he just said."

"Tanner."

Wyck did not answer.

He was sitting against the far wall of the arch with his bad leg out and his arms folded and his jaw set, and he had been that way since the word *Flame* had come out of Rook's mouth the previous afternoon, and he looked up now with his eyes absolutely clear.

"No," he said.

"No what."

"No, I'm not doing the game." Wyck's voice was level. "I know what this is. You're going to go round the four of us and get us all to say the same thing and then you're going to tell us something clever. I've watched you do it twice now." He shifted his leg with both hands, carefully, the way he had learned to in four days. "And I'll tell you the honest reason I'm not playing, since apparently this is a morning for honest reasons. It's because whatever you're about to say, you're about to say it to *them*, and I'm the one who's had one of these in my hand every day for two years, and if it turns out the two years weren't the thing that mattered, I'd rather hear it standing up."

Rook looked at him for a while.

"Fair," he said. "Voss."

---

Toren had the Ember in his hand before he knew he had reached for it.

"You already know mine," he said. "I go loose. I stop telling it. I ask it."

"Ask it what."

"To — " He stopped.

"Say the whole sentence."

"I ask it to please help."

"Right." Rook did not smile. There was nothing in his face at all. "Now leave the asking out of it and tell me what it feels like from the inside. Not what you want. What happens in you when it works."

Toren opened his mouth to say *I don't know*. Instead, without any warning at all, he was back in wet grass under a broken overpass, his hands closing on a warm grey rod, a door coming off its latch at the back of his own head.

"There's a room," he said.

His voice came out wrong. He pushed on.

"When he gave it to me. That's what it was. There was a — I've never said this to anybody. Everyone says the Kindling's like waking up on your arm, or it's like a light, and it wasn't. It was a room." He was looking at his own hands. "A big quiet empty room at the back of a house I'd been living in for thirteen years and never gone all the way to the back of. And when the Ember works, when it actually works — that's where I am. I'm standing in the doorway of it and I'm not touching anything and I'm not asking for anything, and then there's light in my hand."

The rain came down at both ends of the arch.

"Say what he said," Rook told him. "Faust's line. Say it back."

"He said he gets out of the road and it comes up."

"And the girl?"

"She stopped doing something and it was already on."

"And you're standing in the doorway of a room that was already there," said Rook, "and not one of you is describing a device, and not one of you is describing anything arriving from anywhere."

---

He let that sit for a long time. Longer than Toren wanted. He picked up the water can and drank and put it down and wiped his mouth on the back of his wrist, and outside the arch the stream came up in the rain by a visible amount, and nobody said anything at all.

"Here it is," Rook said. "I'm going to say it once, flat, and then we're going to spend a month on it and you're all going to be sick of me.

"It's one thing. It has always been one thing. There's no Ember power and no rift power; there's power, and there are two doors onto it, and every person alive was handed one of them at thirteen and spent the rest of their life thinking the door was the house.

"Faust wasn't given anything by a rift. Nothing reached through a hole in the sky and made him a present. He's got a light in his hand because he found a way to stop being in the way of what was already in him, and he found it at thirteen by accident, and he's been apologizing for it for two years because nobody told him what it was.

"Cole's the same and hers is bigger and she's had nobody to ask.

"And Voss." Rook's eyes came round. "That thing in your hand has never decided anything in its life. It has no opinion. It is a piece of shaped metal your great-great-grandmother's people made a tool out of, and it is very good at what it does, and what it does is make it easier for you to find a door you'd never have found on your own. That's all. That's the entire trick of it. Every time you've gone loose and asked it nicely and got four inches of light, you were the one who made the light, and you thanked a stick."

Toren could not feel his hands.

"That's not—"

"Say it's not."

"It cut something on the road," Toren said. His voice had come apart somewhere in the middle. "I was falling. I wasn't asking for anything, I wasn't — I was falling down a bank and it went off in my hand and it killed a thing, and I didn't do that, I *couldn't* have done that, I'd been standing in the road for a minute before that trying to make it work and it wouldn't—"

"You were standing in the road trying to make it work," Rook said. "Then you fell down a bank and for about one second you stopped trying to make anything do anything at all."

The arch was very quiet.

"I know," Rook said. "It's the worst news you've had since the road. Everybody gets it in that order — you find out you're not being helped, and the first thing you feel is that you've been robbed. Sit in it. It passes."

---

Milo said, "Then why have the Ember at all?"

"Because I can't walk through a wall." Rook got up off the stone and went to the mouth of the arch and stood in the noise of the water with his hands in his pockets. "I use a door. You use a door. Doors are not a moral failing, they're a door. The only thing I'm telling you to stop doing is bowing to it on the way through."

"Has it got a name?" said Milo. "The — the whole of it. The house. Has somebody got a word for it?"

"It hasn't got a name."

"Everything's got a—"

"It hasn't got a name," Rook said, "because it isn't a system and it isn't a school and nobody owns it. It's just what's true. The names are all on the doors." He watched the rain. "People have died over which door. I'd rather not hand you one more word to be right about."

Senna Voss said, from the cart, "My husband would have given three fingers to hear that."

Rook turned his head.

"He was Ember forty years," she said. "He believed every word of the other version. He believed it the way you believe the floor." She shifted the blanket up. "I'd like to tell you he'd have been glad. He'd have been furious, and then he'd have been up all night, and then about a week later he'd have been glad."

---

"Right." Rook came back in out of the mouth of the arch. "Here's the rest of it and it's the part nobody ever believes.

"Every one of you understands what I just said. All four of you. Tanner understood it before I finished and he's the one who's angry about it, which means he got it first and hardest." He stood with the water going past behind him. "And not one of you will be able to do a single thing with it. Not today, not this week. You'll all agree with me by supper and you'll all go out and get exactly what you got yesterday, and some of you will decide that means I'm wrong and some of you will decide it means you're broken, and both of those are going to be wrong."

"Then what's the point of telling us?" said Wyck.

"Because knowing it is worth nothing," Rook said, "and you can't start paying for it until you know it."

He picked up the second axe off the cart and tested the head with his thumb.

"You've got a month of being bad at something you understand perfectly," he said. "That's the whole of the next stretch of your lives. I'd tell you it gets better but you'd only ask when."

---

Toren went out at dusk when the rain had thinned to nothing and the stream had come up over the gravel shelf at the low end, and walked forty paces up the grade so that nobody could see him, and stood in the wet with his father's Ember in his fist.

He knew it now. That was the enormous, appalling thing. He had believed Rook completely somewhere around the middle of the man's third sentence and he had gone on believing him all afternoon, through the cooking and the water and Milo asking eleven more questions and Wyck not saying one word, and it had not moved a hair's breadth since.

*It's mine. It was always mine. There's a room and it's in me and nobody gave it to me.*

He did not go loose. He did not ask. He stood in the wet grass in the last of the light and reached, the way you reach for a thing on a shelf you have used your whole life, because it was his, because it had always been his, because the man had said so and the man had never once lied to any of them about anything.

The Ember put out four inches of white light and held it for two seconds and went out.

He did it again and got nothing.

He did it eleven more times and got nothing, and then he sat down on the wet ballast on the side of the grade with his arms across his knees, and did not cry, and was there a long while.
