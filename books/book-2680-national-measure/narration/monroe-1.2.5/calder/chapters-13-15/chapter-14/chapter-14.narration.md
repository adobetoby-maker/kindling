# Chapter 14 — Kiva: A Voice at the Hem

The third crossing went as the first two had gone, and the hand did not.

It went on Sunday the 15th, at seven, in the Old Yard, on the Circuit's mast, with the frost thinner than it had been and a wet wind coming up the long hill from the river. *Hand, to Ember. Rowan, changing on three.* The ward at her hip, and Orla's stick ringing on it once; the fold, from the far edge, to a penny; the shut, and nobody, and the cold coming into her hand as though a door had been opened on a winter morning. The Ember, glad. Six paces, low, onto a white cross.

"One cliff," said Noa. "Twenty-six. Thirty-one in all. A crossing."

"*Three,*" said Orla.

The hand was white past the wrist. Kiva held it up and looked at it and saw that the white went a good inch up the forearm, under the cuff, and stopped there in a line as sharp as a tide mark. She shut the fingers. They went slowly, all three of them, like a gate in a wind.

Healer Aske had it at seven on Monday morning, under the lamp. He held the wrist for a minute by his watch, and then for another, and did not say anything until he had written.

"Cold to an inch above the wrist," he said. "At seven. Last week, the wrist. The week before, the knuckle." He capped his pen and did not put it away. "I'll see it at noon, and at four, and tonight if I must, and I'll want it in the morning."

At noon it was cold to the wrist. At four it was cold to the knuckle. At nine that night, when she went up the Wren House stair for the third time with her coat over her nightdress and her aunt behind her, it was cool, and the three fingers shut when she told them, nearly at once.

On Tuesday at seven it was warm.

"A night," said Aske. He wrote it. *Warm at seven on Tuesday. A sulk of a day.* "Noon. Four o'clock. A night. I said I'd say so each time, before and not after. I'm saying it." He looked at her over his spectacles, quite steadily. "Your next is Sunday the 22nd, and the card allows it. I'll not stop it on what I've seen. But if it sulks longer again, I'll want to see it warm before I'll let the one after go, and the one after is on a road."

"There's no mast on a road."

"Then the one after was never going to be, and I'll not lose sleep over it." He put the pen away. "Four of six, by Thursday week. I've written that on the card, so that your sponsor can't say that I didn't."

---

Orla Dane went up the Chair's stair on Monday afternoon, the 16th, at three, with her stick and her bad left knee, and she took it a step at a time and did not let the clerk who came down past her offer an arm.

The Placement Chair's office was at the top of the Measure Hall, under the slates, with a window that looked down the hill to the river and the Brickfields. Ysra Crest sat behind a deal desk with nothing on it but a ledger and a pen and a paperweight of green glass. She did not get up. She never got up for anybody, and Orla had always rather liked her for it.

"Dane," said the Chair. "Sit down. You've come about the six."

"I've come about the four."

"Sit down all the same. I'll not be talked at by a woman standing on one knee."

Orla sat. She put her stick across her lap.

"Four," she said. "By Sunday next, if the hand allows it, and the healer says it may not. Two of my six left over, and no mast on the road, and the gate on the first. That's where we are." She looked at the green glass weight. "I'll not sign at four. I'll not sign at five. I told her so on the first day of this month, before anybody had time to hurry, and I'm telling you so now, so that it's said in this room as well."

"I didn't expect you to sign at four."

"No. You expected me to come up your stair and ask whether there was a way to make it six." Orla turned the stick over. "There is. Saltmere has a gate mast. It's sanctioned; the Board's. Your reader's going south with Dray's rule under seal, to read every candidate in this city who has more than one door. He'll be at that mast in the gate fortnight with his tape. I want the fifth and the sixth on it, on her healer's card, one in seven days, as the card allows. And I want it written now, in your ledger, before we go, that the certification of the second crossing waits on those two and not on anybody's convenience."

The Chair looked at her for some time.

"That's not what you came to ask," she said.

Orla did not answer at once. She looked out of the window at the river, which was grey under the wet wind, with a boat on it going down toward the sea.

"No," she said. "I came to tell you that I'm going."

"South."

"South. On the Circuit's transit, with the officers, since I can't sit a carrier's cart for three days with this knee. I've a candidate who'll make two crossings at that gate on my line, and I'll not have a stranger on a crate for them." Her crooked face did not change. "And there's a man at the harbour pump house who wrote her a card that he never wrote to me. He's the fitter with the watch. I've known that since the twelfth month."

"Hart."

"Hart." Orla said it to the window. "I've not decided what I'll say to him. I've decided that I'll not use her to say it. That's as far as I've got in twenty years, and it's taken me since the autumn to get that far, so I'll not be hurried on the rest." She turned back. "I'll be at the gate mast. That's the first thing. The other's my business."

Ysra Crest picked up her pen. She opened the ledger at a page that Orla could not see, and wrote on it, in her level upright hand, for perhaps a minute; and then she turned the ledger round on the desk and pushed it across.

*Rowan, K. The second crossing, hand to Ember, on her sponsor's line. Seen four times at most in Meridian by the 22nd of the second month, as her healer's card allows. The fifth and sixth to be seen at the Saltmere gate mast, read by N. Bexley, her sponsor present, one in seven days and not faster. Not certified until the sixth. No figure on any sheet is to be rounded for the sake of a boat. Y. Crest.*

"*Not faster,*" said Orla.

"I've seen a girl hurried for a boat," said the Chair. "So have you. I'd not have it said that it was done under my seal." She took the ledger back. "Go south, Dane. Sit on a crate at their gate. Find the man at the pump house, or don't." The level eyes did not soften. "You came up those stairs on one knee to tell me two things, and the second wasn't a thing that this office can write anything about. I'd like you to know that I noticed."

Orla got up, by the desk, in two stages.

"I'd have been sorry if you hadn't," she said.

She went down the stair a step at a time, as she had come up it, and at the bottom she stood for a moment in the Measure Hall's cold passage with her hand on the newel post and her stick under her arm. She had told the Chair. She had told nobody else, and she had not decided whether she would.

The girl would know. She would read it in Orla's face on Sunday on the crate, as she had read a thumb and a hip and a heel, and she would say nothing, because she had learned to say nothing. That was a thing that Orla had never had from anybody, in twenty years of teaching, and did not yet know how to bear.

---

Kellan came to the Old Yard on Monday at four, in his own coat, as he had said that he would, and he did not bring Pettit or anybody else.

"One," he said. "Me against your pass. I'll stand at the hurdle. You'll carry Gerald over it on your tap. If I can touch whoever takes it before it's taken, I've read you. If I can't, I haven't."

"And you'll write it down," said Kiva.

"I'll write it down. You told me to." He laced his cuff. "I've been thinking about your tap for six days, on a blue brick floor, and I've a notion how to read it."

"So have we," said Perrin.

They set up the hurdle across the lane, where it had stood on the Tuesday, and laid Gerald in the dirt on the shed side of it, and Kellan stood on the far side, dark, with his hands down. He did not look at Perrin's face. Kiva saw that at once. He looked at Perrin's hands.

Perrin picked up the head poles and carried them to the hurdle, and lifted them onto the hazel's top. Anwen went over the hurdle in one scramble and stood on the far side with her hands under the poles' ends, a pace from Kellan, with her wall unlit.

Perrin tapped once. Knuckles on ash.

Kellan's weight came onto his toes. Kiva saw it go; she had been watching his feet, not his hands, as Jude had, as Pike watched a heel. He was counting. He had a number in his head for the space between one tap and the next, the number that Perrin had used on the Tuesday, and he was going to move on it.

Perrin did not tap again.

He stood there with his knuckles on the pole and did nothing at all; and Anwen, under the poles, did not wait for a tap. She was not watching his knuckles. She was watching his shoulders. When they went down an inch, which was the moment that a man who has been holding up a weight lets the weight begin to be somebody else's, she took the poles.

Perrin let go. Neither of them said a word, and neither of them had tapped.

Kellan was a count late. He came off his toes on the number in his head, and touched Anwen on the arm, light as a tap on a barometer, a full count after the poles were in her hands and Perrin's were empty.

"After," said Jude, from the shed step. "That's after. She'd had it a count."

Kellan stood there with his edge lit on his fist. He looked at Anwen, and at Perrin, and at the litter on the far side of the hurdle, where it ought not to have been yet.

"There wasn't a second tap," he said.

"No," said Perrin.

"You tapped once and then you didn't."

"I tapped once so that you'd count. And then I let her take it when my shoulders said, and not when my knuckles did." Perrin was very red. "Rowan's catcher does it with a heel. Pryce does it with my shoulders. I'd no notion that I had shoulders until she told me on Saturday."

Kellan was quiet for a long moment.

"Again," he said.

They did it again six times. He touched Anwen on the fourth, before the poles were hers, because he had stopped counting taps and begun watching Perrin's shoulders as she did. On the fifth Perrin let the poles go on his hips instead, and on the sixth on a breath, and Kellan was late on both.

"You've changed it," he said.

"Every time," said Perrin. "A different count. Nobody knows it but me and whoever's taking. I said so on Tuesday. You heard me."

"I heard you. I didn't believe it'd be so hard to read."

"Nor did I."

Kellan unlaced his cuff. He stood in the lane with it in his hand, and then he took his pencil out of his pocket and wrote on the inside of it, quickly, two lines that Kiva could not read. Then he held it out to her, turned, so that she could.

*Watch his shoulders. Not his hands. It changes every time. Two in six.*

"That's what a page of you looks like now," he said. "Two in six. It was every time, a week ago."

"It'll be one in six by the gate," said Anwen.

"It'll be one in six by the gate if I'm reading it. A coast four that's never seen it won't get two." He laced the cuff again. "I'll tell Pettit. I'll tell him that I've read it twice in six and I'd not want to try on a floor. That's true, so he'll believe it."

He went up the yard. Jude wrote it on his knee. *Monday the 16th. The Old Yard. The tap, against Renn, alone, at a hurdle. Six passes; two read. Pryce, a wall, 0.06. Cade, a stance, 0.05. Talley, 0.04. Spent, 0.15.* "And Sunday's, which I've not read out. *Hand to Ember, Rowan, the third, 0.31.*" He did it in the margin. "*Held, two and sixty.*"

"Two," he said. "And sixty."

---

The letter came at five, by a Line runner, in a grey envelope with the Home Line's mark on its back and Hollis Vane's square hand on its front. It was addressed *T7, by its captain,* and Perrin opened it on the shed step as though it might bite.

"*The Sag watch, to licensed fours of the Meridian circuit, by the Board's hunt list,*" he read. "*A tear opened on Saturday the 14th at about four in the afternoon on the high ground above Cowl Edge, north of the Sag, on Line ground. It was closed by the Line's crew at eleven that night. Four things came off it before it was closed and are loose on the Edge. Three keep together. One went the other way, alone, slowly, up onto the moor. None has been near a house since. The Line will not take them; it has the Sag's ground to watch and a crew of six. They're posted for any four licensed in its own right that will come for them, on its own entry, under its own captain, with a Line hand to witness and weigh. The Line's hut at Cowl Edge is open. H. Vane.*"

He put the letter down on his knee.

"*Under its own captain,*" he said.

Nobody said anything.

"We've a license since Thursday. We've three drams on the book. We've a sheet and a half and a cord and a Kill with a white boot." He was not looking at any of them. "And there's a thing on a moor that went off on its own, slowly, away from everything, and hasn't been near a house."

"Away from everything," said Kiva.

She had heard it the first time. She had heard it and gone still, as she went still when her two fingers spoke on a rail.

"It went away from the others. Away from the Sag, up onto the moor. There's nothing on the moor. No pumps, no lamps, no tram sheds, no bank." She heard her own voice and made it slower. "Everything that's come up plain since the thaw, which is nothing, would have come up away from things that keep time. If anything's quiet, it'd be that."

"You think it's plain," said Anwen.

"I think it's the only thing in this country that might be. I don't know. I'd want to find out."

Jude had the book under his arm. He had been listening with his head on one side, and now he looked at the letter on Perrin's knee.

"I'll ask a thing," he said. "Since I'd sooner ask than count it up on a moor."

"Ask," said Perrin.

"What's it for?" said Jude. "The quiet one. If it comes up plain, it's for a hand; it goes in the tin with the eight-tenths, and it's for whoever's hurt next, and nobody can spend it on a floor or a crossing or a bag of chips. If it doesn't come up plain, it's for the book; it's a share, and we spend it at Saltmere on lighting doors in front of a Board. And either way it's a thing that went off on its own up a hill and hasn't hurt anybody, and we'll go up after it and kill it." He looked at the letter. "I'm not saying don't. Loose things on Line ground get taken; I know that. I'd like to know which it's for before I cut it, that's all. I've killed a deal of things this year and never once known before I cut whether it was for a hand or a book."

The yard was quiet. The wet wind came up over the bottom wall and moved the frost-burned grass along the south row.

"For a hand," said Kiva. "If it's plain. That's what we go for. That's why we go up after the quiet one and not the three."

"And the three?"

"The three are between it and us," said Perrin.

He had picked the letter up again. He was reading it a second time, with his lips moving.

"*Three keep together.* That's a pack. On the Edge, north of the Sag, which is where the road goes down to the farms. They've not been near a house since Saturday. That's two days, five by the time we're there." He put the letter down. "If we go for the quiet one and walk past three husks that keep a count, and they're at a farm on Friday, then that's under my name as well. It says so on the paper. *Under its own captain.*"

"So it's both," said Jude.

"It's both. The three first, since they're the ones that'll hurt somebody. Then the quiet one, for a hand." Perrin looked round at them, one after the other, and Kiva saw him do it as Coombe did it, slowly, along the rail, counting heads on a quay. "Thursday. The Line's hut at Cowl Edge. I'll write to Vane tonight. Under my name."

He went into the shed to find a pen. He walked like a man carrying a great deal of something that nobody else could see.

---

The card came at eleven on Tuesday night, the 17th, three words long, and Kiva was on the van's side bench at a quarter past with Pru beside her and the rings whining.

Varley sat by the tailboard with a cloth on each shoulder, talking to the left-hand one. Pike sat beside him with his arms on his knees. The dressings were thinner again. Ruddock had taken them down that morning to a band at each wrist, and Pike's hands lay on his knees bare, for the first time since the wharf, grey and swollen and very still, with the skin over the knuckles shining.

He saw Kiva look at them.

"They move," he said. "A quarter. I'd not ask them for a cord."

"Nobody's asking."

"I know nobody is." He looked at Varley's hands on the cloth. "That's the trouble."

The van stopped in the flagged yard behind the Corn Exchange, at the top of the hill, where the farmers' carts stood on market days.

It was the flattest piece of ground in Meridian. Kiva had heard that said since she was small, as a joke; it had been built level on purpose, on a raft of brick, so that the corn factors' scales would read true. It was forty paces square and paved with flags as smooth as a table, and lit at its four corners by gas lamps on iron posts, and on the north side was the Exchange's back wall, high and blind, and on the south a row of warehouses with their doors shut.

There was a string in the middle of it, on end.

It had three knots and it was skinned to the knee, and behind it on the flags there was a white patch, three paces long and two across, as thin as breath on a window.

By the time Kiva looked out past the tailboard, Mace's four had the wall up across the yard's east side, silver, from the Exchange wall to the warehouses. The string's head came round, and found the light, and it walked across the flags to the wall on its four thick legs, without hurry, and leaned there. The skin slid after it on the smooth stone and lay down behind its heels, on the west.

Rhea did not read the slate at once. She stood with it in her hand and looked at the yard for a long time, her eyes going over it as they went over a street.

"Flat," she said.

"As a table," said Mace.

"Then its sheet's behind it and the rung'll come down at its heels and lie there. There's no downhill to turn it to." She lifted the slate to the van's lamp. "*Before. A purse string of three, on end, in the Corn Exchange yard. Its skin's laid down behind it on the west. Its tear's in the Exchange's coal hole, and the Line's on it. The yard's level; there's no fall to turn it on. Nothing lit inside sixty paces but my gate; the warehouses are dark on the warden's word, and there's a night watchman in the Exchange who's been told to stay in his box.*" She did not lower it. "*The Marshal's page: make it carry its sheet uphill, and if you can't, weight the far edge. There's no uphill. So I'll not cut where its heels are. The far edge: the second's. To say. Two words, once.*" She looked up. "Pike."

Pike sat on the tailboard. He looked at the flags.

"No fall," he said. "Nothing'll run. Wherever a rung comes down, it lies. I'd say it comes down at its heels, since it always has, and that's the near edge of the white. And I'd say that Sorn's thinking the same thing that I'm thinking, and I'd like to hear her say it."

"I'll cut it from behind," said Rhea.

There was a pause in the van.

"From the west. From its back. Standing on the near end of the white, where Pike stood on the wharf, since it doesn't mind a boot at the near end. A rung comes off where it's cut and falls away from the edge that cut it, if the edge is heavy enough. I've a Torch's edge." She hung the slate on her belt. "If I cut from behind, it falls forward. Off its own heels, east, onto the flags in front of the wall, under the gate's light. And its sheet's behind it, where I'm standing, with nothing on it."

"And you're standing on its sheet," said Mace.

"At the near end. Where it doesn't mind a boot." Rhea put her hands in her pockets. "I'll want weight on the far end before I go. Sacks; we've six in the rack since the wharf. And the second has the far edge, and if she says two words, I come off it."

"Varley lays east," said Pike. "In front of the thing. Under the gate's light, between its feet and the wall. If Sorn's right, that's where the rung comes down."

"East," said Varley. "In front."

"Go," said Rhea.

---

Kiva knelt at Varley's left hem, east of the string, under the gate's light, with her back to the wall and the thing's four thick legs a yard from her face.

It was the nearest that she had ever been to a string on its feet. She could see the frost on its knees, and the grain of the skin that it wore to the knee, like canvas, grey-white, close-woven. She could see past its legs, to the west, to the white patch lying on the flags behind it, and to six sacks of coal lying along the patch's far edge, shoulder to shoulder, where Purvis and Hobday had slung them from the warehouse side without setting a boot on the white.

That was hers. The far edge, past the legs, past the patch, three paces off and a yard beyond, under six sacks.

Varley knelt square on the canvas with both knees. His cord was in his coat.

Pike knelt behind him, off the canvas, and a yard to the side, where he could see.

And Rhea Sorn walked round the string's south side as she walked on every floor, and stepped onto the white patch at its near end, behind the thing's heels, and stood there.

Kiva saw her do it, and her stomach turned over.

Rhea stood on the skin as Pike had stood on it on the wharf: at the near end, with her whole weight, on a thing that was cold and hard and did not take a boot. She stood a yard behind the string's back legs, with her blade arm down and her left heel flat on the white. She did not look down. She looked up, at the top knot.

"*Not yet,*" said Pike.

His voice was low and thick. He said it to Varley, who was the cloth, and not to anybody else; and Kiva saw Varley's head go a little on one side, listening, as a man listens to a voice in another room.

"*Not yet.*"

Rhea's left heel came down flat.

Kiva saw Pike see it. She saw his bare grey hands on his knees close, both of them, a quarter of the way, as though they had something in them; and she saw his mouth open on the word.

He did not say it.

"*Now,*" said Varley.

He said it off Rhea's heel. Kiva saw that; she saw his eyes on the heel, past the thing's legs, level, as a catcher's eyes are. He had not been told that Pike watched it. Nobody had told him. He had read it on a sheet in a mending room, a week ago, in a girl's square pencil hand, and he had watched the heel.

The yellow light went level along the yard and lit the Exchange's blind wall and the six sacks and Rhea's face, and the rung came off the top of the string.

It fell forward.

It fell off the front of the thing, away from the edge that had cut it, as Rhea had said it would. It came down off the string's chest in a long low heap onto the flags in front of its feet, on Varley's canvas, a pace from Kiva's knee, and lay there, under the gate's light, with nothing white anywhere near it.

"*Dark! One!*" said Varley.

He waited. He waited eleven, looking at the heap.

Kiva did not look at the heap. She looked past the legs, past Rhea standing on the near end of the skin with her blade dark, to the far edge, under six sacks.

It lifted.

It lifted along the row of sacks, the width of a finger, and the sacks creaked, all six, like a gate in a wind, and went white with frost; and it was trying to fold, with a Torch standing on its near end and nothing on it but her, and it could not.

It was trying to fold *her.*

"*Far edge,*" said Kiva. "*Lifting.*"

Rhea came off the skin.

She came off it backward, in one long step, without turning round, the way a woman steps off a kerb when a tram bell rings; and the far edge lay down under its sacks, and was still, and the white patch lay on the flags behind the thing's heels with nothing on it at all.

"*Corners,*" said Varley, at eleven.

---

They took the other two rungs the same way, and Kiva said *far edge* once more, on the third, when two sacks shifted and the white came up between them a hand. Rhea came off it before Kiva had finished the second word.

On the last knot the string itself came down, forward, onto the canvas, as a tree falls when it is cut on the right side. The skin went as the others had gone. It thinned from the edges, and was grey, and was a film of fine ash on the flags of the flattest yard in Meridian, under six sacks of somebody's coal.

Varley tied all three bags. He tied them right-handed, three turns, the left thumb pinning, as the book said. He did it in fifty and in fifty-three and in forty-nine, and on each he put his palm on the neck and held it there.

"Loud," he said, three times.

There was a man in a long coat at the Exchange's coal hole. After a time he called *closed,* not loudly, as a man says that he has shut a gate.

Rhea read the slate under the gas lamp at the yard's corner.

"*Closed, ten minutes to one. Lives: none. Works: six sacks, frosted, none split. Tinned: three bags, at trace, clean. A hundred and seventy-one drams, of a hundred and eighty by the book.*" She turned it over. "*Remarks: two. The yard's flat; there was no uphill to turn it to. I cut it from behind, standing on its near end, so that the rungs fell forward off its own heels and away from its sheet. Weight on the far edge. The skin tried to fold the officer when there was nothing else on it, and the second said so, and the officer came off. That remark's mine, and I'd not do it on a wharf.*" She paused. "*And the catcher called the rung off the blade's heel, which he had off a sheet. That remark's the sheet's.*"

She hung the slate on her belt.

"I'd not have stood on that if I'd not had a second on the far edge," she said. It was not said to Kiva. It was said to the yard. "I'd like that written somewhere that isn't my slate."

Kiva wrote it on the back of a laundry order, in the van, on her knee.

Pike did not say anything all the way down the hill.

He sat by the tailboard with his bare hands on his knees and his eyes on Varley's hands, which were folding the left-hand cloth over and over, smoothing it, while Varley talked to it under his breath. Pru was asleep, and the rings whined. Ruddock leaned on the tiller and hummed something that Tibb usually hummed.

At the gasworks corner Pike got up, carefully, holding the rack with the heels of his hands, and went to the tailboard, and sat down on the very end of it with his back to all of them and his face to the street going by.

Kiva saw his shoulders move.

It was not much. A man of fifty-four in a coat, with his back to a van, on a cold night, going down a hill; anybody might have thought that he was coughing. She watched it for perhaps a minute. Then she looked away, at the canvas against her knees, because it was not hers to watch, and she had learned that in a van as well.

At the hall gate he came back to the bench before the van had stopped, and sat down, and put his hands on his knees.

"Don't write that," he said.

"I won't."

"I'm asking."

"I know you are. I won't." She looked at his hands. "What shall I write?"

Pike was silent while Ruddock backed the van into the yard. The lamp swung.

"He called it off her heel," he said at last. "Off a sheet that you wrote about me. Eleven years I've called *now* off that heel and never told a soul, and he had it in a week, and he did it better than I'd have done it tonight, since his hands were his own and he wasn't thinking about them." He did not look at her. "I've been sitting on that tailboard thinking about what it's like to watch a man do your work with your trick and do it right. I'd want that in a column. Not the other."

"Which column?"

"*Cost,*" said Pike. "Put it under *cost.* Put that it's the cost of having your craft written down. That somebody can do it without you." He moved his bare grey fingers on his knee, a quarter of the way, and opened them. "And put that it's worth it. Put that I said so. I'll read it by the stove and see if I believe it."

She wrote it in the van, by the swinging lamp, under *Cost,* on the back of the laundry order, with Rhea's line above it.

*The cost of a craft written down is that another man can do it. P. says that it is worth it. He will read this and see whether he believes it.*

Varley had stopped folding the cloth. He was looking at the floor of the van, quite still, with the cloth on his knee. He had heard every word. He did not say anything, and he did not look at Pike, and after a moment he began to fold the cloth again, more slowly.

*There you are,* he said to it, under his breath. *There.*

---

Jude read the book that night, late, at the Rowan kitchen table, standing by the dresser, with the hunt list on the oilcloth beside it.

"*Held, two and sixty,*" he said. "The Marshal's line's spent. Of the six crossings, three are spent and three are left: one on Sunday, and two at the gate, if the hand lets them. That's nine-tenths still spoken for, near enough. So it's one and seventy uncommitted. *Sealed, eight-tenths.* And a hunt on Thursday, on our own entry, with a card from the Board that wants four-tenths for the privilege of being let walk up a moor." He put the book down. "I'd want everybody to hear that sum before we go. Since it's ours now."

"Less four-tenths for the card. Less whatever we light. Less a cord if one's frosted, and a sheet if a sheet is. And plus whatever comes up clean, after shares. The Line's card says shares. It doesn't say what."

Mara was at the far end of the table with the tide table, as she was every night now. She looked up, at last, over the lamp.

"If it's quiet, it's a hand," she said.

"That's what it's for," said Jude.

He said it quietly, to the book. Nobody answered him.

Kiva sat with her left hand flat on the oilcloth, under the lamp, where the three fingers could feel the warmth of it and the two could not. She thought about a white line on a forearm an inch above the wrist, and about a moor that she had never seen, and about a thing that had gone up onto it slowly, alone, away from everything that kept time.

She had stood on a skin on a cinder floor with her still door open. She had stood on a road outside a tram shed and watched a pack walk on a count of two for a bank house. She had seen an officer of eleven years step onto a white patch on the flattest yard in the city, because a second would say two words if it lifted.

All of it had been a thing coming toward something that kept time.

She did not know what a thing that went the other way would be. She found that she wanted to know so badly that her hand had closed on the oilcloth, all three of the fingers that reported and the two that did not; and she opened it again, deliberately, and laid it flat.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday," said Perrin. He had the license on the table in front of him, under the coil of tarred rope. "Cowl Edge. I've written to Vane. Under my name."
