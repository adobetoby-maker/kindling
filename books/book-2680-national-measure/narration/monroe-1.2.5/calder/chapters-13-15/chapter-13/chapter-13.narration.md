# Chapter 13 — Kiva: In Its Own Right

"Thumb," said Pru. "Flat. On the first turn. No, flat. You've got it on its edge like a man holding a cigarette."

"I've never held a cigarette."

"Then you'll not know what it looks like, and you'll have to take my word." Pru reached across and pressed Anwen's left thumb down onto the cord with one finger, as one presses a stamp onto an envelope. "There. That's the pin. Now the long end over and under, and you don't lift the thumb, whatever it wants."

It was Wednesday afternoon, the 11th, and the two of them were sitting on the shed step with a sandbag between their knees, and the neck of an old purse sheet drawn up round the sandbag's top. Anwen Pryce, who had the best Hold in the Third Division, was trying to tie three turns on it and making a very poor job of the third.

"It won't go under."

"It's cold. The wax is stiff. That's why you've a little finger." Pru showed her. She did it slowly, as she had done it for Pike by a stove, and the little finger came across the first turn from the far side and held it while the long end went under, and nothing slackened. "There. It's a second pin. You'll not get it today. I didn't get it for a fortnight, and I'd been tying since I was twelve."

"Then why am I learning it?"

"Because you're in the four and I'm not," said Pru.

She said it quite flatly. She went pink a moment after, the way she did when a big thing had come out of her before she meant it to, but she did not take it back. She looked at the sandbag instead.

"On the twelfth there's four of you on that cinder. Rowan's on the hem. Rowan can't tie. She's said so on a list since the ninth month, and it's on mine as well: *the cord, all three turns, P. Finch.* And I'll be on the rail." She picked at the cord. "I'm not going to be anybody's cord. I said so at the Low Lane, the day she saw my thumb, and I meant it. So if I'm not on the floor, somebody else has to be, and it's you, since your wall's down by the time there's a bag, and Talley's standing over what he's cut, and Cade's counting."

Anwen looked at her for a long moment.

"You're giving it away," she said.

"I'm not giving it away. It's still mine. I'm teaching it." Pru went pinker. "Pike taught me on his knee. He didn't stop having it."

Kiva was at the hoist with Perrin and Gerald and the Low Lane's old mast, and she had heard all of it, and she did not turn round.

She had been working, all the morning, at a quarter of a count.

They had the cord across the lane again, from Gerald's belt to the ring in the south wall, taut, with the second sandbag lying under it in its hat. Pru had caught the cord on its slack four times, and folded it, and held the penny out. Kiva had said *nobody,* shorter, as Orla had told her, a word and a swallow, and Pru had shut on the *n* and Kiva had opened the Ember on the end of the word. On the third try the cord had come up into her bar with no more than a jerk. On the fourth it had come up with nothing at all, as clean as a coin from one palm to another.

"A quarter," Noa had said, from the mast. "Or a hair over. Two lines. Nothing across the gap." He had not raised his voice. "That's clean."

It was the second pass that would not come.

She went four paces east in the air with the cord's pull riding along her fist, low, and called *nobody* as she shut the Ember at the top of the bound, and Perrin, planted on his mark with his still door shut, opened it on the end of her word. And every time he opened it late. It was not much. It was half a count, a third. But a third of a count with nobody holding the cord was long enough for it to go back across the lane, a yard, two yards, and jerk the hat on the sandbag, before Perrin's stance caught it and pulled it down into the ground at his feet.

"I'm late," he said, the fifth time. He was red to the hair. "I know I'm late. I can feel it."

"Why?"

"Because you're in the air." He put his hands on his knees and breathed. "You're coming down at me with a cord, and you say a word, and I'm meant to open on the end of it. And all the time you're saying it, I'm watching you come down, and a part of me's saying *wait, wait, she's not there yet, she'll land on you.* And I wait. I don't mean to. I wait till you've landed."

Kiva stood beside him with her Ember shut and her left hand cold.

She understood it. It was the oldest thing in the world. A still door was for standing on ground, and the ground was there; one opened onto something that one could feel under one's heels. He was being asked to open on a word from somebody who was not on any ground at all, in the air, falling, and to believe that by the time his door was open there would be a load coming down into it. It was asking him to arrive before he was there.

"Orla told me a thing once," she said. "On a crate, in the summer, when I couldn't land a crossing. *Arrive before you're there.* I didn't know what it meant. I thought it was something a Torch says to sound clever."

"Is it?"

"It's what you're doing. Waiting till I've landed. It's what I used to do. I'd wait in the gap to feel the ground before I opened the still, and I'd come down on nothing." She looked at him. "You don't open when I'm there. You open when I say I'll be there. You have to believe the word and not your eyes."

"I don't know that I can."

"No. Nor did I." She went back to her mark. "Shut your eyes, then."

He stared at her.

"I'm not joking," said Kiva. "Shut them. Listen for the word. Open on the end of it. If you can't see me coming, you can't wait for me to land."

Perrin shut his eyes. He stood on his mark with his big face screwed up like a boy's at a birthday, waiting for a cake.

Pru caught the cord. *Nobody.* Kiva had it, clean, and went, four paces east, low, and in the air she said it again, short, with the swallow at the end. *Nobody.*

Perrin opened on the end of it.

She came down into a still door that was already open, and the cord's pull went out of her fist and into the dirt at his boots as though it had fallen into a hole, and the hat on the sandbag by the wall lay still.

"*Planted,*" said Perrin, with his eyes shut.

"A quarter," said Noa, at the mast. "At both. Three lines, two gaps, nothing across either." He looked up. His face was very pink. "That's clean. It's all clean."

Perrin opened his eyes.

"I'd like it known," he said, "that I'll not be doing that with my eyes shut in front of a Blaze."

"You'll do it however it works," said Anwen, from the shed step, without looking up from her cord.

Jude wrote it on his knee. *Wednesday the 11th. The Old Yard. The Low Lane's mast, not sanctioned, read by N. Bexley and entered nowhere. A cord passed between three: Finch to Rowan to Cade, on one word, called by Rowan. Seven tries; the last clean at both gaps. Finch, two wards, 0.12. Cade, a stance, 0.03. Rowan, Embers, off her own thimble. Spent, 0.15.* He added it up. *Held, seven and sixty-eight.*

"Tomorrow's four," he said. "And whatever we light."

"I know what tomorrow is," said Perrin.

---

The Pit was full on Thursday morning, and the Marshal's floor was the only thing in it.

There were people on every foot of the rail, three deep. The first company had come, all sixteen, because Coombe had said on Monday that anybody who was not standing a floor that day might watch and learn. Behind them there were the reserves, and a dozen juniors who had come down the hill at six with Bettony at their head, and Orla on a crate that somebody had carried down the steps for her, and Healer Aske by the old mast with his bag. Mara was there. She had not said that she would come. She stood at the west end of the gallery in her work apron with her hands in its pocket, as she stood at a pump house, out of everybody's way.

Pike was there. Kiva saw him on the steps with Greta Stroud, his white arms on his knees.

On the cinder there was the cart, and the grey ward over the cage.

"T7," said Coombe. "Declare."

Perrin declared. He did it straight through, from his cuff, without stopping, and when he came to *Catcher, Rowan, one door, declared, the still,* he did not slow down, and Kiva felt a small hard knot in her chest come undone that she had not known was there.

"Four heads, a dram each," said Coombe. "Four drams to the Board, off your book. Your doors, your ash, on top of it. This is your floor. It's the only one you'll get from me. I'll mark it." She tied a knot. "There's a Flask in that cage, as the Board defines one. A thing that you'd meet on the Longshore in your first season. You'll take it, and you'll bag what it gives, and if I'm satisfied I'll sign a license, and if I'm not I won't. There's no second floor."

"Yes, ma'am."

"There's a lodger on this floor as well. I let it out at six. It's somewhere. I'll not tell you where." Her eyes went along the four of them. "If it goes into one of you, I'll be there before it's finished. I'll not be there before it's started. You'll want to know where it is. On Saturday I kept one off Rowan to teach a rail. This isn't teaching. It's a license, and the Longshore won't keep its lodgers away from her to suit a card."

Nobody said anything. Kiva looked at the cinder, and the race along the south side, and the lattice at the north, and the steps; and she could not see it. There was no grey coil lying anywhere that she could see. There was a great deal of floor.

"Go," said Coombe, and drew the ward back off the cage.

---

It was a string.

It came out of the cage on four thick legs, slowly, the way that the string on the kiln had come out of its wicket, and stood up on the cinder to its full height, which was a little more than two men's. It had three knots, one above another, each as big as a barrel. Its blunt top was turning.

It had a skin.

Kiva saw it as the thing came out. It came out behind the string, over the cage's sill, white and thin and flat, three paces long and two across, like breath on a window; and when the string stepped down onto the cinder, the skin slid after it and lay down on the floor behind its heels.

She went to her place without being told. She had chosen it on the steps, before anybody lit: west of the cage, eight paces back, on ground that she tested with her still door under her right heel, by its corners, wide.

The floor fell.

She felt it through her heel as plain as a stair. The Pit had been dug into a hill and cindered flat, and it was not flat; nothing in Meridian was. It fell to the south, toward the race, a hand in six paces. It was steeper than the Old Yard and gentler than the wharf.

The string had come out facing north. It stood with its head toward the lattice and its skin laid down behind it on the south, downhill, toward the race.

"*South,*" said Kiva. "A hand in six. To the race. Its skin's downhill."

"*South,*" said Anwen. "Mine."

She brought her wall up in front of the string, at its head, on the north side, silver, waist high, across its path. The string walked up to it and leaned. Its head stopped turning.

Then Anwen began to walk it round.

She walked it from the far end, as Mace had told her, as she had done on a yard with two boys and a sheet. She kept her feet where they were and brought the far end of her wall round first, east, a hand at a time, and the near end pivoted at her hip and came after; and the string leaned on the wall with the whole of its weight and came round after it, step by slow step, on its four thick legs, and the white skin came round behind it on the cinder like a train behind a bride.

Kiva watched the skin.

That was hers. It was the far edge, and the far edge was the second's, and she had been the second on two wharves and a road. On this floor there was no second, but she had decided on Tuesday night that whoever was on the hem would have the far edge, because it was the only one of them with nothing better to look at.

The string came round a quarter. It came round a third. Its skin had swung out to the east and was beginning to come round to the north, uphill, toward the lattice.

"*Half,*" said Kiva. "Another quarter and it's facing the race."

"*Mine.*"

And then she saw where the rung would go.

She had not seen it on the steps. She had not seen it until the string was half round and the skin half up the hill. When it was facing due south, toward the race, and the skin was behind it, uphill, then a rung cut off its top would come down at its heels and run downhill, away from the skin, as Coombe had said. And it would run to the race. It would run six paces and go over the lip of a stone channel a yard deep, into the dry bottom of it, among the leaves and grit, where no cloth could be thrown flat and no corners hauled.

"*Stop,*" she said. "Anwen. Not south. South-west."

"I'm half—"

"*South-west.* The race'll have it at south. It runs downhill; it runs into the race. At south-west it runs along the floor." She was already moving her own ground, two paces west, testing it, clean. "I'll be on the run. Stop at south-west."

"*South-west,*" said Anwen. There was no gap between the word and the turn. The far end of her wall stopped coming round, and the near end stopped, and the string leaned on it, facing south-west, with its skin lying behind it, up the hill, north-east, white on the black cinder. "*Stopped.*"

"*One, and,*" said Perrin.

He was at the lattice end, out of everybody's way, with his hands in his pockets and his eyes not on the string. He was counting nothing. There was no count to keep; a string on a gate did not stamp. He was counting because it was his place to count, and because on a floor where a thing might keep time he wanted the number in his mouth before he needed it. And while he counted, his eyes went over the floor, slowly, from the lattice to the race, from the race to the steps.

He was looking for the lodger.

Kiva had not told him to. Nobody had. She saw him doing it and felt something go warm and tight under her breastbone that had nothing to do with any door.

Jude went up the west side of the string in his limping run. He stood at the string's flank, below the top knot, with his kill unlit, and looked down at Kiva.

"*Now?*"

He had never asked her that. He had always cut on her word without asking. Kiva saw him ask, and saw that he had learned it off Kellan the way that one learns a word off somebody across a table, without meaning to.

"*Not yet,*" she said. The string's top was swaying. "*Now.*"

The kill came down out of his shoulder. It went into the top knot level and heavy, and the rung came off the top of the string and fell.

It fell at the string's heels, on the downhill side. It hit the cinder in a long low heap, and lay, and began to run. It ran away from the skin. It ran south-west down the fall of the floor, open, loose, like flour on a tilted board, along the cinder, and not toward the race at all; and Kiva's cloth went over it at the first cast, flat, two paces from where it had begun.

"*Dark! One!*"

She was on her knees on the hem. She had her right hand on the canvas and her left lying beside it with two fingers that said nothing. And she looked at the heap under the cloth, as Pike looked, and as Varley looked, and counted.

It leaned. It leaned a hair to the north, toward the lattice, toward the lit wall of the Wren House cage and whatever was beyond it. Then it settled.

"*Corners,*" she said, at nine.

There was nobody to haul them.

She had known there would not be. She had stood by the shed step last Thursday morning and heard Perrin say it to the Marshal: *Pryce, when her wall's down. Talley, when he's cut.* And Anwen's wall was up, with a string leaning on it, and could not come down; and Jude was standing at the string's flank with his kill spent, because he was waiting for the second knot.

"*Talley,*" said Perrin, from the lattice. "Corner. North."

Jude came down. He came round the west side of the cloth on his bad leg, and knelt, and took the north corner, and hauled. The neck came up between his hands and Kiva's, one-sided, crooked, slow.

"*Pryce,*" said Perrin. "Can you let it down?"

"*No.*"

"Then I'll take the south." And he came off the lattice at a run, and Kiva saw him leave his count, and saw him know that he was leaving it, and say so: "*Count's dropped. Nobody has it. There's nothing to count.*" He knelt at the south corner and hauled, and the neck came up straight.

Kiva could not tie it.

She knelt with the neck drawn up in front of her and her hands on the canvas, and she could not tie it, and she had known since the ninth month that she could not, and there was nobody on the cinder whose hands were free. Anwen was on the wall. Jude and Perrin had a corner each.

"*Anwen,*" she said. "It's sealed or it's lost. It's yours."

There was a pause. It was not long.

"*Rowan's ground,*" said Anwen.

Kiva did not understand her.

"*I can't,*" she said. "I've the still declared. I can't put up a ward."

"Not a ward. Your ground. Put your still door under my heels and *hold* me. I'll let the far end down and keep the near end up, at my hip, a yard of it, and if the string's leaning on a yard of wall that's planted on your ground, it'll stand. I've seen Stroud hold the end of Mace's line with a still-door at her back." Anwen's voice was very quick and very level. "Then I can come off with one hand. One. And tie."

It was not a plank. A plank was two still doors on one patch of ground.

This was a ward on a still door's ground, Anwen's hand door on Kiva's still: two unlike doors on one thing, at once.

"*No,*" said Kiva. "There's a lodger."

"Where?"

"I don't know where. That's why."

Anwen did not answer. Kiva saw her face go white and set, at the end of her wall, with a string leaning on it, and she saw her decide. She did not let the wall down. She did not ask for ground. She put one hand on the near end of the wall, at her hip, flat, and took the other off it altogether, and held the whole of that wall with one hand, for as long as she could, which Kiva knew was not long; and with the other she reached across the cinder toward the neck of the cloth.

She could not reach it. It was two paces off.

"*Bring it,*" said Perrin. "Talley. Bring the corner round."

They brought it. They dragged the whole cloth two paces west on its corners, with the heap inside, across the cinder, until the neck was at Anwen's knee; and she tied it with one hand and her teeth.

It was not three turns. It was two, and the end tucked under, and the little finger across, and her face scarlet.

"*Sealed,*" said Anwen. "I don't know what count."

"Sixty-two," said the Marshal, from the rail. She did not raise her voice. "That's outside. It's a bag. It's a remark."

---

They took the second knot more quickly, and it went worse.

Anwen's wall had held the string on one hand for the best part of a minute, and the string had leaned on it the whole time, and when she put her second hand back on it Kiva saw the silver shake from end to end like a sail in a gust. The string had felt it. Its head had begun to turn again. It was not leaning on the wall now. It was pushing along it, east, toward the end, as a cow pushes along a fence for the gap; and its skin was coming round behind it, away from the uphill side, down the slope, south and east, toward the race and toward Kiva's new ground.

"*It's turning,*" said Kiva. "Anwen. It's coming round on its own. The skin's going downhill."

"*I know.*" Anwen's heels went back in the cinder. "I can't hold its head and walk it. Not on one— I'd want a stance at my back."

"Cade," said Kiva. "Plant at her back."

Perrin was there. He had let go the south corner and come round behind Anwen's wall, and he planted, a pace behind her, his still door open into the cinder, and leaned his shoulder into her back. Kiva saw the wall stop shaking. It stood. The string pushed along it and the wall did not give an inch, and Anwen began, very slowly, to walk its far end back round, west, against the push.

That was when Kiva saw the lodger.

She saw it because she was looking at the race. She had been looking at the race since the string began to turn, because the race was where the rung would go if the turn failed; and so she saw a thing at its lip that she would not have seen from anywhere else on the floor. It was a grey line lying along the edge of the stone channel, a long way off, by the steps, as thick as a wrist. It had been lying there, she supposed, since six. It had looked like a crack in the coping.

Its tip had lifted.

It was turning, slowly, as the tip of a vine turns, and it came round, and it stopped. It was pointing across the cinder at Anwen's wall, and at Perrin's still door planted at the back of it, his shoulder in her back, his stance and her ward on one thing, together, agreeing.

"*Nobody!*" said Kiva.

She said it short, with the swallow. It came out of her without her deciding to say it at all.

Perrin shut.

He did not ask why. He did not look round. He had heard that word fourteen times in two days, on a yard, in a voice from the air, and every time he had heard it he had been told to do one thing on the end of it, and he did it. His still door went shut under his heels and he stepped back off Anwen, a pace, so that his shoulder came away from her back and there was nothing between them but air.

Anwen's wall shook. The string pushed along it. She held it, alone, with both hands.

The lodger's tip hung in the air, by the race, pointing.

Then it swung away, as a dog's head swings when the smell has gone, and lay down along the coping again, and was a crack in the stone.

"*Where?*" said Jude.

"*The race. The east end. By the steps.*" Kiva did not take her eyes off it. "It's lying down. It went for Perrin and Anwen. It's lying down now."

"*Then nobody stands together,*" said Perrin.

He said it in his captain's voice, loud, so that the whole Pit heard it, and Kiva understood that he was saying it for the four of them and not for the rail. "Nobody puts a door on another's. Not on a wall, not on a mark, not on a cloth. If you want a stance at your back, you ask, and Rowan says whether, and she's watching the race."

"*Then I'll walk it alone,*" said Anwen, between her teeth. "Talley. Cut it before I drop it."

Jude went up the west side.

"*Now?*"

"*Now,*" said Kiva, because there was no time to wait for anything.

The second knot came off. The rung fell at the string's heels, on the downhill side, and the skin was half round behind it, half on the slope, and the rung came down on the skin's near edge, half on and half off, and lay there across it, and did not run.

"*Far edge,*" said Kiva. "It's on the skin. Anwen, it's on the—"

"*I can't turn it.*"

"Then I'll weight it."

There was nothing to weight it with. There were no sacks on that cinder; Coombe had not left any. There was the cart, and the cage on the cart, and the four of them.

Kiva threw her cloth over the rung where it lay, half on the skin and half off, and then she did a thing that she had not planned and would think about for a long time afterward. She got up off her knees and went round the downhill side of the cloth, three strides, to the place where the skin's far edge lay on the cinder under the canvas, white, and she stood on it. She stood on it with both boots, as a girl in a laundry had stood on a sheet's trailing hem by accident, and she opened her still door under her own heels, and planted.

The yard took her. The cinder took her, and the skin under the cinder, which was cold and hard and did not take a boot. She felt it under her soles as one feels ice on a pond, and she felt it try to rise. It lifted, a finger's breadth, at the far edge, under her weight.

It did not fold.

*A sheet with a foot on its far hem doesn't fold for anybody.*

"*Far edge,*" she said, standing on it. "*Holding.*"

She heard Perrin say *oh* under his breath from somewhere behind her. She did not look.

"*Corners,*" she said. "Who's free?"

"*Me,*" said Jude. He had come off the string's flank. "And Cade."

They hauled. The neck came up. The rung came into it off the skin's edge, slowly, as Kiva stood on the far edge with her still door planted and two dead fingers that could tell her nothing about whether she was going to be folded into a sack with sixty drams of ash; and Anwen's wall stood with the string leaning on it; and at the race, by the steps, the lodger lay along the coping and did not lift its tip.

Perrin tied it. He had never tied a cord on a live bag in his life. He tied it with his thumb on its edge like a man holding a cigarette, and the third turn slipped, and he said a word that his little sister would not have known, and tied it again.

"*Sealed,*" said Perrin. "Fifty-nine."

"Fifty-nine," said the Marshal, from the rail. "Inside."

---

The last knot was the string itself. Jude cut it on Kiva's word with the string facing south-west and the skin behind it up the hill, and the whole of the thing came down onto the cinder in a heap like a cart's load tipped out, and ran, and Kiva had her second cloth on it and her knees on the hem. It leaned to the lattice, a hair. It settled. Anwen had let her wall down, at last, and came to the north corner, and Jude to the south, and Perrin stood at the lattice end and did not haul, and counted nothing, and watched the race.

Anwen tied it. It was three turns. The little finger came across, on the third, slow. Nothing slackened.

"*Sealed,*" said Anwen. "Fifty-four."

The skin went as the purse string's had gone on the wharf. It thinned from the edges, and was grey, and was a film of fine ash on the cinder, no thicker than dust on a shelf. The Wren House man came down the steps and swept it into a tin.

"There's a lodger," said Coombe. "Kill it."

She had not moved from the rail. She said it as she said everything, without leaning on any word.

Jude looked at Kiva.

"It doesn't keep time," said Kiva. "I don't think. It doesn't stamp, it doesn't sway. It isn't a pack." She looked at the grey line on the coping. "I'd go at it from above. Not near the tip. The tip's what goes looking."

"And if it comes at me?"

"It won't. You're one door." She stopped. "I think. It went for two. It went for the pair in the case and two Pit hands on a sack and Perrin and Anwen. It's never been near one."

"You think," said Jude.

"I think. I've nothing better."

He went. He went along the race on his bad leg, slowly, on the cinder side of the coping, with his kill unlit, and the lodger lay along the stone and did not lift its tip; and when he was above the middle of it he lit, and the kill came down out of his shoulder into the stone of the coping and through the grey cord lying on it.

It did not fall. It came apart. It went to ash all along its length at once, from the cut both ways, as a fuse goes, and lay on the coping in a grey line, a hand high, and then slid off the lip into the dry race, and lay there among the leaves.

Nobody threw a cloth. There was nowhere to throw it.

"Leave it," said Coombe. "That one's mine. I'll have the Wren House shovel it out." She came down the steps for the first time that morning, and stood on the cinder, and looked at the four of them, and at the three bags lying on the floor, and at Perrin at the lattice end with his hands hanging. "Bags."

The Wren House man brought the comb. He held it over each neck in turn. On each the third reed sang, and the seventh sang under it, faint, a thin high thread of a note. *Ward. Full. Trace.*

"Trace," said the Wren House man. "Clean. Three bags."

"Board's yield," said Coombe. "A floor's yield is the floor's." She did not look at the bags again. She looked at Anwen. "Pryce. You tied the first one with one hand and your teeth, at sixty-two."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Why?"

"Because I'd not have Rowan put her still door under my heels with a lodger on the floor, and I'd not drop the string, and there was nobody else." Anwen's chin was up. "It's a remark. I know it's a remark."

"It's a remark. It's the only one." The Marshal turned. "Rowan."

"Ma'am."

"You stood on the skin."

"Yes, ma'am."

"With your still door open. On a thing that folds."

"It was the only weight on the floor," said Kiva. "Pike said dead weight. I wasn't dead weight. I was planted. I don't know that it's the same. It held."

"It held." Coombe looked at her for a long time with her pale eyes. "If it hadn't, you'd have had your boots in a fold with sixty drams of open ash, and I'd have been on that cinder before it finished and not before it started, as I said. I'll not have you do it again. I'll put it on the page, all the same. A planted foot on the far edge, by a catcher who had nothing else. With a line under it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Cade."

Perrin's head came up.

"You dropped your count to haul. You said so, aloud, so that nobody would think they had it. Then you put a stance at your Hold's back when she asked for one. Then your catcher said a word and you shut it and stepped off without asking why." Coombe's voice did not change by a hair. "Then you told your four that nobody stands together, before any of them could think of it for themselves. And you tied a bag like a man holding a cigarette." She paused. "Your catcher said *nobody.* Who taught you to open and shut on it?"

"She did," said Perrin. "Yesterday. With my eyes shut."

The Marshal looked at him. Her hands went still on the rope.

"Up," she said. "All of you. I'll sign it at the top."

---

She signed it on the Pit's rail, standing, on a printed sheet with the Charter Board's seal at its head, in a hand that was square and slow and pressed hard into the paper.

*T7, of the Meridian Collegiate Circuit. A Flask license, in its own right, for the year 2681, on the Marshal's floor of the 12th of the second month. Captain: Cade, P., carry. Catcher: Rowan, K. Hold: Pryce, A. Kill: Talley, J. Remarks: one (a bag sealed at sixty-two). A four licensed in its own right hunts on its own entry, under its captain's name. D. Coombe, Marshal.*

She gave it to Perrin.

He held it in both hands. He read it twice, with his lips moving, and then he looked up at the Marshal, and his face had gone a color that Kiva had never seen on it before.

"*Under its captain's name,*" he said.

"That's what it says."

"So if we hunt, and somebody's hurt. On our own entry. It's not Sorn's slate. It's not an officer's."

"It's yours," said Coombe. "That's what in its own right means. There's no company over you any more on a hunt, and no officer to take a remark for you as Sorn took one on a wharf. You'll go out under your own name, and if you bring a person home with a hand taken, it'll be written under *Cade,* and the Board will want to know why." She tied a knot. "You'll find that's the license. The bit of paper's only the bit of paper."

Perrin looked at the sheet for a long time.

"I'd like it known," he said at last, quite quietly, "that I don't want this either."

"I know you don't," said the Marshal. "That's why it's yours."

---

She came to the Old Yard on Friday afternoon, the 13th, for the last time, and she stood on the shed step for an hour and watched three people pass a cord.

Nobody had asked her to come. She was simply there, at two, with a fresh length of rope. Pike was on the crate beside Orla, with his arms on his knees, and Noa was at the Low Lane's old mast, and Pru had Gerald on the hoist.

"Show me," said Coombe.

They showed her.

Jude swung Gerald, and the cord across the lane went slack for a beat, and Pru's ward came up under it at her right hip, small and silver, and took the tap and rang, and folded from the far edge to a penny in her palms.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

Pru shut on the *n.* Kiva's Ember opened on the end of the word, and the bar came down her right fist and met the penny where Pru's palms had been a moment before, and the cord's pull came up into the Ember without a jerk, like a coin from one palm to another.

She went. Four paces east, low, with the cord riding along her fist, and at the top of the bound, with the struts running out of her calves and the Ember beginning to close, she said it again, short, with the swallow.

*"Nobody."*

Perrin opened on the end of it. His eyes were open. He was looking at her as she came down at him, and he did not wait to see her land. He had heard the word and he believed it, and his still door was open under his heels before her foot came down beside them; the cord went out of her fist and down into the dirt at his boots as though it had been nailed.

The hat on the sandbag lay still.

"*Planted,*" said Perrin.

"A quarter at the first," said Noa. "A quarter at the second. Three lines. Nothing across either gap." He looked up at the step. "Not a stack, ma'am. Not anywhere."

They did it four times. On the third, Perrin opened early, before the end of the word, and his still door was there and waiting and empty for half a count before Kiva came down into it, and Noa said *clean* and then said *early,* and Perrin said that he would rather be early than late, and Coombe said nothing. On the fourth it was a quarter at both.

The Marshal came down off the step.

She walked along the scraped strip of the lane to where the cord went into the dirt at Perrin's boots, and looked at it. She looked at the three of them, one after the other: Pru with her palms open and nothing in them, and Kiva with her Ember shut and her left hand in her coat, and Perrin on his mark.

"I've seen mixed fours pass a load hand to hand on the Longshore," she said. "A ward to a blade, to get a thing off a man. Badly. Every time I've seen it, it was two people grabbing at once and hoping. Twice it killed somebody." She looked down at the rope in her hands. "I've never seen it called."

"It's a crossing," said Jude.

He was at the hoist, with Gerald's rope in his fist, and he said it as he said everything, slowly.

"It's what she does with three doors. Catch, carry, plant. Only it's handed. From one of us to the next." He looked at Kiva. "The Line had a word for it once. The other kind. Vane read it out of a ledger. *A crossing.* I'd call this one handed."

"A handed crossing," said Coombe.

She said it as though she were reading it off a label to see whether she liked it.

"I've no name for it," she said. "I've had thirty years on the coast and no name for it, because there was nothing to name. I'll not give it one now; it's yours." She looked at Kiva. "Write it. In your seven columns, with every hand in it by name. Who catches, who carries, who plants, who calls, and why the caller's the one who's been in both gaps. Write what it costs. And write that it's been done on a yard on a Friday, with a sandbag in a hat, and nowhere else."

"Yes, ma'am."

"If it holds on the Longshore, I'll want the sheet. If it doesn't hold, I'll want it more." The pale eyes went from Kiva to Pru, and to Perrin. "It's three people's. It's not hers. Don't any of you let anybody tell you that it's hers. It's the opposite of hers. It's the thing she does that she can't do, done by three who can."

Nobody said anything. Kiva found that her eyes had gone hot, and she looked at the hat on the sandbag until they stopped.

The Marshal went back up the lane. At the crate she stopped in front of Pike.

"You'll have your hands by the gate," she said.

"I'll have them by the fourth of next month, Ruddock says. Slow."

"Slow will do. A catcher's hands are always slow, by the end." Coombe looked at the white linen. "I'll see you on the Longshore. I'd like to watch you kneel at a hem again. I've a notion I'll learn something."

"I'm not there," said Pike.

"No," said Coombe. "But you will be."

She went on Saturday morning, the 14th, by the station transit to the next circuit on her list, and nobody in Seven saw her go. Perrin went up to the shed door at six to wipe out the *31* and chalk a *30,* and found on the step, in the frost, a length of tarred rope as long as an arm, with a great many knots in it, coiled, and nothing written anywhere.

He did not know what it was for. He took it home anyway.

Jude entered the floor and the yard on Saturday morning, at the Rowan kitchen table, standing by the dresser.

"*Thursday the 12th. The Pit, the Marshal's floor. Four heads at 1.0: 4.0, to the Board. A Flask: a string of three on end with a skin, and a lodger. Three bags, the Board's. Pryce, a wall and a walk and a cord, 0.22. Talley, three cuts and a lodger, 0.14. Cade, two stances, 0.08. Rowan, two stances and a stand, off her own thimble. Spent, 4.44. Licensed, in our own right; one remark.*" He did it twice in the margin. "*Held, three and twenty-four.* That's from seven and sixty-eight."

"And Friday," said Anwen.

"*Friday the 13th. The Old Yard. The handed crossing, called; four tries before the Marshal, on the Low Lane's mast, unsanctioned. Finch, two wards, 0.12. Cade, two stances, 0.06. Rowan, Embers, off her own thimble. Spent, 0.18. Held, three and six hundredths.*" He shut the book. "Sealed, eight-tenths. That adds."

Mara, at the far end of the table, did not look up from the tide table, and her pencil did not move.

"Three," said Perrin. He had the license in front of him on the oilcloth, flat, with the coil of tarred rope beside it. "We had nine on the first. We've three."

"And a license," said Kiva.

"And a license." He looked at it. "Under my name."
