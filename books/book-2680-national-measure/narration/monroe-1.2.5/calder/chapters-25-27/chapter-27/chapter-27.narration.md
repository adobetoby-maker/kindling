# Chapter 27 — Kiva: Twenty-Two Rungs

The water came through the culverts with a noise like a crowd standing up.

Kiva was running before she heard it. The four of them went down the chamber together, forty paces of wet stone, with the granite walls going past on either side and the faces all along the top of them going past as well, a blur of pink and green. The water met them a third of the way. It came along the floor toward them in a thin brown sheet, very fast, and it was cold through her boots at once.

At thirty paces she could see the Purser. At twenty she could see the water round him. It had already found his head. It lay round the canvas of his skull in a ring, and in the ring it was going round and round, because the spouts were falling on the stone a yard beyond him and spreading out in every direction.

She went wide. That was the plan. She went past the Purser's feet on the north side and round his head, under the spouts, so close to the head wall that the spray came down on her like rain. The noise was enormous. She could see the leaded hem of the sailcloth lying in the water at the Purser's crown, a dark heavy seam, and she put both boots on it and opened.

Her still door went down.

It went down through the hem and the stone and the granite under the stone, a long way, to wherever the bottom of the dock was. She felt it go and she felt it hold. The far edge of the sailcloth was under her heels, and it was the ground now, and nothing in the world would shift it while she stood there.

"*Far edge,*" she said. "*Mine.*"

Anwen was at the near edge, at the Purser's shins. Her ward came up at her hip, low and flat, silver in the spray, and she put it under the cloth's hem where it lay on the spar and lifted.

The cloth came up off the Purser like the cover of a book.

It wanted to fall back. Kiva could feel it want to. The lead in its far edge pulled, the way the lead in a fishing net pulls, and if her boots had not been on it, the whole grey sheet would have slid down under Anwen's ward and back over the painted face. But her boots were on it, and her still door was in the ground. The far edge did not move. Anwen walked her ward up the cloth and over, as Mace had taught her to walk a wall, the far end first, and the near half of the sailcloth went up and over and came down in a heavy wet fold on the far half, across the toes of Kiva's boots.

The Purser lay in the water with his face to the roof. The water was at his ears.

"*Line,*" said Jude.

He was on his knees at the spar. His left arm was held away from his side, as Aske had told him, and his edge was lit on his right fist, short and bright. He put it to the first lashing where the tarred line went round the spar and through the iron ring in the floor, and the line parted with a sound like a whip. He went to the second. It parted.

"*Spar,*" said Perrin.

He had planted beside the spar's north end. Kiva saw his boots go into the floor, both of them, square, the way he planted on a gate. He bent and put his two big hands under the oak and stood up with it.

It came up. It was as thick as a man's thigh and as long as a man was tall, and he lifted one end of it off the Purser's shins as easily as he would have lifted a gate off its hinges. He held it there, up at his waist, with his arms straight and his whole body planted, and his face went no colour at all.

"*Out,*" he said.

Anwen had shut her ward. She took the Purser by the collar of his painted coat and dragged him backward from under the spar, out of the water's ring, through the water, two paces, three. He was heavy. He was eighty-five kilos of wet sand in a canvas skin, and the water made him heavier. Jude had his feet.

"*Down,*" said Perrin, and set the spar down on the wet stone where the Purser's legs had been. Then he shut.

Kiva shut. The cloth lay folded at her feet. She stepped off it.

Twenty-six seconds, said a voice somewhere above them, very faint, through the noise of the spouts. It was a man on the lock's lip with a watch, calling it for the crowd.

"*Sling,*" said Perrin.

Anwen had it. It was the Board's sling, a band of grey canvas as wide as a hand with a loop at each end and a buckle in the middle. They had practised it twice in the loft on Monday night, with Jude as the Purser, and Jude had complained the whole time that he was being strangled.

They got the Purser up. It was the worst part. Kiva had known it would be. A canvas man full of sand has no stiffness in him anywhere, and when you lift him he folds, at the knees and the waist and the neck, like a sack folding. Anwen took him under one arm and Jude under the other, and Kiva took his legs, and between them they stood him up against Perrin's back with his head lolling on Perrin's shoulder.

Perrin crouched. He had his hands on his knees.

Anwen put the loops over his shoulders, one each side. She crossed the band over his chest, under the linen that Jude had wound round him that morning. She pulled it tight and buckled it.

Kiva saw Perrin's face when the buckle went home. It did not change. Only his breathing changed. It went short, and stayed short.

"*Up,*" he said.

He stood up with the Purser on his back.

The lockkeeper turned his crank. The shutters went up all the way. The spouts became two great columns, and the noise doubled, and the water in the chamber went from the shins to halfway up the calf in the time it took Kiva to look down at it.

A minute, by the customs house clock. They had beaten the water to his face by most of a minute, and it did not matter now. The Purser's face was over Perrin's shoulder, two feet above the water, and it would stay there unless Perrin went down.

Perrin walked to the ladder. He did not run. He walked through the rising water with the Purser on his back and his head bent forward under the weight, ten paces, with the three of them round him, and stopped at the foot of the north wall where the iron rungs went up.

"*Talley,*" he said. "Go."

Jude went up the ladder.

He went up it fast, with nothing lit, the way a boy goes up a tree. He went up on his good arm and his bad one both, though he had been told not to lean on the bad one. At the top he went over the lip on his stomach and turned round at once and lay flat on the granite with his arms down over the edge, both of them. His face looked down at them, white, from twenty-four feet up.

"Ready," he called.

Perrin put his right boot on the first rung.

Kiva stood in the water at the foot of the ladder, with her face a yard from his heel. Anwen was beside her. The water was at their knees, cold, pushing at the backs of their legs as it went down the chamber toward the tail. The spouts roared. Over all of it, from the top of the lock and the quay and the gates and the roof of the lockkeeper's hut, there was the crowd, and the crowd had stopped making any noise at all.

She watched the boot.

She watched it come down on the iron. She watched the heel find the rung and the sole settle on it, and the weight come into it. She watched the ankle stop moving.

"*Set,*" she said.

Perrin opened.

She felt it go down through the rung, through the granite, into the ground. It was the same thing that she had felt a thousand times in a yard and a pen. It was a still door planting. Only it was going sideways out of a boot on an iron bar, into a wall, and down from there.

He was the ground. He let go of the rung above him with his right hand and reached for the next. He shut.

For a moment he was a man on a ladder with eighty-five kilos on his back and nothing under him but his arms. Kiva saw the muscles in his forearms stand out like ropes. His left boot came up off the first rung and found the second.

She watched the heel. She watched it settle.

"*Set.*"

He opened. He was the ground again.

It went like that. It went like that for a long time.

Afterward, people told her how long it had been. Jude had counted it from the top. The man with the watch on the lip had called it for the crowd. Anwen had counted it under her breath, standing beside her in the water. Kiva did not count it. She did not know how long anything was. She knew only the boot and the rung and the word, over and over.

The boot came down. She watched the heel. *Set.* He opened and was the ground, and reached, and shut, and was a man on a ladder.

The water came up past her knees. It came up her thighs. It was so cold that she stopped feeling her feet, and then she stopped feeling her shins, and she did not look down at it. She kept her eyes on the boot.

The boot came down. *Set.*

By the fifth rung he had it. She could see that he had it. He was quicker. He did not wait to feel the iron. He put his boot on it and listened for her, and when she said the word he opened on the end of it, as Trewin had opened on a yard on Sunday a count late, and as Perrin himself had opened on the Low Lane in the second month a quarter early. Only now it was neither. It was on the word. He was opening as her mouth closed on the *t.*

"Three a rung," said Anwen, very low, beside her. "He's doing three a rung."

Kiva did not answer. The boot came down.

At the ninth rung she heard his breathing change.

It had been short since the buckle. Now it went shorter. It went in and stopped and went in again, the way breath goes when it hurts to take it. His left boot came up off the ninth rung and hung in the air a moment over the tenth, and then came down. She watched the heel.

*Set.*

He opened. He reached. He shut. The right boot came up. It came down on the eleventh rung.

She watched the heel find the iron.

And she heard it.

She heard it through the noise of the spouts and the crowd's silence and the cold. It was not loud. It was a small dull sound inside him, like a green stick breaking under a wet cloth.

Perrin stopped.

He did not open. He did not shut. He stood on the eleventh rung with his right boot on it and his left boot on the tenth, with his arms locked on the rung at his chest and the Purser's painted face over his shoulder, and he did not move at all. She could see his back. It had gone quite still under the canvas band, the way a horse goes still when it is deciding whether to fall.

*If you hear it go, stop.*

He had stopped.

Kiva stood in the water up to her thighs and looked at his boot. The heel was on the iron. It was settled. She had not said the word.

She did not say it now. It was not hers to say. The word was for when the boot was on, and the boot was on, and he had not opened. He was hanging by his arms on a ladder with a broken rib and eighty-five kilos on his back and nothing under him, and if she said *set* he would open because she had said it, and she did not know whether that was what he wanted. She did not know whether he could.

"*Cade,*" said somebody above. It was Jude, from the lip, very high and thin.

Perrin did not answer.

Kiva watched his boot. She heard Anwen beside her draw breath to say something, and stop. The water came up another inch. Somewhere at the far end of the chamber, under the lock's tail gates, the marshal was standing on the stair with his glass, watching, and he did not say anything either.

Two counts. Three.

Then Perrin breathed. She heard it. It was a long breath in, careful, all the way down, and a breath out. And his right heel moved on the eleventh rung. It moved a little, settling, an eighth of an inch, the way a man settles his weight on a floor he is about to stand up on.

She watched it stop.

"*Set,*" said Kiva.

He opened on the end of the word.

She felt the ground take him. She felt the whole of his weight, and the Purser's, go down out of his arms through his boot and into the wall and the earth, all at once, and she saw his arms go slack on the rung above him, just for a moment, as a man lets his arms go slack when somebody else has taken the load. He hung there, planted, the ground, with nothing in his arms.

Then he reached for the twelfth rung.

He was slower after that. Four a rung, Anwen said, and then five. Kiva did not hear her. She heard the boot come down, and watched the heel, and said the word, and felt him plant.

At the fifteenth rung, his left hand missed the iron.

It went up past the rung and slapped the granite and came down again and found it. Kiva's heart went into her throat and stayed there. He was shut. He was a man on a ladder. He hung by one hand for the space of a breath, with the Purser swinging out from his back on the canvas band. Then his other hand closed on the rung beside the first, and his boot came up, and came down on the sixteenth.

She watched the heel. She could hardly see it now. He was sixteen feet up and the spray from the spouts was in her eyes.

*Set.*

At the eighteenth rung, the Purser slipped.

The left loop of the sling had worked down off Perrin's shoulder onto his upper arm. The Purser's weight swung out to the left, away from the wall, and the band across Perrin's chest dragged sideways, and Kiva saw him lean into the granite with his whole body to hold it.

He was planted. He was the ground. The ground cannot fall off a wall. But his arm was in the loop, and his arm was being pulled out from the wall by eighty-five kilos of sand, and he could not reach the next rung with it.

"*Strap,*" said Jude.

He was hanging down over the lip. He had come further than Kiva would have thought anybody could come over the edge of a lock and not fall in; he was out over the drop to his waist, with his boots hooked round something behind him on the quay that she could not see. He reached down with his left arm. It was his bad arm, the frosted one, the one that was meant to go back into its linen after every contest, the one Aske had told him not to lean on.

He got two fingers into the slipped loop and pulled it up.

It came up off Perrin's arm and over his shoulder again. Jude held it there with two fingers. His face over the lip was white as paper.

"Go on," said Jude. "I've got it. Go on."

Perrin's boot came up and found the nineteenth rung.

*Set.*

The twentieth.

*Set.*

At the twenty-first, Jude let go of the loop and took the Purser by the collar instead, both hands, and at the twenty-second, Perrin did not reach for a rung at all. There was no rung. There was the lip of the lock, rounded granite, worn smooth. He planted on the twenty-second rung with his head and shoulders above the lip and the Purser above them, and he put both hands flat on the quay and pushed, and Jude pulled, and the Purser went over the edge and lay on his back on the quay.

Perrin came over after him on his knees.

Kiva lost sight of him then. There was only the lip of the lock, and the sky, and the faces all along the edge, which had begun to shout.

She did not see the last twenty paces. She heard them.

She heard the crowd on the quay, a great noise, going along the lip from the head of the lock toward the lockkeeper's hut. She heard it go up and up. She heard somebody, not Jude, say *mind him, mind him,* and somebody else laugh.

And she heard the marshal at the mark, because his voice was the only flat thing in the whole of the noise.

"*On the mark,*" he said. "*Three minutes and eight seconds. Clean.*"

Anwen was holding her arm.

Kiva had not known that she was. She looked down. Anwen's hand was round her left forearm, above the wrist, and gripping, and Anwen was looking up at the lip of the lock with her mouth a little open. The water was at their waists. It was so cold that Kiva could not feel anything below her ribs at all.

"Three and eight," said Anwen. "He did three and eight. With that."

"The stair," said Kiva. Her teeth were going. "We'll drown."

They went down the chamber toward the tail through the water, which was running with them now, pushing at their backs, pouring in from the head and going out nowhere, because the tail gates were shut. At the stair a Board man in grey reached down and took Anwen's hand and then Kiva's, and pulled, and they came up the wet green steps onto the quay with water running out of their boots and their coats and their hair.

The quay was a crowd.

Kiva could not see Perrin through it. She could see the top of the lockkeeper's hut, and the Mercury's long glass on its stand, swinging round. She pushed. People let her through when they saw that she was wet to the waist, and somebody said *that's the catcher, that's the one on the far edge,* and somebody else said *that's Rowan,* and she kept going.

He was sitting on a bollard by the chalk circle.

The Purser lay on the mark beside him, on his back, with his painted face to the sky and the Board's sling still round his chest. Jude was lying flat on the quay beside the Purser, with his eyes shut and his left arm across his chest and his right arm flung out, as if he too had been carried up a wall in a sling. Aske was kneeling in front of the bollard with both hands flat on Perrin's side under the wet shirt.

Perrin looked up and saw her.

He did not smile. His face was grey and wet and his mouth was set. He looked at her the way he had looked at her on the lip of the lock last night, sitting with his legs over twenty-four feet of empty air.

"You didn't say it," he said. "On the eleventh."

"You hadn't moved."

"I know I hadn't." He drew a breath. It stopped halfway, and he waited, and drew the rest. "I couldn't. For three counts I couldn't. If you'd said it, I'd have opened because you'd said it, and I'd not have been able to stand it, and I'd have come off." He shut his eyes. "You waited for my heel."

"I always wait for the heel. That's the word."

"I know." He opened his eyes again. "I'd like it known."

"Be quiet," said Aske, without looking up. "Both of you. One's gone, Cade. The seventh, on the left. It's cracked, not through. I heard it go from the lip; everybody did." He took his hands away. "You'll sit on this bollard and you'll not move off it till I say. And you'll not lift anything, or plant anything, or carry anything heavier than a cup of tea for a fortnight. I'll write it on a card."

"There's the Longshore tomorrow."

"There's the Longshore tomorrow," said Aske. "And you'll be on it with your hands in your pockets, or you'll not be on it at all." He stood up. "You did three minutes and eight seconds with a cracked rib. I've written that on the card as well. I thought you'd want it there."

---

There were eleven fours still to run.

Kiva had not thought about that. She sat on the quay with her back against the lockkeeper's hut and a blanket round her that somebody had put there, and her boots off, and her feet in Anwen's lap being rubbed by Anwen's hands, which were nearly as cold as her feet. She looked at the chamber. The lockkeeper had shut his shutters. The water was going out through the tail sluices into the basin with a sound like a bath draining. The Board's men in grey were standing on the stair waiting for it to go down, with a dry sailcloth folded over their arms.

"Eleven," said Anwen. "And the last of them's the Ropewalk."

"I know."

"Halden. The Tarn. Old Four. Twenty-Eight."

"I know who they are."

"I'm saying it so I'll stop thinking it." Anwen rubbed her foot. "Three and eight is good. It's very good. The best before us was three and twenty-seven."

"Three and eight is a time," said Kiva. "Somebody'll beat it."

The fourteenth run went. It was a four Kiva did not know, in Westerley's sea blue, and they took the stair and came in at three and twenty-six. The fifteenth was Holloway in dark red. They took the ladder with a rope and were clean and quick, three and twenty-one, and the quay was pleased with them.

The sixteenth was Team Twenty-Eight.

Kiva watched Dray go down the stair onto the chamber floor on two feet, without a stick. She had not seen her run since the autumn. She did not run now. She walked to the chalk line at the tail with Marten and the others and stood there with her face like a shut door, and when the bell went she ran.

She ran well. She ran with a limp, slight, on the left, and nobody who had not seen her in a chair in the ninth month would have known it was a limp. Twenty-Eight took the stair with a litter and a good strong Hold under the cloth. At the spar Dray cut both lashings with one stroke of her edge, the line going taut and parting together, and Kiva heard Jude, beside her on the quay, say *oh* under his breath.

"Three and nineteen," said the marshal.

Dray came up the stair afterward and walked past the lockkeeper's hut. She looked down at Kiva sitting against it in a blanket with her bare feet in Anwen's lap. She did not stop.

"Your captain's got a cracked rib," she said, going past. "The whole quay heard it."

"He has."

"Good climb," said Dray, and went on.

Old Four went at three and sixteen, on the stair, very solid. Brask carried the Purser across his shoulders the whole length of the chamber through water to his waist and never once put him down, and Old Four's Hold walked a wall beside him the whole way to keep the spray off the painted face. They were clean. They were fast. They were eight seconds slower than three minutes and eight.

"Eight seconds," said Jude.

He had sat up. He was sitting on the quay beside the Purser, who nobody had yet taken away, with his left arm held against his chest in his right hand.

"Your arm," said Kiva.

"It's frosted. It was frosted already." Jude did not look at it. "It's not worse. It's just mine. I leaned on it once, for a loop, and I'd do it again." He looked at the chamber. "Eight seconds. That's Brask. Brask's the strongest carry in Meridian."

The Board's men spread a dry sailcloth. They lashed the spar. The water went out.

"*The Tarn,*" said the Clerk. "*First four.*"

Trewin went down the stair as he did everything, without hurry.

He stood at the chalk line with his four in brown and buff behind him and his hat in his hand, and he gave his hat to the marshal to hold, and nodded to him, slowly, as though nodding were a thing that had to be done properly. The marshal took the hat. Somebody on the quay laughed, not unkindly.

The bell went.

Kiva had not seen the Tarn carry before. She had seen them in a lane, a square that came down a pen without any of its corners changing place. They came down the chamber in that square now, and at the Purser they did a thing that she had not expected. They did not lift the cloth. Their Hold put her ward under the near edge and their blade put the flat of his edge on the far edge, the leaded hem, pressing it down into the water, so that when the ward lifted, the cloth folded on itself and did not slide. It was the same thing Kiva had done with her boots. It was slower, because two people were doing it. It was cleaner.

"They watched you," said Anwen.

"They'd have done it anyway."

"They watched you," said Anwen again, "and they did it with a blade instead of a door."

Trewin took the spar off the Purser's shins with his still door planted, and set it down. He bent and picked up the Purser by himself and put him across his shoulders, the way a shepherd carries a sheep, with an arm and a leg in each hand. Then he turned round and walked the fifty paces back down the chamber to the tail stair, through water that was over his knees, at a walk.

He did not run. He did not need to. Kiva watched his feet. He planted on every step he took, and the whole quay went quiet.

She had not seen anybody do that. She did not think anybody could. A still door is a slow thing; it goes down and holds, and you do not walk with it any more than you walk with a post. Trewin walked with it. He put his foot down, and planted, and the water broke round his boot as round a post, and he shut and lifted the other foot, and planted. It was slow. It was so slow that the quay began to murmur. And not once did the Purser's face come within a foot of the water, because the water could not move Trewin an inch.

At the stair he went up the steps the same way, a step and a plant, a step and a plant.

"That's what you did," said Jude, in a low voice. "On the ladder. Only he's doing it on the floor."

"He's doing it on fifty paces," said Kiva. "And a stair. And nobody's saying *set* to him."

The Tarn's four came along the quay behind him at a run. Trewin went the last twenty paces to the mark at his walk, and laid the Purser down on it, quite gently, on his back, and straightened up.

"*Three minutes and twelve,*" said the marshal. "*Clean.*"

The quay roared. It was the first time it had roared all morning. Kiva saw people on the lockkeeper's roof stamping. She saw a woman in brown and buff weeping openly by the hut. And she saw Trewin take his hat back from the marshal and put it on, and look along the quay, face by face, until he found her sitting against the hut in her blanket. He nodded to her once.

She nodded back.

"Four seconds," said Anwen. Her hands had stopped on Kiva's foot. "He was four seconds off. Carrying it on the floor."

"He was safer than we were."

"He was safer than anybody's ever been." Anwen looked at Trewin standing by the mark. "He didn't need a word. He never moves when he doesn't mean to."

---

Halden License went second to last.

Kiva watched Kellan go down the stair in his black and gold, and stand on the chalk line, and not look up. Pettit declared. The bell went.

Halden took the ladder. They did not take a rope. Hesketh's ward went under the cloth's near edge, and Kellan, Kiva saw, put his edge flat on the far edge, as the Tarn had, pressing the lead down. They had watched too. Pettit took the spar. And then Halden did a thing that was neither Seven's nor the Ropewalk's. Pettit and Yarrow went up the ladder side by side, one on each edge of the iron rungs, with the Purser between them in the Board's sling, one loop over Pettit's shoulder and one over Yarrow's. They climbed together, rung for rung, with Kellan below them on the rungs calling *left, right, left,* and the Purser came up the wall between them like a chest between two porters on a stair.

It was quick. It was very quick. Nobody planted anything. Nobody needed to.

"Three and ten," said the marshal. "Clean."

"Two seconds," said Jude.

He had got up. He was standing by the hut with his arm held against his chest and his face set. Kiva had never seen him look like that. He looked like a man watching a pot that he had been told would not boil.

"Two seconds," he said again. "And the Ropewalk."

The Ropewalk's first four went last.

They had watched every run. Kiva had seen them watching from the end of the rope, Carrow with her arms folded and her quick blunt face giving nothing away, and Treen beside her with her eyes on the chamber the whole morning, never once looking at the crowd. Tolver had been talking to people in the crowd. Lyle had been standing by herself.

They went down the stair. Carrow declared, in a voice that carried up the walls. "*Carrow, edge and stance, laid. Lyle, ward and stance, laid. Tolver, stance. Treen, ward.*"

The bell went.

They were very fast. Kiva had known that they would be. They ran the chamber in a body, and at the Purser Lyle laid. Her ward went under the cloth and her feet into the floor together, in one breath, and the cloth went up and over and stayed there, because a laid ward does not move. Tolver took the spar off the shins with his stance, planted. Carrow's edge cut both lashings. Treen had the rope over her shoulder and was at the ladder before the cloth had stopped moving, and up it, light and fast, like a cat up a curtain.

"Twenty," said the man on the lip with the watch.

It was six seconds faster than Seven. Kiva heard Anwen's breath go in.

At the foot of the ladder, Carrow and Lyle and Tolver had the Purser up against the wall, and Tolver was tying the rope round his chest under the arms with quick big hands, and Carrow was holding the Purser's head.

The water was at the ladder's foot. It was coming up. The lockkeeper had turned his crank at the minute, as he had turned it at the minute for twenty-three fours before them, and the spouts were full, and the water came down the chamber and pooled at the head end where the ladder was, and it was at the Ropewalk's knees.

Tolver tied the knot. He tied it twice. He was being careful, because a knot that slips on a wall is twenty seconds.

The Purser's head lolled forward.

Carrow had it in her hands. She had been holding it up. But she was holding it with nothing open. A laid girl cannot walk, and to lift and walk she had shut both doors. And the Purser was heavy, and wet, and folding at the neck, and his chin went down onto his chest. His painted face went down into the brown water to the eyebrows and came up again.

"*Face,*" said the marshal.

It was flat. It was the only flat thing in the whole roar.

Carrow did not look up. She did not stop. She lifted the head again and held it till Lyle had it, and went up the ladder after Treen, and Tolver said *up,* and Carrow and Treen hauled from the top, and the Purser went up the north wall of the Inner Lock on the end of a rope, steady and fast, with Tolver planted at the bottom keeping him off the stone. It was beautiful. It was the fastest thing anybody had done all morning.

They ran him to the mark.

"*Two minutes and fifty-nine seconds,*" said the marshal. "*Ten for the face. Three minutes and nine.*"

---

It was very quiet for a moment.

Then the Clerk came along the quay in her grey coat, and stood by the mark, and the quiet went quieter.

"The Carry," she said. "The second board. Twenty-four fours run. No four struck." She did not look at a paper. "First. Team Seven, Meridian. Three minutes and eight. Second. The Ropewalk School, first four. Three minutes and nine. Third. Halden License, Meridian. Three minutes and ten. Fourth. The Tarn, first four. Three minutes and twelve. Fifth. Old Four, Meridian. Three minutes and sixteen. Sixth. Team Twenty-Eight, Meridian. Three minutes and nineteen." She paused. "The rest will be posted at the tent at six, with the standings on two boards."

She turned away. Then she turned back.

"There is a protest," she said, "from Old Four, Meridian. Lodged at the mark by its captain, Brask, at eleven minutes past twelve."

The quay murmured. Kiva saw Perrin, on his bollard, go very still.

"The protest is that the carry of Team Seven planted a still door in a wall and not in a floor," said the Clerk. "Its captain cites the rule. A still door plants in the floor, and does not plant in a square." She did not raise her voice. "I have read the rule. It says a still door does not plant in a square. There are no squares on a ladder. The protest is dismissed." She looked along the quay. "Captain Brask, I thank you for the reading. It was close. It was not close enough."

There was a laugh. It went round the quay and grew.

Kiva looked for Brask. He was standing by the stair with Old Four round him, a big mild young man who read people as other people read the labels on jars, and he did not laugh. He did not look angry either. He looked across the quay at Perrin on his bollard for a long moment, and then he lifted one hand, flat, and held it there, the way a referee holds up a hand for a call that was fair. Then he turned away.

"He taught me to say thank you to the ground," said Perrin, very low. "In the junior lane. When I was thirteen."

He did not lift his hand back. He sat on his bollard with Aske's linen round his chest and the whole quay looking at him, and his face had gone a strange colour, neither pale nor red. Kiva had never seen it before.

"First," said Anwen. "Kiva. First."

"I heard."

"By a second. By one second, off the Ropewalk." Anwen let go of her foot at last and put both hands over her face, and took them away again. "On a ladder. With his rib."

Jude had sat down again on the quay. He had the book out on his knee and he was writing, very slowly, with the pencil in his right hand and his left arm held against his chest.

"*Tuesday the 15th,*" he said aloud, as he wrote. "*The Carry, the Inner Lock. First, three minutes and eight. Cade, twenty-two rungs, twenty-two hundredths, and a rib. Pryce, nine, for the cloth. Talley, four, for two lashings. Rowan on the far edge and at the foot, her own thimble.*" He looked at it. "*Thirty-five. Held, three and a half.*"

He shut the book.

"I'd want it said," he said, to nobody in particular, "that I was at the top. That I saw his face come up over the lip." He was quiet a moment. "I'll not write what it looked like. It's not that kind of book."

---

The Mercury's man got to Perrin before anybody could stop him.

He was a young man with ink on his cuffs and a notebook already open. He came pushing through the people round the bollard and stood in front of Perrin and said, in a quick coast voice, "Mr. Cade. The Mercury. How did you think of it, sir? The rungs. Nobody's ever planted a ladder."

Perrin looked at him.

"I sat on the lip," he said. "Last night. For an hour."

"And it came to you?"

"No. I worked it out." Perrin's voice was hoarse. "A rung's iron. It's in granite. The granite's in the ground."

The young man wrote it down. He wrote it with his mouth moving, the way Pru read. "*The granite's in the ground,*" he said. "That's very good. That's a line. And the girl at the foot, sir, calling? That was the catcher? Rowan?"

"That was the word," said Perrin. "The ladder was mine."

The young man looked up from his book. He looked at Perrin's face, and then at Kiva in her blanket against the hut, and something in his own face changed, as if he had found something more interesting than what he had come for.

"Yes, sir," he said. "Of course. *The ladder was mine.*" He wrote that down too. "And how does it feel, sir? To beat the Ropewalk on their own quay? People will be talking about it for years. They're talking about it now." He jerked his head at the crowd. "Half the low town's down here. They came to see the Ropewalk. They'll go home talking about you."

Perrin did not answer.

The young man waited, and then, when he saw that no answer was coming, he touched his hat and went away into the crowd with his notebook, to find somebody who would talk.

Kiva saw them then. She had not been looking. Now she saw how many people were looking at Perrin.

They were all along the quay. They were Saltmere green, and Holloway red, and Westerley blue, and the buff of the Tarn, and Meridian. They were looking at him the way people look at a man who has done a thing that they wish they had done. Some of them were smiling. Some of them were not. A boy in green by the stair said something to the girl beside him, low, and the girl laughed, and they both looked at Perrin, and the girl stopped laughing. By the hut, one of Old Four was saying to another, not quite low enough, *he'll not stand tomorrow, any road. Not with that.*

Perrin heard it. Kiva saw him hear it.

He sat on his bollard with his hands on his knees. He did not look at anybody. He looked at the Purser, lying on the mark with his painted face to the sky and the Board's sling round his chest.

"Kiva," he said.

She got up, with the blanket round her, and went and stood beside the bollard. Her bare feet were on the cold granite.

"I wanted a thing with only my name on it," said Perrin, very low. "All winter. I told you so, out loud, at home. I minded that it was always you."

"I know."

"Now it's got only my name on it." He was still looking at the Purser. "And everybody on this quay's looking at it. And half of them want it." He breathed in, carefully, as far as he could go, and out. "I thought it would feel like having something."

"What does it feel like?"

He thought about it for a long time. He was honest about things. He always had been, even when it cost him, even in the autumn when he had stood in a yard and said *I mind* out loud.

"Like being looked at," he said. "That's all. Like being looked at by people who'd like to be where I am, and think I'm lucky, and don't know about the rib or the lip or the hour." He looked up at her at last. "That's what it was like for you. Wasn't it. All year."

Kiva did not answer at once.

She thought of the gate at home in the autumn, and the Pit, and the Old Yard, and the white arm. She thought of every floor she had stood on since the spring with people looking at her, and of a boy who had stood up in front of his own four and said *I mind,* out loud, and had been Perrin.

"Yes," she said. "It's that."

"It's not better," said Perrin. "Being envied. I thought it would be better than the other thing. It isn't."

"No."

"It's not worse either." He looked back at the Purser. "It's just the other side of the same thing. I'd not have known that. I'd have gone on thinking that you had something I hadn't." He was quiet. "I'd like it known that I know now."

"It's known," said Kiva.

Aske came back through the crowd with a cup of tea in each hand. He gave one to Perrin and one to Kiva, and looked at them both.

"Drink that," he said. "Neither of you is to be looked at by anybody for at least a quarter of an hour. I'll stand here and see to it."

He stood in front of the bollard with his bag in his hand and his back to the quay, a narrow grey man who never raised his voice, and for a quarter of an hour nobody came near them at all.
