# Chapter 3 — Kiva: In Her Own Copy

Mara heard the whole of it standing up, with a soldering iron cooling in its rest and a hall panel open on the bench in front of her.

She did not interrupt. That was her way with a fault report. When Kiva had finished, her aunt took the card, and held it to the shop lamp, and read it as she read a maker's plate.

"A pump-fitter," she said.

"At Saltmere."

"Whose harbour bank went into step at twenty past two on the seventeenth." Mara turned the card over. There was nothing on the back. "I've twelve sheets gone down that river to a harbour pump house. I addressed them to a keeper whose name I don't know. It's a small trade. I shouldn't wonder if he's had his thumbs on my tape already."

"He doesn't say."

"He wrote this before they'd left my dresser." She gave it back. "And Dane's had it since the fourteenth."

"Ten days. She said why. She said three whys."

"I'm sure that she did, and I'm sure that the third was the true one; she's that kind." Her aunt wiped her left hand on her apron, slowly, a finger at a time. "I knew that she'd had a candidate hurt. Every operator in the Works knows it; it's what's said when her name comes up. I didn't know that he'd a hand left, or a trade. I find that I think the better of him for the trade."

"I'm going to make him a copy."

"Of what?"

"The method. And what was got wrong first."

"Then you'll send something worth the postage." Mara sat down, on the high stool, which she seldom did in the shop. "I've a thing to ask, and I'll ask it once. There's a page of mine in a press at the Compact Library, under a seal, with the hour on it. You know what's on that page. Nobody else living does."

"Yes."

"I'd like to know that it isn't going south in pieces."

Kiva had known that it would come. She had seen it coming from the far side of the yard, while Orla was still talking about a pile.

"It isn't in my book," she said. "You know that. I've never written it. Not the roof, nor the rest."

"Lumb's page is in your book."

"A line that says there *is* a page. *Lumb's book for 2604, one leaf, to be kept; see M.R.* Orla's read it. It told her that the Works had an old book."

"It'd tell a man more than that, if his own bank fell in on the same night as ours and he knew it by a bad hand." Mara's voice was quite dry. "I'm not forbidding anything. I told you in the spring that I'd not own what you chose, and I've found it a great deal harder than forbidding. I'm asking what you mean to do."

"I don't know yet. I've not got to that page."

"When you get to it, you'll tell me. Not ask. Tell."

"Yes."

"Good." Her aunt took up the iron again and looked at its tip. "There's Works paper in the second drawer. It's ruled in seven. I'd not see you send a method to a fitter on a laundry ticket."

---

It took a fortnight of evenings, and for the first three Kiva did not write a word.

She read. She took the book from where it lay by the brass weight, and she began at the spring, and she tried to go through it as a man might who had never stood in the Old Yard in his life.

It was a strange, uncomfortable thing to do. She had always read that book as one reads a diary, hurrying, knowing how each day came out; now she made herself stop at every line and ask what a pump-fitter on the south coast would be able to do with it.

Mostly he could do nothing.

*Stance clean. Ground took me. Orla says wider.*

*Crossed. Landed. Cold to the wrist after. 0.31.*

*Stacked again. Soft. I knew as I did it.*

It was all true, and it was all written by somebody who had been there to somebody who had been there, and there was not a page in the first forty that told a stranger where to put his feet.

She ruled a sheet of the Works paper on the fourth evening. It was good stiff stuff, with the seven columns printed faint blue and nothing at their heads, and she wrote the heads herself, as they stood in a manual three flights down under the Library and on a sheet in a tin case at the Home Line hall. *What. How. Went wrong. Cost. Second try. Why.* The first column had no name. It was only for what the thing was called and where it lay.

Then she began to move the book across.

*A crossing, Ember to still. At Meridian, in the yard under the south wall, and since on sanctioned masts.*

*What: one door is shut before the next is opened. Between them there is a gap with nothing open at all.*

*How: test the ground first. Open the still door where you mean to land, by its corners, and shut it again; if there is a room underneath, do not land there. Then say aloud what you are doing, to a count, so that anybody near can hear which door and when. Bound low. Shut the first door at the top of the bound. Wait until the last of it has run home along the ribs. Then open the second, wide, before the foot comes down.*

She read that over. It was longer than anything in the green pages; it had cost her a summer, and it went into nine lines.

*Went wrong: opened the second door before the first was shut. Three times. That is a stack. It is soft, and it costs a third of the price, and it lands. It is also two doors open together, and open to anything that is near. I am forbidden it, and I was right to be.*

*Cost: three-tenths of a dram, about, of which twenty-six hundredths is the gap. And the left hand cold to the wrist for an hour after.*

*Second try: counted it aloud, and had it said back.*

She stopped at the last column.

*Why.* She knew why; she had known since a night on a hill. But she had never once written it, because nobody who read her book had needed to be told.

*Why: because a door that is open is open to whatever is by. A gap is dear, and it is shut.*

It looked very bare. She left it.

There were five entries by the end of the first week. There was the crossing, and there was the ground test, by itself, with its own *went wrong:* a cross in the dirt that she had landed on all summer, with eight feet of drain under it. There was the list of things that her two fingers could not do, set out as a method, since that was what it had become: *say it before, by name, to whoever has the other end.* There was a drum on the Brickfields road, and two people saying *now.*

And there was the long thing.

She had not known whether to send it. He was twin-kindled, Ember and hand, and his doors had fought on a live cell in front of four thousand people; it seemed a hard thing to post to such a man that somebody had since done it with three.

But he had asked what she had written down, and it was in the book. She had written it three times.

*Three doors in sequence. Hand, then Ember, then still. Not certified. Not classified. Not to be asked of anybody; the Chair has written so.*

*What: catch, carry, plant. Two gaps.*

*How:*

She sat with the pencil over that square for the best part of an hour.

She could write the order. She could write that the ward went under the thing on its slack, bent like the bowl of a spoon, for one tap. She could write that it was folded from the far edge to a penny before the shove arrived.

She could not write the fold; it was not hers. She had learned it off a first-year on a pail, and did it with two fingers stuck out like a fork; and when she tried to put down what the hands did, she found that she was describing Pru's.

She could not write the second gap either.

The first was only dear, and one paid for it. The second came, each time, out of somewhere that had already been emptied, and something paid all the same, and it went out through her left arm; she had felt it pass the wrist. That was not a method. It was a thing that had happened to her three times and that she did not understand.

And at the end of it there was a line of Orla's. *Arrive before you're there.* It was the truest instruction that she had ever been given, and it only worked when it was said aloud, by a woman on a crate, to somebody already in the air.

She looked at the sheet.

She had always thought that she wrote everything down, and a pump-fitter thought so, two hundred miles off, on the strength of one line in a letter. And here were three places where she had written that a thing happened, for a spring and a summer and an autumn, and had never once said how.

It struck her, and not pleasantly, that those were the three places where the hand was.

She did not fill them; that would have been the tidying kind of lie. She ruled each square off with a line across its corner, as a clerk voids a cheque, and under the first she wrote what was so.

*I cannot say this so that another person could do it. I have done it. I have not got it in words yet.*

Then *Went wrong,* which was easy, and long.

Then *Cost.* She had the cost by heart: it was in a foreman's ledger, and on a healer's card, and it had been said aloud both times before she put a hand up. *The first two fingers of the left hand, for good, at the second. They bend and grip and report pressure and nothing else. At the third, the arm, without feeling to above the elbow, for four days; it came back to where it was. Her healer does not know that it would a fourth time. Nor do I.*

She wrote *her,* and saw that she had, and left that as well.

---

She came to the page of the sixth on a Thursday, with the lamp turned up and her aunt across the table reconciling the trunk.

It was a short entry; she had been very tired when she wrote it.

*Pump house, about one. M.R.'s reading hand starved to the wrist. Eased by Aske with 1.2 of our sealed W. A.R. held the comb: plain. Lumb's book for 2604, one leaf, to be kept; see M.R.*

Kiva read the last line, and then she did not read anything for a while.

It was not that she had forgotten; one did not forget it. But she had not let herself look straight at it since the night in the pump house, with three pumps going and a calf book open under a desk lamp.

She had been thirteen the first time, and it had come again in the spring, on Classification Day, with a fever after it, and once more in the Pit, in the fifth month. There had been no roof. That was the part that she could never get said properly, even to Mara: it was not that the roof was gone and one saw the dark. There was nothing over her at all, and the nothing was not empty. It was enormous, and it was paying attention.

A woman had written that down, lifetimes ago, in a house on a hill, and a pump keeper had written it in 2604, in brown ink, with his needles standing up. *Not dark. Nothing.*

And on the seventeenth of the ninth month, at twenty past two, a harbour bank had fallen into step two hundred miles off, and a man had known what it was with a hand that reported very little.

*Somebody at Saltmere had a temperature as well.* Lumb had said that, rubbing his nose.

She could ask him.

It would go into four lines. *Did you have a fever that night, or any night? Did you dream that there was no roof?* He was the only person whom she had ever heard of who might say yes and be alive to say it. She found that she wanted the answer so much that her hand had gone to the pencil of its own accord.

She made herself take it away again and think like somebody who had been brought up over an instrument shop.

What would she do with the card when it came?

It would come in a round hand, by the coast boat, to Coper Street, and it would say yes or it would say no; either way it would be an answer to a question, and the question would have had the roof in it.

There was a page under a seal in the Library's press. Mara had written it and had the hour put on it, and she had done that for one reason: so that nobody could ever say that a girl of seventeen had read an old Master's fever and borrowed it. It was worth something only for as long as it had been got clean.

A needle told one nothing if it had been told beforehand what to read. She had heard that across a shop counter since she was five.

If she wrote to a stranger, *did you dream that there was no roof,* and he wrote back, *I did,* then that was not a second reading. That was her own, come home by boat.

So it was not to be asked; it was not even to be hinted. If Jessamy Hart had a thing to say about a fever, he would have to say it without her having put the words in his mouth; and if he never said it, then she would never know, and that was the price.

It was a good deal to give up for the sake of a seal that might never be broken. She sat and felt how much.

Then she took a clean line on the Works paper, and she did not copy the entry of the sixth. She copied the drum on either side of it.

"Mara."

"Mm."

"I've got to the page. It isn't going. Not the line about Lumb's leaf. And I'm not asking him anything."

Her aunt put down her pen.

"I'd have understood the line. Why not a question?"

"Because whatever he said back would be mine. I'd have told the needle what to read." Kiva kept her eyes on the blue columns. "You sealed yours so that nobody could say I'd borrowed. If I start writing to people about roofs, I'm lending. Anything they say after that is worth half."

Mara was silent for a moment.

"That's a better reason than mine," she said. "I only didn't want you hurt. I'd not thought about the evidence. And me with a license." She took up the pen again, and did not write with it. "You wanted to ask."

"Yes."

"Very much?"

"I'd my hand on the pencil."

"I'm sorry for it. I'll not pretend that I'm not relieved as well; I'd be lying, and you'd see." Something happened at the corner of her mouth, and went again. "You'll say that there's a line left out."

"On the cover. I told Orla that I would. I'll not say which."

"Then he'll know that there's a door shut, and that you shut it. I'd think the more of a correspondent for that. It's the ones with no gaps that I'd watch."

Kiva wrote the drum.

It was the easiest sheet of the six. *One at the needle. One at the water. Neither counts.* It went across almost as it stood.

---

The card went back to Rhea Sorn on Monday the first, at six, in the Graywater hall yard, with four names on the back of it in brown ink.

Rhea read the back before she read the front. She stood under the hooded lamp with her coat open, and she turned the slate-blue card to the light as a grader turns a tin.

"*Pryce. Cade. Renn. Rowan.*" She did not look up. "I've been an officer of this company for eleven years. I've never had a card come back with a team written on it. I don't know that there's a column."

"There wasn't one for *before,* either," said Pike.

He was by the stove, in the open door of the mending room, with a purse sheet across his knees. He had a needle in his right hand. Kiva saw him shut the hand on it twice, slowly, as a man works a stiff glove, before he set a stitch.

"So it's signed," said Rhea. "Then it stands as it's written, and I'll not add to it. The first of the first is a Friday. I don't ride on a Friday for choice; a bell chooses. You'll have a card from a boy on the night, and it'll say *yard, now.* Two in the month."

"And my conditions."

"The *before* column, read aloud, to all. Your four first." The pale eyes rested on her. "I wrote them on the slate the day you asked. I'd not have you think that I carry them in my head. I'd lose them."

"Have you any advice?"

"I had my piece for the autumn. I spent it on the cinders, telling you that you'd hate it." Rhea put the card inside her coat. "You'll get another when it's winter by the almanac. I ration them. It's the only thing in this hall that I've never run short of."

She went in. Kiva heard her boots on the iron stair.

Pru had come, since the card said *Finch.* She stood at Kiva's shoulder with a hank of waxed cord round her wrist, and she was already making turns on it, small and quick, without looking.

"Stop that," said Pike. "No. Don't stop. Do it again."

Pru did it again.

"Your little finger."

"It's a second pin." She went pink. "The thumb was lifting. On the third, when the wax is hard. It was letting the first turn go."

Pike laid the needle down. He took her wrist, quite gently, and turned it to the stove, and had her make the knot four times while he watched.

"Thirty in thirty-four," he said. "I counted the four that were wrong. I had you on my knee in that van for the best part of a month, as near as I am now. I never saw why." He let go. "Who showed you?"

"Nobody showed me. I found the pin."

"Who saw the thumb?"

Pru looked at Kiva, and said nothing, in a way that could have been heard from the street.

"I was a corner," said Kiva. "I was kneeling next to it. There wasn't anything else on that floor for me to do."

"So you watched a pair of hands."

"Yes."

"And said what was there."

"I said that the thumb came up. I didn't know that it mattered. I can't feel a cord; I'd no notion what the turn was doing underneath."

Pike did not say anything for some time. He was a square grey man, and he had a square grey way of being silent. It was not approval. Kiva had been looked at with approval, and with a good deal else, and she knew this for what it was. He was put out.

"Six-and-twenty years," he said at last. "I've trained a dozen corner-hands and four catchers. I'd have told you that I knew what every one of them did with a cord." He picked the needle up again. "And that's what I've asked you to do to me. I wrote it into a Torch's card. I've had three weeks to think better of it."

"You could say no. It's on the card. *Either of us.*"

"That's between her and you. I'm only the man you'll be kneeling by." He nearly smiled. It did not get far. "I'll tell you what a second does, now that you've begun without being told. You'll be bad at it. You'll see what's wrong, and you'll say that. Any fool can see what's wrong; I've a healer at the tiller who does it for nothing. I don't want what's wrong."

"What do you want?"

"What's there. All of it. What I do with my knees and where I look and which hand I use, and when. The things that I'd not think to say, because I've done them since before you were born." He set a stitch. "And you'll not get that by looking for mistakes. You got the thumb because you weren't looking for one. Go and do that for two months."

"To whom?"

"To people who can't send you home. I can, and Sorn can. Find somebody who's good at a thing, and has been for longer than is decent, and has to put up with you." He bit the thread. "And write it. I'm told that you write."

"In what?"

"I don't care if it's in the margin of a hymn book. Bring it in the new year. I'll want to see what you make of somebody else before I let you loose on me."

Pru had gone on making knots the whole time. She did not drop one.

"He's cross," she said, on the tram.

"He's cross with himself. It comes out the same."

"I told you he'd not like it." She looked rather pleased. Then she looked at Kiva's left cuff, which had come undone, and did it up without a word.

---

Mrs. Pryce could not send her home, since Kiva had been in and out of that laundry from the age of nine, and had been fed there.

It was Saturday the sixth. The streets off drum twenty-nine, which had been fouled on the night of the pump house and was never one of the seven, had come off their boxes that morning, a month to the day, and all Coper Street was washing. The ironing room was full of steam and the smell of starch. There was a deal table in the middle of it, eight feet long, scrubbed white, and Mrs. Pryce stood at the end of that table with her sleeves rolled and folded double sheets by herself.

It took two to fold a double. Everybody knew that. Kiva and Anwen had done it in the drying yard since they were small, a girl at each end, walking in.

Mrs. Pryce did one in the time that it took to say so.

"Watch if you like," she said. "You'll not learn anything. It's only folding."

Kiva sat on the mangle bench with a laundry ticket on her knee, face down, and a pencil.

She had been told not to look for mistakes. There were none to look for; the woman had been at that table for thirty years. So she did what she had done with a thumb. She looked at one thing at a time, as though she were going to be asked for an inventory.

The sheet came off the pile damp, in a rope. Mrs. Pryce shook it down the length of the table, away from her. Then she did not walk round to the far end.

She leaned. She put her hip against the table's edge, hard, and the near hem was caught between the two. That was her second pair of hands.

*Hip pins the near hem. Both hands free.*

She reached for the far hem and did not pick it up in the middle. She took it by its two corners, with her arms wide, and brought the corners to her, and the whole far edge came over the top of the sheet in one piece and was laid on the near edge, corner to corner. She never let go of it. There was not a moment, from the far side of the table to her own chest, when the last of that hem was out of her hands.

*Folds from the far edge. Always has the last of it.*

Kiva looked at what she had written.

She knew that fold. She had learned it off a first-year, on a pail, and it was not done with linen. Pru put up a ward the size of a tea tray and brought the far edge of it home to her palms in just that way; and for the same reason, very likely, though neither she nor a laundress had ever said what the reason was.

She wrote, small, in the corner of the ticket: *Same as P.F.'s ward.* Then she went on looking.

The third fold was the odd one. Mrs. Pryce brought the doubled sheet up off the table and snapped it, once, like a carter's whip, so that it cracked. Then she laid it down with the thick side away from her.

*Snaps it at the third. Why? Thick edge to the far side. Why?*

There was a girl at the copper, wringing. Her name was Dilys. She was thirteen, and new since the tenth month, and she had been tried at the table on Monday and had put a double on the floor.

"Dilys," said Kiva. "Would you do one? I'll say it to you. I can't do it; I've a hand that won't. I only want to know whether I've seen it right."

Dilys looked at Mrs. Pryce's back, which was bent over the starch tub, and came.

"Shake it down the table. Now don't go round. Put your hip against the edge, on the hem. Harder. That's holding it for you."

"It's wet."

"I know. Now the far corners. One in each hand, arms out. Bring them to you over the top, and don't let go of either until they're on the near ones."

The far edge came over. It came crooked, and then it came straight.

"Up. Snap it. Once."

It cracked like a flag. Dilys laughed out loud.

"Thick side away from you. Lay it."

It lay on the scrubbed deal, square, a little damp, a double sheet folded by one person of thirteen who had put its fellow on the floor five days before.

"What's that?" said Mrs. Pryce.

She had turned round. She had flour on one forearm and starch on the other, and she looked at the sheet as she might have looked at a strange dog in her scullery.

"I did it," said Dilys. "She told me how you do it."

"I don't *do* it any way. I fold it."

"You put your hip on the hem," said Kiva. "So that you needn't walk round."

"I never do."

"You do, Mam," said Anwen.

She was in the doorway, with a basket. Kiva had not heard her come.

"I want it said. You've done it since before I could see over the table. I thought that every mother had a wet patch on her apron just there." She put the basket down. "I've folded with you a thousand times and I never knew that it was for anything."

Mrs. Pryce folded another. She did it slowly this time, and watched her own hip arrive at the edge of the table, and went red, exactly as her daughter did.

"Well, I'll go to sea," she said. "It's for the weight. A wet double's as heavy as a child of three, and I'd one of those on my arm for the best part of ten years. If I'd gone round the table I'd have had to put her down." She looked at Kiva with a certain respect and no warmth at all. "And you sat there and wrote that on a ticket."

"I didn't write why. I didn't know why. I wrote that you did it."

"And the snap?"

"I've got *why* against that as well. And the thick side."

"The snap's for the air. A damp sheet folds a bubble in, and it sours in the press. The thick side goes away since that's the side the next one's laid against." She said it crossly, and all in one breath, like a woman turning out a drawer. "Nobody ever asked me. I couldn't have told you that I knew."

Kiva wrote it in. It went on the back of the ticket, in seven small columns that she had ruled without thinking, and she noticed when she had done that there were two hands in it. *What* and *how* were hers. She had seen those from a mangle bench. *Why* had had to come from the person at the table, and it had come out sideways, and cross.

She had not thought of it so before. She had supposed that a second saw the whole of a thing. A second saw the half that the doer had stopped being able to see; and the other half was still the doer's, and had to be asked for.

"Do another," said Mrs. Pryce to Dilys. "Since you're so clever."

Dilys shook one down. She was in a hurry now, and she had left her peg bag at the far end of the table, on the sheet.

She pinned the hem with her hip. She took the far corners and brought them over. And the peg bag came with them. It rode home on the fold, quite quietly, and was laid in the middle of the sheet with the far edge on top of it, and vanished.

"There," said Mrs. Pryce. "That's what it's *not* for. You never fold with a thing lying on the far side. It all comes to you. I've found a flat-iron in a bolster case." She fished the bag out. "And you're standing on it."

Dilys looked down. The sheet had slipped off the table's end, and she had a boot on the trailing hem. She hauled at the corners and nothing came.

"A sheet with a foot on its far hem doesn't fold for anybody. Get off."

Kiva wrote both of those down too, under *Went wrong,* since they were there.

She did not think about them again for eleven weeks.

---

The copy went down to the boat on Monday the eighth. It was six sheets in seven columns, squared and taped, and she had tied the tape herself with her right hand and her teeth.

Orla was at the Hythe stair. She had not been asked to come. She stood with her stick planted, in the wet, and did not look at the parcel.

"You said a card," she said.

Kiva gave it to her.

It was a plain one, from the shop. It had her own square hand on it, and she had written it four times before it would go into the space.

*You asked to read it. This is a copy, in my own hand: the method, and what went wrong first.*

*Three places are ruled off and left empty. I could not say them so that another person could do them.*

*One line is left out on purpose. It is not method, and it is not only mine.*

*K. Rowan, catcher.*

Orla read it. She read it as she read the book on Sundays, with her lips shut.

"It doesn't mention me."

"No."

"Good. I looked for it. I'd have thought the less of both of us." She gave it back. "Three empty. What's in them?"

"The hand, mostly."

"Mm." Her sponsor looked down the stair at the boat. "I never got that into words either. I thought that it was because I taught badly. It may be that I did; but I begin to think that there's a part of it that doesn't go."

"It has to go. If it doesn't, then nobody can do it but me."

"And is that so dreadful? There's many a girl would like it."

"I've done it three times," said Kiva. "It cost me two fingers and an arm for four days. If it only lives in my hand, then every time it's wanted somebody has to turn round and not look at me. I'd sooner that it were a thing with handles."

Orla did not answer. She looked, for a moment, like somebody who had been handed a card of her own.

The mate was on the plank with his wax lit.

"Papers," he said. "Same method?"

"A different one. It's mine."

"Who to?"

"J. Hart. Pump-fitter. At the harbour pump house, by favour of the keeper."

He wrote it, with his tongue in his cheek. He pressed the seal and held it down.

"*Her hand,*" he said. "Sign."

She signed with her right, and the boat's bell rang, and six sheets with three holes in them went up the plank under a stranger's arm.
