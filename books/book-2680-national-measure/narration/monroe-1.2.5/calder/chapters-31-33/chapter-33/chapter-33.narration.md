# Chapter 33 — Kiva: The Week's Crossing

The printer's boy came up Quay Street at five on Thursday morning with a canvas bag on his hip and a stack of pages under his arm, and he was knocking at every door that had a light in it.

Kiva heard him from the loft. She had been awake since four, lying on her back by the north window with her right hand on her chest, because the blisters across her knuckles had come up in the night as Aske had said they would and had woken her with their own small heat. She heard the knocking go along the street toward the Company's office, and stop, and come back. Then she heard Quiller's voice below, cross and thick with sleep, and the boy's voice answering, high and patient.

"Every catcher, the Marshal says. Every one in the town. It's paid for."

She got up and went down the outside stair in her coat with her boots unlaced.

The boy was on the step of the chandlery with Quiller in the doorway above him in his nightshirt. He was perhaps twelve, with ink on both thumbs. When he saw Kiva on the stair he looked at the linen on her right hand, and then at her face, and held out a page without being asked.

"You're the Meridian one," he said. "Mr. Tregear said there'd be one with a bound hand. He said give it to her first if she's up."

She took it in her left hand. It was one sheet, printed on both sides, still faintly damp, and the ink smelled of oil. Across the head of it, above the title, there was a line in a heavier type than the rest.

*After D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company, at Mallow Creek, the 16th of the third month, 2681, who went in by the book.*

Under it, smaller, was the title she had seen in the Marshal's own hand on a wharf at home. *On a Thing That Folds from the Far Edge. The Marshal's page.* Then came the page itself, which was short. Make the thing carry its sheet uphill, if it will. If it will not, put weight on the far edge. The far edge belongs to the second, as a place, and not to the corner-hand. Somebody watches the far end of the thing all through the slack, even while the slack holds. The book is written for a sheet.

That was all. It did not say *a stow* anywhere, or *a sleeve.* It did not need to. Anybody who had stood on the causeway yesterday would read *sheet* and see the other thing.

"Mr. Tregear set it himself," said the boy. "All night. He knew Mr. Penhale's father." He shifted the bag on his hip. "I've two hundred to do before six, miss. The vans and the Company yard and the beats."

"Go on, then," said Kiva. "Thank you."

He went off down the street at a trot. Quiller looked at the page in her hand over the top of his spectacles, and then he took the second copy the boy had left on his step, and went back inside without a word and shut the door.

Kiva stood in the grey street and read it again. She did not cry. She had thought on the cart yesterday that she might, sometime, and had found it was not that kind of thing. It was a page that had been held back in case it was wrong, and it was out now with a dead man's name at the top, and every catcher on the coast would have it in a pocket by breakfast. She folded it in four and put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where the disk was.

---

Ivey came up the street at half past five with Kiva's sheets under her arm, tied in their tape.

She had not slept. It was in her face, and in the way she walked, square and steady and a little too carefully, as people walk who have been up all night and mean to go on being up. She stopped at the foot of Quiller's stair and held the bundle out.

"Every one copied," she said. "Twice. One for Yeo and one for me. I've read them to the closing crew's officer already. I'll read them to the crew at six, before the boats." She looked at the Marshal's page showing at the edge of Kiva's coat. "You've had that."

"Five minutes ago."

"So have I. The boy came to the Company yard first." Ivey's square dark hands were empty now, and she put them in her pockets. "Yeo said to tell you two things. She wants your columns for every crew she has, not mine only. And she wants to know who taught you to keep a second's sheet, because she means to have a second on every stow from now to the autumn and she doesn't know where to find one."

"Pike," said Kiva. "Graywater's catcher. He let me watch him."

"Then she'll talk to Pike." Ivey nodded once. "She said, if you'd stand it, she'd like you on the Company yard one evening before you go home, to tell the crews the far edge in your own words. Not the Marshal's. Yours." She half turned to go. "I said you'd say no if it was a Lane night. She said, then not a Lane night."

"Tell her yes," said Kiva. "Not a Lane night."

Ivey went away down Quay Street toward the low town and the Company yard, where the vans were. Kiva stood at the foot of the stair with her own sheets under her arm, which smelled faintly now of the Company's tar and somebody else's lamp, and found she did not mind it.

---

Aske held her left hand at seven, by the north window, for two minutes by his watch.

The loft was full of people dressing. Halden License was up at the south end, where Pettit was reading something aloud off a slip of paper in a voice too low to carry. Old Four was arguing about who had taken whose boots. Selka Dray sat on the next bed to Seven's with both feet on the floor, lacing a boot, and did not look round. Pru was on her own bed with her hands held up in front of her in their ten little linen caps, waiting her turn. Perrin was sitting very upright on the end of his, in his shirt, with the strapping showing under it, being careful not to breathe.

Aske let go.

"Warm," he said. "Four mornings. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and today." He wrote it on the card against the window frame. "Thursday's come. That's what I said. Soonest."

"I know."

"Then I'll say the price, as I said I would." He did not sit down. He looked at her over his spectacles, and his voice did not change at all. "The fifth went to a hand below the elbow and sulked three nights. The sixth went to the elbow, with an ache in the joint, and sulked four. I think the seventh goes past the elbow. An inch, maybe two. I think it sulks five nights, and I'll not be surprised by six." He put the pen in his pocket. "That's the hand. Here's the other thing, and it isn't the hand at all. It's the calendar. One in seven days. If you go today, the next is Thursday week. If you go Saturday, the next is the Saturday after. There's no crossing between, whatever's on fire."

He did not say *Wednesday.* He did not need to. Everybody within three beds of the window knew what Wednesday was. Dray's hands had stopped on her boot lace.

Kiva sat on the edge of her bed with the hand in her lap.

She had been thinking about it since the cart yesterday, coming back along the causeway with the Longshore going under brown water and Bridie Hebden's legs white under a sheet in the tent behind them. She had thought about it in the printer's street at five, and on the stair with Ivey. She had not said it to anybody, because nobody had asked, and because she had wanted to be sure it was hers before she said it.

On Wednesday at nine minutes past six, if two people and a sealed paper were right, the basin would go still for twenty minutes. Somewhere in it, if anybody asked her, she would be calling a thing that three people did, catch and carry and plant, with a count that was not hers. And if one of the three failed, there was exactly one thing in her certificate that could stand in the gap, which was a crossing, on a sanctioned mast, read by the office's reader, one in seven days.

Nobody had asked her for Wednesday. That was the point. If somebody asked on Tuesday, she wanted to be able to say yes without asking anybody to write a new line in a ledger.

"It's not for this week," she said.

Perrin looked up from the end of his bed. He was the captain. He was entitled to ask, and he did.

"Not wanted in the Lane?"

"Not in the Lane. Not today, nor Friday, nor Saturday." She found she could say it quite plainly. "It's the week's crossing. I'd like to keep it for Wednesday. If nobody wants it on Wednesday, I'll have kept it for nothing, and that's all right. But if I spend it on a lane, I can't have it back."

"You'll fight Saturday on one door," said Perrin. "Whoever we draw."

"I'll fight every day on one door. I've done it before."

He looked at her for a long moment, with his big hands on his knees and his face going the colour it went when he was thinking very hard. Then he nodded.

"Then it's the captain's as well," he said. "Not wanted in the Lane. I'll say so if anybody asks, and they will."

Aske had not moved.

"I've said the price once now," he said, "for a crossing nobody's going to make. I'll say it again on Saturday morning, at this window, in case anybody's changed their mind by then." He looked at her steadily. "Twice for nothing is cheaper than once too late. Put that in your book, on the right."

"I will."

Beside her, Dray finished lacing her boot. She pulled the knot tight and stood up and went down the room toward the stair, and she did not say anything, and she did not look at anybody. But Kiva saw her pause at the head of the stair for a moment, with her hand on the post, as a person pauses who has heard a figure that changes a sum.

Aske turned to Perrin.

He did not say anything first. He sat down on the bed beside him, which made Perrin go rigid, and he put two fingers flat on the strapping over the seventh rib on the left side, very lightly, and held them there.

"Breathe in," he said.

Perrin breathed in. It did not get very far.

"Out." Aske moved his fingers an inch. "Again." He listened to something, or seemed to. Then he took his fingers away and looked at the strapping itself, at the place over the rib where the linen had been wound on Tuesday at two. One edge of it had pulled. It had pulled hard enough to fold back on itself, and a crease ran across it like a seam.

"When did you lift?" said Aske.

Perrin went scarlet from the collar up. It was the first time Kiva had ever seen him go the colour that Pru went.

"I didn't," he began, and stopped, because Aske was looking at him over his spectacles with no expression whatever. "At the spur," he said. "Yesterday. The boards. When they carried him up the bank. I took the head of the boards at the lip, for the last two paces. They'd have had to set him down on the stones otherwise." He swallowed. "I knew I shouldn't. I knew it while I was doing it. I'd like it known that I'd do it again."

"I'd have done it myself," said Aske. "I'd have been wrong too." He did not raise his voice. "Here's the price of it, because you didn't ask for it first. A crack across a rib is a crack. A crack through is a break, and a break can go in, and in is your lung. You lifted a dead man's weight on a crack, and the strapping took it, and so did the bone, and I'm glad. It'll not take it twice." He took out his card and wrote, standing, against the window frame, slowly, so that Perrin could read every word over his arm. "*Lifted against the card at the spur, Wednesday the 16th. A fortnight from Wednesday, not from Tuesday. No plant, no lift, nothing heavier than a cup.*" He capped the pen. "That's one day more. I'd have given you a week more if you'd lied to me about it."

"I nearly did."

"I know. You stopped." Aske put the card away. "That's why it's one day."

He looked down the row. Jude was standing by the window with his good arm on the frame, as always, looking at the sea, and his left arm hanging at his side in the sleeve of his shirt.

"Talley," said Aske. "Your arm."

"It's not worse."

"So you've said, on the boat, on the quay, at the Carry, and on the causeway. You've said it to everybody but me." Aske held out his hand. "Roll up the sleeve. Here, in the light, where I can see it. I'll not ask a fourth time, and I'll not stand you in a lane with it on your word."

Jude looked at him. Then he scratched his jaw with his right hand, and came over, and sat down on Kiva's bed, and rolled up his left sleeve to the shoulder.

Kiva had not seen it since the moor. She had seen the sling, and the linen, and Jude holding the arm away from his side as though it belonged to somebody else. She had not seen the arm. From the wrist to a hand's breadth below the elbow the skin was a dull mottled red, shiny in places, with a dry grey edge where the frost had stopped. Along the outside of the forearm there was a darker stripe two fingers wide, where something had pressed. It was not white. It was not black. It looked, Kiva thought, like a burn that somebody had been carrying about in their pocket for a month.

Aske looked at it for a long time without touching it. Then he touched it, very lightly, at the wrist, at the middle, and at the stripe, and watched Jude's face each time.

"That stripe's new," he said.

"The Carry. The sling's loop. Two fingers."

"Two fingers on a frosted forearm with eighty-five kilos of canvas and a carry hanging off it." Aske laid the arm down gently on Jude's knee. "It's mending. You'll keep a mark the length of the stripe, and it'll be stiff in the cold for a year, and you'll never again be able to say it's not worse with a straight face, because I'll know." He took out the card again. "*Talley. Left forearm, frost, mending. The edge on the right fist only. Nothing gripped with the left till Monday.*" He looked up. "You'll fight one-handed. Can you?"

"I've an edge on the right," said Jude. "I've always had. The left was for holding."

"Then hold nothing." Aske stood up. "Finch."

Pru held up her ten caps.

"Ten," said Aske, before he had taken one off. "I know. I did them." He took the first cap off and looked at the fingertip under it, which was blistered and pink and peeling at the edge, and put the cap back on, and did the next. It took him four minutes. Nobody spoke. Dray had come back up the stair for something she had forgotten and stood at the head of it, waiting for him to finish, with her face shut.

"Your door's in your hand," said Aske at last, to Pru. "It's not in your fingertips. You'll catch. You'll not grip a pole with those today, and if you try, you'll tear them, and then you'll not catch on Saturday either." He looked round the four of them, and at Kiva last. "That's your hurt four. A captain who can't plant. A blade with one arm. A catcher who can't grip, and another with a burned hand that writes left-handed." He shut his bag. "I've seen worse go into a lane. Not often."

He went down the stair to his breakfast. Dray stepped aside to let him by, and then went to her bed and took up a glove she had left on the blanket and went down after him.

Perrin sat on the end of his bed and looked at the four of them.

"Well," he said. "I'd hoped to say something about being a team that's been tested. I'd had it ready since five." He breathed carefully. "I think we'll just go and look at the draw."

---

Anwen Pryce climbed Tar Lane at eight on Thursday with her hands in her sleeves, because she had said *tomorrow* to Maud Lyle on a bench, and tomorrow had come.

The Ropewalk's doors were open. Inside, the long boards ran away under the windows as they had on Wednesday night, bare and black, but they were not empty now. The Ropewalk's first four were at the near end in their shirtsleeves with nobody watching, and Juniper Carrow was walking.

She was not walking alone. Treen walked beside her, a pace back and to the left, with her sleeves rolled and her cord at her belt, and she was talking under her breath. Anwen could not hear the words at first. Then she could.

"*Set.*"

Carrow's right foot came down, and her edge came up on her fist, and stayed. Treen said nothing for three paces. Then she said it again. *Set.* And again Carrow's foot came down hard and square and the edge lit, and Anwen understood that she was not lighting it when she chose. She was lighting it on the word.

Anwen stopped in the doorway.

She had heard that word before. She had heard it from the foot of a ladder in the Inner Lock on Tuesday, in Kiva's voice, flat and clear, every time Perrin's heel came onto a rung. *Set.* Half of Saltmere had been standing on the lock's lip with their mouths shut, and had heard it twenty-two times.

"Pryce," said Lyle, from the bench. "You came."

"I said I would."

"I know you did." Lyle got up. She had the towel round her neck. "She's been at it since six," she said, without looking at Carrow. "Treen calls, and she lights. She says it's the worst thing she's ever done. She says she's never once lit a door on anybody's word but her own, and she feels like a carthorse." Lyle's square face did not change. "She's very good at it already. She's good at everything she hates."

Anwen did not say whose word it was. She thought that Carrow knew perfectly well whose word it was, and that it had cost her something to take it, and that it was not Anwen's business to say so on Carrow's own floor.

"Show me your heels," she said instead.

Lyle opened her stance. Her feet went into the boards, and Anwen felt them go, a post driven into the ridge, sure and heavy. There was nothing at her hip. There was nothing on her hands. She stood planted in the middle of the Ropewalk with her arms at her sides, and she looked, Anwen thought, like a house with the front taken off.

"Lean on me," said Anwen. "Not a wall. You. From the floor."

She put her own shoulder against Lyle's, sideways, as two people stand at a crowded rail. She did not open anything. Lyle leaned. She leaned from the shoulders, as people did who had always had a wall to lean with, and nothing moved, and her stance held her where she was like a nail holds a coat.

"That's from your back," said Anwen. "You're pushing with your back and holding with your feet, and the two don't know each other. That's what a wall did for you. The wall knew both." She stepped away. "Again. Bend your knees. Put your weight down into the floor first, and then let the floor push. Not you. The floor."

Lyle bent her knees. She put her weight down. Anwen saw it go, a long way down, through the boards, into the ridge, and then she saw it come back up, slowly, through Lyle's heels and calves and thighs and the small of her back, until it arrived in her shoulder.

It moved Anwen half a pace.

Anwen stood where she had been put and looked at the boards between her boots.

"That's it," she said. "That's leaning from the floor."

Lyle did not smile. Her face was white, and there was sweat at her hairline, and she was breathing as though she had run the length of the shed. But she looked at the half pace on the boards, and then at Anwen, and something went across her square careful face and stayed there for a moment longer than it usually did.

"Again," she said.

They did it eleven times. On the seventh, Lyle moved her a whole pace. On the ninth, Anwen stopped thinking about it and leaned back, from her own floor, with her own heels right. The two of them stood shoulder to shoulder in the middle of the Ropewalk, neither of them moving, both of them working as hard as they could, and Anwen thought it was the strangest thing she had ever done on a lane floor. There were no doors in it at all, except one still door, and that one was not hers.

At the far end of the near boards, Carrow had stopped walking. She stood with her edge shut and her hands at her sides, watching them, and Treen beside her.

"Pryce," said Carrow. "That's yours, that lean?"

"It's a man called Mace's. On a van's line, at home."

"It's yours now. She's got it off you." Carrow looked at Lyle, and then back at Anwen, with her quick blunt face giving nothing away. "If the lot falls that way tomorrow, she'll lean on your four with it. You know that."

"I know it."

"And you came up the lane anyway."

"I said I would," said Anwen. "She asked me on a bench with her ward gone. I'd not say no to that because of a lot that hasn't been drawn." She took her hands out of her sleeves. "If she leans on us tomorrow, I'll be glad she leans well. That's all."

Carrow considered that.

"In this town," she said, "we'd call that daft."

"In mine we'd call it the same," said Anwen. "I've been called daft before."

Treen laughed. It was a short, startled sound, and she put her hand over her mouth at once, and went red under her dark skin. Carrow did not laugh. But she turned her head and looked at her catcher, and something in her shoulders eased, as though Treen had called a word she had been waiting for.

"*Set,*" said Treen, through her fingers.

Carrow's foot came down. Her edge came up. She walked on down the Ropewalk, ten paces, and turned, and walked back, lighting on the word.

Anwen went down Tar Lane at nine with her shoulder aching and her heels sore. She did not know whether she had done a stupid thing. She thought probably she had. She found that she did not want to have done it any other way, and that she would tell Kiva so, and that she would not tell Perrin, who would only look pleased with her and say something kind.

---

The draw had gone up on the tent pole at the gate at nine the night before, under the lamp, while the Mercury's boy was selling his late sheet on the Long Quay. Kiva had read it then over the heads of a crowd in green. She read it again now, at a quarter to ten, in daylight, with Perrin at her shoulder and the timber pond lying black and quiet under the boards.

*The Lane. Three lanes for every four, one a day. Thursday's drawn by the standing after three boards: first against twenty-fourth, second against twenty-third, and so down. Friday's and Saturday's drawn by lot at this pole at six the evening before, winners against winners and losers against losers, no four to meet a four twice. A four is placed by lanes won, and then by points for less points against, and marked by place, twenty-four to the first and one to the last.*

And under it, Thursday's twelve lanes, with their hours. Halden License against the twenty-fourth at nine. Old Four at ten. The Ropewalk's first four against the twenty-first at half past ten, in the Graving, before the whole town. And halfway down:

*Two o'clock. Team Seven, Meridian (9), against the Tarn, second four (16).*

"The Tarn," said Perrin.

"Their second four."

"I know which four. I've read it nine times." He had his hand pressed to his side under his coat, where the strapping was. "Their first four's in the healers' tent with their feet in a basin. Their second four's been watching from the tiers all week, and every one of them was on the causeway yesterday afternoon when we came up the bank with Hebden on a stretcher." He was quiet a moment. "They'll not be pleased to see us. They'll not hate us either. I'd sooner be hated. You know where you are."

Beside the draw there was a second board, newer, that Kiva had not seen the night before. It was a plain deal board on two trestles, with a strip of green baize along its top and a row of brass drawing pins. Somebody had chalked across the head of it, in a big careful hand: *DRAFT. Cards may be put up by any chartered company and taken down by the student named. A card is not a seat.*

There were already eleven cards on it.

Kiva did not go close. She saw Jude go close. He went past her without seeming to see her, with the book under his good arm, and stood in front of the board for a long time, reading one card near the middle, in the Company's green. Then he put out his right hand and touched the card's corner, as a man touches the edge of a table in the dark. He did not take it down.

He came back with his face very still and the book under his arm.

"Talley," said Perrin.

"I'll read it again tonight," said Jude. "It's not going anywhere."

He walked on toward the Graving. Perrin looked after him, and then at the board, and then, very carefully, not at the two cards in the bottom corner that had *Cade, P.* written on them in two different hands.

---

The Graving was half full at two, which was full enough.

It was not the Ropewalk's lane, and the town knew it. The Ropewalk had beaten the twenty-first at half past ten, seven to nothing, in front of every tier in the dock. The green had gone home to its dinner and come back slowly, a few at a time, and it sat now in the lower tiers in the grey light from the glass roof, eating pasties and talking about the morning. The Tarn's brown and buff sat in a knot near the middle. Meridian's colours were above them. Orla was there on the end of a tier, with Aske beside her. Rhea Sorn sat two tiers higher with Pike, whose hands lay on his knees closed halfway.

Down on the floor, eighty feet below the lip, the pen was chalked on the stone, twenty paces wide, with its hurdles and its posts and its seven chalk squares. On its north side, between the pen and the dock wall, the Board had stood a mast. It was not as tall as the gate mast. It came up to a drum at about twice a man's height, with eight pens on eight wires, and two reading desks at its foot. Noa stood at one with his pencil behind his ear. Polglase stood at the other in his steel spectacles, threading tape.

Perrin declared at the marshal's box. He did not go into the pen. He stood behind Seven's hurdle in his coat, with his hands at his sides, and his voice came up the dock's stone walls clear and flat.

"*Team Seven. Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Finch, ward, catcher. Rowan, stance, declared, by her captain's voice. Cade, captain, standing out on his healer's card.*"

There was a murmur round the tiers. *Standing out.* Kiva heard it go round and come back, and she let it go past her. She was looking at the Tarn's second four, coming out of the dock's side door in brown and buff.

They were big. Everybody from the Tarn was big, as if the hills grew them on purpose. Their captain was a thin dark girl with a still door, who walked as though the stone under her were a field she knew. The others came behind her: a heavy blade, a Hold with long arms, a catcher with a fresh bandage on one wrist. They stood behind their hurdle and looked down the pen at Seven, and Kiva saw them look at the linen on her right hand, and Pru's ten caps, and Jude's left arm hanging, and Perrin standing outside the pen with his hand pressed to his side.

Their captain looked longest at Perrin. Then she looked at Kiva, and inclined her head an inch. It was the same nod Trewin gave, done properly.

"She was on the causeway," said Anwen, very low. "Yesterday. She carried one end of Hebden's stretcher to the tent."

"I know," said Kiva.

"*Declared,*" said the marshal. "*Ready.*" He turned his glass.

The first carry showed them what they could not do.

They had made the plan at the long table at eleven, on Perrin's slate, with Perrin drawing it and nobody else allowed to touch the chalk. It was a plain plan. Kiva would plant at the head of the litter with her stance and nobody could shift it while she stood there. Anwen's wall would go up on the south side. Jude would take the north with his edge on his right fist. Pru would stand back of the litter with her ward low and her eyes on hands, and call. When the moment came, Anwen and Jude would lift and walk the Commodore home, and Kiva would shut and run ahead to the hurdle, and she and Pru would take it over from the far side, two to two, top to top.

It went well until the hurdle.

The Tarn came in a square, as the first four had come against Pru in the seeding lane. Their Hold's wall met Anwen's with the sound of a shut drawer, and the two walls leaned, and Anwen's heels were right and the Tarn's were not, and Anwen walked her wall from the far end round in a quarter circle while the other girl was still leaning. Pru called *hands, south* off the Tarn blade's fingers, and Jude was there, and the Tarn blade stopped short of his edge. Kiva stood planted at the litter's head with her stance in the stone and her hands on nothing. Nobody tried to shift her. Nobody wasted the time.

"*Now,*" said Pru.

Anwen and Jude lifted. Anwen had both hands on the head poles, and Jude had his right hand on the foot, with his left arm held against his chest, and the Commodore came up crooked, heavy at the foot, and they walked him. Kiva shut her stance and ran. She vaulted Seven's hurdle and turned on the far side, as Carrow had turned in the Ropewalk, and put up her hands for the head.

Her right hand would not close.

She had known it would not. She had known it at the table at eleven with the chalk in Perrin's fist, and said it, and Perrin had said *wrist, then,* and she had said *wrist.* But a wrist under a pole is not a hand round it, and the head of the Commodore came over the hazel at her and she took it on her left hand and her right wrist and it went down on the right. Pru had the foot. Pru had put her capped fingers round the foot poles, because that was where the foot was. Kiva heard her draw her breath in through her teeth.

The Commodore's foot went down on the hazel.

"*Touched,*" said the marshal. "Dropped. Anybody's."

It was the Tarn's. Their still door was there before Kiva had got her wrist out from under, and their Hold behind her, and they walked the Commodore away from Seven's hurdle as though it had been left out for them. Jude got a half on the Hold's arm as she turned. It was not enough. The Tarn blade touched Anwen on the shoulder as she came round her own wall, quite gently, and Anwen knelt. The glass ran out with the Commodore lying across the middle line on the Tarn's side of it.

"*A down to the Tarn. A point to the Tarn by the half,*" said the marshal. "Two to the Tarn. Nothing to Seven."

The tiers made the warm sound, not loud. Kiva stood on the far side of her own hurdle with her right hand held against her chest. The blisters under the linen had opened. She could feel them wet.

Pru was standing beside her with her hands held up in front of her face. Two of the caps had come off on the poles. The fingertips under them were bleeding a little.

"Don't," said Kiva. "Don't say you're sorry."

"I wasn't going to." Pru's face was white, not scarlet. "I was going to say that I can't grip, and you can't grip, and that's half our hands, and we've two more carries." She looked at the bleeding fingertips with a sort of fury. "Aske said. He said *you'll tear them.* I tore them."

Perrin had come to the hurdle from the outside. He stood with his hands on the hazel, not leaning on it, because leaning would have been a lift of a kind, and looked at them.

"We can't carry it over," he said. "Not two to two. Not with these hands. Not today."

"Then we don't carry it," said Kiva.

They looked at her.

"There's a point at the glass for the half it's lying in." She heard herself say it, and the thing came clear as she said it, as a sum comes clear when you say the numbers aloud. "We can't carry. But I can sit on it." She looked at Perrin. "You said it on the Low Lane, the first year, with a litter pole for a pointer. *They can't carry what they can't lift.* You wanted me on the litter like a hen."

Perrin's face did something complicated.

"I did," he said. "I'd forgotten I said it."

"I'll plant on the poles at the head. Nobody shifts it. Pryce keeps their carry off the foot. Talley takes whoever comes near enough to try. Finch calls." Kiva looked down the pen. "And it's a point. Every carry. And their downs."

"That's not three," said Anwen.

"No. It's one. And whatever Talley cuts." Kiva held her wet right hand against her coat. "And in the third, if it's close, we try the hurdle again. With our wrists and our elbows, and no fingers at all."

Pru was looking at her own hands, at the crooks of her elbows. She lifted her forearms together in front of her, as a girl lifts them to carry a basket of washing on her arms, and looked at the gap between them.

"Elbows," she said. "I can take a pole in my elbows. I've carried coal like that."

"Then that's the third," said Perrin. He took his hands off the hazel. "Go on. Sit on him."

Kiva sat on the Commodore at the start of the second carry and did not move for three minutes and forty seconds.

It was not elegant. The Tarn had expected Seven to go for the carry and had come in their square to stop it, and found Seven's catcher already at the litter's head before their still door had crossed the middle line, kneeling astride the head poles with her stance open and both forearms flat along the canvas. Her feet were in the stone. Kiva felt them go, down through the Graving's floor, which was granite and old and very deep, and she felt it hold her as the floor of the Ropewalk had held Lyle. Nobody could shift her. The Tarn's still door came and put both hands on the poles beside her and pulled, and nothing moved, and the girl looked at Kiva from a foot away with an expression of honest surprise.

"That's not a carry," she said.

"No," said Kiva.

"It's not even trying."

"It's trying very hard," said Kiva, "at sitting."

The girl laughed. She could not help it, and Kiva saw that she could not, and liked her for it. Then the Tarn blade came up on Kiva's right, with his edge lit, to touch her; and Pru called *hands, right* from behind the litter; and Jude was there before the blade had lifted his arm, with his own edge on his right fist, and touched the blade on the chest.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "The Tarn."

The blade knelt, a pace from Kiva, with an expression of the deepest disgust.

After that the Tarn tried three things, and Kiva learned something from each. First they tried to drag the foot, with their Hold and their catcher on the foot poles together, and Anwen's wall came up on the south side between them and the rest of the pen. Anwen did not lean it on anybody. She held it there, low and silver, a door with somebody standing behind it, and the Tarn could drag the foot round in an arc but not away. A planted head and a dragged foot made a litter turn on its own length like a gate on its hinge. Kiva felt it turn under her, and held, and the Commodore went round in a slow half circle and lay, after all that pulling, exactly where it had started, pointing the other way.

Then they tried to wait her out. The glass was running and the litter was in the middle and the Tarn's captain stood back and folded her arms, and Kiva understood that the point at the glass would go to whichever half the litter's middle lay in, and the litter's middle was on the line. Pru saw it too. Pru said, very quietly, "*Head's ours. Foot's theirs. Middle's on the chalk.*" Kiva did not move her stance. She could not, without shutting. But she leaned. She leaned from the floor, as Anwen leaned, with her knees bent and her weight down, and let the floor push, and the head of the Commodore went a hand's breadth west under her forearms with a scrape of canvas on stone.

The third thing the Tarn tried was their catcher. He was a small fair boy with a fresh bandage on his wrist, and he came in low on the north side with his ward up at his hip, small, the width of a doormat, and set it under the litter's middle. It was clever. He meant to lift the litter's middle on his tray and walk it back east over the line while Kiva sat on the head. A ward lifts. That is what a catcher's tray is for.

Kiva felt it come up under the canvas. She could not do anything about it with her one door, which was in the floor.

"*Hands,*" said Pru. "*His. North.*"

Jude touched the boy on the arm. It was a half. The boy kept his tray where it was and lifted, and the middle of the Commodore came up off the stone a finger, two fingers, and Jude touched him again on the other arm.

"*Two halves,*" said the marshal. "*Down.* The Tarn."

The boy let go and knelt. The Commodore's middle came down on the stone with a thump, a hand's breadth west of the line. And the glass ran out.

"*The litter's in Seven's half. A point. Two downs.*" The marshal wrote. "Three to Seven. Two to the Tarn."

Kiva shut her stance and got up off the Commodore. Her knees had gone stiff and her right hand was bleeding through the linen, and when she stood she found the Tarn's captain standing in front of her with her arms still folded and her head on one side.

"I've never seen that done," said the captain. "Not in a lane. Not once."

"It's an old one," said Kiva. "From home. My captain thought of it in our first year, to beat four still doors with."

"It's horrible," said the captain. She said it quite cheerfully. "It's the worst lane I've ever stood. I'll do it to somebody next year." She unfolded her arms. "Hebden's my cousin. Her legs are white, not grey. The healer says she'll stand on Friday."

"I'm glad."

"So am I." The captain looked at Kiva's bleeding hand, and at Pru's. "I'd rather not beat you. I'd rather not lose to you either. It's an awkward thing to feel in the middle of a lane." She turned away. "Third carry. Mind your elbows."

They carried the Commodore over the hurdle in the third carry with no fingers at all.

The Tarn had learned. They did not let Kiva near the head this time. Their still door planted on it before the glass had turned, and sat there in her turn, astride the poles, with an air of having been taught something and meaning to use it at once. So Seven went for the foot.

It was Anwen and Jude again on the near side, but Anwen took the foot alone. She took both foot poles in her two good hands and lifted, and the Tarn's still door at the head could not be shifted. So she did not try to shift her. She walked the foot round. She walked it in a great half circle, as she had been taught to walk a wall from its far end, with the planted girl at the head turning on the spot like a hinge, until the Commodore lay with his foot toward Seven's hurdle and his head still under the Tarn girl's forearms.

"*She's got to let go to come,*" said Pru, quite loud. "*Watch her hands.*"

The Tarn girl had to shut to move. Kiva saw her see it. If she stayed planted, Seven would drag the foot home round her; if she came, she would have to shut and stand and lift, and while she lifted she would have nothing open. She chose to come. She shut and came up off the poles with the head in her hands, meaning to run it back east. And Jude was there with his edge on his right fist, and touched her on the back as she turned.

"*Down.* The Tarn."

She knelt. She let go. The head of the Commodore was nobody's.

Anwen had the foot. Jude took the head poles in his right hand only, and the two of them walked the Commodore down the pen to Seven's hurdle, quite fast, crooked, with the Tarn's blade coming and Pru calling *hands* behind them and Anwen's wall coming up and going down again as she needed it. At the hurdle they lifted. Kiva was on the far side. Pru was beside her.

"*Elbows,*" said Pru.

The head of the Commodore came over the hazel. Kiva did not put up her hands. She put up her forearms, crossed at the wrists, as one holds out one's arms for somebody to lay a coat on them. The poles came down into the crooks of her arms and lay there across the linen and the left sleeve, and she held them against her chest as a girl holds a basket of washing. Beside her Pru had done the same with the foot, her capped fingers spread wide and up and out of the way, her forearms crooked.

They walked backward three paces with the Commodore on their arms, clear of the hazel. Nothing touched it.

"*Over,*" said the marshal. "*Three to Seven.*" He leaned over his box and looked at them, at the two catchers standing behind Seven's hurdle with a canvas dummy lying across their arms, and his face did something that a marshal's face is not meant to do. "Seven to Seven. Two to the Tarn."

The tiers made a sound that was not the warm sound. It was somebody laughing, high up, and then a great many people laughing, and then the laughing turned into something else. It was clapping, which Kiva had not heard in the Graving all week except for the Ropewalk. It was not loud. It went on a long time.

She and Pru put the Commodore down on the stone together, carefully, as one puts down a sleeping child.

"Elbows," said Pru. She was scarlet now, all the way up. "I said. I've carried coal like that."

"I know. You said."

"I'm saying it again." Pru looked at her own arms, and then at Kiva. "I didn't call that. You didn't call that either. We just both did it." She held up her bleeding fingertips. "That's the first thing I've ever done in a lane that wasn't on somebody's word."

Kiva looked at her. She thought of the yard behind the Long Quay on Sunday, and Pike saying from a tailboard, *you call from the air before you've landed,* and Pru on a seeding floor calling *hands off, left* before the Tarn's carry had moved.

"It wasn't on anybody's," she said. "That's why it worked."

---

The board went up at six with Thursday's twelve lanes on it.

Kiva read it at the back of the crowd with Pru beside her and Perrin behind, under the lamp, while the tide went out of the timber pond's black water under the boards. Every one of the twelve lanes had gone to the four that stood higher. The Tarn's first four had stood with Trewin on frosted feet and a reserve at Bridie's place, and had won by a single point and the glass. Halden License had beaten the twenty-fourth six to two. The Ropewalk's seven to nothing stood at the head of the column, alone.

*Team Seven (9) beat the Tarn, second four (16). Seven to two.*

"Seven," said Perrin. "With no fingers."

"With elbows," said Pru.

Under the twelve lanes, a clerk stood on a crate and pinned up Friday's draw, drawn by lot at the pole a minute before in front of everybody, out of a canvas bag with the Board's seal on it. He pinned it sheet by sheet, and the crowd round the pole went quiet in pieces as it read. Kiva read it over a green shoulder.

*Friday. Winners against winners.*

*Eleven o'clock. The Ropewalk School, first four, against Team Seven, Meridian.*

Somebody at the front said *oh,* quite softly, as a person says it at a card turned over on a table. Then the green began to talk all at once, and turned to look for Seven in the crowd, and found them.

Perrin did not say anything for a while.

"Carrow," he said at last.

"On one door," said Kiva.

"On one door. With Lyle on one door, and Treen calling, and the whole town on the tiers." He took his hand away from his side and looked at it, as though he expected to find something on it. "We beat her second four. She beat us seven to two on her own floor with both doors open. I'd like to know what she does with one."

"So would she," said Kiva. "That's why she shut it."

---

At a quarter to seven she went to the end of the boards where the timber pond met the Graving's old wall, and laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron of a mooring ring set into the coping.

She did it by the clock on the Board's tent, which she could see from there. She did it lightly, without leaning. Her right hand was in fresh linen that Aske had wound at five with his mouth shut tight, and it throbbed, and she held it against her coat and did not let it into the reading.

*It keeps time.*

She wrote it with her left hand, badly. *Thursday the 17th. A quarter to seven. A mooring ring in the coping by the Graving. Yes.*

Then, under it, on the right, where the reasons went, she wrote a line she had promised Aske.

*Kept the week's crossing for Wednesday. Said so at seven. Nobody has asked for it. If nobody does, I've kept it for nothing, and that's all right.*

She looked at the line for a while. It was a plain thing to have decided. She had decided harder things on worse mornings. But she had never before decided not to use a thing she was let use, on purpose, so that it would be there for somebody else, and she found it sat in her differently from a refusal. It did not feel like a door shut. It felt like a coin put by.

Behind her, along the boards, Noa was still at the gate mast with Polglase, in the last of the light, with the day's tape unrolled on his desk. A boy from the Board's wire office was standing beside him with a fistful of yellow forms. Noa was reading them one after another, with his pencil not moving, and his narrow face had a look on it that she had not seen there before.

He looked up and saw her, and lifted the pencil, as a man lifts a hat.

"Rowan," he called. "Have you a minute? I've had the first returns."
