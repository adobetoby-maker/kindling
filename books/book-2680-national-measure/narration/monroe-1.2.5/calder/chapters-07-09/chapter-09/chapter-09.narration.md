# Chapter 9 — Kiva: The Near Gap

Perrin had rubbed out the *44* on the shed door before anybody else was in the yard, and he was standing back from it with the chalk in his fist when Kiva came through the side gate.

*43.*

"I'd like it known that I did that properly," he said. "I wiped it with my sleeve. A captain doesn't leave the old number showing through."

"It was showing through."

"It was a ghost of a four. Nobody could have read it but me." He put the chalk in his pocket. "It's Sunday. It's the first of the second month. I've been awake since five, and I find that a number on a door is a great deal heavier at five than it was on Saturday afternoon."

The Old Yard was white with frost from the sheds to the bottom wall, and the black posts stood up out of it in their two rows like the piles of a pier at low water. Somebody had already been down the lane with a broom. There was a scraped place in the middle, two strides across, with a chalk mark at either end of it, and on the far side of the scraped place stood the Circuit's travelling mast on its three legs, with its brass drum and its tape and a tarpaulin over the top against the damp.

Noa Bexley was taking the tarpaulin off. He did it as he did everything, in the right order and without being hurried, and he had a red flag and a white one stuck in his belt.

Orla was on her crate, a pace north of where it had stood for one-and-twenty years, with her stick between her boots and a rug over her knees that she would have denied owning.

"You're late," she said.

"It's seven."

"I said seven. I meant that you'd be here before it, as you always are, so that I needn't wait. I've been waiting." Her crooked face did not change. "Sit. Not on the step. I want to say a thing before anybody lights, and I want it said to you sitting, so that you'll hear it and not be thinking about your feet."

Kiva sat on the end of the crate, which was the only place there was.

"Six," said Orla. "Of mine. On the Chair's mast, read by Bexley, with me on this crate, from this month. That's the certificate's word and it's mine. Hand to Ember. What you did in the eighth month is in your book, and in your book it stays. I'll not count a thing you told the mast didn't count."

"I know."

"I know that you know. I'm saying it aloud so that it's been said." She turned the stick between her palms. "Now the rest. Your healer's card says one crossing in seven days, at the most. This month has thirty days in it. Count them."

Kiva had counted them on Friday night, at the kitchen table, on the back of a tram ticket, and again at three in the morning without the ticket.

"Five," she said. "If I go today, and on every seventh day after it. Today, the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second, and the twenty-ninth. And on the twenty-ninth I'll be on the road."

"So it's four, or it's five on a road with no mast on it."

"Four. In this city."

"Then the sixth is at Saltmere, on their gate mast, or it isn't at all. And it may be that the fifth is too." Orla said it flatly, as one reads off a gauge that has stuck. "I'll write that today, before anybody has time to hurry. I've seen a girl hurry a crossing because a clerk wanted a sixth before a boat. She's a fitter now, and she's never lit a door since."

"I'll not hurry."

"No. You'll be hurried, which is different, and you'll have to say no to it. I'm telling you now so that you'll know whose no it is." She looked down the lane at the mast. "It's yours. I'm only the woman who wrote the number."

Kiva sat with that for a moment.

She had supposed, all the way up the hill in the dark, that it would be the hand that set the pace of the month. She saw now that it would be the hand and a calendar together, and that between them they had already said *four,* and that anybody who wanted six would have to argue with a healer's card and thirty days and not with her. It was a queer kind of comfort. It was the comfort of a sum that has been done honestly and comes out short.

"Then I'd like them on Sundays," she said. "So that Aske has the hand on a Monday morning, at seven, and so that it's a full week before the next, and nobody can say that I took six days and called it seven."

"Sundays." Orla nodded once. "Bexley. Did you hear?"

"Sundays," said Noa, from the mast. "I've written it. The Chair's office is shut on a Sunday, but the mast isn't. I asked."

---

The others came up the lane while Noa was threading his tape.

Jude had the team book under his arm. He had carried it since the Friday of the warrant, and he carried it as though it might go off: flat against his ribs, with his forearm across it, the way a carter carries a parcel marked *glass.* Anwen came with him, and Pru behind, and Pru had a hank of cord round her wrist and was making turns on it without looking, as she did now whenever her hands were not wanted.

"It's on the book," said Jude. "Before anybody asks. Three-tenths, or thereabouts. I've left the line open."

"It'll be three-tenths and a hair," said Noa. "It always is, from the ward. The ward's dearer to shut than the Ember. I've three of hers from the summer in the office, and the hand's always the dear end."

"Then I'll leave room for the hair."

Kiva walked out to the scraped place.

The first chalk mark was hers to stand on. The second was six paces on, down the lane, a white cross on the black dirt. She opened the still door under the second mark, by its corners, the way that she had opened it a thousand times since the summer, and she felt the ground come up through it, packed and dry and solid to a depth that she could not find the bottom of. She shut it.

"Clean."

"Then say it," said Orla.

She turned to the mast, where Noa had the white flag up. Pru had stopped making turns.

"*Hand, to Ember. Rowan, changing on three.*"

"*One,*" said Orla.

The hand door came up at her right hip.

It was low and silver and about the size of a tea tray, as it always was now, and it lay across the eight fingers that reported like a thing that had been set down there and was not heavy. The first two fingers of her left hand stood clear of it, stiff, like the prongs of a fork. She had stopped trying to make them do otherwise; they were passengers on a ward, and went where it went and held nothing.

Orla leaned forward off her crate and tapped the ward with the end of her stick, once.

It rang. It was a small, clear, cold sound, like a spoon on a glass, and Kiva felt it come up through the silver and into her palms. That was the tap that a ward was for, the one tap, the shove of a thing that has to be caught. A catcher's ward that had been tapped had done its work, and the next thing that it did was fold.

"*Two.*"

She folded it from the far edge. The far edge came home over the near, and the near over itself, and the whole bright tray went down in her hands, as a sheet goes down on a laundress's table, until there was a penny of silver between her right palm and her left and nothing else. Then she shut the door on the penny.

It was the near door, and always had been. The hand door lay closest of the three, just under the skin of her palms, and it went shut there with a small cold click that she felt in the bones of both wrists at once.

"*Three.*"

Nobody.

She had known it would be nobody. She had been nobody in the air over an oak floor a month ago, between an Ember and a stance, and it had been as she remembered it: a quarter of a dram going out of her like a breath, and nothing open, and nothing to stand on, and the left hand cold. This was not that. This was nearer.

The other gap had been in the air between two places. This one was in her hands.

The cold did not wait to be paid for. It was in the first two fingers at once, which could not feel it, and then in the rest of the hand, which could. It went up over the knuckles and the back of the hand toward the wrist while she was still nobody, as though the door that had just shut had been the only thing between that hand and a great deal of winter.

*Arrive before you're there.*

The Ember door opened under her breastbone.

It came hot, and it came gladly, the way the Ember always came, as though it had been waiting at the top of a stair to be asked down. The struts ran down the backs of her calves and along the soles of her feet in their dull orange, and the three heat-lines across her chest drew tight from the breastbone to the left shoulder, and she went. It was one low bound, six paces, with the frost going by under her in a grey smear and the black posts going by on either hand.

Her right foot came down on the white cross.

She shut the Ember at the landing and stood on her own two feet in the Old Yard with every door closed, breathing smoke.

"Landed," said Kiva.

Noa did not answer at once. He had his face close to the drum, and the tape was still running through his fingers, and when he looked up he looked first at the tape and then at her and then at the tape again, as he always did, to be certain that the two of them agreed.

"One cliff," he said. "Twenty-six. Two lines, a ward and an Ember, and a gap between them that I could put my thumb in. Nothing open across it. Thirty-one in all." He pulled the red flag out of his belt and did not raise it. "That's a crossing. Hand to Ember. Not a stack."

"Write it," said Orla. "*One.*"

Kiva held up her left hand.

It was white. It was white to the wrist and a little past, and the skin over the knuckles had the look of a candle end. When she shut the hand, it shut slowly, all of it, the three fingers as well as the two, as though the order had to go a long way to reach them. There was no pain in it. There was simply a great deal of cold, in a place that she was used to listening to, and the listening had stopped.

Pru had come up beside her. She took the wrist without asking and held it, two fingers on the pulse, and looked at the shed clock through the open door.

"Ninety-eight," said Pru. "That's you, not it. You're breathing like Cade on a stair."

"I'll stop."

"You'll stop when you stop. I'm only telling you what it is." She did not let go. "Is it worse than the other way?"

Kiva thought about that honestly, because she had been asked.

"It isn't worse," she said. "It's nearer. Ember to still, the gap's out there, between two places, and you pay it and you're across. This one's in the hand. You shut the ward and the hand's the thing with nothing in front of it." She turned it over and looked at the white palm. "Orla. Is that what you meant? *The wrong half.*"

"I meant that a catcher's crossing begins at her ward." Orla had not moved off the crate. "Everything that you catch, you catch there. If you've a thing on your ward and you need to be somewhere else with it, that's the gap that matters, and it's the near one. Any fool can cross from a bound to a stance; it's a dancing master's trick. You'd learned the far gap and not the near one. Now you've begun on it."

"It costs the hand."

"It costs the hand. I'd not have asked if I'd thought it would cost less." Her sponsor's voice did not soften by a hair, and Kiva was glad of that. "Five more. Three in this city. And the hand seen every Monday by a man who won't round down."

Jude was writing in the book on his knee, on the shed step, with his tongue between his teeth.

"*Sunday the 1st,*" he read out. "*The Old Yard, on the Circuit's travelling mast, read by N. Bexley; certified sponsor present. Hand to Ember, Rowan. One crossing. 0.31.*" He looked up. "*Held, nine and thirty-three.* That's off the line we spoke for. There's one and forty-nine left on it, if anybody's keeping that column."

"Somebody's keeping it," said Kiva.

"I know. I'm the somebody. I'm telling the others so that they know that I'm keeping it." He scratched his jaw. "Kellan said I count everything and trust none of it. I've found it's true. I added that line three times."

Perrin had gone to the shed door and was looking at the *43.*

"One," he said, to the door. "I'd like it known that I'm not chalking those as well. I've one number to wipe off every morning and I find it's plenty."

Kiva put her left hand inside her coat, against her ribs, where it could borrow some warmth, and found the disk there in the inside pocket where she kept it. It was cold, as she had known it would be. She looked anyway, as she always looked, because there was a column in a book on a creaking stair that wanted the word written within the week, and she would not write a word that she had not checked.

---

Healer Aske had the hand at seven on Monday morning, under the lamp at the Wren House, with the frost still on the window.

He did it with the pin and the penny and the cup and the cord, in the same order as always, and then he did a thing that he had not done before. He took her left wrist between his finger and thumb and held it, quite still, with his eyes on his own watch, for a whole minute by the second hand.

"Cold to the knuckle," he said. "Not past. The palm's warm. The two that don't report are as they were. The other three are slow to shut, and they'll be slow until about noon, I'd think, and then they won't be." He let go. "It's sulking."

"It did that in the summer."

"It did that in the summer after the other kind. This is a different kind, and I'll want to see whether it sulks the same each time or longer." He wrote on her card, against the wall, standing. "*Monday the 2nd. After one crossing, hand to Ember. Cold to the knuckle at seven; expected warm by noon.* I'll see it again at noon, if you're anywhere near."

"I'll be at the Pit."

"Then I'll walk down. I've a man there with a shin that a husk breathed on last week, and I'd as soon look at the two of you in one trip." He capped his pen. "The card stands. One in seven, at the most. Your next is Sunday."

"Yes."

"I'll be honest with you, since that's what I'm paid for. If it sulks longer each time, I'll say so each time, and I'll say it before you go and not after." He looked at her over his spectacles. "I don't yet know what longer means. I'd like you to know that I don't."

She found that she liked him the better for saying it, and that she had never yet heard him say anything else.

---

The Pit was sunk below the Measure Hall's east yard like a dry dock with its water let out, and on that Monday morning it had sixteen students standing round its rail, and a cart in the middle of its floor.

Kiva knew the Pit, as everybody on that rail knew it. It was a floor of packed cinder forty paces by twenty, with a stone race running the length of its south side and a lattice of black iron across the north end, where the Wren House kept what it kept. In the spring she had stood on that cinder for a Flask qualifier with her heart in her mouth, and in the autumn there had been a square of skinned husks over the race with a white cord between them. There was a sixty-year-old mast on the gallery and a rail of worn oak round the top, and the steps down were steep and narrow and slick in frost.

The cart was new.

It was a low flat dray with iron wheels, and on the dray stood a cage. It was not like the Wren House's lattice, which was a wall. It was a box of the same black iron, about the size of a hen house, with a ward laid over it on the outside. The ward was grey, not silver. It was old and thick and very steady, and it did not flicker, and Kiva could feel it from the rail as one feels a stove from the doorway of a room.

There were three things in the cage.

She could not see them well. She could see a stir of grey behind the bars, like smoke in a jar, and then a shape that might have been a shoulder, and then nothing. She could hear them. It was a low dry sound, not loud, and it came in fours.

"Fours," said a voice behind her. "By your captains. Declare."

It was not loud either. It did not need to be.

There was a woman on the steps.

She was small, and she was broad, and she had shoulders that a carter would have been glad of. She wore a Board's grey greatcoat buttoned to the chin, and her hair was iron grey and cut as short as a boy's, and her face had been out in weather for so long that it had gone the color and grain of a boat's rail. Kiva would have put her at sixty. She had a length of tarred rope in her right hand, as long as an arm, with knots in it, and as she came down the last three steps she tied another knot in it without looking.

She did not say who she was. She did not need to say that either. There were sixteen people on that rail who had read the Board's conditions three times, and one name in them had a rank against it that nobody in Meridian had ever stood near.

*D. Coombe. Blaze.*

"Fours," said the Hunt Marshal again. "I'll not ask a third time."

It was Wilf Marten who went first, because he had a chair to push and was used to being first. Twenty-Eight's captain declared his four by role and by door. Selka Dray was in it, on two sticks, standing; she was the blade, and she declared her own door herself before Marten could, and he let her. Then Old Four, in Lowen Brask's flat voice, though Brask was not its captain and said so. Then Halden License, by Pettit, who was.

*Blade: Renn, K. Ember.*

Kellan stood at the end of the black-and-gold line with his own cuff laced. He did not look along the rail. Kiva did not look at him, either. It was a very ordinary thing to be doing, on a Monday morning, with a Blaze in front of them.

"Seven," said Coombe.

Perrin went red. It started at his collar and went up.

"Seven. T7, of the Meridian Collegiate Circuit." He had it written on his cuff, Kiva saw, in ink. "Captain, Cade, carry. Still. Hold, Pryce. Hand. Kill, Talley. Catcher, Rowan—" He stopped. He had come to the line that he had been dreading since the Friday of the warrant, and he looked at it on his cuff as though it might have changed in the night. "Catcher, Rowan. One door. Declared. The still. Reserve, Finch, catcher. Hand."

"Again," said Coombe. "The catcher. From the beginning of the line."

"Catcher, Rowan. One door, declared: the still."

"That's better. I could hear the first one being afraid of itself." The Marshal did not raise her eyes from her rope. "Rowan."

"Ma'am."

"I've read your card. The Chair sent it me in the first month, with every line on it, and I read it on the boat." She tied a knot. "On my floors you're recovery and nothing else. You'll catch. Nobody in this Pit will ask you for one thing more than your card says, and if anybody does, I'll want his name, and he'll want to have been somewhere else." Her eyes came up at last. They were pale, and very steady, and they went along the rail from one end to the other, slowly, as though she were counting heads on a quay. "That's said once. It doesn't need saying again."

Nobody spoke. Kiva felt the whole rail decide not to look at her, all at the same moment, and was grateful.

"I've six circuits and a month," said Coombe. "I've given this one twelve days. The other five have sent a four to the coast in living memory, and you haven't, in eleven years, and the Board thinks you'll drown." She turned to the cage. "So. A pack."

---

She did not explain what a pack was. She opened the cage.

She did it by laying her palm flat on the grey ward and drawing it aside, as one draws back a curtain, and the ward came away from one end of the box in a single heavy fold and hung there from her hand. It did not tremble. Kiva had seen wards held by Torches, and by Doran Mace's whole line at once, and by Anwen at the end of a long wall with her teeth set. She had never seen one held as this was, by one hand, without the least sign that it was being held at all.

The three things came out onto the cinder.

They were husks. Kiva had seen husks since she was thirteen, and these were like all the others and not like any of them. Each was the height of a tall man, a column of grey stuff like a sack of fine ash stood on end, belted at the middle by a knot as thick as a man's arm. Each stood on three thin legs, set wide, like a milking stool. They had no faces. They had a breath, which came out of them all over, as frost comes off a field at dawn, and wherever the breath touched the cinder it went white.

They stood in a row. Then they stamped.

It was all three of them at once. They lifted their legs together and put them down together, *one,* with a dry crunch on the cinder, as men put their feet down when they are marching. Then they leaned. All three leaned together a little to the left, toward the race. *Two.* They came back. *Three.* They stamped again. *Four.*

Then again, from the start.

Kiva had both hands on the oak rail. She took the right one off, and the left lay there alone, with the first two fingers flat on the worn wood, and the iron of the rail's bolt under them.

*Something's pushing. And not. And pushing.*

She did not believe it. Her heart was going hard, and she knew that it was, and she knew what her fingers did when it went. She lifted them until they barely touched.

*Pushing. And not.*

It was slow, and it was not her pulse, which was quick. It might have been the husks. It might have been the cart, or the race, or the old mast, or the bank of lamp-cells in the Measure Hall's cellar, sixty yards off through the ground. A needle with no scale said that something was keeping time, and said nothing about what.

"Who'll go first?" said Coombe.

Nobody moved. Then Perrin did.

"Seven," he said. He sounded astonished. "We'll go. I'd like it known that I haven't asked anybody."

"You're the captain. You needn't ask anybody." The Marshal stepped back toward the cage. "Kill one. That's all I want. One husk, dead, on that cinder, and your catcher's cloth on it. Your doors, your book, your ash. The floor is mine and the yield is the Board's. Go."

They went down the steps.

Kiva had been on that cinder before and she knew where she wanted to be, and she went there: east of the husks, eight paces back, on ground that she tested under her heel through her door before she put her weight on it, with her cloth over her shoulder and her left hand in her coat. Anwen went to the south, between the husks and the race, and brought her wall up low across the cinder, a pace long and then two and then four, silver, steady, her hands down by her hips. Perrin went to the middle, where he could see everything and be in the way of nothing. Jude went forward.

He walked toward the near husk with his long arms loose and his head on one side, as he always walked toward a thing that wanted killing. Kiva watched him come into the frost that the three of them breathed, and saw his boots go white at the toes.

*One.* They stamped. *Two.* They leaned. *Three.* They came back.

"*Now,*" said Kiva.

Jude cut.

It was a good cut. It came down out of his shoulder, heavy and straight, and went in at the near husk's knot from above. Kiva saw it go through, and the knot part like rope under an axe, and the top half of the grey column begin to slide.

*Four.*

The other two stamped.

And the near husk did not fall. The cut closed. It closed from the bottom up, as water closes behind an oar, and the knot was whole again. The two husks beside it had leaned in toward it on the stamp, one from each side, close enough to touch, so that for the length of a breath there were not three things standing on that cinder but one, broad, with nine legs.

Then the three of them stepped forward together onto Jude.

He went back. He was quick for his size and he went back fast, but the left-hand husk had leaned its breath over his shin as he turned, and Kiva saw the frost go up his boot to the knee like a stocking being pulled on.

"*Mine!*" said Anwen. The wall swung. It came round from the race side in a long low arc, as she had learned to walk it on Mace's line, and stood across the cinder between Jude and the three husks; and the husks put their breath against it, all together, *one,* and leaned, *two,* and her teeth showed.

Perrin had Jude by the belt. He did not ask. He took hold and pulled, as he would have pulled a man off a tram line, and the two of them went back across the cinder in a tangle and fetched up against the steps.

"Dark," said Coombe. "Out."

Anwen let the wall down a pace at a time, backing, and came out of it with her hands shaking. The husks did not follow. They went back to the middle of the floor, in their row, and stood, and stamped, and leaned. *One. Two. Three. Four.* It was as though nothing had happened.

"Talley," said the Marshal. "Your leg."

"It's cold." Jude was sitting on the bottom step with his boot out in front of him. The leather had gone white and stiff and stood away from his shin. "I can feel my toes. I'd say that's good."

"It's good. It's a burn, not a taking. You'll limp till Thursday." Coombe had not come any nearer. She had not lit anything, and she had not moved since she stepped back toward the cage, and Kiva understood that she would have moved if it had been needed, and that she had judged to a hair that it was not. "What did they do?"

---

Nobody answered her. She waited.

It was a long wait. The husks stamped twice in it.

"It healed," said Jude at last. "The cut. It went through and it healed."

"That's what it did. I asked what *they* did."

"They closed up on it," said Anwen. "The other two. Before it could fall."

"Closer."

Kiva had taken her left hand out of her coat. She had come back to the foot of the rail, and she laid the hand flat on the iron upright of the steps, light, the two dead fingers on the cold metal and the other three held off it.

*Pushing. And not. And pushing.*

It was slower than her heart. She was nearly sure.

"They keep time," she said. "All three. Together. I can't say what time. I've two fingers that tell me a thing's keeping it and never what." She heard how small that sounded, on a cinder floor in front of a Blaze, and she said the rest anyway. "Jude cut on my word. My word was on the stamp. I said *now* when the near one stood still, since it stood still on the stamp. So he cut in time with them."

"And?"

"And the other two were there. On the same count. They're always there on the count." She stopped. "I don't know that. It's what I saw."

"It's what you saw. I'll take what you saw. And whose is the count?"

Kiva had the answer to that. She had had it on a list since the ninth month, and she had said it on a pavement on the Brickfields road with a warden listening.

"Not mine," she said. "Somebody's who can count."

"Then who?"

The rail was very quiet. It was quiet in the particular way that a classroom goes quiet when a question has been put that has an answer, and nobody wishes to be the one who gives it, in case it is wrong.

"I was a clock," said Perrin.

He said it in a voice of deep injury. He was standing at the foot of the steps with Jude's belt still in one hand, and he was looking at the three husks with the expression of a man who has been asked to sing.

"On a Saturday. In the tenth month. With a turnip on my knee. I called sixty for every bag in the Low Lane, aloud, and I said at the time that I minded, and I've minded since." He swallowed. "One. Two. Three. Four. They stamp on one. They lean on two. They're back on three. There's a stamp on four that isn't as heavy as the one on one. I'd want somebody to check that."

"Count it aloud," said Coombe.

"*One.*" He did. "*Two. Three. Four.*" His voice was too loud, as the warden's had been at the drum, and it bounced back off the lattice. "*One. Two.*"

"And between?"

"There's nothing between."

"There's always something between. Say *and.*"

"*One, and. Two, and.*" Perrin stopped, and went on. "*Three, and. Four, and.* It's nursery. My little sister did this with a skipping rope."

"Your little sister had the right of it." The Marshal turned her pale eyes on the rail. "Halden. Your blade. You'll cut the far one on your catcher's word, as you would. Old Four, the same, after them. I want to see what a good four does with it when it hasn't been told anything."

Halden went down the steps in black and gold.

Kiva watched them as she had watched a laundress, with her hands still and her head still, because it was a thing worth watching. They were very good. Hesketh had her wall up before her boots were on the cinder, and Pettit was in the middle, and Yarrow was east with his cloth over his shoulder, almost where Kiva had stood. Kellan went up the north side on his struts in two long low bounds, dark, and stood behind the far husk with his edge unlit, and looked over his shoulder.

"*Now?*"

He said it to Yarrow, and Kiva's stomach did a thing that she had not expected and did not care for.

"*Now,*" said Yarrow.

The edge came blue-white down Kellan's fist. It went in at the far husk's knot, level, exact, the best cut that Kiva had seen on that cinder in her life; and it went in on the stamp, because the stamp was when the far husk stood still. The knot closed behind it. The three leaned in. Kellan was gone before they stepped, back along the north side in one bound, dark, untouched, and he landed behind a post of the lattice and stood there with his hand down.

"Out," said Coombe.

Old Four went after, and Gower cut on Brask's count, and Brask's count was *four.* Brask had seen it. Kiva watched him see it from the rail, with his notebook in his hand, at the second stamp; and he chose the light stamp, the fourth, where Perrin had said it was lighter. It was a good choice. It made no difference. The knot closed. The pack leaned. Gower went back with a white sleeve.

"Out." The Marshal had tied three knots in the time. "That's four good fours and three cuts and one burned shin, and that pack's had its breakfast. Seven. Your Kill can stand?"

"I can stand," said Jude.

"Then stand. You'll cut on your captain's *and.* Not on the count. On the *and.* After the stamp, before the lean. Cade, you'll count it aloud from the steps, as you did. Talley cuts on the word that isn't a number." Coombe looked at Kiva for the first time since the husks had come out. "Rowan. Your cloth."

Kiva went back to her tested ground.

She stood on it with the cloth on her shoulder and nothing open, and she saw Jude walk out again into the frost with his white boot and his head on one side, slower than before, favoring the left leg. She heard Perrin begin.

"*One, and. Two, and. Three, and.*"

Jude was there. He was a pace from the near husk, in the breath, with his arm up.

"*Four, and—*"

He cut.

It came down a half count after the stamp, when the near husk had set its three feet and the other two had not yet leaned. It went through the knot. And the near husk fell.

It fell as a sack falls off a cart: the top half one way, and the bottom half the other, and then neither, as the whole grey column went to a heap on the cinder with a sound like sand poured on a floor.

The other two stopped.

Kiva saw it. They did not stamp. They stood on their six legs, on either side of the heap, quite still, and it was exactly like watching two men in a file when a third has missed his step: the jerk, and the stumble, and the instant in which nobody knows which foot is meant to be down.

"*Dark! One!*"

That was her. She had thrown before she had said it. The cloth went out flat across the heap at the first cast and lay there, and she was on her knees at the hem with her right hand on the canvas and nothing open, and the ash under the cloth was a Handful, and it was lying where it had fallen.

"Finch," said Coombe. "Down. Cord."

Pru came down the steps so fast that she nearly went over. She was at the neck before Kiva had called the corners, and Kiva called them, *corners,* and Perrin had left his count to haul the north one and Anwen the south, and the neck came up, and Pru's hands went in.

*One. Two. Three.* The little finger came across. Nothing slackened.

"Sealed," said Pru. "Forty-nine."

Then the two husks found their count again.

Kiva felt it before she saw it. It was through the canvas, through the cinder under her knee, slow, *pushing, and not;* and then they stamped together, both of them, *one,* and leaned, *two,* and began to come.

They came for Jude. He was still nearest, and he was standing a pace from the bag with his cut spent and his arm down, and his left leg would not have carried him away at a run if he had begged it. They stepped onto the cinder where the heap had been, over the sealed bag, one from each side, and their breath went over the canvas and Pru's hands, and Pru snatched her hands back with a noise.

"*Count!*" said Jude. "Cade! I've lost it!"

Perrin was on the north corner of the cloth, on his knees, with the canvas in his fist. He had stopped counting when he hauled. He opened his mouth, and Kiva saw him try to find the beat again, and saw that he could not; he had dropped it on the floor of the Pit with the corner, and it had rolled away.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen.

The wall came down between Jude and the two husks like a shutter. She had not walked it this time. She had put it up where she stood, at the end of her own arms, all at once, and it stood there with the two husks leaning on it, and her heels went back an inch in the cinder, and another.

"Dark," said Coombe. "Out. All of you. Leave the bag."

They went up the steps backward, the five of them, with Anwen's wall coming down a pace at a time in front of them until it was a hand's width high and then nothing, and the two husks stood in the middle of the floor over a sealed bag and stamped.

---

"One," said Coombe, at the rail. "Out of three."

She said it without any expression. She had not lit, and she had not moved, and she had a rope with a great many knots in it.

"It fell," said Jude. He was sitting on the top step now, with Aske's colleague from the Wren House kneeling at his boot. "It fell, ma'am. On the *and.*"

"It fell. I saw it fall." The Marshal turned and looked at him. "And then you stood there over it, with your cut spent and your captain on his knees, and the other two had their count back in four beats, and they'd have had you by the knees in six if Pryce had been half a count slower. You didn't nearly have it. Don't tell me you nearly had it. You had one of three, and a man down, and you'd have had a man taken on any ground of mine with a tide coming in."

Jude did not argue. He looked at his white boot.

"That's what a pack is," said Coombe, to the rail. "On the Longshore they come in fives. Cut one in time and the others close it. Cut one out of time and you've broken it, and you've three counts, perhaps four, while they find their feet. In those three counts you've to cut the next. Out of time again. And the count they find, when they find it, isn't always the one they had. Who had the count when the first one fell?"

"Me," said Perrin. "And then I hadn't."

"Why?"

"I was hauling a corner."

"Then you'd two things to do and one mouth. That's not a fault in your mouth. That's a fault in your four." She did not soften it. "A pack keeps a count among itself. You'll have to keep one among yourselves, and somebody will have to be kept free to keep it. Think about who. Don't tell me now. I'll know on Thursday whether you've thought."

She went down the steps, alone, and walked across the cinder between the two stamping husks as though they were cows in a lane, and picked up the sealed bag by its neck, and walked back. Neither husk turned its head. Kiva did not breathe until the Marshal was on the bottom step again.

"Board's yield," said Coombe, and gave the bag to the Wren House man, who put a tag on it. She looked down at the cart, and at the grey ward lying folded over its open end, and closed the ward again with one hand, as one draws a curtain, with the two husks inside. Kiva never saw them go in. "That's not your floor, Seven. That was mine. Your floor's the twelfth, at a dram a head, and you'll have one, and I'll mark it. Before then I want three things, and I'll be at your yard to see them, and I'll not be told about any of them. I'll be shown."

"Three, ma'am?" said Perrin.

"A pack. A sheet." Coombe tied the last knot in that length of rope, and coiled it, and put it in her coat. "And a door."

She went up the steps to the gallery. Halfway up she stopped, and said over her shoulder, to nobody in particular, "Rowan. Your fingers said they kept time."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Were they right?"

"I don't know," said Kiva. "I'd have to ask somebody with a whole hand."

"Then you'd better find one before the twelfth," said Coombe, and went on.

---

Jude read the book out that evening at the Rowan kitchen table, standing by the dresser, where there had never been a chair for him and now never would be, because he had decided that he liked it there.

"*Monday the 2nd. The Pit, the Marshal's floor, not ours. A pack of three, Board's cage. Talley, two cuts, 0.09. Pryce, a wall, twice, 0.14. Cade, a stance and a haul, 0.05. Rowan, nothing; dark; a catch. Finch, nothing; a cord. Spent, 0.28. One husk killed, out of time. Yield, the Board's.*" He looked up. "*Held, nine and five hundredths.* That adds."

"You're saying it for Kellan," said Anwen.

"I'm saying it since somebody has to. I've found I like the sound of it. I'd no notion." He shifted against the dresser and winced, because he had moved his leg. "And *burned, Talley, the left shin, a husk's breath, to the knee; not taken.* I've written that too. I'd want it in."

Mara was at the far end of the room, at her half of the oilcloth, with Saltmere's tide table spread under the lamp like a railway guide. She had a pencil behind her ear and another in her hand, and she had not spoken since they came in. Perrin sat on the stool with his head in his hands. Pru was on the floor by the stove with her back against the dresser's end, and she had a hank of cord round her wrist and was not making turns on it.

"I was a clock," said Perrin, through his fingers. "I said it was nursery. I said it to a Blaze."

"She didn't seem to mind," said Kiva.

"She minded. I could see her not minding." He took his head out of his hands. "I was good at it for nine beats. Then Pryce said *corners,* and I've been a corner-hand since the fifth month, and my hands went to the cloth before I'd told them. I'd the count in my mouth and the canvas in my fist and when I looked up it had gone. I couldn't find the one. Do you know what that's like? You've had the one for nine beats and then it's just three grey things standing on a floor, and you're trying to remember which foot they had down."

"Yes," said Kiva.

He looked at her. He had forgotten, she saw, and then he remembered.

"Yes," he said. "I suppose that you do."

She had the Works paper out. There were only four sheets of it left, and she had ruled one in seven on the tram, standing, against the window. She put the first column in.

*A pack. Three husks, in the Board's cage, in the Pit. Taken on the Longshore.*

*What: three things that keep one count among themselves. They stamp on one, lean on two, come back on three, stamp light on four. When one is cut on the count, the other two lean in on the same count and it closes. When one is cut between counts, it falls, and the other two lose their count for three or four beats.*

"Is that right?" she said. "Perrin. Read it."

He read it over her shoulder, breathing on her ear.

"*Stamp light on four,*" he said. "You've put that."

"You said it."

"I said I'd want somebody to check it."

"Then I'll write that you said so." She did. *C.: the fourth is lighter. Not checked.*

*How:* She stopped.

She knew how. It was the thing that Coombe had made them do: a count aloud, and a cut on the *and,* and somebody kept free to keep the count. She could write that. She could not write who.

"Who keeps it?" she said.

Nobody answered. It was the Pit again, the same quiet, with a question in it that had an answer.

"It's not me," she said. "I'll say that first so that it's said. I can't count a thing that keeps time; I've two fingers that tell me it's there, and on a floor they'll tell me about my own heart as soon as anything else. And I'm on the cloth. A catcher's on her knees at a hem with her hands full and her eyes on a heap; you all saw where my eyes were when the first one fell." She looked at the sheet. "It can't be Jude. He's cutting. He has to be looking at a knot, not counting feet."

"It can't be me," said Anwen. "I'm in a wall. I see the wall."

"Then it's me," said Perrin. He said it quite flatly, as though a doctor had told him. "It's the carry. A carry's got nothing to carry until there's a thing down. I'm the only one on that floor who's got nothing to do until somebody's hurt."

"You hauled a corner."

"I hauled a corner because I've always hauled a corner. It's habit. It's a good habit; you can't get a neck up with fewer than two." He scratched the back of his neck. "So somebody else hauls it. Finch, if she's on the floor. Pryce, if her wall's down. Anybody. And I stand at the side like a man at a football match and say *one and two and.*"

"And if somebody's hurt?" said Jude.

"Then I've two things to do and one mouth, and I'll have to choose." Perrin looked down at his own big hands. "She said that. *That's not a fault in your mouth.* I'd like it known that I didn't understand it when she said it, and I do now, and I don't like it any better."

Kiva wrote, under *How: The count is the captain's. He hauls nothing. If he has to haul, he says so first, and somebody else takes the count, aloud, by name.*

"Takes it from where?" said Pru.

It was the first thing that she had said since they sat down. She was looking at the hank of cord on her wrist.

"From the floor?" said Kiva.

"From *him.* If Cade's counting and he has to go, and Pryce takes it: does she take it from him or from the pack? Because those aren't the same. If he says *four* and she says *one,* she's on his count. But if he's lost it—" Pru went pink, and kept going. "If he's lost it, then she's taking a wrong count off him. And the pack doesn't care what we say."

"She'd take it off the pack," said Anwen. "She'd have to look at their feet."

"Then she ought to be looking at their feet already," said Pru. "Before he hands it over. Not after."

The kitchen was quiet.

Kiva looked at the sheet. She had *mine* before *yours* on a sheet of her own, in this same hand, from a cobbled yard and a litter on a wall. *They take it. Then you let go.* And here it was again, coming out of the mouth of a first-year by a stove, about a thing that was not a litter and not a mark, but a count.

It was the same thing. She had not seen that it would be.

She wrote it under *Second try,* because that was where it went. *Whoever takes the count is counting before it's given. Then it's said: "yours." Then "mine." P.F.*

"That's yours," she said. "I've put your initials."

"I didn't want them."

"I know. They're there."

Pru looked at the sheet upside down from the floor. She did not say thank you. She did something rather better, in Kiva's estimation: she began making turns on the cord again, quickly, without looking, as she did when her hands were content.

Mara had put her pencil down.

"Your fingers," she said, from the far end of the table. "In the Pit. You told a Blaze they said that the things kept time."

"Yes."

"And she asked you whether they were right."

"Yes. I said that I'd have to ask somebody with a whole hand."

"Did you?"

"I asked Perrin," said Kiva. "He counted it. He said four, and a light stamp."

"Then you asked the right person." Her aunt picked up the pencil again, and looked at the tide table, and did not write anything. "Your needle has no scale on it. His has. Put the two together and you've an instrument." Her pencil tapped once on the tide table. "I've been trying to make one out of a fitter's watch and a table of the sea for a month. It's a comfort to see it done at a kitchen table in an hour."

"It didn't work. It broke one in three."

"It broke one. Before this morning it broke none." Mara bent over the table. "Write down which one. I'd want to know."

Kiva wrote it.

*Went wrong: the count was the captain's, and the captain hauled. Nobody had it when it was wanted most, which was after the first fell. Cost: Talley's shin, burned to the knee; 0.28 on the book; one of three.* She thought about the *Why* column, and the white cold space at the bottom of it, and the faces of three grey things that had none.

*Why they keep a count: not known. Why the cut closes when it is in time: not known.*

She had not expected to fill it. She had found that she was easier with an empty *why* than she used to be. A square that was left white was a square that somebody might fill one day without having to rub anything out first.

She held up her left hand to the lamp before she put the pencil down. It had been warm since noon, as Aske had said it would be, and he had walked down to the Pit to see it and said so, and written it, and gone back up again with his colleague, who had seen to Jude's boot. The three fingers shut when she told them, and the two stuck out, as before.

She wrote that too, on the right, small: *Hand to Ember, one. Cold to the knuckle at seven; warm at noon. Five more wanted. Three more can be had here.*

Then, because it was true, and because she had promised herself in the autumn that she would write a thing on the right side of the book when she thought it and not when it was convenient, she wrote one line more.

*My fingers knew it kept time and Perrin knew which. That's two people and one instrument. I've been looking for one person.*

She shut the book and laid it by the brass weight, for her aunt to read.
