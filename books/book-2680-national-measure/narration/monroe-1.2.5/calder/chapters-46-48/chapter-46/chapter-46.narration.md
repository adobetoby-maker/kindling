# Chapter 46 — Kiva: What Was Written

At a quarter to seven, on the floor of the Graving, with the flood standing brown against the Master's ward and the Barrel's held water lying grey against the other side of it, Kiva had laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the wet stone, because it was the hour.

She had done it with her right hand. She had picked up the left by the wrist and set it down on the stone, palm down, with the two dead fingers flat, the way you set a cup on a table for somebody who cannot lift it. Then she had waited.

Nothing. Not *something's pushing.* Not *keeps time.* Not the stillness of the slack, which was a thing in itself, and which the fingers had always told her about. Nothing at all. The hand lay on the stone and the stone lay under it and the one did not say anything about the other.

She had written it in her book with her right hand, for the first time since the Hunt, with the pencil held badly in fingers that had only just finished peeling. *Wednesday the 23rd. A quarter to seven. The floor of the Graving, forty-five paces in. No answer.*

It was the first entry in the column that was not *yes.* She had looked at it for a while. Then she had shut the book.

---

Aske took the hand at twenty past seven, on the sill of the shut gate, after he had finished with Bannock, and with Jude's and Gower's knuckles, which were white across the backs and not taken.

He held it as he always held it, palm down and then palm up, and pressed the backs of the fingers and the heel and the inside of the wrist. Then he did a thing he had not done before. He took a pin out of his lapel and pricked the ball of her thumb with it, very lightly, and watched her face.

"Nothing," said Kiva.

"I know. I watched." He put the pin back. A small bright bead of blood stood up on her thumb and she did not feel it. He pricked the inside of her wrist, an inch above where the hand ended. "There?"

"Yes. That's me."

"That's you." He wrote. "To the wrist. Clean. Not cold; gone." He looked at the hand a moment longer, lying in his own. "The seventh's cold never started. It hadn't come up the arm when you lit again, and the second took it with it: one bill, not two, and this is it. The rest of the arm will ache to the shoulder for a few days, I'd think, the way it ached after the sixth. Then it'll stop. The hand stays like this. Three weeks, if I guessed right on Monday. Then it comes back as it was, with your two fingers as they are, and nothing more."

"And the card."

"I'll write the card tomorrow morning, at the window, when I've seen the hand after a night, the way I always have." He capped his pen. "You know what I'll write. I said it twice. I'll not say it a third time on a sill at twenty past seven with a dead hand in my lap." He put her hand back on her knee, carefully, the way he had once put it back on a blanket. "That's the bill. It's paid. It's what I said it would be. I'd like that written somewhere too: that I said it right."

"I'll write it."

"Good." He stood up, slowly, with his bag. "Now there's a girl at the pump house with a hand starved white to the wrist on a cell, your aunt says, and she sent for me half an hour ago, and I've been here." He looked across the shut gate at the basin, where the flood had covered the mud and the six outfalls poured into it on six counts, each its own. "A starved hand's an easy thing, if there's plain. Ward-trace, plain, a dram and a little more. The Order's four jars are at the end of the Mole with a woman who's just been carried there on a cart and won't be fit to unlock her own door till noon." He looked down at her. "Where's your tin?"

---

The tin was in Jude's satchel, under the book, where it had been since the second month.

Seven stood round it in the keeper's room at the harbour pump house at twenty-five past seven: Perrin with his ribs strapped, Jude with his knuckles white across the backs where the cask had wrapped them, Anwen with her cap gone and her hair down, Pru with her caps torn on two fingers from the catchers' plank, and Kiva with her left hand hanging. Peverell had given them the room. On the keeper's table, where the three training cells had stood on Thursday night, there was nothing now but the tin, small and round and sealed with Anwen's wax, with the label on its lid in Perrin's slow careful capitals.

*W. PLAIN. 2.0. FOR A HAND. NOT FOR A BANK, A FLOOR, A CROSSING OR A SALE.*

Loveday Hosken sat on the keeper's stool beside it with her right hand in her lap. It was white from the fingertips to the wrist, the fingers half curled and not closing. Mara stood behind her with one hand on the girl's shoulder. Aske stood at the table with his bag open.

"It's a hand," said Perrin.

Nobody answered him for a moment.

"I'm saying it," said Perrin, "because the Harbour Committee's chairman asked for this tin in the Salt Hall on Friday night for the bank, and I said no in front of the whole town, and I'd said it was for a hand." He looked at Loveday's white hand in her lap. "It's a hand. It went white on case nineteen at the bank, keeping it on because she was told to. That's not for the bank. That's a hand." He breathed carefully. "I'd like that known before it's opened. Not after."

"It's known," said Anwen.

Jude opened the book on the table and found the page and took the pencil from its string. He wrote, and turned the book round so that they could all read it.

*Wednesday the 23rd. The keeper's room, the harbour pump house, at twenty-five past seven. Sealed medical, W, plain, opened for a hand: L. Hosken, fitter, the right, starved to the wrist on case nineteen. By the healer, Aske. Used: as the healer says.*

"Sign it," said Jude. "Cade and Rowan. It's bound in both books."

Perrin signed. He gave Kiva the pencil. She signed it right-handed, worse than she had ever signed anything, and gave the pencil to Jude.

Anwen broke her own wax with her thumbnail. Aske took the lid off.

The ash inside was grey and fine and lay in the tin like flour. Aske did not comb it. It had been combed at the gate the day they came, and every day since it had sat sealed in Jude's satchel under the book. He measured it out with a horn spoon onto a square of linen, a little at a time, watching the girl's face, and folded the linen round her white hand and held it there between his own two hands.

It took ten minutes. Kiva watched the whole of it. She had seen it done once before, on a kitchen table in Coper Street in the autumn, to her aunt's reading hand, and she had been too frightened then to watch properly. She watched now. She watched the white go out of the girl's fingertips first, slowly, from the ends in, the way frost goes off a window when the room warms, and the colour come back after it, pink and then red. She saw Loveday Hosken bite her lip when the feeling came back, because coming back hurts, and saw Mara's hand tighten on her shoulder.

"There," said Aske at last.

He unwrapped the linen. The hand lay in it, red and whole and closing. Loveday closed it, and opened it, and closed it again, and looked at it.

"I can feel the stool," she said.

"You'll feel everything for a day, too much," said Aske. "Then it'll settle." He tipped what was left on the linen back into the tin with great care, and looked at the tin, and then at the scale he had taken out of his bag. "A dram and two-tenths. The same as your aunt's hand took in the autumn, Miss Rowan. Starved hands are all much of a size." He put the lid back on. "Eight-tenths left."

"Eight-tenths," said Jude, and wrote it. *Used, 1.2. Left, sealed, 0.8. Bound as before.*

Anwen took a stick of wax out of her pocket and a box of matches, and sealed the tin again, on the table, with her thumb pressed into the warm wax where the old seal had been.

"It's eight-tenths," said Perrin. "Like it was. Before the Order." He looked at it. "It was eight-tenths in the autumn and Pryce said it'd stay eight-tenths till somebody killed something quiet."

"Nobody's killed anything quiet," said Pru. "Nobody's going to. There isn't anything quiet."

"Then it'll stay eight-tenths," said Perrin, "till somebody's hand goes white again. That's all right. That's what it's for."

Loveday Hosken stood up off the stool. She was a head shorter than Pru, with ink on her apron and her red hand held against her chest the way she had held a jar against it along the Long Quay at half past five. She looked at the five of them round the table, and at the tin with Anwen's wax going hard on it.

"I don't know any of you," she said. "I'd like to say thank you, and I don't know whose it was."

"It was the four's," said Kiva. "And the Order's. The Master gave them a dram and two-tenths in the second month to make it up to two, for a hand." She looked at the girl's fingers, closing and opening. "It's gone back to a hand. That's whose it was."

---

They bagged the purse from half past seven until eleven, and Kiva could not help with any of it.

The Graving was shut. The gate stood seated on its sill with the flood brown against its outer side, and the Company's men had closed every sluice in it and in the dock's walls by a quarter to eight. Inside, from the gate to Noa's chalk forty-five paces up and a little beyond, the dock floor lay under a shallow grey pool, a hand deep at the mouth and less further in, perfectly still. The Barrel's ash lay on the stone under it in long soft drifts where the held water had carried it, grey on grey.

"It's a purse," said Coombe, at the top of the dock-mouth steps, looking down at it. "Shut at both ends. Nothing gets out. It was always a purse; it's only that nobody'd ever drawn a thing this size into one before." She took her rope off her shoulder. "Catchers. Sheets."

The catchers went down into it with their own sheets.

Pru went first, because she had her own sheet: a new one, bought in the second month after the old one, with her name on its pole, had gone over a race lip at a mill. She had written her name on the new one's hem in ink. She waded out into the pool in her boots to the ankle with the sheet folded over her arm and Treen beside her with the Ropewalk's, and they laid the sheets on the stone under the water at the edge of the first drift and drew them in from the far edge, the way a catcher draws a purse string, and the ash came in onto them with the water running off.

Kiva stood on the sill with her left hand in her coat and watched.

She had never watched a bagging she could not kneel at. She had knelt at every one since the spring, at a hem or a corner or a far edge, with her hands dark or lit, saying two words or none. She stood on the granite now with her right hand in her pocket and her left hanging. She watched Pru on her knees in a hand of grey water with her capped fingers on the edge of a sheet, folding it in from the far side over a drift of the Barrel's ash: Treen at the other end of it saying *now,* and Pru saying *yes,* and the two of them lifting together.

Pru did not look up at her once. She did not need to.

"She's good," said Coombe, beside Kiva on the step.

"I know."

"I don't mean she's good because you taught her. I mean she's good." Coombe was watching Pru's hands. "She'd be good if she'd never met you. That's the best thing I can say about anybody."

Marten and Yarrow went in with Twenty-Eight's sheet and Halden's. Wace went in with Old Four's. By nine the sheets were coming up out of the pool one after another, heavy and dripping, and going into canvas bags on the sill, and the bags going up the dock-mouth steps on the Company's men's shoulders to a cart at the top that would take them along the quay to the Board's gate to be combed.

At eleven the last drift was off the stone. The catchers came up out of the pool with their boots grey to the ankle and their caps and gloves grey, and stood on the sill, and Pru looked at Kiva for the first time since half past seven.

"That's all of it," said Pru. "Except what's on my boots."

"Leave it on your boots," said Coombe. "It's red. It'll not hurt anybody on a boot."

It was red. Kiva went along the quay with the cart to the gate and stood by the comb while the Board's combers did it, bag after bag, under the Clerk's eye. Every bag sang full on the seventh reed, like a kettle, as Jude had said Yeo said it would. It had kept the town's time for six months. It could not do anything else.

"Six hundred and eighty-six drams," said the Board's clerk at the scale, at a quarter to twelve, "and four-tenths. Every dram of it red. Combed at the gate, under the Clerk." He wrote. "To the Board's press, as evidence."

"Who claims it?" said the Clerk.

She said it the way she said everything, without expression, to the gate in general. Halden's counsel was not there. Nobody was there from the Counting House at all. Kellan Renn, who had given the Clerk a folded sheet of fault paper at a quarter past eight, stood at the back of the small crowd at the gate with his mother's watch in his pocket and did not say anything.

"Nobody claims it," said the Clerk, answering herself. "As of twenty-five minutes to seven this morning, by a line on the harbour pump house's fault paper in the Proprietor's own hand, witnessed, which I have in my press. The foundation's counsel has sent me nine pages saying the same thing in longer words since, and I have those too." She looked at the bags. "So it's the Board's, as evidence. The warrant pays no share on red. Write that, please. *No share paid. No claim. Held.*"

The clerk wrote it.

---

Jude went to Ropery Row at half past nine, while the catchers were still in the pool, and Kiva went with him because there was nothing in the Graving she could do with one hand.

The water was gone. It had come up through the gratings in the low town's gutters at twenty-two minutes past six, when the six pumps stood, and spread along Ropery Row and the lanes off it, brown and quiet: four inches in the street, more in the cellars. At twenty-nine minutes past, when the sixth pump started, it had begun to go down. By half past nine it was gone from the street. There was a line on every doorstep, a dark tide mark on the stone four inches up. The cobbles were wet and shining, and the gratings in the gutters gurgled and rattled.

They did not rattle four and four. Kiva noticed it on the first grating she stepped over. She stopped and stood on it. Through the sole of her boot it went three and a quick, and then two, and then something long, like six bands at a fair, as Hart had said. It belonged to nobody.

People were out in the street with brooms. A man was sweeping water out of his front room into the gutter in long strokes, with his trousers rolled to the knee. Two children were sitting on a step with their feet in a bucket, for no reason Kiva could see, laughing. A woman at an upstairs window was hanging a rag rug over the sill to dry. Nobody looked frightened. They looked like people who had been wet before and would be wet again, and had moved the coal up a stair in the dark, and were now moving it down.

Jude went along the row looking at the doors. Kiva did not ask him which he was looking for. She knew.

It was the seventh house, a narrow one with a green door. The door was open. Inside, in a stone-floored kitchen two steps down from the street, a woman of about sixty was sweeping water toward the back door with a stiff broom, in long strokes. She was small and square, with grey hair pinned up and her sleeves rolled. There was a copper in the corner on a brick stand, and a mangle, and lines of washing across the ceiling on pulleys, out of the wet.

Jude stood in the street at the top of the two steps.

"Mrs. Penhale," he said.

The woman stopped sweeping. She looked up at him, a tall young man in a Meridian coat with his left arm held a little away from his body and his right hand white across the knuckles.

"I was at Mallow Creek," said Jude. "On the Wednesday. I helped carry him up the bank." He did not move from the step. "I'd want it said I'm sorry. I'd not have come except that it's this morning, and your kitchen was wet, and I wanted to see if it was dry."

Mrs. Penhale looked at him for a long time. Then she leaned her broom against the copper.

"It's dry enough," she said. "Four inches. I've had a foot in a gale." She looked at the water still lying in the low corner by the back door. "Davy used to sweep it out for me. He'd come down off the Longshore wet to the waist and sweep my kitchen before he'd change his own boots." She looked back up at Jude. "You'd best come down and finish it, if you've come all this way to look."

Jude went down the two steps and took the broom.

Kiva stood at the top of the steps in the street and watched him sweep a dead man's mother's kitchen floor with one good hand and one bad, in long strokes toward the back door, carefully, getting into the corners. Mrs. Penhale stood by the mangle with her arms folded and watched him too. Neither of them said anything for a long while.

"They say it's out," said Mrs. Penhale at last. "The bank."

"It's out," said Jude. "Each cell on its own count. Not two alike. The pumps on their own six each."

"And the water came in for seven minutes and went out again, and that's the price of it." She looked at the tide mark on her doorstep. "Davy'd have liked to see that. He was always saying the town kept one count in everything like soldiers, and soldiers fall over all together when one trips." She was quiet. "He'd have been on the mud this morning, if he'd been here. On the wall. He'd have counted it in fathoms."

Jude finished the corner. He leaned the broom against the copper, where she had leaned it.

"Ma'am," he said. "The Company's offered me his place. Not his. A place. On Mrs. Yeo's closing crew. From the first of next month."

"I know. Mrs. Yeo told me on Wednesday night. She came down to tell me before she carded you." Mrs. Penhale looked at him with her small hard grey eyes. "She asked if I minded. I said I didn't know you. Do you mean to take it?"

"I said I'd answer after Wednesday." Jude scratched his jaw with his right hand. "It's after Wednesday."

From behind Kiva, in the street, somebody said, "It is."

Yeo was standing on the cobbles in her green coat with gold at the collar, with her notebook in her hand and her boots wet to the ankle, as though she had been walking the whole of the low town looking at doorsteps, which she had. She looked down the two steps at Jude in the wet kitchen, and at the broom, and at Mrs. Penhale.

"I came to see the line on her step," said Yeo. "Four inches. It's on every step from here to the Tanyard." She looked at Jude. "Well, Talley."

Jude looked up at her from the kitchen floor for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "With one thing on the card."

"Say it."

"I asked you what ash was for," said Jude, "on the Ropewalk steps. You said keeping the sea out. On Tuesday I asked you what the Barrel's ash was for, when it was cut, and you said nothing; it'd be red, and go in the Board's press, and stay there." He did not look away. "I'll carry it up a bank for six months. Every bag. Put it on my card that I'm told where every bag goes after, and what it's for. Even if it's for nothing. I'll not carry ash up a bank and not know."

Yeo looked at him. Her flat brisk face did not change, and Kiva, who had watched it decide a thing at Mallow Creek in three seconds that was in no book, watched it now take longer than that.

"No Company hand's asked that in my time," said Yeo. "Nor in my mother's." She opened her notebook and wrote in it, standing in the street, fast, in her brisk upright hand. "*Talley, J. Closing crew, under the officer. From the 1st of the fourth month. Six months, and then we look again. To be told where every bag he carries goes after, and what it's for, by the officer, in writing, whatever the answer is.*" She shut the notebook. "I'll have it on a proper card by tonight. You'll sign it at the yard."

"I'll sign it," said Jude.

Mrs. Penhale had picked up her broom again.

"He'll do," she said to Yeo, as though Jude were not there. "He swept the corners." She looked at Jude. "Davy never did the corners."

Jude came up the two steps into the street. His face was doing something Kiva had not seen it do before, which was nothing at all, very carefully.

"I'd want it said," he said to her, when they were twenty paces down Ropery Row and the green door was behind them, "that I've seen the sea come in over somebody, and go out of somebody's kitchen, both. In a week." He stopped on a grating and stood on it, and it went three and a quick under his boots, and two.

---

The Board's black iron box with three locks was carried out of its tent onto the boards of the timber pond at noon, and set on a trestle in the open, because Mara had asked that it be opened in daylight where anybody who wanted to could stand and hear.

A good many wanted to. The boards were crowded to the edge of the black water and along the brick wall and up the Graving's landward lip, where people stood at the rail looking down at the shut purse eighty feet below and then back at the box. The Mercury's young man was at the front with all three of his pencils. Kiva stood near the trestle with her left hand in her coat, beside Hart, who had come from the landing with his book of slacks in his jacket and his bad hand out of it.

Mara had named four people to stand at the box. Hart. Peverell, in her apron, who had not slept, again. Kiva. And Loveday Hosken, who stood very straight at the end of the row with her red hand held against her chest, because Mara had said that the fitter with case nineteen had been the last to say *mine,* and somebody ought to stand at the box who had been the last to say anything. The Board had named Yeo and Rusk and the Chair.

The Clerk unlocked the three locks herself.

She took out the paper and held it up so that the boards could see the wax, the Board's seal and the hour. Then she broke it and read it aloud in her even voice, every word, as it had been written on the 11th at the keeper's desk.

When she had read it she did not lower it.

"The Board has the morning's record," she said. She looked at Hart. "It will read each line against what happened, and say right or wrong, and the man who wrote it will say whether he agrees. Mr. Hart."

"Yes, ma'am."

"*The slack in the basin will begin at nine minutes past six by the pump house clock, and not before seven past nor after twelve past.*"

"It began at eight past," said Hart. "Miss Rowan's hand had it a breath before mine." He did not look at his book. "Inside the limits. A minute off the figure. I've never been early, and I was early. I don't know why."

"Right in the limits," said the Clerk, "wrong in the minute." The Board's clerk wrote it. "*It will last not less than eighteen minutes nor more than twenty-two.*"

Hart was quiet for a moment.

"I can't give you an honest length," he said. "From the first quiet at eight past to the ache at thirty-one is twenty-three minutes. That's over the limit by one. But in the middle of it, from sixteen past to twenty-two past, my hand ached. It ached because the pumps were going on Mr. Halden's count, and nothing else in the basin was holding one." He looked at the Clerk. "That's our doing. We put it there. When he let go, it went quiet again, and stayed quiet till thirty-one, though six pumps were running by then, every one on its own count." He stopped. "I don't know why six counts at once didn't ache it. I'd guess. I'll not give the Board a guess."

"Then it's wrong," said the Clerk. "Twenty-three, and not a clean figure. Write that, please, as he said it." She went on. "*When it begins, the ring at the outfall will rise from the mud. It will stand at four feet eleven inches on the basin's staff, and not less than four feet ten.*"

"Four foot eleven," said Hart. "By my level, at nine past. Right."

"*It will lie down when the slack ends.*"

There was a short silence on the boards.

"Not tested," said Mara. It was the first thing she had said. "It never got the chance to lie down. We made sure it couldn't." She did not look at anybody. "Write that line as *not tested, by design,* and not as right."

"*Not tested, by design,*" said the Clerk. "*While it stands, the water inside it will keep the bank's count, four and four, at fifty-seven to the minute or faster.*"

"Fifty-seven," said Hart. "Miss Dray counted it from the wall, by her watch, for every pass. She's given me her figures. Fifty-seven, every minute she counted. Right."

"*Nothing else in the basin will keep any count, by the hand on the grating, though the pumps run.*"

Hart looked at his bad hand.

"Wrong," he said. "By my hand, once: the ache from sixteen past to twenty-two past, when the pumps kept Mr. Halden's count because we'd asked him to hold them. By what we did, twice: from nine past, the wards and the Embers and the still doors on the mud kept Miss Dray's count, on purpose, so the thing would come to them, and my hand never felt that from the grating." He did not look up. "We wrote that line on the 11th before we knew either. It's wrong because we made it wrong. Write both."

"Both written," said the Clerk. The clerk wrote.

She lowered the paper at last.

"The Board records," she said, "that a slack was predicted under seal twelve days before it came, by a fitter and a reader. The prediction was right in the limits of its hour, right in the height of the ring, and right in its count. It was wrong in its length, by a minute, for reasons the man who wrote it does not know; wrong in its last line, once by the hand on the grating and twice by the operation's own doing; and not tested in one line, by design. That's a better record than most sealed papers the Board has opened in thirty years. The Board would like that written too."

Then she looked down at a second sheet on the trestle beside the box, which Kiva had not noticed, in Noa's narrow hand.

"There's one thing that's on no line of this paper," said the Clerk, "because nobody could have written it on the 11th. The Board has it from four places. The Board's reader, at the lane mast: a hair on every pen at twenty-two minutes past six and eleven seconds, nobody on the mast, lasting nine seconds by his clock. Every lamp on the Graving's tiers, and the Long Quay, and the low town, and the landing, to a point, and back; seen by three thousand people, and the Board will not ask any of them to say what colour."

She did not change her voice. "And three accounts, written separately before any of them had spoken to the others, on Graywater's officer's slate, in the Order's book in the Master's words, and on Mr. Renn's sheet of fault paper, each saying that somebody counted and that the writer doesn't know what."

The boards were very quiet.

"Three people who have dreamed what the Board has called the unroofed sky," said the Clerk, "were seen to count together, in the same nine seconds, from three places, by three witnesses who did not know what they were counting." She folded Noa's sheet once. "The Board writes it down. The Board draws no line under it. It goes in the press with the paper."

She put both sheets in the black iron box and locked the three locks, one after another, and the boards let out their breath.

Kiva stood by the trestle with her left hand in her coat and her right in her pocket. She had not known about the three accounts. Rhea had said *I've written it.* She had not known about Kellan's sheet. She had thought, sitting on the dock floor with her lips moving, that she was counting by herself.

Beside her, Hart had taken his bad hand out of his jacket and was looking at it.

"I've been right about the slack for six months," he said, quite low, to nobody. "I wrote it down twice a day. I was wrong about the length this morning by a minute, and I don't know why, and it's in the Board's press with my name on it." He put the hand back in his jacket. "I'd not have missed it."

Orla Dane went to the officers' lodging at four in the afternoon, because the Chair had sent for her, and sat on the room's one hard chair with her stick across her knees and watched Ysra Crest strike her name out of a ledger.

The Chair did it standing, at the narrow table, with the pen in her level hand. Orla had watched that hand write every condition Kiva Rowan had ever been given, line by line, since the spring of last year. She watched it now draw one line through a paragraph, neatly, from margin to margin, so that the words under it could still be read.

*Sponsor: O. Dane, Torch, the Home Line. Accountable. Recovery only, dark hands; and one crossing—*

The pen went through all of it. Then the Chair wrote beside it, in the margin, small.

*Struck, Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, 2681, at the request of the sponsor of the 20th, on evidence. A crossing in the field, Ember to still, inside the certificate, on the candidate's own word, read at the time by the office's reader from a sanctioned mast; at the harbour basin, Saltmere, at eleven minutes past six. The certificate stands in the candidate's own name. Its conditions stand as written. Y. C.*

She blotted it and turned the ledger round.

Orla read it twice, with her lips shut.

"There was a second," she said.

"There was. I've entered it on the next page." The Chair turned the leaf. Orla read that too. *A second crossing, Ember to still, in the same hour, declared by her captain's voice, against her healer's card of one in seven days and against the rate on her certificate, the price said first by her healer; on the officer's warrant; read from the same sanctioned mast. Not an incident: the mast was sanctioned, but a warranted response is not a sanctioned event, and the rate is the healer's to enforce. Entered. Her healer reports the cost.* "It doesn't touch your line. Your line was struck on the first. The first was what you asked for."

"I know what I asked for." Orla looked at the struck paragraph. "Twenty years I've been somebody's sponsor. Hers for a year. I'd thought I'd feel something when it went."

"Do you?"

"I feel as if my knee hurts," said Orla, "which it does." She put both hands on her stick. "That's all, Crest. Thank you. It wants doing before, and it's done after, and I'll take it." She got up, stick first. "I'm going to the pump house."

The Chair did not ask why.

Hart was on the gallery, as he always was at that hour, with the oil can, between the north and south rows of cases. The thirty-six cells turned their lights on either side of him, each on its own count, slow and quick and uneven, so that the long hall was full of light that went every way at once and kept no time. Below the galleries the six pumps went up and down, each at its own pace, and the hall that had hummed like a held note for six months was full of a sound like a market. Orla came up the iron stair a step at a time with her stick ringing on each tread, and he heard her at the bottom, as he had on Sunday, and did not turn round until she was at the top.

"Dane," he said.

"Jess."

He put down the oil can.

"Crest's struck my line," said Orla. "On evidence. The girl's certificate stands in her own name."

"I thought she might. I heard both crossings called this morning, from the landing." Hart looked along the gallery at the cases, every one keeping its own time. "She crossed inside it. On a mast. On her own word." He was quiet. "She crossed the second outside it, with the price said first, on her captain's voice. Same hour."

"She did."

"I went onto a Bench in 2660 with a door I knew wasn't ready," said Hart, "and nobody said a price, and nobody declared it, and nobody read it, because I didn't let them." He looked at his bad hand on the gallery rail, the fingers half curled. "She did both of hers in front of everybody. And her hand's gone to the wrist for three weeks, and it'll come back." He was quiet a long time. "Mine didn't."

Orla stood by the stair-head with both hands on her stick.

"I'll not say that's my fault," she said. "I said it on Sunday. I'll not say it twice, because it's not a thing that gets truer for saying." She looked at the cases. "I'll not say it's yours either. I'll say I watched her do it right this morning from a crate, and every hand on that mud knew who'd taught her to wait for the price, and it wasn't me and it wasn't you. It was a healer and a girl's own sense." She was quiet. "I'd liked to have taught you that. I didn't know it."

"No," said Hart. "Nor did I."

He picked up the oil can again, and bent to the fourth pump's crank, which was going up and down on a slow heavy count like a man walking uphill, and put a drop of oil on it very carefully with his left hand.

"It doesn't make the Bench right," he said, not looking up.

"No."

"Good." He straightened. "I'd not want it to. Go down, Dane. Your knee hurts. I can see it from here."

She went down. At the foot of the stair she did not stop and look back, as she had on Sunday. She found that she did not need to. It had been said on Sunday and it was still said, and nothing about it was mended, and a girl's certificate stood in her own name, and the pumps behind her were going on six counts like a market, and her knee hurt.

That was enough for one day, Orla thought. She had not expected it to be.

---

Kiva did not have a fever that night.

She had expected one. Two Glory masters in one hour, and touching, and her lips counting on a dock floor; she had sat in the loft from four o'clock with Aske's watch on the sill and waited for the heat in her hands and the backs of her knees. It did not come. At six Aske took her pulse and her temperature and wrote *Seventy-six. Normal. No fever. Two masters, and touching, and nothing,* and turned the card round to show her, and looked at it himself afterward for some time.

"I don't know," he said. "Write that I said so."

At nine Mara came up the outside stair to the loft and knocked twice and once on the door, which made Kiva laugh for the first time that day.

They sat on the bed by the north window with the quay lamps lit along the black water outside, white and steady, each one a flame and nothing more. Under the floor, under the chandlery and the quay, there was no count. Kiva had noticed it at five, coming up the stair, and had stopped on the third step with her hand on the rail, and felt nothing in it at all except the rail. Six pumps a quarter of a mile off, on six counts, and none of them carried this far through the stone. The bedstead under her was only iron.

"Kellan says you said it would be on your name," said Kiva.

"It was on my name." Mara had her reading hand in her lap, palm up. "It was on my name on Friday in the Salt Hall, and it was on my name at twenty-four minutes past six, and if he hadn't lifted his hand by then I'd have called it off, and it would have been on my name that I had." She looked at the hand. "I didn't own your crossings. Either of them. I heard Noa say *cliff* twice from the far side of the basin and I kept my watch hand still. That was the hardest thing I did this morning."

"I know it was."

"You don't. You were in a dock." Mara's dry exact face did not change. "I'm telling you so that you know."

Kiva looked at her aunt's reading hand, lying palm up on the blanket.

"Did you touch one?" she said. "After."

"Case nineteen. At half past six. When it was done and nobody needed it." Mara turned the hand over. "I wanted to know. It was the girl's mother's hymn, slow, and under it there was nothing but more of itself." She was quiet. "I'd decided not to touch one till it was nobody's need. I kept to it. And then I decided to, and I did." She looked at Kiva. "Both are in my book. Not yours."

Mara put her hand on Kiva's knee, briefly, below the dead hand where it lay. "And you counted. On the dock floor. Rhea Sorn told me at the box. Four and a rest."

"I don't know what."

"Then don't know." Mara took her hand away. "I sealed a page for you once so that nobody could say you'd borrowed a thing. We broke the seal on Sunday, you and I, both hands. It's done. It's in the Board's press with the morning, and with three accounts of three people counting, and nobody's drawn a line." She stood up. "That's the right place for it. I'll not ask you anything about it ever again, and I'll mean it."

"You've never asked me anything about it."

"I know," said Mara. "I've been very good." She went to the door, and knocked on its inside twice and once, as a joke, and went down the stair.

Kiva lay down on the bed by the north window with her book under her pillow and her dead hand on the blanket beside her like a glove somebody had left there. Outside, the quay lamps burned white on their fluted posts, every one a flame. Under her, under the floor, there was nothing at all keeping time.

She found she could sleep in it.
