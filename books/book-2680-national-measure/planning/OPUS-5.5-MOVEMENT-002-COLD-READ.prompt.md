# Chapter 9 — Kiva: The Near Gap

Perrin had rubbed out the *44* on the shed door before anybody else was in the yard, and he was standing back from it with the chalk in his fist when Kiva came through the side gate.

*43.*

"I'd like it known that I did that properly," he said. "I wiped it with my sleeve. A captain doesn't leave the old number showing through."

"It was showing through."

"It was a ghost of a four. Nobody could have read it but me." He put the chalk in his pocket. "It's Sunday. It's the first of the second month. I've been awake since five, and I find that a number on a door is a great deal heavier at five than it was on Saturday afternoon."

The Old Yard was white with frost from the sheds to the bottom wall, and the black posts stood up out of it in their two rows like the piles of a pier at low water. Somebody had already been down the lane with a broom. There was a scraped place in the middle, two strides across, with a chalk mark at either end of it, and on the far side of the scraped place stood the Circuit's travelling mast on its three legs, with its brass drum and its tape and a tarpaulin over the top against the damp.

Noa Bexley was taking the tarpaulin off. He did it as he did everything, in the right order and without being hurried, and he had a red flag and a white one stuck in his belt.

Orla was on her crate, a pace north of where it had stood for one-and-twenty years, with her stick between her boots and a rug over her knees that she would have denied owning.

"You're late," she said.

"It's seven."

"I said seven. I meant that you'd be here before it, as you always are, so that I needn't wait. I've been waiting." Her crooked face did not change. "Sit. Not on the step. I want to say a thing before anybody lights, and I want it said to you sitting, so that you'll hear it and not be thinking about your feet."

Kiva sat on the end of the crate, which was the only place there was.

"Six," said Orla. "Of mine. On the Chair's mast, read by Bexley, with me on this crate, from this month. That's the certificate's word and it's mine. Hand to Ember. What you did in the eighth month is in your book, and in your book it stays. I'll not count a thing that I didn't see on a tape."

"I know."

"I know that you know. I'm saying it aloud so that it's been said." She turned the stick between her palms. "Now the rest. Your healer's card says one crossing in seven days, at the most. This month has thirty days in it. Count them."

Kiva had counted them on Friday night, at the kitchen table, on the back of a tram ticket, and again at three in the morning without the ticket.

"Five," she said. "If I go today, and on every seventh day after it. Today, the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second, and the twenty-ninth. And on the twenty-ninth I'll be on the road."

"So it's four, or it's five on a road with no mast on it."

"Four. In this city."

"Then the sixth is at Saltmere, on their gate mast, or it isn't at all. And it may be that the fifth is too." Orla said it flatly, as one reads off a gauge that has stuck. "I'll write that today, before anybody has time to hurry. I've seen a girl hurry a crossing because a clerk wanted a sixth before a boat. She's a fitter now, and she's never lit a door since."

"I'll not hurry."

"No. You'll be hurried, which is different, and you'll have to say no to it. I'm telling you now so that you'll know whose no it is." She looked down the lane at the mast. "It's yours. I'm only the woman who wrote the number."

Kiva sat with that for a moment.

She had supposed, all the way up the hill in the dark, that it would be the hand that set the pace of the month. She saw now that it would be the hand and a calendar together, and that between them they had already said *four,* and that anybody who wanted six would have to argue with a healer's card and thirty days and not with her. It was a queer kind of comfort. It was the comfort of a sum that has been done honestly and comes out short.

"Then I'd like them on Sundays," she said. "So that Aske has the hand on a Monday morning, at seven, and so that it's a full week before the next, and nobody can say that I took six days and called it seven."

"Sundays." Orla nodded once. "Bexley. Did you hear?"

"Sundays," said Noa, from the mast. "I've written it. The Chair's office is shut on a Sunday, but the mast isn't. I asked."

---

The others came up the lane while Noa was threading his tape.

Jude had the team book under his arm. He had carried it since the Friday of the warrant, and he carried it as though it might go off: flat against his ribs, with his forearm across it, the way a carter carries a parcel marked *glass.* Anwen came with him, and Pru behind, and Pru had a hank of cord round her wrist and was making turns on it without looking, as she did now whenever her hands were not wanted.

"It's on the book," said Jude. "Before anybody asks. Three-tenths, or thereabouts. I've left the line open."

"It'll be three-tenths and a hair," said Noa. "It always is, from the ward. The ward's dearer to shut than the Ember. I've three of hers from the summer in the office, and the hand's always the dear end."

"Then I'll leave room for the hair."

Kiva walked out to the scraped place.

The first chalk mark was hers to stand on. The second was six paces on, down the lane, a white cross on the black dirt. She opened the still door under the second mark, by its corners, the way that she had opened it a thousand times since the summer, and she felt the ground come up through it, packed and dry and solid to a depth that she could not find the bottom of. She shut it.

"Clean."

"Then say it," said Orla.

She turned to the mast. Noa had the white flag up. Pru had stopped making turns.

"*Hand, to Ember. Rowan, changing on three.*"

"*One,*" said Orla.

The hand door came up at her right hip.

It was low and silver and about the size of a tea tray, as it always was now, and it lay across the eight fingers that reported like a thing that had been set down there and was not heavy. The first two fingers of her left hand stood clear of it, stiff, like the prongs of a fork. She had stopped trying to make them do otherwise. They were passengers on a ward. They went where it went and held nothing.

Orla leaned forward off her crate and tapped the ward with the end of her stick, once.

It rang. It was a small, clear, cold sound, like a spoon on a glass, and Kiva felt it come up through the silver and into her palms; and that was the tap that a ward was for, the one tap, the shove of a thing that has to be caught. A catcher's ward that had been tapped had done its work, and the next thing that it did was fold.

"*Two.*"

She folded it from the far edge. The far edge came home over the near, and the near over itself, and the whole bright tray went down in her hands, as a sheet goes down on a laundress's table, until there was a penny of silver between her right palm and her left and nothing else. Then she shut the door on the penny.

It was the near door. It always had been. The hand door lay closest of the three, just under the skin of her palms, and it went shut there with a small cold click that she felt in the bones of both wrists at once.

"*Three.*"

Nobody.

She had known it would be nobody. She had been nobody in the air over an oak floor a month ago, between an Ember and a stance, and it had been as she remembered it: a quarter of a dram going out of her like a breath, and nothing open, and nothing to stand on, and the left hand cold. This was not that. This was nearer.

The other gap had been in the air between two places. This one was in her hands.

The cold did not wait to be paid for. It was in the first two fingers at once, which could not feel it, and then in the rest of the hand, which could; and it went up, over the knuckles and the back of the hand, toward the wrist, while she was still nobody, as though the door that had just shut had been the only thing between that hand and a great deal of winter.

*Arrive before you're there.*

The Ember door opened under her breastbone.

It came hot, and it came gladly, the way the Ember always came, as though it had been waiting at the top of a stair to be asked down. The struts ran down the backs of her calves and along the soles of her feet in their dull orange, and the three heat-lines across her chest drew tight from the breastbone to the left shoulder, and she went. It was one low bound, six paces, with the frost going by under her in a grey smear and the black posts going by on either hand.

Her right foot came down on the white cross.

She shut the Ember at the landing and stood on her own two feet in the Old Yard with every door closed, breathing smoke.

"Landed," said Kiva.

Noa did not answer at once. He had his face close to the drum, and the tape was still running through his fingers, and when he looked up he looked first at the tape and then at her and then at the tape again, as he always did, to be certain that the two of them agreed.

"One cliff," he said. "Twenty-six. Two lines, a ward and an Ember, and a gap between them that I could put my thumb in. Nothing open across it. Thirty-one in all." He pulled the red flag out of his belt and did not raise it. "That's a crossing. Hand to Ember. Not a stack."

"Write it," said Orla. "*One.*"

Kiva held up her left hand.

It was white. It was white to the wrist and a little past, and the skin over the knuckles had the look of a candle end; and when she shut the hand, it shut slowly, all of it, the eight fingers as well as the two, as though the order had to go a long way to reach them. There was no pain in it. There was simply a great deal of cold, in a place that she was used to listening to, and the listening had stopped.

Pru had come up beside her. She took the wrist without asking and held it, two fingers on the pulse, and looked at the shed clock through the open door.

"Ninety-eight," said Pru. "That's you, not it. You're breathing like Cade on a stair."

"I'll stop."

"You'll stop when you stop. I'm only telling you what it is." She did not let go. "Is it worse than the other way?"

Kiva thought about that honestly, since she had been asked.

"It isn't worse," she said. "It's nearer. Ember to still, the gap's out there, between two places, and you pay it and you're across. This one's in the hand. You shut the ward and the hand's the thing with nothing in front of it." She turned it over and looked at the white palm. "Orla. Is that what you meant? *The wrong half.*"

"I meant that a catcher's crossing begins at her ward." Orla had not moved off the crate. "Everything that you catch, you catch there. If you've a thing on your ward and you need to be somewhere else with it, that's the gap that matters, and it's the near one. Any fool can cross from a bound to a stance; it's a dancing master's trick. You'd learned the far gap and not the near one. Now you've begun on it."

"It costs the hand."

"It costs the hand. I'd not have asked if I'd thought it would cost less." Her sponsor's voice did not soften by a hair, and Kiva was glad of that. "Five more. Four in this city. And the hand seen every Monday by a man who won't round down."

Jude was writing in the book on his knee, on the shed step, with his tongue between his teeth.

"*Sunday the 1st,*" he read out. "*The Old Yard, on the Circuit's travelling mast, read by N. Bexley; certified sponsor present. Hand to Ember, Rowan. One crossing. 0.31.*" He looked up. "*Held, nine and thirty-three.* That's off the line we spoke for. There's one and forty-nine left on it, if anybody's keeping that column."

"Somebody's keeping it," said Kiva.

"I know. I'm the somebody. I'm telling the others so that they know that I'm keeping it." He scratched his jaw. "Kellan said I count everything and trust none of it. I've found it's true. I added that line three times."

Perrin had gone to the shed door and was looking at the *43.*

"One," he said, to the door. "I'd like it known that I'm not chalking those as well. I've one number to wipe off every morning and I find it's plenty."

Kiva put her left hand inside her coat, against her ribs, where it could borrow some warmth, and found the disk there in the inside pocket where she kept it. It was cold. She had known it would be. She looked anyway, as she always looked, since there was a column in a book on a creaking stair that wanted the word written within the week, and she would not write a word that she had not checked.

---

Healer Aske had the hand at seven on Monday morning, under the lamp at the Wren House, with the frost still on the window.

He did it with the pin and the penny and the cup and the cord, in the same order as always, and then he did a thing that he had not done before. He took her left wrist between his finger and thumb and held it, quite still, with his eyes on his own watch, for a whole minute by the second hand.

"Cold to the knuckle," he said. "Not past. The palm's warm. The two that don't report are as they were. The other eight are slow to shut, and they'll be slow until about noon, I'd think, and then they won't be." He let go. "It's sulking."

"It did that in the summer."

"It did that in the summer after the other kind. This is a different kind, and I'll want to see whether it sulks the same each time or longer." He wrote on her card, against the wall, standing. "*Monday the 2nd. After one crossing, hand to Ember. Cold to the knuckle at seven; expected warm by noon.* I'll see it again at noon, if you're anywhere near."

"I'll be at the Pit."

"Then I'll walk down. I've a man there with a shin that a husk breathed on last week, and I'd as soon look at the two of you in one trip." He capped his pen. "The card stands. One in seven, at the most. Your next is Sunday."

"Yes."

"I'll be honest with you, since that's what I'm paid for. If it sulks longer each time, I'll say so each time, and I'll say it before you go and not after." He looked at her over his spectacles. "I don't yet know what longer means. I'd like you to know that I don't."

She found that she liked him the better for saying it, and that she had never yet heard him say anything else.

---

The Pit was sunk below the Measure Hall's east yard like a dry dock with its water let out, and on that Monday morning it had sixteen students standing round its rail, and a cart in the middle of its floor.

Kiva knew the Pit. Everybody on that rail knew it. It was a floor of packed cinder forty paces by twenty, with a stone race running the length of its south side and a lattice of black iron across the north end, where the Wren House kept what it kept; and in the spring she had stood on that cinder for a Flask qualifier with her heart in her mouth, and in the autumn there had been a square of skinned husks over the race with a white cord between them. There was a sixty-year-old mast on the gallery and a rail of worn oak round the top, and the steps down were steep and narrow and slick in frost.

The cart was new.

It was a low flat dray with iron wheels, and on the dray stood a cage. It was not like the Wren House's lattice, which was a wall. It was a box of the same black iron, about the size of a hen house, with a ward laid over it on the outside. The ward was grey, not silver. It was old and thick and very steady, and it did not flicker, and Kiva could feel it from the rail as one feels a stove from the doorway of a room.

There were three things in the cage.

She could not see them well. She could see a stir of grey behind the bars, like smoke in a jar, and then a shape that might have been a shoulder, and then nothing. She could hear them. It was a low dry sound, not loud, and it came in fours.

"Fours," said a voice behind her. "By your captains. Declare."

It was not loud either. It did not need to be.

There was a woman on the steps.

She was small, and she was broad, and she had shoulders that a carter would have been glad of. She wore a Board's grey greatcoat buttoned to the chin, and her hair was iron grey and cut as short as a boy's, and her face had been out in weather for so long that it had gone the color and grain of a boat's rail. Kiva would have put her at sixty. She had a length of tarred rope in her right hand, as long as an arm, with knots in it, and as she came down the last three steps she tied another knot in it without looking.

She did not say who she was. She did not need to say that either. There were sixteen people on that rail who had read the Board's conditions three times, and one name in them had a rank against it that nobody in Meridian had ever stood near.

*D. Coombe. Blaze.*

"Fours," said the Hunt Marshal again. "I'll not ask a third time."

It was Wilf Marten who went first, since he had a chair to push and was used to being first. Twenty-Eight's captain declared his four by role and by door. Selka Dray was in it, on two sticks, standing; she was the blade, and she declared her own door herself before Marten could, and he let her. Then Old Four, in Lowen Brask's flat voice, though Brask was not its captain and said so. Then Halden License, by Pettit, who was.

*Blade: Renn, K. Ember.*

Kellan stood at the end of the black-and-gold line with his own cuff laced. He did not look along the rail. Kiva did not look at him, either. It was a very ordinary thing to be doing, on a Monday morning, with a Blaze in front of them.

"Seven," said Coombe.

Perrin went red. It started at his collar and went up.

"Seven. T7, of the Meridian Collegiate Circuit." He had it written on his cuff, Kiva saw, in ink. "Captain, Cade, carry. Still. Hold, Pryce. Hand. Kill, Talley. Catcher, Rowan—" He stopped. He had come to the line that he had been dreading since the Friday of the warrant, and he looked at it on his cuff as though it might have changed in the night. "Catcher, Rowan. One door. Declared. The still. Reserve, Finch, catcher. Hand."

"Again," said Coombe. "The catcher. From the beginning of the line."

"Catcher, Rowan. One door, declared: the still."

"That's better. I could hear the first one being afraid of itself." The Marshal did not raise her eyes from her rope. "Rowan."

"Ma'am."

"I've read your card. The Chair sent it me in the first month, with every line on it, and I read it on the boat." She tied a knot. "On my floors you're recovery and nothing else. You'll catch. Nobody in this Pit will ask you for one thing more than your card says, and if anybody does, I'll want his name, and he'll want to have been somewhere else." Her eyes came up at last. They were pale, and very steady, and they went along the rail from one end to the other, slowly, as though she were counting heads on a quay. "That's said once. It doesn't need saying again."

Nobody spoke. Kiva felt the whole rail decide not to look at her, all at the same moment, and was grateful.

"I've six circuits and a month," said Coombe. "I've given this one twelve days. The other five have sent a four to the coast in living memory, and you haven't, in eleven years, and the Board thinks you'll drown." She turned to the cage. "So. A pack."

---

She did not explain what a pack was. She opened the cage.

She did it by laying her palm flat on the grey ward and drawing it aside, as one draws back a curtain, and the ward came away from one end of the box in a single heavy fold and hung there from her hand. It did not tremble. Kiva had seen wards held by Torches, and by Doran Mace's whole line at once, and by Anwen at the end of a long wall with her teeth set; she had never seen one held as this was, by one hand, without the least sign that it was being held at all.

The three things came out onto the cinder.

They were husks. Kiva had seen husks since she was thirteen, and these were like all the others and not like any of them. Each was the height of a tall man, a column of grey stuff like a sack of fine ash stood on end, belted at the middle by a knot as thick as a man's arm; and each stood on three thin legs, set wide, like a milking stool. They had no faces. They had a breath, which came out of them all over, as frost comes off a field at dawn, and wherever the breath touched the cinder it went white.

They stood in a row. Then they stamped.

It was all three of them at once. They lifted their legs together and put them down together, *one,* with a dry crunch on the cinder, as men put their feet down when they are marching. Then they leaned. All three leaned together a little to the left, toward the race. *Two.* They came back. *Three.* They stamped again. *Four.*

Then again, from the start.

Kiva had both hands on the oak rail. She took the right one off, and the left lay there alone, with the first two fingers flat on the worn wood, and the iron of the rail's bolt under them.

*Something's pushing. And not. And pushing.*

She did not believe it. Her heart was going hard, and she knew that it was, and she knew what her fingers did when it went. She lifted them until they barely touched.

*Pushing. And not.*

It was slow. It was not her pulse, which was quick. It might have been the husks. It might have been the cart, or the race, or the old mast, or the bank of lamp-cells in the Measure Hall's cellar, sixty yards off through the ground. A needle with no scale said that something was keeping time, and said nothing about what.

"Who'll go first?" said Coombe.

Nobody moved. Then Perrin did.

"Seven," he said. He sounded astonished. "We'll go. I'd like it known that I haven't asked anybody."

"You're the captain. You needn't ask anybody." The Marshal stepped back toward the cage. "Kill one. That's all I want. One husk, dead, on that cinder, and your catcher's cloth on it. Your doors, your book, your ash. The floor is mine and the yield is the Board's. Go."

They went down the steps.

Kiva had been on that cinder before and she knew where she wanted to be, and she went there: east of the husks, eight paces back, on ground that she tested under her heel through her door before she put her weight on it, with her cloth over her shoulder and her left hand in her coat. Anwen went to the south, between the husks and the race, and brought her wall up low across the cinder, a pace long and then two and then four, silver, steady, her hands down by her hips. Perrin went to the middle, where he could see everything and be in the way of nothing. Jude went forward.

He walked toward the near husk with his long arms loose and his head on one side, as he always walked toward a thing that wanted killing; and Kiva watched him come into the frost that the three of them breathed, and saw his boots go white at the toes.

*One.* They stamped. *Two.* They leaned. *Three.* They came back.

"*Now,*" said Kiva.

Jude cut.

It was a good cut. It came down out of his shoulder, heavy and straight, and went in at the near husk's knot from above. Kiva saw it go through, and the knot part like rope under an axe, and the top half of the grey column begin to slide.

*Four.*

The other two stamped.

And the near husk did not fall. The cut closed. It closed from the bottom up, as water closes behind an oar, and the knot was whole again; and the two husks beside it had leaned in toward it on the stamp, one from each side, close enough to touch, so that for the length of a breath there were not three things standing on that cinder but one, broad, with nine legs.

Then the three of them stepped forward together onto Jude.

He went back. He was quick for his size and he went back fast, but the left-hand husk had leaned its breath over his shin as he turned, and Kiva saw the frost go up his boot to the knee like a stocking being pulled on.

"*Mine!*" said Anwen. The wall swung. It came round from the race side in a long low arc, as she had learned to walk it on Mace's line, and stood across the cinder between Jude and the three husks; and the husks put their breath against it, all together, *one,* and leaned, *two,* and her teeth showed.

Perrin had Jude by the belt. He did not ask. He took hold and pulled, as he would have pulled a man off a tram line, and the two of them went back across the cinder in a tangle and fetched up against the steps.

"Dark," said Coombe. "Out."

Anwen let the wall down a pace at a time, backing, and came out of it with her hands shaking. The husks did not follow. They went back to the middle of the floor, in their row, and stood, and stamped, and leaned. *One. Two. Three. Four.* It was as though nothing had happened.

"Talley," said the Marshal. "Your leg."

"It's cold." Jude was sitting on the bottom step with his boot out in front of him. The leather had gone white and stiff and stood away from his shin. "I can feel my toes. I'd say that's good."

"It's good. It's a burn, not a taking. You'll limp till Thursday." Coombe had not come any nearer. She had not lit anything, and she had not moved since she stepped back toward the cage, and Kiva understood that she would have moved if it had been needed, and that she had judged to a hair that it was not. "What did they do?"

---

Nobody answered her. She waited.

It was a long wait. The husks stamped twice in it.

"It healed," said Jude at last. "The cut. It went through and it healed."

"That's what it did. I asked what *they* did."

"They closed up on it," said Anwen. "The other two. Before it could fall."

"Closer."

Kiva had taken her left hand out of her coat. She had come back to the foot of the rail, and she laid the hand flat on the iron upright of the steps, light, the two dead fingers on the cold metal and the other eight held off it.

*Pushing. And not. And pushing.*

It was slower than her heart. She was nearly sure.

"They keep time," she said. "All three. Together. I can't say what time. I've two fingers that tell me a thing's keeping it and never what." She heard how small that sounded, on a cinder floor in front of a Blaze, and she said the rest anyway. "Jude cut on my word. My word was on the stamp. I said *now* when the near one stood still, since it stood still on the stamp. So he cut in time with them."

"And?"

"And the other two were there. On the same count. They're always there on the count." She stopped. "I don't know that. It's what I saw."

"It's what you saw. I'll take what you saw." Coombe tied a knot. "And whose is the count?"

Kiva had the answer to that. She had had it on a list since the ninth month, and she had said it on a pavement on the Brickfields road with a warden listening.

"Not mine," she said. "Somebody's who can count."

"Then who?"

The rail was very quiet. It was quiet in the particular way that a classroom goes quiet when a question has been put that has an answer, and nobody wishes to be the one who gives it, in case it is wrong.

"I was a clock," said Perrin.

He said it in a voice of deep injury. He was standing at the foot of the steps with Jude's belt still in one hand, and he was looking at the three husks with the expression of a man who has been asked to sing.

"On a Saturday. In the tenth month. With a turnip on my knee. I called sixty for every bag in the Low Lane, aloud, and I said at the time that I minded, and I've minded since." He swallowed. "One. Two. Three. Four. They stamp on one. They lean on two. They're back on three. There's a stamp on four that isn't as heavy as the one on one. I'd want somebody to check that."

"Count it aloud," said Coombe.

"*One.*" He did. "*Two. Three. Four.*" His voice was too loud, as the warden's had been at the drum, and it bounced back off the lattice. "*One. Two.*"

"And between?"

"There's nothing between."

"There's always something between. Say *and.*"

"*One, and. Two, and.*" Perrin stopped, and went on. "*Three, and. Four, and.* It's nursery. My little sister did this with a skipping rope."

"Your little sister had the right of it." The Marshal turned her pale eyes on the rail. "Halden. Your blade. You'll cut the far one on your catcher's word, as you would. Old Four, the same, after them. I want to see what a good four does with it when it hasn't been told anything."

Halden went down the steps in black and gold.

Kiva watched them as she had watched a laundress, with her hands still and her head still, because it was a thing worth watching. They were very good. Hesketh had her wall up before her boots were on the cinder, and Pettit was in the middle, and Yarrow was east with his cloth over his shoulder, almost where Kiva had stood; and Kellan went up the north side on his struts in two long low bounds, dark, and stood behind the far husk with his edge unlit, and looked over his shoulder.

"*Now?*"

He said it to Yarrow, and Kiva's stomach did a thing that she had not expected and did not care for.

"*Now,*" said Yarrow.

The edge came blue-white down Kellan's fist. It went in at the far husk's knot, level, exact, the best cut that Kiva had seen on that cinder in her life; and it went in on the stamp, since the stamp was when the far husk stood still. The knot closed behind it. The three leaned in. Kellan was gone before they stepped, back along the north side in one bound, dark, untouched, and he landed behind a post of the lattice and stood there with his hand down.

"Out," said Coombe.

Old Four went after, and Gower cut on Brask's count, and Brask's count was *four.* Brask had seen it. Kiva watched him see it from the rail, with his notebook in his hand, at the second stamp; and he chose the light stamp, the fourth, where Perrin had said it was lighter. It was a good choice. It made no difference. The knot closed. The pack leaned. Gower went back with a white sleeve.

"Out." The Marshal had tied three knots in the time. "That's four good fours and three cuts and one burned shin, and that pack's had its breakfast. Seven. Your Kill can stand?"

"I can stand," said Jude.

"Then stand. You'll cut on your captain's *and.* Not on the count. On the *and.* After the stamp, before the lean. Cade, you'll count it aloud from the steps, as you did. Talley cuts on the word that isn't a number." Coombe looked at Kiva for the first time since the husks had come out. "Rowan. Your cloth."

Kiva went back to her tested ground.

She stood on it with the cloth on her shoulder and nothing open, and she saw Jude walk out again into the frost with his white boot and his head on one side, slower than before, favoring the left leg. She heard Perrin begin.

"*One, and. Two, and. Three, and.*"

Jude was there. He was a pace from the near husk, in the breath, with his arm up.

"*Four, and—*"

He cut.

It came down a half count after the stamp, when the near husk had set its three feet and the other two had not yet leaned. It went through the knot. And the near husk fell.

It fell as a sack falls off a cart: the top half one way, and the bottom half the other, and then neither, as the whole grey column went to a heap on the cinder with a sound like sand poured on a floor.

The other two stopped.

Kiva saw it. They did not stamp. They stood on their six legs, on either side of the heap, quite still, and it was exactly like watching two men in a file when a third has missed his step: the jerk, and the stumble, and the instant in which nobody knows which foot is meant to be down.

"*Dark! One!*"

That was her. She had thrown before she had said it. The cloth went out flat across the heap at the first cast and lay there, and she was on her knees at the hem with her right hand on the canvas and nothing open, and the ash under the cloth was a Handful, and it was lying where it had fallen.

"Finch," said Coombe. "Down. Cord."

Pru came down the steps so fast that she nearly went over. She was at the neck before Kiva had called the corners, and Kiva called them, *corners,* and Perrin had left his count to haul the north one and Anwen the south, and the neck came up, and Pru's hands went in.

*One. Two. Three.* The little finger came across. Nothing slackened.

"Sealed," said Pru. "Forty-nine."

Then the two husks found their count again.

Kiva felt it before she saw it. It was through the canvas, through the cinder under her knee, slow, *pushing, and not;* and then they stamped together, both of them, *one,* and leaned, *two,* and began to come.

They came for Jude. He was still nearest, and he was standing a pace from the bag with his cut spent and his arm down, and his left leg would not have carried him away at a run if he had begged it. They stepped onto the cinder where the heap had been, over the sealed bag, one from each side, and their breath went over the canvas and Pru's hands, and Pru snatched her hands back with a noise.

"*Count!*" said Jude. "Cade! I've lost it!"

Perrin was on the north corner of the cloth, on his knees, with the canvas in his fist. He had stopped counting when he hauled. He opened his mouth, and Kiva saw him try to find the beat again, and saw that he could not; he had dropped it on the floor of the Pit with the corner, and it had rolled away.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen.

The wall came down between Jude and the two husks like a shutter. She had not walked it this time. She had put it up where she stood, at the end of her own arms, all at once, and it stood there with the two husks leaning on it, and her heels went back an inch in the cinder, and another.

"Dark," said Coombe. "Out. All of you. Leave the bag."

They went up the steps backward, the five of them, with Anwen's wall coming down a pace at a time in front of them until it was a hand's width high and then nothing, and the two husks stood in the middle of the floor over a sealed bag and stamped.

---

"One," said Coombe, at the rail. "Out of three."

She said it without any expression. She had not lit, and she had not moved, and she had a rope with a great many knots in it.

"It fell," said Jude. He was sitting on the top step now, with Aske's colleague from the Wren House kneeling at his boot. "It fell, ma'am. On the *and.*"

"It fell. I saw it fall." The Marshal turned and looked at him. "And then you stood there over it, with your cut spent and your captain on his knees, and the other two had their count back in four beats, and they'd have had you by the knees in six if Pryce had been half a count slower. You didn't nearly have it. Don't tell me you nearly had it. You had one of three, and a man down, and you'd have had a man taken on any ground of mine with a tide coming in."

Jude did not argue. He looked at his white boot.

"That's what a pack is," said Coombe, to the rail. "On the Longshore they come in fives. Cut one in time and the others close it. Cut one out of time and you've broken it, and you've three counts, perhaps four, while they find their feet. In those three counts you've to cut the next. Out of time again. And the count they find, when they find it, isn't always the one they had." She tied a knot. "Who had the count when the first one fell?"

"Me," said Perrin. "And then I hadn't."

"Why?"

"I was hauling a corner."

"Then you'd two things to do and one mouth. That's not a fault in your mouth. That's a fault in your four." She did not soften it. "A pack keeps a count among itself. You'll have to keep one among yourselves, and somebody will have to be kept free to keep it. Think about who. Don't tell me now. I'll know on Thursday whether you've thought."

She went down the steps, alone, and walked across the cinder between the two stamping husks as though they were cows in a lane, and picked up the sealed bag by its neck, and walked back. Neither husk turned its head. Kiva did not breathe until the Marshal was on the bottom step again.

"Board's yield," said Coombe, and gave the bag to the Wren House man, who put a tag on it. She looked down at the cart, and at the grey ward lying folded over its open end, and closed the ward again with one hand, as one draws a curtain, with the two husks inside; Kiva never saw them go in. "That's not your floor, Seven. That was mine. Your floor's the twelfth, at a dram a head, and you'll have one, and I'll mark it. Before then I want three things, and I'll be at your yard to see them, and I'll not be told about any of them. I'll be shown."

"Three, ma'am?" said Perrin.

"A pack. A sheet." Coombe tied the last knot in that length of rope, and coiled it, and put it in her coat. "And a door."

She went up the steps to the gallery. Halfway up she stopped, and said over her shoulder, to nobody in particular, "Rowan. Your fingers said they kept time."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Were they right?"

"I don't know," said Kiva. "I'd have to ask somebody with a whole hand."

"Then you'd better find one before the twelfth," said Coombe, and went on.

Jude read the book out that evening at the Rowan kitchen table, standing by the dresser, where there had never been a chair for him and now never would be, because he had decided that he liked it there.

"*Monday the 2nd. The Pit, the Marshal's floor, not ours. A pack of three, Board's cage. Talley, two cuts, 0.09. Pryce, a wall, twice, 0.14. Cade, a stance and a haul, 0.05. Rowan, nothing; dark; a catch. Finch, nothing; a cord. Spent, 0.28. One husk killed, out of time. Yield, the Board's.*" He looked up. "*Held, nine and five hundredths.* That adds."

"You're saying it for Kellan," said Anwen.

"I'm saying it since somebody has to. I've found I like the sound of it. I'd no notion." He scratched his jaw and then winced, because he had moved his leg. "And *burned, Talley, the left shin, a husk's breath, to the knee; not taken.* I've written that too. I'd want it in."

Mara was at the far end of the room, at her half of the oilcloth, with Saltmere's tide table spread under the lamp like a railway guide. She had a pencil behind her ear and another in her hand, and she had not spoken since they came in. Perrin sat on the stool with his head in his hands. Pru was on the floor by the stove with her back against the dresser's end, and she had a hank of cord round her wrist and was not making turns on it.

"I was a clock," said Perrin, through his fingers. "I said it was nursery. I said it to a Blaze."

"She didn't seem to mind," said Kiva.

"She minded. I could see her not minding." He took his head out of his hands. "I was good at it for nine beats. Then Pryce said *corners,* and I've been a corner-hand since the fifth month, and my hands went to the cloth before I'd told them. I'd the count in my mouth and the canvas in my fist and when I looked up it had gone. I couldn't find the one. Do you know what that's like? You've had the one for nine beats and then it's just three grey things standing on a floor, and you're trying to remember which foot they had down."

"Yes," said Kiva.

He looked at her. He had forgotten, she saw, and then he remembered.

"Yes," he said. "I suppose that you do."

She had the Works paper out. There were only four sheets of it left, and she had ruled one in seven on the tram, standing, against the window. She put the first column in.

*A pack. Three husks, in the Board's cage, in the Pit. Taken on the Longshore.*

*What: three things that keep one count among themselves. They stamp on one, lean on two, come back on three, stamp light on four. When one is cut on the count, the other two lean in on the same count and it closes. When one is cut between counts, it falls, and the other two lose their count for three or four beats.*

"Is that right?" she said. "Perrin. Read it."

He read it over her shoulder, breathing on her ear.

"*Stamp light on four,*" he said. "You've put that."

"You said it."

"I said I'd want somebody to check it."

"Then I'll write that you said so." She did. *C.: the fourth is lighter. Not checked.*

*How:* She stopped.

She knew how. It was the thing that Coombe had made them do: a count aloud, and a cut on the *and,* and somebody kept free to keep the count. She could write that. She could not write who.

"Who keeps it?" she said.

Nobody answered. It was the Pit again, the same quiet, with a question in it that had an answer.

"It's not me," she said. "I'll say that first so that it's said. I can't count a thing that keeps time; I've two fingers that tell me it's there, and on a floor they'll tell me about my own heart as soon as anything else. And I'm on the cloth. A catcher's on her knees at a hem with her hands full and her eyes on a heap; you all saw where my eyes were when the first one fell." She looked at the sheet. "It can't be Jude. He's cutting. He has to be looking at a knot, not counting feet."

"It can't be me," said Anwen. "I'm in a wall. I see the wall."

"Then it's me," said Perrin. He said it quite flatly, as though a doctor had told him. "It's the carry. A carry's got nothing to carry until there's a thing down. I'm the only one on that floor who's got nothing to do until somebody's hurt."

"You hauled a corner."

"I hauled a corner since I've always hauled a corner. It's habit. It's a good habit; you can't get a neck up with fewer than two." He scratched the back of his neck. "So somebody else hauls it. Finch, if she's on the floor. Pryce, if her wall's down. Anybody. And I stand at the side like a man at a football match and say *one and two and.*"

"And if somebody's hurt?" said Jude.

"Then I've two things to do and one mouth, and I'll have to choose." Perrin looked down at his own big hands. "She said that. *That's not a fault in your mouth.* I'd like it known that I didn't understand it when she said it, and I do now, and I don't like it any better."

Kiva wrote, under *How: The count is the captain's. He hauls nothing. If he has to haul, he says so first, and somebody else takes the count, aloud, by name.*

"Takes it from where?" said Pru.

It was the first thing that she had said since they sat down. She was looking at the hank of cord on her wrist.

"From the floor?" said Kiva.

"From *him.* If Cade's counting and he has to go, and Pryce takes it. Does she take it from him or from the pack? Because those aren't the same. If he says *four* and she says *one,* she's on his count. But if he's lost it—" Pru went pink, and kept going. "If he's lost it, then she's taking a wrong count off him. And the pack doesn't care what we say."

"She'd take it off the pack," said Anwen slowly. "She'd have to look at their feet."

"Then she ought to be looking at their feet already," said Pru. "Before he hands it over. Not after."

The kitchen was quiet.

Kiva looked at the sheet. She had *mine* before *yours* on a sheet of her own, in this same hand, from a cobbled yard and a litter on a wall. *They take it. Then you let go.* And here it was again, coming out of the mouth of a first-year by a stove, about a thing that was not a litter and not a mark, but a count.

It was the same thing. She had not seen that it would be.

She wrote it under *Second try,* since that was where it went. *Whoever takes the count is counting before it's given. Then it's said: "yours." Then "mine." P.F.*

"That's yours," she said. "I've put your initials."

"I didn't want them."

"I know. They're there."

Pru looked at the sheet upside down from the floor. She did not say thank you. She did something rather better, in Kiva's estimation: she began making turns on the cord again, quickly, without looking, as she did when her hands were content.

Mara had put her pencil down.

"Your fingers," she said, from the far end of the table. "In the Pit. You told a Blaze they said that the things kept time."

"Yes."

"And she asked you whether they were right."

"Yes. I said that I'd have to ask somebody with a whole hand."

"Did you?"

"I asked Perrin," said Kiva. "He counted it. He said four, and a light stamp."

"Then you asked the right person." Her aunt picked up the pencil again, and looked at the tide table, and did not write anything. "Your needle has no scale on it. His has. Put the two together and you've an instrument." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "I've been trying to make one out of a fitter's watch and a table of the sea for a month. It's a comfort to see it done at a kitchen table in an hour."

"It didn't work. It broke one in three."

"It broke one. Before this morning it broke none." Mara bent over the table. "Write down which one. I'd want to know."

Kiva wrote it.

*Went wrong: the count was the captain's, and the captain hauled. Nobody had it when it was wanted most, which was after the first fell. Cost: Talley's shin, burned to the knee; 0.28 on the book; one of three.* She thought about the *Why* column, and the white cold space at the bottom of it, and the faces of three grey things that had none.

*Why they keep a count: not known. Why the cut closes when it is in time: not known.*

She had not expected to fill it. She had found that she was easier with an empty *why* than she used to be. A square that was left white was a square that somebody might fill one day without having to rub anything out first.

She held up her left hand to the lamp before she put the pencil down. It had been warm since noon, as Aske had said it would be, and he had walked down to the Pit to see it and said so, and written it, and gone back up again with Jude's colleague's boot under his arm. The eight fingers shut when she told them. The two stuck out. It was as before.

She wrote that too, on the right, small: *Hand to Ember, one. Cold to the knuckle at seven; warm at noon. Five more wanted. Four can be had here.*

Then, because it was true, and because she had promised herself in the autumn that she would write a thing on the right side of the book when she thought it and not when it was convenient, she wrote one line more.

*My fingers knew it kept time and Perrin knew which. That's two people and one instrument. I've been looking for one person.*

She shut the book and laid it by the brass weight, for her aunt to read.

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-10.md

# Chapter 10 — Kiva: Uphill

There were twelve juniors on the Old Yard at half past six on Tuesday morning, in grey smocks, with their breath going up, and every one of them had been told that she was a husk.

"You stamp on one," said Kiva. "All together. Then you lean, on two, toward the sheds. Back on three. And four's a stamp, but lighter. Like this."

She did it. She did it badly, because her knee was strapped and she had never in her life marched in step with anybody; but they watched her the way juniors watch a second-year, with their mouths a little open, and when she had done it twice they did it after her, and on the third time the twelve of them came down on the frozen dirt together with a crunch like a cart going over gravel.

"That's horrible," said the sandy-voiced one, with great satisfaction. "Do it again."

Her name was Bettony. She had a plait to her waist and a gap in her teeth, and she had sat on the shed step in the autumn and asked what *declared* meant, and she had come up the hill in the dark this morning with eleven others behind her because somebody had told her that the Marshal might come.

"The Marshal's not coming till Thursday," said Kiva.

"Then we'll be good by Thursday."

They were put in three files of four, each file a husk, with a junior at the front to hold the count and three behind to keep it. Kiva gave them the rule that she had worked out on the tram, and that Coombe had not given anybody, because Coombe had not stayed long enough to be asked. *When one of you is cut out of time, that file sits down. The other two files stop dead, and you count to three under your breath, and then you find your count again and come on. When one of you is cut in time, nobody sits down. You lean in, and you go on.*

"How do we know if it's in time?" said Bettony.

"You'll know. You'll be stamping when it comes."

Jude stood at the north end of the lane with a broom handle in his fist for an edge. He was still limping. The Wren House had put a white salve on the burn and bound it, and it had stiffened in the night, and he moved like a man getting out of a cold bath; but he had come up the hill at six without being asked and stood on the frost waiting, and when Kiva asked whether he ought to be on his feet, he said that a Kill who couldn't stand on a Tuesday wasn't going to be much use on the twelfth.

Perrin stood on the shed step with nothing in his hands.

He had been told to do that. He had told himself, on Monday night, aloud, at the kitchen table, and he had come up the hill and stood on the step and put his hands in his pockets, and he looked like a man who has been asked to wait in a corridor while a thing is done to somebody he loves.

"I'd like it known that this is the hardest thing I've ever been asked," he said. "Harder than three-eighty. I'm made to lift things. I'm not made to stand on a step."

"Count," said Anwen.

"I'm counting. *One, and. Two, and.*"

The three files stamped. Jude walked in among them with his broom handle, slow, his head on one side.

"*Three, and. Four, and—*"

He tapped the front junior of the nearest file on the shoulder, on the *and.*

The file sat down. It sat down so suddenly that two of them sat on each other, and somebody shrieked, and the other two files stopped dead with their feet in the air, exactly as they had been told.

*One. Two. Three,* said twenty-four lips, under twelve breaths.

Then the middle file stamped. The right-hand file stamped half a beat after it. And for perhaps four counts there were two husks on that yard that were not keeping time with each other at all, one heavy and one late, stamping and leaning and treading on their own heels; and Kiva, who was on her knees on the north side with a cloth on her shoulder and no ash under it, felt something in her chest go tight and glad.

"There," she said. "Look. They've each found a count, and it isn't the same one."

"Which one do I count?" said Perrin.

Nobody answered him.

He stood on the step with his hands in his pockets, and the two files stamped out of step with each other in front of him, and he looked from one to the other with his lips moving and nothing coming out of them. Then he said, quite loudly, "*One*—" on the middle file's stamp, and then "*One*—" on the right-hand file's, and stopped.

"You can't count two," said Jude, from among them.

"I know I can't count two. I'm finding that out."

"Then pick one."

"Which?"

"The one that's nearest me," said Jude. "I can't cut two either."

It went like that all morning. It went badly, and then it went less badly, and then for a quarter of an hour it went worse than it had gone at the start, because the juniors had learned to change their count when a file sat down, and twelve children were a great deal better at changing a count than three husks had been. By eight Perrin had lost the count four times and found it three. Jude had cut eleven juniors on the *and* and three on the beat, and the three on the beat had leaned in on him with their arms out, cackling, and pretended to freeze his boots. Anwen had stood behind Perrin with her eyes on the files' feet, not on him, counting under her breath, and twice when Perrin lost it she had said *yours* to him, and he had said *mine* back, and she had taken it up without a gap, off the feet.

"She was counting before," said Pru, who had a turnip watch and a slate. "Before he lost it. Like I said."

"You said it on Monday," said Kiva. "It's on a sheet."

"I know where it is. I'm only saying it worked."

Bettony came over at eight, red-faced and limping in the way that the juniors had all begun to limp, out of sympathy with Jude.

"The tall one cuts heavy," she said. "He doesn't mean to. But when he taps you, it's like being leaned on by a door."

"He's a Kill."

"I know. I'm only saying." She looked at Kiva's knees on the frost, and the empty cloth. "Why are you down there? There's nothing to catch."

"There's nothing to catch today. When there is, I'll be here."

Bettony considered that. Kiva watched her decide whether it was a good answer, and then decide that it was an answer, and then go back to her file. It was a thing that she had noticed about juniors: they took one at its word. One could not stand in front of twelve of them and say a thing that was not so; they would do it.

She wrote it down on the shed step, while the juniors went in to breakfast and Perrin sat with his head against the doorpost and his eyes shut.

*Pack drill, with juniors. Went wrong: a broken pack comes back as two counts, not one. The captain can count one. Second try: he counts the one nearest the Kill, and says which, aloud, so that the Kill knows which he's on. A.P. counts behind him off their feet, and takes it by "yours" and "mine."*

"*Says which,*" said Perrin, without opening his eyes. "I'd like it known that I found that one."

"You found it."

"Put my initials."

She put them.

---

Coombe came to the Old Yard on Wednesday at two, and she did not say that she had come.

She was simply on the shed step when Kiva looked round, as Noa was always simply at the end of a notice board, with her grey coat buttoned and a fresh length of tarred rope in her hand. Orla was on her crate. Beside Orla, on the other end of it, in his coat, with his two arms laid along his knees and bound white to the elbow, sat Pike.

He had not been asked. He had come on the Kiln tram and walked up from the corner with Greta Stroud at his elbow, and Stroud had gone back down again.

"I'm not here," he said.

"You said that on Saturday," said Orla.

"It was true on Saturday."

The Marshal looked at the two of them on the crate, and at the bound arms. She did not ask. She tied a knot.

"Show me," she said.

So they showed her the sheet.

It was Mrs. Pryce's double, grey with washing, with its seam down the middle; it had come back from Saturday unharmed and been washed again on Monday as a matter of principle. Dilys had come up the hill with it in a basket, as she had come on Saturday, and stood at the far end with Anwen, a corner each. Gerald hung from his pole across the trestles. There were four sandbags by the south row.

Pru threw the cloth. Jude, limping, slung a sandbag onto the far hem before Gerald dropped, as Pike had said that he would try. Gerald came down in the upper middle of the sheet with his sound of a dropped mattress.

"*Dark! One!*"

Kiva had the left hem. She said nothing. She was looking down the lane at the far edge, where Dilys stood grinning with her corner, and the far edge did not lift, because it had a hundredweight of sand lying across it.

"*Corners,*" said Pru, at her ten.

The neck came up. The cord went round. It was clean.

"Again," said Coombe.

It was clean again. It was clean five times out of five, with the weight first and the cloth after and Kiva's two words never said, because there was never anything to say them about.

"Very nice," said the Marshal.

Kiva did not like the way that she said it.

"Now," said Coombe. "Make it walk."

---

"Walk?" said Perrin.

"It walks. The thing that laid that sheet. It's on its feet." Coombe had not moved off the step. "It laid its skin down behind it on a wharf because it was standing on a wharf, and it stood still because there was a gate in front of it that it liked. On the Longshore there's nothing to like. They walk, and the skin comes after, as a cloak comes after a man. Show me what your sacks do when the thing walks."

They looked at one another.

"Talley, Cade," said Coombe. "You're the string. Take your hundredweight on its pole between you. Tie that sheet's top hem to your belts with a yard of cord, so that it trails behind you on the ground. Walk up the yard. Somebody cuts your rung when I say. Rowan's on the far edge. Go."

It took them five minutes to rig. Jude and Perrin stood shoulder to shoulder with Gerald's pole across both their necks, like a yoke, and the sheet tied to their belts behind; and when they walked, the sheet came after them up the frozen dirt with a soft dry hiss, and the four sandbags that Jude had slung onto its far hem came with it, dragged, bumping, like a sledge.

"Cut," said Coombe.

Pru let Gerald go. He came down behind them, on the sheet, as a rung comes down at a string's heels.

"*Dark! One!*"

And the string kept walking.

It did not mean to. Perrin and Jude had been told to walk, and nobody had told them to stop, and so they went on, two paces, three, with the sheet tied to their belts; and the sheet went with them, and Gerald on it, and the sandbags along its far edge; and the whole lot slid up the yard toward the sheds, away from Pru's hands, away from Kiva's knee, like a tablecloth pulled off a table with the supper still on it.

"*Far edge!*" said Kiva. It came out of her before she knew. "It's going!"

It was. It was all going. The far edge had a hundredweight of sand along it and it did not lift, and it did not fold, and it did not need to; it simply went, with everything on it, away from everybody who had meant to catch it.

"Stop," said Coombe.

Perrin and Jude stopped. Perrin looked over his shoulder at the sheet, a yard behind his heels, with Gerald lying on it and four sandbags at its far end and nobody's hands within three paces of any of it.

"Oh," he said.

"That's a rung gone," said Coombe. "On a moving thing, with your weight on its skin, you've pinned the skin, and the skin's tied to the thing, and the thing's walking. So your weight goes with it." She tied a knot. "On the Longshore we lost a cloth that way the first season. Then we lost a man's hand, the second, trying to get it back. It's the same lesson that your catcher learned on a wharf. A thing that folds from the far edge doesn't need to fold if it can walk away with the whole cloth."

Nobody said anything.

Kiva was looking at the yard. She had been looking at it for some time, while the others looked at the sheet.

The Old Yard was not flat. Nothing in Meridian was flat; the city was built on a hill, and every yard in it fell somewhere. This one fell toward the bottom wall. She had known it since the spring. She had stood on it a hundred times with her still door open, and she had felt it through the soles of her feet as one feels which way a ship is listing; a hand in ten paces, perhaps, down from the sheds to the wall, and the water that ran off it in a thaw ran down the lane and out under the wall through a drain the size of a fist.

On the wharf the ground had fallen south, a hand in two paces, to the river. Pike had read it with his heel. *If a rung runs here, it runs to the river.* And the white skin had lain downhill of the thing's legs, on the river side, where the rung would run.

"Ma'am," she said.

"Rowan."

"Which way does the rung go? When it's cut. On a wharf, it ran downhill. On the cloth. Since the skin was downhill."

"The rung goes where rungs go," said Coombe. "Down, and then to the low side, while it's open. Your catcher said so. He's written it; it's in your columns."

"Then the skin's only under the rung if it's downhill of the thing."

"Yes."

"And it's downhill if the thing's facing uphill. Since it trails behind."

"Yes."

"So if the thing were facing downhill—" Kiva heard her voice go up, and brought it down again. "If it were facing downhill, the skin would be behind it, uphill. And the rung would come down at its heels and run the other way. Away from the skin. Onto ground."

The Marshal did not say *yes.* She did not say anything. She looked at Kiva for a long moment with her pale steady eyes, and then she turned them on Pike.

Pike was sitting on the crate with his bound arms on his knees, looking at the frozen yard. He was looking at it as Kiva had seen him look at a road, before he threw a cloth. His square grey face had gone very still.

"Make it carry its own sheet uphill," said Coombe. "That's what we say on the Longshore. We've said it for three seasons. Nobody's written it down, and the Board's printer has a page of mine on it that it won't print until the Measure's over, in case it's wrong." She tied a knot. "How do you make a thing face downhill when it wants to face something else?"

"You give it something to face," said Anwen.

She was at the far end, with her corner. She had let it go. Kiva had not seen her let it go.

"A gate," said Anwen. "It liked the gate on the wharf. It walked up to it and leaned. So you put the gate where you want it to face, and it walks to it, and it faces the way it walked."

"And if it's already facing the wrong way? Already walking? With its skin laid down?"

"Then you move the gate." Anwen had gone a little pale. "You walk it. Round. While it's leaning on it. It follows the wall. And when it's facing downhill, you stop, and it stops, and its skin's behind it, uphill."

"Who calls that?"

Anwen opened her mouth, and looked at Kiva, as she had looked at Kiva for every call since the spring.

Then she shut it.

"I'd want to," she said. "I'd want to call it myself. I'm in the wall. I can feel where it's leaning. I'll know before anybody whether it's following or not." Her voice was quite level and very quick, the way it went when a wall had a thin place in it. "Rowan reads the fall. She says *downhill's south,* or whichever. Then it's mine. I walk it round and I say *stopped* when it's facing, and nobody cuts until I say."

"Then do it," said Coombe.

It took them the rest of the afternoon, and it cost Anwen two-tenths and more off the book.

They rigged it again. Jude and Perrin were the string, with Gerald on his yoke and the sheet on its cords behind them; and this time Anwen brought a wall up in front of them, waist high, silver, across the lane at the shed end, and they walked up to it, as they had been told that a string would, and leaned their yoke on it.

Kiva opened her still door on the lane under her right heel, wide, by its corners, and felt the yard go down away from her toward the bottom wall, as plain as a stair.

"South-west," she said. "A hand in ten. The bottom wall's low."

"South-west," said Anwen. "Mine."

She began to walk.

Kiva had seen her walk a wall before. She had seen her come down the length of Mace's line on a night in the autumn, step by step, with the silver going along in front of her like a curtain on a rail. This was not that. This was turning a thing that was leaning on the curtain, with its whole weight, and wanted to stay where it was.

The wall moved a foot. The yoke came with it. Perrin and Jude shuffled, as they had been told to shuffle, a foot, with their heads down, following the light.

Then Anwen's wall moved another foot, and Jude, who had been told to be a string and not a student, leaned the other way.

The wall bent. It did not break. It bowed in the middle like a sail, and Anwen's heels went back in the frost, and her face went white and set.

"*He's leaning off,*" she said. "Talley. Off."

"I'm a string," said Jude. "I've decided I don't like being turned."

"Then I'll turn you anyway." She took a step, and the wall went with her, and the yoke came round another foot. "Rowan. Where's south-west?"

"Half round. Another quarter."

"*Mine.*"

It took a long time. It took, by Pru's turnip, two minutes and twenty seconds to walk a lazy string through half a circle on a frozen yard, and at the end of it Anwen was standing at the bottom end of the lane with her hands shaking and her wall in front of her, and Jude and Perrin were leaning their yoke on it, facing the bottom wall, and the sheet lay behind them on the frost on its cords, uphill, toward the sheds.

"*Stopped,*" said Anwen.

"Cut," said Coombe.

Pru let Gerald go.

He came down behind the string's heels, on the sheet's near edge, and lay there; and Kiva, who was kneeling a pace downhill with her own cloth, thought for one sick moment that it had not worked. Then Pru, who had been told to make the rung *run* as an open rung runs, put her boot to him and rolled him off the near edge of the sheet and down the fall of the yard, a yard, two yards, to the low side, onto bare frozen dirt.

Kiva's cloth went over him at the first cast.

"*Dark! One!*"

There was nothing under him but ground. There was nothing behind her to fold, and nothing at the far edge of her cloth but the bottom of the Old Yard, and she knelt on the hem with her two dead fingers lying on the canvas and called the corners at her ten, as a catcher does on a quiet night.

"*Corners.*"

Pru tied. It was clean.

"Again," said Coombe.

They did it again. They did it five times in all, and it went wrong twice. Once Anwen's wall bowed so far that it broke in the middle and she had to bring it up again from nothing, and the string walked off south in the meantime, dragging its sheet, and Kiva had to say *far edge* and Coombe said *stop.* Once the turn went right and the cut was early, before Anwen had said *stopped,* because Pru's hand on the hoist had slipped; and the rung came down half on the sheet and half off, and lay across the near edge with its top on the skin, and Kiva looked at it and could not tell which way it would go.

"*Far edge,*" she said. "I don't know. It's on the hem."

"Then you don't throw," said Coombe. "You wait. You let it run, and you throw where it runs to. If it doesn't run, if it lies on the skin, you put your weight on the far edge and pray. Weight's not a mistake. Weight's what you do when the turn's failed." She tied a knot. "Your catcher's notion was a good one. It's the second line. It isn't the first."

On the fifth, it went as it had gone on the first.

Jude and Perrin unyoked themselves, and sat down on the frost, and Perrin put his hand to his ribs and said that they were telling him things. Dilys came down the yard with her corner of the sheet bunched up in her arms and said that she had never seen a bedsheet do so much and that she'd not dare tell her mistress. Anwen stood at the bottom of the lane where her wall had been, with nothing up, looking at her own hands.

"*Cost,*" said Jude, from the frost. He had the book open on his knee. "Pryce. Three turns and two halves. I'd call it two-tenths."

"Two and four," said Anwen. "I'll not be rounded."

"Two and four." He wrote. "*Held, eight and eighty-one.*"

Coombe came down off the step at last.

She did not go to Anwen, or to Kiva, or to the sheet. She walked along the south row, with her rope in her hand, to the crate where Orla and Pike were sitting, and stood in front of Pike.

"You put your heel on it," she said.

"I did."

"I had a man on the Longshore did the same, three seasons gone. Twenty years a catcher, and a good one. He called it ground and laid on it." The Marshal's voice did not change. "He's a lock-keeper on the canal at Hollins now. He keeps a very tidy lock."

Pike looked at his arms.

"Was there a page," he said, "in your book, on it? When you had him?"

"There was a line. *A hem that lies down is ground.* The same line as yours. It was the right line for forty years." Coombe tied a knot. "I've written a page. The printer has it. It says to make the thing carry its sheet uphill, and if that fails, to put dead weight on the far edge. It says the weight was Graywater's, by name, at a wharf below the Hythe. I had that off your Torch's slate. I don't steal."

"The weight was a laundress's."

"Then I'll have the printer put a laundress." For the first time Kiva saw something move in that weathered face, at one corner, and go again. "And the far edge?"

"The second has it," said Pike. "Two words, once. My Torch wrote that on a slate at four in the morning, and I've thought since that it was the best thing she ever wrote."

"Whose second?"

Pike nodded down the yard at Kiva, without turning his head.

Coombe looked. She looked for some time.

"I've never heard it put so," she said at last. "I've stood on the Longshore for thirty years and never heard anybody put it so. We give the far edge to the corner-hand on that side. He's got a corner in his fist and he's hauling, and he hasn't the time to look." She turned back to Pike. "I'll put that in too."

"Put her name."

"I'll put the second's. It's a place, not a person. A place is what the printer wants." The Marshal looked at the two white bound arms. "How long?"

"Six weeks. Four to go."

"And you came up a hill on a tram to sit on a box and watch somebody else's four turn a bedsheet."

"I'm not here," said Pike.

"No," said Coombe. "I can see that you're not."

She went back up the yard. On the shed step she stopped, as she had stopped on the Pit's stair, and said over her shoulder, "Pryce."

Anwen's head came up.

"That was yours. You called it, and you said it before anybody asked. On the Longshore the wall leads in a turn, and the catcher reads the ground, and the blade waits. I'd have told you that on Thursday if you hadn't found it." She tied a knot. "You found it. I'll not tell you."

Anwen Pryce went down to the Graywater hall yard that night at eight, alone, with her coat buttoned to the neck and her hands in her sleeves, and she did not tell anybody that she was going.

The hall yard was lit under its one hooded lamp. Doran Mace had his line out on the cinders, as he had most nights that the van was not called: six wards in slate blue, Tibb and Stiles and Greta Stroud and three more, walking a wall along the length of the yard and back, slow, as a crew rows when the boat is going nowhere. She had walked forty paces of that wall in the first month. She knew how it went. She had not come for that.

"Pryce," said Mace. He did not stop the line. "It's Wednesday."

"I know what day it is."

"You've a place for the summer. It's the summer you've a place for. It's the second month."

"I want to learn to turn it," said Anwen.

He looked at her then.

He was a big slow man with a board under his arm, and he had been the ward-chief on that van for longer than she had been alive, and in the autumn he had left her name where it was on his line, with a note beside it that said she had said so as she went. She had never asked him for anything. She had told him things. She had found, since the spring, that it was easier to tell people things than to ask them, and she had been thinking about that on the tram.

"Turn it," he said.

"A wall. With a thing leaning on it. Round, so that it faces where I want." She kept her hands in her sleeves. "I did it today, on a yard, with two boys and a sandbag. It took me two minutes and twenty seconds to get half round, and the wall bowed, and once it broke. A string's heavier than Talley. It's heavier than Talley and Cade together."

"It is."

"On the coast they say the wall leads in a turn. The Marshal said so."

"Did she." Mace said it without any expression. "I'd heard she was in the city."

"She was in our yard."

"Then you've had better teaching this afternoon than I could give you in a month, and you've come down here at eight at night to ask me for more." He held up his hand, and the line stopped, and six wards stood along the cinders with their wall up and looked at the two of them with frank interest. "Why?"

Anwen had known that he would ask. She had decided on the tram that she would answer the true thing and not the tidy one, because she had found that she minded when other people gave her the tidy one.

"Because I didn't know I'd want to call it until I opened my mouth," she said. "I've been in a wall since I was fourteen. A wall stands where it's told. In the spring it was told by Rowan, and before that by the Hold-master, and in the summer by you. I'm good at it. I've the best Hold in the Third Division, and I've stood where I was put for three years and been proud of it." Her voice was quite level. "Today the Marshal asked who'd call the turn, and I looked at Rowan, as I've looked at Rowan for every call since the fifth month. And then I didn't. I wanted it. I wanted to be the one who said *stopped.*"

"And now you've said it, you're frightened you'll be wrong."

"No," said Anwen. "Now I've said it, I'm frightened I'll be slow."

Mace was quiet for a moment. Then he said, without turning his head, "Stroud. You've the end."

Greta Stroud came off the line with her hands still up, and the end of the wall went with her, silver, a yard of it, bending at the corner where it left the others.

"Bring it here," said Mace. "Pryce. Put yours against it. Lean."

Anwen brought her wall up.

It came at her hips, low, as it always came, and she put it against the end of Stroud's and leaned, and Stroud leaned back. It was like leaning on a door with somebody behind it.

"Now turn her," said Mace.

She tried. She took a step to the left and the wall went with her, and Stroud's end bent and slid along it, and stopped. Stroud was a Graywater ward of fifteen years and a woman who had held the end of a line on a coal wharf with a string's whole weight against it. She did not mean to be turned.

"You're walking," said Mace. "That's your fault. You think a wall goes where your feet go. It doesn't. It goes where your hands go, and your feet catch up." He came and stood behind her, not touching. "Your hands. Not your feet. Turn the wall from the far end first. The end that's away from you. Bring that round, and the near end will come of itself. A wall's a sheet, Pryce. It's a long one, and it's stiff, and it's standing up, but it's a sheet."

Anwen stood very still.

"Fold it from the far edge," she said.

"I've no notion what you're talking about. I'm telling you how a wall turns." Mace had his board under his arm. "Try."

She tried. She kept her feet where they were. She brought the far end of her wall round first, a hand, and then another, as one brings the far corner of a sheet over the top on a laundry table, with her arms wide; and the near end of the wall pivoted on its own foot, at her hip, and came round after, and Stroud's wall came with it, sliding, and Stroud said *oh* under her breath, and was turned.

"Again," said Mace.

She did it again. She did it four times, and the fourth time she did not look at her feet at all, and she brought the far end round and said *stopped* before she knew that she was going to, and Stroud's wall stopped on the word, as though it had been told.

"That's the trick," said Mace. "You'll not have it on a string by Thursday week. You'll have it by the summer, if you come down here on nights that aren't yours and annoy my line." He held up his hand, and the line began to walk again behind him. "Pryce."

"Yes."

"You wanted it. You said so. I'd have you say it to Rowan as well, before you say *stopped* on a floor in front of a Blaze. She's your catcher. She's used to having the call."

"I know she is," said Anwen. "That's why I'll say it."

She went up the hill on the last tram with her arms aching to the shoulder and her coat open, because she was warm. She was thinking about her mother's hip against a table, and a far corner brought over the top, and the queer comfort of having been told by a big slow man with a board that the thing she had been proudest of for three years was a sheet.

She had not known that a wall could lead. She had supposed, without ever saying it to herself, that a wall was the thing that stood in front of the people who did.

She thought that she might tell Kiva on Thursday, before the yard. She found that she did not want to wait until Thursday. She got off at Coper Street instead of going on, and went up the shop stair, and knocked.

# Chapter 11 — Kiva: A Stack with Four Legs

Coombe watched the pack drill on Thursday morning from the shed step for the whole of an hour, and in that hour she said one word, four times.

"Again."

Bettony's twelve stamped. Perrin stood on the step below the Marshal with his hands in his pockets and counted aloud, and when Jude's broom handle tapped a junior on the *and* and a file sat down, Perrin said "*Nearest,*" and named the nearer of the two counts that were left, and counted it; and Anwen, behind him, with her eyes on the files' feet, counted the other under her breath, so that when Perrin lost his she could say *yours,* and he could say *mine,* and nobody was left holding nothing. Jude cut a second junior out of time inside four beats. Twice, the third file never found its count at all before Jude's handle was on its shoulder.

The fourth time, Pru was on the north corner of the empty cloth, hauling, and Anwen on the south, so that the captain had nothing in his hands but a number.

"Who hauls?" said Coombe.

It was the first thing that she had said that was not *again.* Perrin turned round on the step.

"Finch, ma'am. And Pryce, when her wall's down."

"Finch isn't on your floor. Your floor's a four." The Marshal tied a knot. "On the twelfth there'll be four of you on that cinder and I'll mark four. On the Longshore there'll be four. Who hauls?"

Perrin looked at Kiva. Kiva looked back, and did not help him, and saw him see that she was not going to.

"Pryce," he said slowly. "When her wall's down. And Talley, when he's cut. He's nothing to do once a thing's fallen but stand over it, and I've seen what happens when he stands over it." He swallowed. "And if Pryce's wall's up and Talley hasn't cut, then nobody hauls, and Rowan waits on her hem with the cloth on the heap, and the bag's slow. I'd sooner have a slow bag than a lost count."

"Would you."

"Yes, ma'am. I've thought about it. I lost one on Monday."

The Marshal looked at him for a moment with her pale eyes, and then she looked at the files of juniors, red-faced, stamping in the frost.

"Better," she said. "You've thought." She coiled her rope. "Now think about what you do on the Longshore when the count your pack finds isn't one that a child can stamp."

She went down the yard and out by the side gate without saying what she meant, and Perrin sat down on the step where she had been and put his head against the doorpost.

"I'd like it known," he said, "that I've never in my life been so tired from standing still."

The card came at half past nine on Friday night, by a boy on a bicycle, three words long. *Yard. Now. Finch.*

The van went out of the Graywater hall yard at ten to ten with its rings whining and Ruddock on the tiller, and it had one more person on its side bench than it had carried in the first month and one fewer pair of hands.

He was Imrie's catcher, lent, and Kiva had met him on Tuesday in the mending room for a quarter of an hour, which was as long as anybody had seemed to think that a meeting ought to take. He was a lean man of thirty-five or so with very clean hands and a long patient face, and he talked to his cloths. He did it under his breath, as another man might whistle, and he did it now, folding the corner of the left-hand one over his knee and smoothing it. *There you are. There.*

Pike sat beside him on the bench, in his coat, with his two arms laid along his knees in their linen.

The dressings were thinner than they had been. Ruddock had changed them on Wednesday, and the white went only to a hand above each wrist now, and the fingers showed at the ends of it, grey and stiff and swollen, like the fingers of a glove that has been left out in the rain. He could move them a little. He had shown Kiva on Tuesday. "She read them in the mending room with her coat on, standing, as she reads everything. Then she took the pack sheet up the stair with her and didn't give it back." He looked at the floor of the van. "I'd say that's a yes."

Kiva knew the sheds. Everybody in Meridian knew them: two long brick barns with arched roofs of glass and iron, where the trams went in at night to be swept and greased and have their rings looked at, and behind them, in a squat brick house of its own with a chimney, the bank that drove the whole Brickfields line. *The big tram sheds,* said the Halden patent, in a sentence that she had heard read aloud in a yard in the autumn. *Stepped.* Every cell in that squat house had been salted late with a pinch of counting ash in the year that the line was built, and every one of them beat with all the others, and one man in a glass box drove the lot.

There were three things in the road outside the shed doors.

Cut one in time and it closes.* I had that off a second's sheet, who had it off the Marshal on Monday. *The far edge: the second's. To say. "Nairn."

"Three rungs, one a husk. The near one's a hand nearer the doors than the others; it's the leader, if they've a leader. I'd take the far one first, from the levee side, so the others aren't between me and my way out."

"West of the near one," said Varley. "Seam up the fall."

"Then it's three husks, on the ward-chief's count and the catcher's word," said Rhea. "Go."

---

Kiva knelt at Varley's left hem, on the cold setts, a pace and a half down from the corner.

It was not the same as kneeling at Pike's. She had known that it would not be, and it was not, in ways that she had not foreseen. Varley threw the cloth once, and it lay flat at the first cast, but its seam ran a hand off true up the fall, and he knelt and looked at it and left it. He knelt with the gate at his back, as the book said. He put his left knee on the hem, a hand from hers, as Pike had; and then he put his right knee down as well, beside it, so that he was kneeling square on the canvas with both, like a man at prayer, and both his hands were on his thighs.

His cord was in his coat. She saw the bulge of it.

Pike knelt behind them both, off the canvas, on the setts, with his white arms on his knees. He had not been given a place on the slate. He had taken one, at the head of the cloth and a yard back, where he could see the heel of whichever blade was going to cut; and Rhea had looked at him taking it and said nothing.

"*One, and,*" said Mace, from the wall. "*Two, and.*"

He said it at the pitch of a man calling a horse. It went out over the road and the frost and the three grey backs, and Kiva felt every person on that floor fall in with it, as one falls in with a band in the street without meaning to. *Three, and. Four, and.*

Nairn went up the levee side in two long low bounds, dark, on his struts. He landed behind the far husk.

"*Now?*" he said. Not to Varley. To Mace.

"*Two, and—*" said Mace. "*Three—*"

"*And,*" said Varley.

Nairn cut.

It was on the *and.* Kiva saw it go in level at the far husk's knot, a half count after the stamp, before the lean; and the far husk fell. It went down onto the setts in a heap like poured sand, beyond the near husk's legs, a good six paces from Varley's cloth. The other two stopped dead, with their feet up.

"*Dark!*" said Varley. "*One!*"

He did not throw. The heap was not on his cloth. It was on the setts, beyond the near husk, open, and it began, very slowly, to run east down the fall of the road toward the gutter and the shed doors.

"*Rhea,*" said Pike.

He said it from behind them, quite quietly. It was not a call. It was a name.

Rhea Sorn was already moving. She came round the end of Mace's wall on the shed side, walking, as she always walked on a floor, with her blade arm down; and the middle husk, which had stopped dead with its feet up, put them down. It stamped. It was alone. The near husk, beside it, stamped half a beat later, and the two of them were out of step, heavy and late, treading on each other's count, exactly like two files of juniors on a frozen yard.

"*Nearest,*" said Mace. "*The middle. One, and—*"

Rhea's left heel came down flat on the setts.

Pike's head came up. Kiva saw it. She saw his eyes go to the heel, as they had gone to it for eleven years, and saw his mouth open; and then she saw him shut it, with his teeth, as she had once shut hers on a Hobday's corner, because there was a man kneeling square on that cloth whose word it was and not his.

"*—and,*" said Mace.

Rhea cut. The middle husk went down where it stood, in the road, a pace from the cloth's west edge, and lay.

"Mine," said Varley, to nobody. He reached out with the long hook of the cloth's pole and drew the near edge of the canvas over the middle heap, a yard, without getting off his knees, and it went under, and he had it. "*Dark! One!*"

The near husk found its count.

Kiva felt it come. It was through the canvas and the setts under her knee, slow, *pushing,* and her fingers were on the hem and her heart was going like a tram bell and she could not have sworn to a thing. But she saw it as well. The near husk stamped, alone, heavy, and leaned, and came back, and it was not leaning toward the gate. It was leaning toward the shed doors. It took a step toward them, on *one,* and another on *one,* and the frost went off it onto the doors' iron sills and they went white.

"*It's going for the bank,*" said Mace. "*I've lost it. It's not a four. It's a—*"

"Two," said Pike. "It's on a two. It's walking on a two."

It was. Kiva could see it now that she had been told. It stamped and stepped, stamped and stepped, *one* and *two,* toward the shed doors, as a man walks who has stopped marching and is only going somewhere.

"*Mace,*" said Rhea. "Wall. Across the doors. Walk it."

Mace walked it. The silver went along in front of the shed doors like a curtain on a rail, with four wards behind it, and the near husk walked up to it on its two and leaned on it, and stood there, a yard from the iron sills, stamping on the wall.

"*One,*" said Mace. He had it again. "*And. Two. And.*"

Rhea's heel came down.

"*And,*" said Varley.

It fell on the setts in front of the wall. It lay. It was a long way from the cloth, and from Varley's knees, and it began to run down the fall to the gutter, toward the gap under the doors, where the bank house's drain went in.

Kiva watched it run.

She had nothing to do. It was not hers to do anything. She had the far edge of a cloth that had two heaps on it already, and the far edge lay flat on the setts, and there was nothing white anywhere near it; and the third heap was running into a drain twenty paces off, and the only person on that floor whose business it was to stop it was kneeling square on a canvas with both his knees, and he had not moved.

He did not need to. Purvis was at the doors already, with the second cloth, which he had brought off the van on his shoulder without being told because he had nine years of a man who liked to have two; and he threw it over the running heap from the shed side, flat, at the first cast, and put both his boots on its downhill hem.

"*Dark,*" said Purvis, rather breathless. "*One.* I've no catcher."

"*You've a catcher,*" said Varley, without turning his head. "Hold your hem. I'll come to you when these are sealed." He looked down at his own cloth, with two heaps on it under the canvas, and his two knees on the hem. "*Corners.*"

---

It took nineteen minutes from the gate to the last seal, and Kiva said nothing in any of them.

She watched. She watched Varley wait eleven counts on the first heap before he called the corners, and nine on the second; she watched him look at each heap while he waited, as Pike looked, and saw that each of them leaned a hair toward the shed doors and the bank house behind them, and settled; and she saw that he saw it, because his long face did not change, and he called the corners as though it were an ordinary thing. She watched his cord come out of his coat warm. She watched him tie the three turns with his left thumb pinning and his right taking the long end under, the right way round, the way that the book said, because his right thumb was not slow.

On the first bag she watched Pike watch him, and saw the white bound hands on Pike's knees close, both of them, a quarter of the way, on the third turn, and open again.

"Sealed," said Varley. "Fifty-one. Ward, full. This winter's skin."

"Sealed. Forty-eight."

He went to Purvis at the shed doors on his knees, dragging nothing, and knelt on the second cloth's hem, and looked at the third heap for nine counts while Purvis held his boots on the downhill edge, and then he called the corners to two corner-hands who had never in their lives hauled a cloth that their own catcher had not thrown.

"Sealed. Fifty-six." He sat back on his heels. "That's slow. It ran a yard before Purvis had it."

"It ran four," said Purvis.

"Then it's slow and lucky." Varley put his palm on the neck. He held it there a moment longer than he had on the others. "Loud. As loud as the other two. All three."

There was a man in a long coat bent over the levee drain at the Gasworks corner, a quarter of a mile off; Kiva had not seen him come. After a time he lifted his head and called *closed,* faintly, down the road, as a man says that he has shut a gate.

Rhea read the slate in the road with the van's lamp on it.

"*Closed, a quarter to eleven. Lives: none. Works: the shed doors' sills, frosted. Tinned: three bags, at trace, clean. A hundred and fifty-five drams, of a hundred and sixty-two by the book.*" She turned it over. "*Remarks: one. The pack was counted by the ward-chief and cut between his counts, off a method on a second's sheet which the second had from the Charter Board's Marshal on Monday. When it broke, it came back on a count of two and walked for the bank house. I've not seen that. It's the pack's remark, and the method's, and it's a good one.*"

She hung the slate on her belt, and turned, and looked at Kiva for the first time that night.

"Your sheet," she said. "The pack. I've had it on my desk since Wednesday. I'd like it copied for Imrie's van, in your hand, with the Marshal's name on it where you got it from. I'll pay for the paper."

"I've nothing on it about a count of two."

"No. I'll write that myself." Rhea put her hands in her pockets. "It's in my *Remarks* column now. Your column's the *How.* Mine's the *what it did that nobody said it would.* I've found that they go well on the same sheet."

Kiva sat in the van on the way back with her knees against the canvas and Pru asleep against her shoulder, and she watched Pike.

He sat by the tailboard with his white hands on his knees and his eyes on Varley's hands, which were folding the left-hand cloth over and over on Varley's knee, smoothing it, while Varley talked to it under his breath. Pike did not say anything all the way to the hall.

At the hall gate, as Ruddock was backing the van in, he said, to nobody, "He waits eleven."

"He does," said Kiva.

"I wait eight. When it's quiet." Pike looked at his hands. "He waits eleven when it's quiet. I'd not have known that if you hadn't counted mine."

"Is eleven wrong?"

"No," said Pike. He said it slowly, as though it cost something to get out. "No. It's a longer look. On a night like this one, with a bank house leaning at every heap, I'd say eleven's better." He was quiet a moment. "I'd have called it at eight, and been right, and not seen the lean. I'd want that in your column."

"Which column?"

"Not mine," said Pike. "His."

---

The Pit was cold on Saturday morning and full, and this time the Marshal had something on her cart that nobody could see at all.

The cage stood in the middle of the cinder where it had stood on Monday, under its grey ward. There were two stools beside it, eight paces apart, and on each stool stood a lamp-cell of the Wren House's pattern, brass-bound, the size of a loaf; and a Wren House man in a brown apron stood between them with his hands in his sleeves.

The first company was on the rail, all sixteen, and the reserves behind. Selka Dray was at the end, on her two sticks, upright, with Marten beside her. Old Four had Brask's notebook open before anybody had said a word. Kellan stood with Halden in black and gold at the west end, and did not look along the rail, and Kiva did not look at him.

"Rowan," said Coombe, from the steps.

"Ma'am."

"The far gallery. By the mast. Twenty paces from the cage at the least, and you stay there."

Kiva went. She did not ask why. She went round the rail with every head on it turning to watch her go, and stood by the old mast at the east end, by herself, with her hands on the cold oak; and she saw the Marshal watch her all the way there, and nod once when she stopped.

"I've no notion what this would do near her," said Coombe, to the rail at large. "None. She's three doors and declares one, and I've read her tapes. It may be that it would do nothing. I'll not find out on a Saturday morning in a Pit to teach sixteen people a thing they can learn without her." She tied a knot. "That's said once."

She laid her palm on the ward and drew it back.

At first Kiva thought that the cage was empty. Then she saw, on its floor, lying in loose coils like a rope that has been thrown down on a quay, something grey. It was as thick as her wrist and as long as a man, perhaps longer; she could not see where it began. It did not breathe. It did not stamp. One end of it was lifted a hand's breadth off the iron floor of the cage, and turning, very slowly, as the tip of a vine turns in a greenhouse when nobody is looking.

"On the Longshore the crews call that a lodger," said Coombe. "Since it moves in. It's a cord, without a string. It came out of a tear on the flats in the eleventh month by itself and lay on the sand for a week and went nowhere, and the crew that found it thought it was dead. Then a girl came down the flats from the Ropewalk School on a Saturday, with her two doors laid, as they lay them at the Ropewalk, to look at it." She tied a knot. "Light the left cell. Alone."

The Wren House man put his palms over the left lamp-cell and it lit, a steady yellow, alone on its stool.

The lodger's tip did not turn to it.

"Now the right. Two cells, in one case. Lit together. Tuned to agree."

The man went to the right-hand stool. There were two cells in its brass case, Kiva saw now, side by side, and he put a palm over each, and lit them both at once, and turned a screw at the case's foot with his thumb, and the two lights went from a yellow and a slightly paler yellow to one yellow, steady, exactly the same, as two voices on a hymn find the same note.

The lodger's tip turned.

It came out of the cage. It came out over the iron sill, slowly, the whole grey length of it following the tip as a rope follows its end over a gunwale, and it went across the cinder toward the right-hand stool, not hurrying. It went past the left-hand stool and the one lit cell on it without lifting its tip an inch. It reached the right-hand stool and went up the leg.

Somebody on the rail made a small sound.

The tip came to the brass case. It touched the place where the two cells sat side by side, at the seam between them, and stopped there, as a finger stops on a latch.

Then it went in.

Kiva did not see how. She saw the two yellow lights go grey, both together, as though somebody had breathed on a window; and she saw the tip of the cord go into the seam between them as though there were a door there and the door had been left open, and the grey length on the cinder behind it draw up after it, and grow shorter. The two cells went dark.

"Quench," said Coombe.

The Wren House man pulled the case's handle. There was a dry click. The lodger lay on the stool and down its leg and across the cinder, quite still, with its tip in the dark brass seam.

"That girl on the flats had two doors laid," said the Marshal. "She's alive. She's one door now. She was two." She did not raise her voice. "That's a laid door. At the Ropewalk they open two together and twist them to agree, and they win lanes with it, and nobody that I know of has been hurt by it in a lane in forty years. On the Longshore, in the eleventh month, it was an open door. That's what you'll be standing next to at the gate. I'll say it plainly, so that it's been said: I don't know why. I know where it went."

Nobody said anything.

Selka Dray had her sunk grey eyes on the brass case.

"Two doors in one person," she said. "Open together. Agreeing."

"That's a lay, at the Ropewalk. It's a stack, in this city." Coombe did not look at her. "Now I'll show you the part that isn't in anybody's rules."

She lifted the lodger off the stool. She did it with the grey ward, drawn under it like a hand under a sleeping cat, and the whole length of it came up off the cinder and went back into the cage, coil by coil, and lay there with its tip turning.

"Two of you," she said, to two Pit wards in brown who had been standing at the lattice. "A sack."

There was a sandbag on the cinder, ten paces from the cage. The first ward put a hand door under it, a tray of silver, low, as Kiva put hers under a thing for a tap. The second put a still door on it from above, a stance, his boot on the sack's top.

"Both," said Coombe. "Together. You've the sack between you. Agree on it."

They held it. The ward under and the stance on, both on one sandbag, both open at once.

The lodger's tip turned.

It turned toward them. Kiva saw it, from twenty paces, by the old mast; she saw it lift a hand off the floor of the cage and swing, slowly, past the dark cells on their stool, and stop, pointing, at a sandbag on the cinder with two people's doors on it.

"*Off,*" said Coombe. "One of you. Now."

The still-door ward took his boot off the sack.

The lodger's tip hung in the air a moment, pointing. Then it swung away, as a dog's head swings away when the smell it had has gone, and lay down again on the floor of the cage.

---

"Two people," said Kiva.

She had not meant to say it aloud. She was twenty paces off by the mast, and it came out of her quite flatly, and carried, because the Pit was very quiet.

"Two people," said Coombe. "A ward and a stance, on one thing, at once, agreeing. I don't know that it's a stack. I don't know what it is. I've no rule for it and no reading. I know that the lodger went to it." She looked along the rail, slowly, from one end to the other. "Every one of you has done it. You've put two hands on one litter. You've had a ward under a thing and a stance on it and called it a good hold. On a floor with one of those on it, you'll want to know whether you're doing it, and for how long."

"*Mine before yours,*" said Perrin.

He said it under his breath, and Kiva heard it from the other end of the rail, because she had been thinking it.

They had made it in the eleventh month, on the litter over the wall. *They take it, and then you let go. I'd sooner be caught and then let go.* Perrin had turned it on a piece of ground in the Old Yard, against Old Four, with dirt down his neck: her stance under his boots, and then his off. And she had had it from a first-year by a stove on Monday, about a count.

It was the best thing that they had. It had been a hand of four people on the poles of a litter, all of them dark, with nothing open. It had been a still door and a still door on one patch of dirt, like doors, which the Line had a word for and had always called a plank.

It had never been a ward and an Ember on one load.

She had meant it to be. She had lain awake on Thursday night with her book, and the old word that Hollis Vane had read out of a ledger in the autumn, *crossing,* and Pru's fold, and Pike's columns; and she had worked out, at about three in the morning, that the thing she did alone with three doors could be done by three people with one each, and that the place where it would have to go from one of them to the next was the place where *mine* would have to come before *yours.*

"Ma'am," said Kiva. "If it's three people. A ward and an Ember and a stance. Passing one thing. And each of them takes it before the last lets go."

"Then you've a ward and an Ember on one thing, at once, agreeing, for as long as it takes," said Coombe. "And then an Ember and a stance. And I'd not stand near it with a lodger in my Pit." She tied a knot. "Who's thinking of doing that?"

"I am," said Kiva.

The Marshal looked at her across twenty paces of cinder, by the old mast, with her pale eyes.

"Then you'd best find out what your mast says about it," she said. "Before your floor."

---

They found out that afternoon, in the Old Yard, and it was worse than she had thought.

Noa brought the mast. It was not the Circuit's; that went back to the Chair's office on Saturdays and was not to be had. It was the old practice mast from the Low Lane, sixty years old, on a handcart, with its drum and its tape and a brass plate on its foot that said *Not sanctioned* in letters a quarter of an inch high.

"It reads anything that's lit in front of it," said Noa. "It always has. It draws one line, for whoever. The Chair says it can read whatever it likes and enter nothing, which I've always thought was a kind thing to say about a mast." He threaded the tape. "Nothing that it draws goes anywhere but my notebook. I want that said, before anybody lights."

"It's said," said Anwen.

They rigged it as Kiva had drawn it on the back of the tide table at three in the morning, with her aunt's leave.

Gerald hung from the hoist on his pole. A cord ran from his belt across the lane to a ring in the south wall, taut, a yard off the ground; and under the cord, at the wall end, lay a second sandbag with a hat on it, which was the person who was pinned. It was the hill again, in the ninth month: a corner pinning Selka Dray to the ground, and the thing that had had to be done was to take the cord off her and carry it four paces east and plant it, so that the people who had her could pull her out.

She had done that alone, once, with three doors.

"Pru catches," said Kiva. "A ward under the cord, when Jude swings Gerald and it goes slack. One tap. Fold it to a penny. Then it's mine. I take it in my Ember and go four paces east, low. Then it's Perrin's. He's planted on the mark, and he takes it into the ground, and the cord's pinned to the dirt at his feet and not to the sack." She looked at the three of them. "*Mine* before *yours.* At both passes. Pru doesn't shut until I've said *mine.* I don't shut until Perrin has."

"I don't like it," said Pru.

"Nor do I. I'd like to see what the mast says."

They lit. Jude swung Gerald, and the cord went slack for a beat, and Pru's ward came up under it at her right hip, small and silver, and took the tap and rang, and folded from the far edge to a penny in her two palms. She held it out, the way one holds out a coin.

Kiva opened the Ember door.

The bar came down her right fist, long and narrow and dull orange, and she put the end of it to the penny in Pru's palms, as she had planned, and felt the cord's pull come up into the Ember through the touch like the tug of a fish on a line; and Pru had not shut yet, because *mine* had not been said, and for that moment the cord's whole pull lay between them on two doors at once, Pru's silver under and Kiva's orange over, and they agreed. She could feel them agree. It was soft. It was very soft, and very easy, and it cost nothing at all.

She knew that feeling. She had had it three times in the summer, and written it down: *Stacked again. Soft. I knew as I did it.*

"*Mine,*" she said.

"*Yours.*" Pru shut.

Noa made a noise at the mast.

It was not a word. It was the noise that a boy makes when he has put his hand on a stove. Kiva did not look round. She had the cord in her Ember, and Perrin was on his mark four paces east, and she went: one low bound, with the cord's pull riding along her fist, and landed by Perrin's boots with his still door open under them, and put the bar's end to the dirt at his feet.

"*Mine,*" said Perrin.

"*Yours.*" She shut.

The cord went down into the ground at Perrin's boots as though it had been nailed there. The sandbag with the hat on it, at the wall, lay free.

"*Planted,*" said Perrin. "That's— Noa?"

Noa had not moved from the mast. He had the tape in his fingers, and he was looking at it.

"It's one line," he said.

Nobody understood him.

"It draws one line," said Noa. "For whoever's lit. I said. And when Finch had her ward up, it drew a ward line, and when Rowan lit, it drew—" He stopped. "It didn't draw two. It drew one. A ward line and an Ember line on top of each other, agreeing, for a count and a half. One line. It can't tell that there were two of you. There's nothing on this tape that says there were two of you." He looked up, and his face was white. "On a sanctioned tape, I'd have to call that a stack."

The yard was very quiet.

"Whose?" said Anwen.

"I don't know whose. A tape doesn't know whose. It knows that two doors were open on one thing and agreed." Noa held the tape out, at arm's length, as though it might go off. "And there's the same again at the second pass. Ember and stance. One line. A count."

Kiva stood by Perrin's mark with her Ember shut and her left hand cold.

She thought about the Chair's sheet, pasted under Seven's entry outside the ash office, in a level upright hand. *Read from a sanctioned mast by a reader of this office. A stack so read is an incident. One incident at a sanctioned board ends her season.*

If Noa had been at the Circuit's mast instead of the Low Lane's, and the Chair's office had not been shut on a Saturday, and a clerk had written down what he saw, she would be finished. Not for anything that she had done with her doors. She had had one door open the whole time, declared, and it had been the Ember. She would be finished for a thing that two people had done, together, with a cord, in a yard, because two people had agreed on it for a count and a half.

"It isn't you," said Pru. She had come up beside her. Her round face was set. "It's *us.* It isn't yours."

"I know that it isn't."

"I'll say so to anybody."

"I know you will." Kiva looked at the hat on the sandbag by the wall, lying free. "It doesn't matter whose. Coombe said. The lodger doesn't care whose. It went to two Pit wards on a sack who'd never laid a door in their lives."

"Then we don't do *mine* before *yours,*" said Jude.

He had come down from the hoist. He had the team book under his arm, flat, and his white boot.

"Not between a ward and an Ember. Not between the doors." He scratched his jaw. "On the poles, aye. On a mark, under Cade's boots, that's two stances; the Line calls that a plank and it's been a plank for two hundred years and nobody's said it was a stack. But when it's two unlike doors on one thing, then one of you has to let go first. And then there's nobody."

"For how long?" said Perrin.

"I don't know for how long. As long as it takes the next one to open."

"We'll find out," said Kiva.

---

They found out at the second try, and it was too long.

Pru caught the cord on its slack, and folded it, and held the penny out; and this time she shut first, on Kiva's *now,* and Kiva opened the Ember after, on the shut. And between the shut and the open, which was perhaps a count, perhaps a little more, the cord's pull went out of the penny and back into the cord, and the cord went back across the lane to the sandbag with the hat on it, as a stretched spring goes back, and pinned it again.

Gerald swung on his hoist. The penny of silver was gone out of Pru's palms. Kiva had an Ember open on nothing.

"Dropped," said Noa, from the mast. "Two lines, a gap between, nothing across it. Not a stack. That's clean." He looked at his tape. "And the cord's back on the sack. That's a count and a third, the gap. I've measured it."

"That's too long," said Kiva.

"How do you know?"

"Because it's a count and a third." She shut the Ember. "Nobody's a quarter of a count. When I cross, the gap's a quarter. I've been it. I know how long nobody is. I've been nobody in the air over an oak floor and on a hill and in this yard, and it's never once been a whole count. If it were a whole count I'd have hit the ground."

"Then how do we make it a quarter?" said Perrin.

Kiva looked at Pru, and at Perrin on his mark, and at the cord going across the lane to a hat.

"Somebody calls it," she said. "Not *mine* and *yours.* That's two words, and the gap's between them, and it's as long as the time it takes to hear the first and say the second. Somebody calls the gap itself. One word. Pru shuts on the start of it. I open on the end of it. Then it's as long as a word."

"Whose word?"

"Mine." She heard herself say it and did not take it back. "I'm the one who's been in it. I'm the only one on this yard who knows how long a gap is from the inside. And I'm the next door. If I call it, I'm calling my own opening; I'll not be late on my own word."

"And the second pass? You into me?"

"Mine as well. I shut on the start, you open on the end. I'll be in the air."

"You'll be calling while you're nobody."

"Yes," said Kiva. "I'll be nobody with my mouth open. Orla would say that's how I've always been."

Nobody laughed. Pru looked at her for a long moment, and then down at her own hands, at the little finger that came across a cord.

"What's the word?" she said.

Kiva had not thought of one. She found that she had one ready, all the same; it had been lying at the back of her mouth since a Thursday on an oak floor, a month ago, in the air.

"*Nobody,*" she said.

They tried it four more times, before the light went, and it did not work. It was better each time. The gap went from a count and a third to a count, and to two-thirds, and on the last it was a half; and on the last the cord went back across the lane only a hand's width before the Ember caught it, and jerked, and came, and Kiva went four paces east with it and called *nobody* in the air and Perrin opened late, and it went back again, all the way, to the hat.

"Half," said Noa. "Clean. Two gaps, nothing across either." He wound the tape. "It isn't a quarter."

"No," said Kiva. "It isn't yet."

# Chapter 12 — Kiva: A Count Everybody Can Hear

Kellan Renn had written down everything that he knew about Team Seven by half past five on Monday afternoon, on four sheets of the refinery's yellow foolscap, and when he had finished he sat on the bench in the Halden locker room and read it over and found that he did not like himself very much.

It was a good list. That was the trouble.

He had not had to think about any of it. It had come out of the end of his pencil as fast as he could write, in the order that it happened on a floor, as though it had been waiting behind his teeth since the Regional to be asked for. *The catcher tests ground before she stands on it. Always. In the open, a full count before she uses it. Brask had a boy stand on her marks. Since the sixteenth of last month she tests under Cade instead: he plants, she opens under his heels, she says "mine," he says "yours," and steps off. Three counts. In those three counts Cade can't move.* He had drawn a line under that one without meaning to.

*On a litter over a wall they take before they let go. "Mine," then "yours." Two to the poles for half a count.*

*Cade calls "off" early. He calls it when it's wanted, she'd say. I'd say early.*

*Pryce walks a wall. Forty paces. On Wednesday she turned one round a moving thing; I wasn't there, I had it from Hesketh, who had it from a junior. She calls her own turn now.*

*Talley cuts heavy, from the shoulder, and stays over what he's cut. He's learning not to. He's not learned it.*

*Last Monday, at the Pit, Cade counted a pack aloud from the steps. One-and-two-and. He's to count on any floor where a thing keeps time, and he's to haul nothing while he counts. He minds.*

And at the bottom, by itself, because he had written it last and then sat looking at it for some time:

*They say everything aloud. It's their whole method. They say what they'll do and whose it is and when it changes hands, and they say it so that anybody can hear it.*

He had taught them some of that. He had stood in the Old Yard in the spring with his ears white and asked a catcher *now?* over his shoulder for the first time in his life, because he had been made to see that a blade who cuts on his own word is a blade who cuts on the wrong one. He had been proud of it. He was still proud of it.

And he had written it down on yellow paper, in a locker room, for Pettit.

"You've been an hour," said Pettit, from the door.

Pettit was a heavy fair boy with a still door and a slow, sure, rather sleepy way of standing that made people underestimate him, which he liked. He had been Halden License's captain since the Regional. He had never once asked Kellan what Seven would do, in all the weeks that Kellan had cut for him; and Kellan had been grateful, and had also noticed it, and wondered what it meant.

"It's done," said Kellan.

"You needn't."

"I know I needn't. Birch said. You said, at the Regional. *Nothing owed.*" Kellan squared the four sheets on his knee. "That's why I've done it."

Pettit came in and sat on the bench opposite and did not reach for the sheets.

"Tell me why," he said. "Before I read it. I'd like to know what I'm being given."

Kellan had worked that out on the tram, too, as he worked everything out, and he had not cared for the sum.

"I stood up at a kitchen table a fortnight ago," he said, "and told four people and a first-year that I wanted to know what I am. I said it to their faces. I said I wanted to stand in the best four in the country and find out whether I'm the weak corner." He looked at the yellow paper. "Tomorrow there's a lane. The Marshal's put us against them. If I go out on that yard and cut as a blade cuts, and don't use what I know, then I'm not standing for Halden. I'm visiting. I'll have kept a piece of myself back for them, and it'll be the best piece, and I'll never know whether I'm the weak corner because I'll never have stood in your four with the whole of me."

"And if you use it, and they lose?"

"Then they'll have lost to a four that read them," said Kellan. "Which is what'll happen at Saltmere, on the first board that matters, to a four from the coast that's never heard of the Old Yard. Brask read them in a fortnight with a notebook. I've had a year." He held the sheets out. "Better me than a stranger. That's the other half. I'd not have said it first, since it sounds noble, and it isn't. The first half's the true one."

Pettit took the sheets. He read them slowly, all four, with his lips not moving, the way that a man reads who intends to remember; and when he came to the last line he read it twice.

"*They say everything aloud,*" he said.

"Yes."

"And you'll be listening."

"I'll be listening," said Kellan. "I taught Rowan's four half their words. I'd know them in my sleep."

Pettit folded the sheets and put them in his jersey.

"Yarrow'll want them," he said. "And Hesketh. I'll not have them read in the yard. Not where Birch can see; he'd think we'd put you up to it." He got up. "Renn. I'll say a thing, since you've said yours. We didn't ask you because we thought you'd say no. And then we'd have had to know that you'd said no."

"I'd have said yes."

"I know that now," said Pettit, and went out.

He had given away nothing that was not already on their own sheets, in Rowan's own hand, in seven columns. # Chapter 13 — Kiva: In Its Own Right

"Thumb," said Pru. "Flat. On the first turn. No, flat. You've got it on its edge like a man holding a cigarette."

"I've never held a cigarette."

"Then you'll not know what it looks like, and you'll have to take my word." Pru reached across and pressed Anwen's left thumb down onto the cord with one finger, as one presses a stamp onto an envelope. "There. That's the pin. Now the long end over and under, and you don't lift the thumb, whatever it wants."

It was Wednesday afternoon, the 11th, and the two of them were sitting on the shed step with a sandbag between their knees, and the neck of an old purse sheet drawn up round the sandbag's top; and Anwen Pryce, who had the best Hold in the Third Division, was trying to tie three turns on it and making a very poor job of the third.

"It won't go under."

"It's cold. The wax is stiff. That's why you've a little finger." Pru showed her. She did it slowly, as she had done it for Pike by a stove, and the little finger came across the first turn from the far side and held it while the long end went under, and nothing slackened. "There. It's a second pin. You'll not get it today. I didn't get it for a fortnight, and I'd been tying since I was twelve."

"Then why am I learning it?"

"Because you're in the four and I'm not," said Pru.

She said it quite flatly. She went pink a moment after, the way she did when a big thing had come out of her before she meant it to, but she did not take it back. She looked at the sandbag instead.

Anwen looked at her for a long moment.

She had been working, all the morning, at a quarter of a count.

They had the cord across the lane again, from Gerald's belt to the ring in the south wall, taut, with the second sandbag lying under it in its hat. On the third try the cord had come up into her bar with no more than a jerk. On the fourth it had come up with nothing at all, as clean as a coin from one palm to another.

And every time he opened it late. It was not much. It was half a count, a third. "Why?"

"Because you're in the air." He put his hands on his knees and breathed. "You're coming down at me with a cord, and you say a word, and I'm meant to open on the end of it. I wait till you've landed."

Kiva stood beside him with her Ember shut and her left hand cold.

She understood it. It was the oldest thing in the world. A still door was for standing on ground, and the ground was there; one opened onto something that one could feel under one's heels. He was being asked to open on a word from somebody who was not on any ground at all, in the air, falling, and to believe that by the time his door was open there would be a load coming down into it. It was asking him to arrive before he was there.

"Orla told me a thing once," she said. "On a crate, in the summer, when I couldn't land a crossing. *Arrive before you're there.* I didn't know what it meant. I thought it was something a Torch says to sound clever."

"Is it?"

"It's what you're doing. Waiting till I've landed. It's what I used to do. I'd wait in the gap to feel the ground before I opened the still, and I'd come down on nothing." She looked at him. "No. Nor did I." She went back to her mark. "Shut your eyes, then."

He stared at her.

"*Rowan's ground,*" said Anwen.

Kiva did not understand her. Then she did.

"*I can't,*" she said. "I've the still declared. I can't put up a ward."

"Not a ward. Your ground. Put your still door under my heels and *hold* me. I'll let the far end down and keep the near end up, at my hip, a yard of it, and if the string's leaning on a yard of wall that's planted on your ground, it'll stand. I've seen Stroud hold the end of Mace's line with a still-door at her back." Anwen's voice was very quick and very level. "Then I can come off with one hand. One. And tie."

It was a plank.

It was a ward on a still door's ground. It was two unlike doors on one thing, at once.

"*No,*" said Kiva. "There's a lodger."

"Where?"

"I don't know where. That's why."

Anwen did not answer. Kiva saw her face go white and set, at the end of her wall, with a string leaning on it, and she saw her decide. She did not let the wall down. She did not ask for ground. She put one hand on the near end of the wall, at her hip, flat, and took the other off it altogether, and held the whole of that wall with one hand, for as long as she could, which Kiva knew was not long; and with the other she reached across the cinder toward the neck of the cloth.

She could not reach it. It was two paces off.

"*Bring it,*" said Perrin. "Talley. Bring the corner round."

They brought it. They dragged the whole cloth two paces west on its corners, with the heap inside, across the cinder, until the neck was at Anwen's knee; and she tied it with one hand and her teeth.

It was not three turns. It was two, and the end tucked under, and the little finger across, and her face scarlet.

"*Sealed,*" said Anwen. "I don't know what count."

"Sixty-two," said the Marshal, from the rail. She did not raise her voice. "That's outside. It's a bag. It's a remark."

---

They took the second knot more quickly, and it went worse.

Anwen's wall had held the string on one hand for the best part of a minute, and the string had leaned on it the whole time, and when she put her second hand back on it Kiva saw the silver shake from end to end like a sail in a gust. The string had felt it. Its head had begun to turn again. It was not leaning on the wall now. It was pushing along it, east, toward the end, as a cow pushes along a fence for the gap; and its skin was coming round behind it, away from the uphill side, down the slope, south and east, toward the race and toward Kiva's new ground.

"*It's turning,*" said Kiva. "Anwen. It's coming round on its own. The skin's going downhill."

"*I know.*" Anwen's heels went back in the cinder. "I can't hold its head and walk it. Not on one— I'd want a stance at my back."

"Cade," said Kiva. "Plant at her back."

Perrin was there. He had let go the south corner and come round behind Anwen's wall, and he planted, a pace behind her, his still door open into the cinder, and leaned his shoulder into her back. Kiva saw the wall stop shaking. It stood. The string pushed along it and the wall did not give an inch, and Anwen began, very slowly, to walk its far end back round, west, against the push.

That was when Kiva saw the lodger.

She saw it because she was looking at the race. She had been looking at the race since the string began to turn, because the race was where the rung would go if the turn failed; and so she saw a thing at its lip that she would not have seen from anywhere else on the floor. It was a grey line lying along the edge of the stone channel, a long way off, by the steps, as thick as a wrist. It had been lying there, she supposed, since six. It had looked like a crack in the coping.

Its tip had lifted.

It was turning, slowly, as the tip of a vine turns, and it came round, and it stopped. It was pointing across the cinder at Anwen's wall, and at Perrin's still door planted at the back of it, his shoulder in her back, his stance and her ward on one thing, together, agreeing.

"*Nobody!*" said Kiva.

She said it short, with the swallow. It came out of her without her deciding to say it at all.

Perrin shut.

He did not ask why. He did not look round. He had heard that word fourteen times in two days, on a yard, in a voice from the air, and every time he had heard it he had been told to do one thing on the end of it, and he did it. His still door went shut under his heels and he stepped back off Anwen, a pace, so that his shoulder came away from her back and there was nothing between them but air.

Anwen's wall shook. The string pushed along it. She held it, alone, with both hands.

The lodger's tip hung in the air, by the race, pointing.

Then it swung away, as a dog's head swings when the smell has gone, and lay down along the coping again, and was a crack in the stone.

"*Where?*" said Jude.

"*The race. The east end. By the steps.*" Kiva did not take her eyes off it. "It's lying down. It went for Perrin and Anwen. It's lying down now."

"*Then nobody stands together,*" said Perrin.

He said it in his captain's voice, loud, so that the whole Pit heard it, and Kiva understood that he was saying it for the four of them and not for the rail. "Nobody puts a door on another's. Not on a wall, not on a mark, not on a cloth. If you want a stance at your back, you ask, and Rowan says whether, and she's watching the race."

"*Then I'll walk it alone,*" said Anwen, between her teeth. "Talley. Cut it before I drop it."

Jude went up the west side.

"*Now?*"

"*Now,*" said Kiva, since there was no time to wait for anything.

The second knot came off. The rung fell at the string's heels, on the downhill side, and the skin was half round behind it, half on the slope, and the rung came down on the skin's near edge, half on and half off, and lay there across it, and did not run.

"*Far edge,*" said Kiva. "It's on the skin. Anwen, it's on the—"

"*I can't turn it.*"

"Then I'll weight it."

There was nothing to weight it with. There were no sacks on that cinder; Coombe had not left any. There was the cart, and the cage on the cart, and the four of them.

Kiva threw her cloth over the rung where it lay, half on the skin and half off, and then she did a thing that she had not planned and would think about for a long time afterward. She got up off her knees and went round the downhill side of the cloth, three strides, to the place where the skin's far edge lay on the cinder under the canvas, white, and she stood on it. She stood on it with both boots, as a girl in a laundry had stood on a sheet's trailing hem by accident, and she opened her still door under her own heels, and planted.

The yard took her. The cinder took her, and the skin under the cinder, which was cold and hard and did not take a boot. She felt it under her soles as one feels ice on a pond, and she felt it try to rise. It lifted, a finger's breadth, at the far edge, under her weight.

It did not fold.

*A sheet with a foot on its far hem doesn't fold for anybody.*

"*Far edge,*" she said, standing on it. "*Holding.*"

She heard Perrin say *oh* under his breath from somewhere behind her. She did not look.

"*Corners,*" she said. "Who's free?"

"*Me,*" said Jude. He had come off the string's flank. "And Cade."

They hauled. The neck came up. The rung came into it off the skin's edge, slowly, as Kiva stood on the far edge with her still door planted and two dead fingers that could tell her nothing about whether she was going to be folded into a sack with sixty drams of ash; and Anwen's wall stood with the string leaning on it; and at the race, by the steps, the lodger lay along the coping and did not lift its tip.

Perrin tied it. He had never tied a cord on a live bag in his life. He tied it with his thumb on its edge like a man holding a cigarette, and the third turn slipped, and he said a word that his little sister would not have known, and tied it again.

"*Sealed,*" said Perrin. "Fifty-nine."

"Fifty-nine," said the Marshal, from the rail. "Inside."

---

The last knot was the string itself. Jude cut it on Kiva's word with the string facing south-west and the skin behind it up the hill, and the whole of the thing came down onto the cinder in a heap like a cart's load tipped out, and ran, and Kiva had her second cloth on it and her knees on the hem. It leaned to the lattice, a hair. It settled. Anwen had let her wall down, at last, and came to the north corner, and Jude to the south, and Perrin stood at the lattice end and did not haul, and counted nothing, and watched the race.

Anwen tied it. It was three turns. The little finger came across, on the third, slow. Nothing slackened.

"*Sealed,*" said Anwen. "Fifty-four."

The skin went as the purse string's had gone on the wharf. It thinned from the edges, and was grey, and was a film of fine ash on the cinder, no thicker than dust on a shelf. The Wren House man came down the steps and swept it into a tin.

"There's a lodger," said Coombe. "Kill it."

She had not moved from the rail. She said it as she said everything, without leaning on any word.

Jude looked at Kiva.

"It doesn't keep time," said Kiva. "I don't think. It doesn't stamp, it doesn't sway. It isn't a pack." She looked at the grey line on the coping. "I'd go at it from above. Not near the tip. The tip's what goes looking."

"And if it comes at me?"

"It won't. You're one door." She stopped. "I think. It went for two. It went for the pair in the case and two wards on a sack and Perrin and Anwen. It's never been near one."

"You think," said Jude.

"I think. I've nothing better."

He went. He went along the race on his bad leg, slowly, on the cinder side of the coping, with his kill unlit, and the lodger lay along the stone and did not lift its tip; and when he was above the middle of it he lit, and the kill came down out of his shoulder into the stone of the coping and through the grey cord lying on it.

It did not fall. It came apart. It went to ash all along its length at once, from the cut both ways, as a fuse goes, and lay on the coping in a grey line, a hand high, and then slid off the lip into the dry race, and lay there among the leaves.

Nobody threw a cloth. There was nowhere to throw it.

"Leave it," said Coombe. "That one's mine. I'll have the Wren House shovel it out." She came down the steps for the first time that morning, and stood on the cinder, and looked at the four of them, and at the three bags lying on the floor, and at Perrin at the lattice end with his hands hanging. She tied a knot. "Bags."

The Wren House man brought the comb. He held it over each neck in turn. On each the third reed sang, and the seventh sang under it, faint, a thin high thread of a note. *Ward. Full. Trace.*

"Trace," said the Wren House man. "Clean. Three bags."

"Board's yield," said Coombe. "A floor's yield is the floor's." She did not look at the bags again. She looked at Anwen. "Pryce. You tied the first one with one hand and your teeth, at sixty-two."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Why?"

"Because I'd not have Rowan put a ward under my heels with a lodger on the floor, and I'd not drop the string, and there was nobody else." Anwen's chin was up. "It's a remark. I know it's a remark."

"It's a remark. It's the only one." The Marshal turned. "Rowan."

"Ma'am."

"You stood on the skin."

"Yes, ma'am."

"With your still door open. On a thing that folds."

"It was the only weight on the floor," said Kiva. "Pike said dead weight. I wasn't dead weight. I was planted. I don't know that it's the same. It held."

"It held." Coombe looked at her for a long time with her pale eyes. "If it hadn't, you'd have had your boots in a fold with sixty drams of open ash, and I'd have been on that cinder before it finished and not before it started, as I said." She tied a knot. "I'll not have you do it again. I'll put it on the page, all the same. A planted foot on the far edge, by a catcher who had nothing else. With a line under it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Cade."

Perrin's head came up.

"You dropped your count to haul. You said so, aloud, so that nobody would think they had it. Then you put a stance at your Hold's back when she asked for one. Then your catcher said a word and you shut it and stepped off without asking why." Coombe's voice did not change by a hair. "Then you told your four that nobody stands together, before any of them could think of it for themselves. And you tied a bag like a man holding a cigarette." She paused. "Your catcher said *nobody.* Who taught you to open and shut on it?"

"She did," said Perrin. "Yesterday. With my eyes shut."

The Marshal looked at him. Something happened at one corner of her weathered mouth, and went again.

"Up," she said. "All of you. I'll sign it at the top."

She signed it on the Pit's rail, standing, on a printed sheet with the Charter Board's seal at its head, in a hand that was square and slow and pressed hard into the paper.

*T7, of the Meridian Collegiate Circuit. A Flask license, in its own right, for the year 2681, on the Marshal's floor of the 12th of the second month. Captain: Cade, P., carry. Catcher: Rowan, K. Hold: Pryce, A. Kill: Talley, J. Remarks: one (a bag sealed at sixty-two). A four licensed in its own right hunts on its own entry, under its captain's name. D. Coombe, Marshal.*

She gave it to Perrin.

He held it in both hands. He read it twice, with his lips moving, and then he looked up at the Marshal, and his face had gone a color that Kiva had never seen on it before.

"*Under its captain's name,*" he said.

"That's what it says."

"So if we hunt, and somebody's hurt. On our own entry. It's not Sorn's slate. It's not an officer's."

"It's yours," said Coombe. "That's what in its own right means. There's no company over you any more on a hunt, and no officer to take a remark for you as Sorn took one on a wharf. You'll go out under your own name, and if you bring a person home with a hand taken, it'll be written under *Cade,* and the Board will want to know why." She tied a knot. Perrin looked at the sheet for a long time.

"I'd like it known," he said at last, quite quietly, "that I don't want this either."

"I know you don't," said the Marshal. "That's why it's yours."

Nobody had asked her to come. She was simply there, at two, with a fresh length of rope; and Pike was on the crate beside Orla, with his arms on his knees, and Noa was at the Low Lane's old mast, and Pru had Gerald on the hoist.

"Show me," said Coombe.

They showed her.

Jude swung Gerald, and the cord across the lane went slack for a beat, and Pru's ward came up under it at her right hip, small and silver, and took the tap and rang, and folded from the far edge to a penny in her palms.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

Pru shut on the *n.* Kiva's Ember opened on the end of the word, and the bar came down her right fist and met the penny where Pru's palms had been a moment before, and the cord's pull came up into the Ember without a jerk, like a coin from one palm to another.

She went. Four paces east, low, with the cord riding along her fist, and at the top of the bound, with the struts running out of her calves and the Ember beginning to close, she said it again, short, with the swallow.

*"Nobody."*

Perrin opened on the end of it. His eyes were open. He was looking at her as she came down at him, and he did not wait to see her land; he had heard the word and he believed it, and his still door was open under his heels before her foot came down beside them, and the cord went out of her fist and down into the dirt at his boots as though it had been nailed.

The hat on the sandbag lay still.

"*Planted,*" said Perrin.

"A quarter at the first," said Noa. "A quarter at the second. Three lines. Nothing across either gap." He looked up at the step. "Not a stack, ma'am. Not anywhere."

They did it four times. On the third, Perrin opened early, before the end of the word, and his still door was there and waiting and empty for half a count before Kiva came down into it, and Noa said *clean* and then said *early,* and Perrin said that he would rather be early than late, and Coombe said nothing. On the fourth it was a quarter at both.

The Marshal came down off the step.

She walked along the scraped strip of the lane to where the cord went into the dirt at Perrin's boots, and looked at it. She looked at the three of them, one after the other: Pru with her palms open and nothing in them, and Kiva with her Ember shut and her left hand in her coat, and Perrin on his mark.

"I've seen mixed fours pass a load hand to hand on the Longshore," she said. "A ward to a blade, to get a thing off a man. Badly. Every time I've seen it, it was two people grabbing at once and hoping. Twice it killed somebody." She tied a knot. "I've never seen it called."

"It's a crossing," said Jude.

He was at the hoist, with Gerald's rope in his fist, and he said it as he said everything, slowly, scratching his jaw.

"It's what she does with three doors. Catch, carry, plant. Only it's handed. From one of us to the next." He looked at Kiva. "The Line had a word for it once. The other kind. Vane read it out of a ledger. *A crossing.* I'd call this one handed."

"A handed crossing," said Coombe.

She said it as though she were reading it off a label to see whether she liked it.

"I've no name for it," she said. "I've had thirty years on the coast and no name for it, because there was nothing to name. I'll not give it one now; it's yours." She looked at Kiva. "Write it. In your seven columns, with every hand in it by name. Who catches, who carries, who plants, who calls, and why the caller's the one who's been in both gaps. Write what it costs. And write that it's been done on a yard on a Friday, with a sandbag in a hat, and nowhere else."

"Yes, ma'am."

"If it holds on the Longshore, I'll want the sheet. If it doesn't hold, I'll want it more." The pale eyes went from Kiva to Pru, and to Perrin. "It's three people's. It's not hers. Don't any of you let anybody tell you that it's hers. It's the opposite of hers. It's the thing she does that she can't do, done by three who can."

Nobody said anything. Kiva found that her eyes had gone hot, and she looked at the hat on the sandbag until they stopped.

The Marshal went back up the lane. At the crate she stopped in front of Pike.

"You'll have your hands by the gate," she said.

"I'll have them by the fourth of next month, Ruddock says. Slow."

"Slow will do. A catcher's hands are always slow, by the end." Coombe looked at the white linen. "I'll see you on the Longshore. I'd like to watch you kneel at a hem again. I've a notion I'll learn something."

"I'm not there," said Pike.

"No," said Coombe. "But you will be."

She went on Saturday morning, the 14th, by the station transit to the next circuit on her list, and nobody in Seven saw her go. Perrin went up to the shed door at six to wipe out the *31* and chalk a *30,* and found on the step, in the frost, a length of tarred rope as long as an arm, with a great many knots in it, coiled, and nothing written anywhere.

He did not know what it was for. He took it home anyway.

Licensed, in our own right; one remark.*" He did it twice in the margin. "*Held, three and twenty-four.* That's from seven and sixty-eight."

# Chapter 14 — Kiva: A Voice at the Hem

The third crossing went as the first two had gone, and the hand did not.

It went on Sunday the 15th, at seven, in the Old Yard, on the Circuit's mast, with the frost thinner than it had been and a wet wind coming up the long hill from the river. *Hand, to Ember. Rowan, changing on three.* The ward at her hip, and Orla's stick ringing on it once; the fold, from the far edge, to a penny; the shut, and nobody, and the cold coming into her hand as though a door had been opened on a winter morning. The Ember, glad. Six paces, low, onto a white cross.

"One cliff," said Noa. "Twenty-six. Thirty-one in all. A crossing."

"*Three,*" said Orla.

The hand was white past the wrist. Kiva held it up and looked at it and saw that the white went a good inch up the forearm, under the cuff, and stopped there in a line as sharp as a tide mark. She shut the fingers. They went slowly, all eight of them, like a gate in a wind.

Healer Aske had it at seven on Monday morning, under the lamp. He held the wrist for a minute by his watch, and then for another, and did not say anything until he had written.

"Cold to an inch above the wrist," he said. "At seven. Last week, the wrist. The week before, the knuckle." He capped his pen and did not put it away. "I'll see it at noon, and at four, and tonight if I must, and I'll want it in the morning."

At noon it was cold to the wrist. At four it was cold to the knuckle. At nine that night, when she went up the Wren House stair for the third time with her coat over her nightdress and her aunt behind her, it was cool, and the eight fingers shut when she told them, nearly at once.

On Tuesday at seven it was warm.

"A night," said Aske. He wrote it. *Warm at seven on Tuesday. A sulk of a day.* "Noon. Four o'clock. A night. I said I'd say so each time, before and not after. I'm saying it." He looked at her over his spectacles, quite steadily. "Your next is Sunday the 22nd, and the card allows it. I'll not stop it on what I've seen. But if it sulks longer again, I'll want to see it warm before I'll let the one after go, and the one after is on a road."

"There's no mast on a road."

"Then the one after was never going to be, and I'll not lose sleep over it." He put the pen away. "Four of six, by Thursday week. I've written that on the card, so that your sponsor can't say that I didn't."

Orla Dane went up the Chair's stair on Monday afternoon, the 16th, at three, with her stick, and she took it a step at a time and did not let the clerk who came down past her offer an arm.

The Placement Chair's office was at the top of the Measure Hall, under the slates, with a window that looked down the hill to the river and the Brickfields. Ysra Crest sat behind a deal desk with nothing on it but a ledger and a pen and a paperweight of green glass. She did not get up. She never got up for anybody, and Orla had always rather liked her for it.

"Dane," said the Chair. "Sit down. You've come about the six."

"I've come about the four."

"Sit down all the same. I'll not be talked at by a woman standing on one knee."

Orla sat. She put her stick across her lap.

"Four," she said. "By Sunday next, if the hand allows it, and the healer says it may not. Two of my six left over, and no mast on the road, and the gate on the first. That's where we are." She looked at the green glass weight. "I'll not sign at four. I'll not sign at five. I told her so on the first day of this month, before anybody had time to hurry, and I'm telling you so now, so that it's said in this room as well."

"I didn't expect you to sign at four."

"No. You expected me to come up your stair and ask whether there was a way to make it six." Orla turned the stick over. "There is. Saltmere has a gate mast. It's sanctioned; the Board's. Your reader's going south with Dray's rule under seal, to read every candidate in this city who has more than one door. He'll be at that mast in the gate fortnight with his tape. I want the fifth and the sixth on it, on her healer's card, one in seven days, as the card allows. And I want it written now, in your ledger, before we go, that the certification of the second crossing waits on those two and not on anybody's convenience."

The Chair looked at her for some time.

"That's not what you came to ask," she said.

Orla did not answer at once. She looked out of the window at the river, which was grey under the wet wind, with a boat on it going down toward the sea.

"No," she said. "I came to tell you that I'm going."

"South."

"South. On the Circuit's transit, with the officers, since I can't sit a carrier's cart for three days with this knee. I've a candidate who'll make two crossings at that gate on my line, and I'll not have a stranger on a crate for them." Her crooked face did not change. "And there's a man at the harbour pump house who wrote her a card that he never wrote to me. He's the fitter with the watch. I've known that since the twelfth month."

"Hart."

"Hart." Orla said it to the window. "I've not decided what I'll say to him. I've decided that I'll not use her to say it. That's as far as I've got in twenty years, and it's taken me since the autumn to get that far, so I'll not be hurried on the rest." She turned back. "I'll be at the gate mast. That's the first thing. The other's my business."

Ysra Crest picked up her pen. She opened the ledger at a page that Orla could not see, and wrote on it, in her level upright hand, for perhaps a minute; and then she turned the ledger round on the desk and pushed it across.

*Rowan, K. The second crossing, hand to Ember, on her sponsor's line. Seen four times at most in Meridian by the 22nd of the second month, as her healer's card allows. The fifth and sixth to be seen at the Saltmere gate mast, read by N. Bexley, her sponsor present, one in seven days and not faster. Not certified until the sixth. No figure on any sheet is to be rounded for the sake of a boat. Y. Crest.*

"*Not faster,*" said Orla.

"I've seen a girl hurried for a boat," said the Chair. "So have you. I'd not have it said that it was done under my seal." She took the ledger back. "Go south, Dane. Sit on a crate at their gate. Find the man at the pump house, or don't." The level eyes did not soften. "You came up those stairs on one knee to tell me two things, and the second wasn't a thing that this office can write anything about. I'd like you to know that I noticed."

Orla got up, by the desk, in two stages.

"I'd have been sorry if you hadn't," she said.

She went down the stair a step at a time, as she had come up it, and at the bottom she stood for a moment in the Measure Hall's cold passage with her hand on the newel post and her stick under her arm. She had told the Chair. She had told nobody else, and she had not decided whether she would.

The girl would know. She would read it in Orla's face on Sunday on the crate, as she had read a thumb and a hip and a heel, and she would say nothing, because she had learned to say nothing; and that was a thing that Orla had never had from anybody, in twenty years of teaching, and did not yet know how to bear.

Kellan came to the Old Yard on Monday at four, in his own coat, as he had said that he would, and he did not bring Pettit or anybody else.

"One," he said. "Me against your pass. I'll stand at the hurdle. You'll carry Gerald over it on your tap. If I can touch whoever takes it before it's taken, I've read you. If I can't, I haven't."

"And you'll write it down," said Kiva.

"I'll write it down. You told me to." He laced his cuff. "I'd like it known, as Cade says, that I've been thinking about your tap for six days, on a blue brick floor, and I've a notion how to read it."

"So have we," said Perrin.

They set up the hurdle across the lane, where it had stood on the Tuesday, and laid Gerald in the dirt on the shed side of it, and Kellan stood on the far side, dark, with his hands down. He did not look at Perrin's face. Kiva saw that at once. He looked at Perrin's hands.

Perrin picked up the head poles and carried them to the hurdle, and lifted them onto the hazel's top. Anwen went over the hurdle in one scramble and stood on the far side with her hands under the poles' ends, a pace from Kellan, with her wall unlit.

Perrin tapped once. Knuckles on ash.

Kellan's weight came onto his toes. Kiva saw it go; she had been watching his feet, not his hands, as Jude had, as Pike watched a heel. He was counting. He had a number in his head for the space between one tap and the next, the number that Perrin had used on the Tuesday, and he was going to move on it.

Perrin did not tap again.

He stood there with his knuckles on the pole and did nothing at all; and Anwen, under the poles, did not wait for a tap. She was not watching his knuckles. She was watching his shoulders. When they went down an inch, which was the moment that a man who has been holding up a weight lets the weight begin to be somebody else's, she took the poles.

Perrin let go. Neither of them said a word, and neither of them had tapped.

Kellan was a count late. He came off his toes on the number in his head, and touched Anwen on the arm, light as a tap on a barometer, a full count after the poles were in her hands and Perrin's were empty.

"After," said Jude, from the shed step. "That's after. She'd had it a count."

Kellan stood there with his edge lit on his fist. He looked at Anwen, and at Perrin, and at the litter on the far side of the hurdle, where it ought not to have been yet.

"There wasn't a second tap," he said.

"No," said Perrin.

"You tapped once and then you didn't."

"I tapped once so that you'd count. And then I let her take it when my shoulders said, and not when my knuckles did." Perrin was very red. "Rowan's catcher does it with a heel. Pryce does it with my shoulders. I'd no notion that I had shoulders until she told me on Saturday."

Kellan was quiet for a long moment.

"Again," he said.

They did it again six times. He touched Anwen on the fourth, before the poles were hers, because he had stopped counting taps and begun watching Perrin's shoulders as she did; and on the fifth Perrin let the poles go on his hips instead, and on the sixth on a breath, and Kellan was late on both.

"You've changed it," he said.

"Every time," said Perrin. "A different count. Nobody knows it but me and whoever's taking. I said so on Tuesday. You heard me."

"I heard you. I didn't believe it'd be so hard to read."

"Nor did I."

Kellan unlaced his cuff. He stood in the lane with it in his hand, and then he took his pencil out of his pocket and wrote on the inside of it, quickly, two lines that Kiva could not read; and then he held it out to her, turned, so that she could.

*Watch his shoulders. Not his hands. It changes every time. Two in six.*

"That's what a page of you looks like now," he said. "Two in six. It was every time, a week ago."

"It'll be one in six by the gate," said Anwen.

"It'll be one in six by the gate if I'm reading it. A coast four that's never seen it won't get two." He laced the cuff again. "I'll tell Pettit. I'll tell him that I've read it twice in six and I'd not want to try on a floor. That's true, so he'll believe it."

He went up the yard. Jude wrote it on his knee. *Monday the 16th. The Old Yard. The tap, against Renn, alone, at a hurdle. Six passes; two read. Pryce, a wall, 0.06. Cade, a stance, 0.05. Talley, 0.04. Spent, 0.15.* And Sunday's, which I've not read out. *Hand to Ember, Rowan, the third, 0.31.*" He did it in the margin. "*Held, two and sixty.*"

"Two," he said. "And sixty."

The letter came at five, by a Line runner, in a grey envelope with the Home Line's mark on its back and Hollis Vane's square hand on its front. It was addressed *T7, by its captain,* and Perrin opened it on the shed step as though it might bite.

"*The Sag watch, to licensed fours of the Meridian circuit, by the Board's hunt list,*" he read. "*A tear opened on Saturday the 14th at about four in the afternoon on the high ground above Cowl Edge, north of the Sag, on Line ground. It was closed by the Line's crew at eleven that night. Four things came off it before it was closed and are loose on the Edge. Three keep together. One went the other way, alone, slowly, up onto the moor. None has been near a house since. The Line will not take them; it has the Sag's ground to watch and a crew of six. They're posted for any four licensed in its own right that will come for them, on its own entry, under its own captain, with a Line hand to witness and weigh. The Line's hut at Cowl Edge is open. H. Vane.*"

He put the letter down on his knee.

"*Under its own captain,*" he said.

Nobody said anything.

"We've a license since Thursday. We've three drams on the book. We've a sheet and a half and a cord and a Kill with a white boot." He was not looking at any of them. "And there's a thing on a moor that went off on its own, slowly, away from everything, and hasn't been near a house."

"Away from everything," said Kiva.

She had heard it the first time. She had heard it and gone still, as she went still when her two fingers spoke on a rail.

"It went away from the others. Away from the Sag, up onto the moor. There's nothing on the moor. No pumps, no lamps, no tram sheds, no bank." She heard her own voice and made it slower. "Everything that's come up plain since the thaw, which is nothing, would have come up away from things that keep time. If anything's quiet, it'd be that."

"You think it's plain," said Anwen.

"I think it's the only thing in this country that might be. I don't know. I'd want to find out."

Jude had the book under his arm. He had been listening with his head on one side, and now he scratched his jaw, slowly, and looked at the letter on Perrin's knee.

"I'll ask a thing," he said. "Since I'd sooner ask than count it up on a moor."

"Ask," said Perrin.

"What's it for?" said Jude. "The quiet one. If it comes up plain, it's for a hand; it goes in the tin with the eight-tenths, and it's for whoever's hurt next, and nobody can spend it on a floor or a crossing or a bag of chips. If it doesn't come up plain, it's for the book; it's a share, and we spend it at Saltmere on lighting doors in front of a Board. And either way it's a thing that went off on its own up a hill and hasn't hurt anybody, and we'll go up after it and kill it." He looked at the letter. "I'm not saying don't. Loose things on Line ground get taken; I know that. I'd like to know which it's for before I cut it, that's all. I've killed a deal of things this year and never once known before I cut whether it was for a hand or a book."

The yard was quiet. The wet wind came up over the bottom wall and moved the frost-burned grass along the south row.

"For a hand," said Kiva. "If it's plain. That's what we go for. That's why we go up after the quiet one and not the three."

"And the three?"

"The three are between it and us," said Perrin.

He had picked the letter up again. He was reading it a second time, with his lips moving.

"*Three keep together.* That's a pack. On the Edge, north of the Sag, which is where the road goes down to the farms. They've not been near a house since Saturday. That's two days, three by the time we're there." He put the letter down. "If we go for the quiet one and walk past three husks that keep a count, and they're at a farm on Friday, then that's under my name as well. It says so on the paper. *Under its own captain.*"

"So it's both," said Jude.

"It's both. The three first, since they're the ones that'll hurt somebody. Then the quiet one, for a hand." Perrin looked round at them, one after the other, and Kiva saw him do it as Coombe did it, slowly, along the rail, counting heads on a quay. "Thursday. The Line's hut at Cowl Edge. I'll write to Vane tonight. Under my name."

He went into the shed to find a pen. He walked like a man carrying a great deal of something that nobody else could see.

The card came at eleven on Tuesday night, the 17th, three words long, and Kiva was on the van's side bench at a quarter past with Pru beside her and the rings whining.

Varley sat by the tailboard with a cloth on each shoulder, talking to the left-hand one. Pike sat beside him with his arms on his knees. The dressings were thinner again; Ruddock had taken them down that morning to a band at each wrist, and Pike's hands lay on his knees bare, for the first time since the wharf, grey and swollen and very still, with the skin over the knuckles shining.

He saw Kiva look at them.

"They move," he said. "A quarter. I'd not ask them for a cord."

"Nobody's asking."

"I know nobody is." He looked at Varley's hands on the cloth. "That's the trouble."

The van stopped in the flagged yard behind the Corn Exchange, at the top of the hill, where the farmers' carts stood on market days.

It was the flattest piece of ground in Meridian. Kiva had heard that said since she was small, as a joke; it had been built level on purpose, on a raft of brick, so that the corn factors' scales would read true. It was forty paces square and paved with flags as smooth as a table, and lit at its four corners by gas lamps on iron posts, and on the north side was the Exchange's back wall, high and blind, and on the south a row of warehouses with their doors shut.

There was a string in the middle of it, on end.

It had three knots and it was skinned to the knee, and behind it on the flags there was a white patch, three paces long and two across, as thin as breath on a window.

"Gate," said Rhea.

Mace's four brought the wall up across the yard's east side, silver, from the Exchange wall to the warehouses. The string's head came round, and found the light, and it walked across the flags to the wall on its four thick legs, without hurry, and leaned there; and the skin slid after it on the smooth stone and lay down behind its heels, on the west.

Rhea did not read the slate at once. She stood with it in her hand and looked at the yard for a long time, the pale eyes going over it as they went over a street.

"Flat," she said.

"As a table," said Mace.

"Then its sheet's behind it and the rung'll come down at its heels and lie there. There's no downhill to turn it to." She lifted the slate to the van's lamp. "*Before. A purse string of three, on end, in the Corn Exchange yard. Its skin's laid down behind it on the west. Its tear's in the Exchange's coal hole, and the Line's on it. The yard's level; there's no fall to turn it on. Nothing lit inside sixty paces but my gate; the warehouses are dark on the warden's word, and there's a night watchman in the Exchange who's been told to stay in his box.*" She did not lower it. "*The Marshal's page: make it carry its sheet uphill, and if you can't, weight the far edge. There's no uphill. So I'll not cut where its heels are. The far edge: the second's. To say. Two words, once.*" She looked up. "What's there? Mace."

"A join that I like. Stiles is tired."

"Nairn."

"Three rungs. I'd come at it from the east, over the wall, as it leans."

"Pike."

Pike sat on the tailboard. He looked at the flags.

"No fall," he said. "Nothing'll run. Wherever a rung comes down, it lies. I'd say it comes down at its heels, since it always has, and that's the near edge of the white. And I'd say that Sorn's thinking the same thing that I'm thinking, and I'd like to hear her say it."

"I'll cut it from behind," said Rhea.

There was a pause in the van.

"From the west. From its back. Standing on the near end of the white, where Pike stood on the wharf, since it doesn't mind a boot at the near end. A rung comes off where it's cut and falls away from the edge that cut it, if the edge is heavy enough. I've a Torch's edge." She hung the slate on her belt. "If I cut from behind, it falls forward. Off its own heels, east, onto the flags in front of the wall, under the gate's light. And its sheet's behind it, where I'm standing, with nothing on it."

"And you're standing on its sheet," said Mace.

"At the near end. Where it doesn't mind a boot." Rhea put her hands in her pockets. "I'll want weight on the far end before I go. Sacks; we've six in the rack since the wharf. And the second has the far edge, and if she says two words, I come off it."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Varley lays east," said Pike. "In front of the thing. Under the gate's light, between its feet and the wall. If Sorn's right, that's where the rung comes down."

"East," said Varley. "In front."

"Go," said Rhea.

---

Kiva knelt at Varley's left hem, east of the string, under the gate's light, with her back to the wall and the thing's four thick legs a yard from her face.

It was the nearest that she had ever been to a string on its feet. She could see the frost on its knees, and the grain of the skin that it wore to the knee, like canvas, grey-white, close-woven. She could see past its legs, to the west, to the white patch lying on the flags behind it, and to six sacks of coal lying along the patch's far edge, shoulder to shoulder, where Purvis and Hobday had slung them from the warehouse side without setting a boot on the white.

That was hers. The far edge, past the legs, past the patch, three paces off and a yard beyond, under six sacks.

Varley knelt square on the canvas with both knees. His cord was in his coat.

Pike knelt behind him, off the canvas, and a yard to the side, where he could see.

And Rhea Sorn walked round the string's south side, slowly, as she walked on every floor, and stepped onto the white patch at its near end, behind the thing's heels, and stood there.

Kiva saw her do it, and her stomach turned over.

Rhea stood on the skin as Pike had stood on it on the wharf: at the near end, with her whole weight, on a thing that was cold and hard and did not take a boot. She stood a yard behind the string's back legs, with her blade arm down and her left heel flat on the white. She did not look down. She looked up, at the top knot.

"*Not yet,*" said Pike.

His voice was low and thick. He said it to Varley, who was the cloth, and not to anybody else; and Kiva saw Varley's head go a little on one side, listening, as a man listens to a voice in another room.

"*Not yet.*"

Rhea's left heel came down flat.

Kiva saw Pike see it. She saw his bare grey hands on his knees close, both of them, a quarter of the way, as though they had something in them; and she saw his mouth open on the word.

He did not say it.

"*Now,*" said Varley.

He said it off Rhea's heel. Kiva saw that; she saw his eyes on the heel, past the thing's legs, level, as a catcher's eyes are. He had not been told that Pike watched it. Nobody had told him. He had read it on a sheet in a mending room, a week ago, in a girl's square pencil hand, and he had watched the heel.

The yellow light went level along the yard and lit the Exchange's blind wall and the six sacks and Rhea's face, and the rung came off the top of the string.

It fell forward.

It fell off the front of the thing, away from the edge that had cut it, as Rhea had said it would; it came down off the string's chest in a long low heap onto the flags in front of its feet, on Varley's canvas, a pace from Kiva's knee, and lay there, under the gate's light, with nothing white anywhere near it.

"*Dark! One!*" said Varley.

He waited. He waited eleven, looking at the heap.

Kiva did not look at the heap. She looked past the legs, past Rhea standing on the near end of the skin with her blade dark, to the far edge, under six sacks.

It lifted.

It lifted along the row of sacks, the width of a finger, and the sacks creaked, all six, like a gate in a wind, and went white with frost; and it was trying to fold, with a Torch standing on its near end and nothing on it but her, and it could not.

It was trying to fold *her.*

"*Far edge,*" said Kiva. "*Lifting.*"

Rhea came off the skin.

She came off it backward, in one long step, without turning round, the way a woman steps off a kerb when a tram bell rings; and the far edge lay down under its sacks, and was still, and the white patch lay on the flags behind the thing's heels with nothing on it at all.

"*Corners,*" said Varley, at eleven.

---

They took the other two rungs the same way, and Kiva said *far edge* once more, on the third, when two sacks shifted and the white came up between them a hand; and Rhea came off it before Kiva had finished the second word.

On the last knot the string itself came down, forward, onto the canvas, as a tree falls when it is cut on the right side. The skin went as the others had gone. It thinned from the edges, and was grey, and was a film of fine ash on the flags of the flattest yard in Meridian, under six sacks of somebody's coal.

Varley tied all three bags. He tied them right-handed, three turns, the left thumb pinning, as the book said; and he did it in fifty and in fifty-three and in forty-nine, and on each he put his palm on the neck and held it there.

"Loud," he said, three times.

There was a man in a long coat at the Exchange's coal hole. After a time he called *closed,* not loudly, as a man says that he has shut a gate.

Rhea read the slate under the gas lamp at the yard's corner.

"*Closed, ten minutes to one. Lives: none. Works: six sacks, frosted, none split. Tinned: three bags, at trace, clean. A hundred and seventy-one drams, of a hundred and eighty by the book.*" She turned it over. "*Remarks: two. The yard's flat; there was no uphill to turn it to. I cut it from behind, standing on its near end, so that the rungs fell forward off its own heels and away from its sheet. Weight on the far edge. The skin tried to fold the officer when there was nothing else on it, and the second said so, and the officer came off. That remark's mine, and I'd not do it on a wharf.*" She paused. "*And the catcher called the rung off the blade's heel, which he had off a sheet. That remark's the sheet's.*"

She hung the slate on her belt.

"I'd not have stood on that if I'd not had a second on the far edge," she said. It was not said to Kiva. It was said to the yard. "I'd like that written somewhere that isn't my slate."

Kiva wrote it on the back of a laundry order, in the van, on her knee.

Pike did not say anything all the way down the hill.

He sat by the tailboard with his bare hands on his knees and his eyes on Varley's hands, which were folding the left-hand cloth over and over, smoothing it, while Varley talked to it under his breath. Pru was asleep. The rings whined. Ruddock leaned on the tiller and hummed something that Tibb usually hummed.

At the gasworks corner Pike got up, carefully, holding the rack with the heels of his hands, and went to the tailboard, and sat down on the very end of it with his back to all of them and his face to the street going by.

Kiva saw his shoulders move.

It was not much. A man of fifty-four in a coat, with his back to a van, on a cold night, going down a hill; anybody might have thought that he was coughing. She watched it for perhaps a minute. Then she looked away, at the canvas against her knees, because it was not hers to watch, and she had learned that in a van as well.

At the hall gate he came back to the bench before the van had stopped, and sat down, and put his hands on his knees.

"Don't write that," he said.

"I won't."

"I'm asking."

"I know you are. I won't." She looked at his hands. "What shall I write?"

Pike was silent while Ruddock backed the van into the yard. The lamp swung.

"He called it off her heel," he said at last. "Off a sheet that you wrote about me. Eleven years I've called *now* off that heel and never told a soul, and he had it in a week, and he did it better than I'd have done it tonight, since his hands were his own and he wasn't thinking about them." He did not look at her. "I've been sitting on that tailboard thinking about what it's like to watch a man do your work with your trick and do it right. I'd want that in a column. Not the other."

"Which column?"

"*Cost,*" said Pike. "Put it under *cost.* Put that it's the cost of having your craft written down. That somebody can do it without you." He moved his bare grey fingers on his knee, a quarter of the way, and opened them. "And put that it's worth it. Put that I said so. I'll read it by the stove and see if I believe it."

She wrote it in the van, by the swinging lamp, under *Cost,* on the back of the laundry order, with Rhea's line above it.

*The cost of a craft written down is that another man can do it. P. says that it is worth it. He will read this and see whether he believes it.*

Varley had stopped folding the cloth. He was looking at the floor of the van, quite still, with the cloth on his knee. He had heard every word. He did not say anything, and he did not look at Pike, and after a moment he began to fold the cloth again, more slowly.

*There you are,* he said to it, under his breath. *There.*

Jude read the book that night, late, at the Rowan kitchen table, standing by the dresser, with the hunt list on the oilcloth beside it.

"*Held, two and sixty,*" he said. "The Marshal's line's spent. Of the six crossings, three are spent and three are left: one on Sunday, and two at the gate, if the hand lets them. That's nine-tenths still spoken for, near enough. So it's one and seventy uncommitted. *Sealed, eight-tenths.* And a hunt on Thursday, on our own entry, with a card from the Board that wants four-tenths for the privilege of being let walk up a moor." He put the book down. "I'd want everybody to hear that sum before we go. Since it's ours now."

"One and seventy," said Anwen.

"Less four-tenths for the card. Less whatever we light. Less a cord if one's frosted, and a sheet if a sheet is." Jude scratched his jaw. "And plus whatever comes up clean, after shares. The Line's card says shares. It doesn't say what."

"Half," said Mara.

She had been at the far end of the table with the tide table, as she was every night now, and she had not looked up.

"On Line ground. A thing killed on Line ground by anybody who isn't the Line is shared civic first, half, since the city owns the light. Then the casualty reserve, an eighth. Then the Line, an eighth, for its ground and its closing and its hand that weighs. Then the Board, an eighth, on a hunt card; that's new this year. Then the four." She made a mark on the tide table. "An eighth. I had it off Rusk on Saturday; the Works has been arguing with the Board about it since the first month. I didn't think you'd want to hear it before you'd a license."

"An eighth," said Perrin.

"Of whatever's clean," said Mara. "Nothing of whatever isn't. And nothing at all of anything that comes up plain, since plain doesn't go on a hunt card. Plain's medical. It goes in a tin with a seal on it, and nobody shares it, and it's for whoever's hurt." She looked up, at last, over the lamp. "I'd not go up a moor thinking a quiet thing was going to pay for a gate. If it's quiet, it's a hand. That's all it is."

"That's what it's for," said Jude.

He said it quietly, to the book. Nobody answered him.

Kiva sat with her left hand flat on the oilcloth, under the lamp, where the eight fingers could feel the warmth of it and the two could not. She thought about a white line on a forearm an inch above the wrist, and about a moor that she had never seen, and about a thing that had gone up onto it slowly, alone, away from everything that kept time.

She had stood on a skin on a cinder floor with her still door open. She had stood on a road outside a tram shed and watched a pack walk on a count of two for a bank house. She had seen an officer of eleven years step onto a white patch on the flattest yard in the city, because a second would say two words if it lifted.

All of it had been a thing coming toward something that kept time.

She did not know what a thing that went the other way would be. She found that she wanted to know so badly that her hand had closed on the oilcloth, all eight of the fingers that would close; and she opened it again, deliberately, and laid it flat.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday," said Perrin. He had the license on the table in front of him, under the coil of tarred rope. "Cowl Edge. I've written to Vane. Under my name."

# Chapter 15 — Kiva: Something Quiet

The carrier put them down at the foot of Cowl Edge at a quarter to three on Thursday afternoon, with their sheets and their poles and a sack of bread, and turned his cart round in the lane without getting off it.

"I'll be here Saturday at noon," he said. "If you're not here Saturday at noon, I'll be here Saturday at four. If you're not here at four, I'll tell the Line." He looked at the five of them standing in the mud with their gear round their boots. "I've carried a deal of things up this road. I've never carried a team that paid me to go and find something."

"We're licensed," said Perrin.

"I don't doubt it. I've a nephew in the Fourth." The carrier clicked to his horse. "He'd give his ears for it."

The Edge stood up over them in the grey afternoon like the side of a house.

Kiva had never seen it. She had heard of it all her life, as one hears of the sea: a long gritstone scarp on the north side of the Sag, sixty feet high in places, black, with a fringe of dead bracken at its foot and heather on its top; and below it, to the south, the whole country fell away into the great shallow bowl of the Sag itself, where the Line kept its oldest watch. From the lane she could see the bowl's far rim, twelve miles off, blue, with the road to the coast going round it like a thread round a cup.

The Line's hut stood at the Edge's foot, where a path went up through a cleft in the rock. It was built of the same black stone, with a turf roof and one window, and there was smoke coming out of its chimney.

Hollis Vane was standing in its door.

"Cade," he said. "You've come under your own name."

"Yes, sir."

"Then I'll say what I am, once, so that it's said." The Home Line's foreman was a square brown man with a weathered face and a slate under his arm, and he said it in his outdoor voice, as he said everything. "I'm the Line's hand on this ground. I'm a witness and I've a scale. I'll weigh what you bag and comb it and write it on the Board's card. I'll not say a word on your floor. If you're hurt, I'll carry you down. That's all I am." He held out his hand. "The card's four-tenths, to the Board, before you go up."

Jude counted it out of the team's tin into Vane's palm. It was the first ash that Kiva had ever seen a team pay to be allowed to go and do something dangerous, and she watched it go.

"Four-tenths," said Vane, and wrote it. "Come in. I've a fire and a map, and you'll want both."

---

The map was on the hut's table, weighted at its corners with four stones, and it was the Line's own: the Edge and the moor above it, and the gills that came down off the moor into the bowl, drawn in brown ink by somebody who had walked every yard of them.

"The tear was here," said Vane. He put his finger on a place on the moor's top, a mile north of the hut, where three gills began. "On the Saturday, at four. I'd six on the Sag and two up here; we had it shut by eleven. Four came off it first." He moved the finger. "Three went down this gill. Together. Slow. I've had a lad on the Edge with a glass every day since, and they've gone a little way each day, down, and stopped, and gone on. Yesterday they were here." He tapped a place where the gill came out of the moor's edge into the head of a narrow wooded valley. "This morning they were here."

His finger had moved half a mile.

"What's there?" said Perrin.

"Garside's mill." Vane did not look up. "A fulling mill, on the beck. Old. Garside and his wife and two children and a man. It has a wheel."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"A wheel," said Kiva.

"An overshot wheel, eighteen feet. It turns all day and all night, since he has cloth in the stocks for a merchant at the Sag and he's late with it. I sent the lad down this morning to tell him to stop it. He said he'd stop it when the Line paid for the cloth." Vane's voice did not change. "He's not wrong. The Line can't."

"And the fourth?" said Jude.

Vane's finger went the other way. North, up the moor, away from the gills and the valley and the mill, a long way, two miles, to a place where there was a small brown mark in the ink and a word that Kiva could not read upside down.

"*Kiln,*" said Vane. "An old limekiln on the tops. Nobody's burned lime there in a hundred years. It went up there on the Sunday, alone, slow, and it's sat in the kiln's mouth since. It hasn't moved. The lad's been to look twice. It doesn't come out."

"It's gone away from everything," said Kiva.

"It's gone as far from everything as there is on this ground," said Vane. "There's no house within three miles of that kiln. No lamp, no wheel, no water but a spring. There's sheep. It's not touched a sheep." He looked at her for the first time. "I've been on this moor thirty years, and I've never seen a thing come off a tear and go *up.* They come down. They go to houses. They go to lamps and wheels and drains and banks. That one went up and sat down."

Kiva found that she had put her left hand flat on the map, on the brown ink of the moor, and she took it off again.

"The three first," said Perrin.

He said it at once, before anybody else could. He was standing at the table's end, with the license in his coat and the coil of tarred rope over his shoulder like a bandolier, which he had begun to carry without explaining why.

"The three are going to the mill. The mill has a wheel and four people and a man. If we go up for the quiet one first and walk past three husks on their way to a wheel, and they're at the mill tomorrow night, that's under my name." He looked round at them. "So it's the three, at first light. And if we're still on our feet, the kiln."

"At first light," said Vane, and wrote it on his slate. "I'll say nothing. I'll be behind you with a scale."

---

Kiva lay awake for a long time that night on the hut's plank bench, with Pru asleep against the wall beside her and the fire going down.

She was thinking about a wheel.

She had stood on a pavement on the Brickfields road in the tenth month with her palm on a flagstone and felt a push that came, and then a long nothing, and then came again; and it had been a pump, two hundred paces off, driving the water under the street, and the drum that her aunt had filled had fallen in with it. She had stood on the setts outside the tram sheds a week ago and watched a pack walk for a bank house on a count of two.

Everything in this country that came off a tear went toward something that kept time. A string had gone to a drain. A pack had gone to a bank. A cell joined to a bank by nothing but water had fallen into step with it. Three husks were walking down a gill toward an eighteen-foot wheel that turned all night.

And one had gone the other way.

She did not know what that meant. She was very careful, lying on the plank with her eyes open in the dark, not to decide what it meant. She had seen her aunt not decide things for a whole autumn, at a kitchen table, with a pencil, and she had learned how it was done; one wrote down what had happened, and left the square at the end white, and waited.

She only knew what she wanted it to mean. She wanted it so much that it frightened her.

"You're awake," said Jude, from the other bench.

"Yes."

"So am I. I'm thinking about what it's for." His voice was very low in the dark. "I've asked it three times. I'll stop asking it after tomorrow. I'll know."

---

They went down the gill in the grey before sunrise, with the frost on the bracken and their breath going up, and they found the husks' track before they had gone a quarter of a mile.

It was a line of white. Each print was three small round marks, set wide, like the feet of a milking stool, frosted into the dead grass so hard that they crunched under a boot; and there were three lines of them, side by side, and every third print in each line was deeper than the others.

"Stamp," said Perrin, crouching. "That's the one. Every fourth step, they've stamped." He counted along the line with his finger. "One, two, three, *four.* That's light. They're stamping light on four. Like the Pit."

"Then they've still their own count," said Anwen.

"Up here. They had their own count up here." Perrin stood up and looked down the gill, where it went into trees. "We'll see what they've got at the bottom."

They went down in the order that they had settled at the hut, by Perrin's word, and nobody argued. Anwen went first, because a wall wanted to be in front. Then Perrin, with nothing in his hands. Then Kiva with a cloth on each shoulder and her left hand in her coat, and Jude behind her on his bad leg, which was better, and Pru last, with the cords and the spare poles and the comb in a bag. Vane came after all of them, a long way back, with his scale on a strap; Kiva did not hear him once.

The gill went down steeply through birches and then through oaks, with the beck loud beside it, white over black stones. The light came up grey. At the bottom of the trees the gill opened out into a small steep valley with the beck going down the middle of it in a cut of its own, and fields either side, and walls; and at the foot of the valley, a quarter of a mile off, there was a stone building with a slate roof and a chimney smoking, and against its end, black and wet and turning, a wheel.

Kiva heard it before she could see it properly. It was a deep slow sound under the noise of the water, a creak and a heavy wet slap, over and over, as the buckets came round and emptied.

*Creak. Slap.* A long nothing. *Creak. Slap.*

She stopped on the track. She put her left hand flat on the top stone of the wall beside her, light, with the first two fingers on the cold grit and the eight held off it.

*Pushing. And not.* It was very slow. *Pushing.*

"Something's keeping time," she said. "Through the wall. I can't say what time."

"The wheel," said Perrin.

"I'd think so. I can't weigh it."

He stood on the track with his eyes shut, listening, his lips moving. Kiva watched him do it, and saw the moment when he had it.

"*Creak,*" he said. "*And.* It's a two. A slow two. The creak's the one and the slap's the and." He opened his eyes. "Where are they?"

They were in the mill yard.

Kiva saw them from the bottom wall of the last field, where the track came down to the beck. They were standing on the cobbles between the mill's end and the wheel-race, in a row, three of them, tall and grey, with the frost going off them in the grey light and the cobbles white round their feet. They faced the wheel.

They were stamping.

*Creak.* They stamped, all three. *Slap.* They leaned, all three, toward the wheel. *Creak.* They stamped.

It was not their count. It was not the count of four, with a light stamp at the end, that they had walked down the moor on. It was the wheel's. They had come down a gill for two days and stood in a mill yard in front of an eighteen-foot wheel that turned all night, and they had stopped keeping their own time and begun keeping its.

There was a light in the mill house's window. Kiva saw a child's face at it, small and round, and then a woman's hand pulling the child away.

"Under my name," said Perrin, very quietly, to nobody.

He went down to the yard gate, and the others went after him, and he stood at the gate and looked at the yard as Rhea Sorn looked at a street.

"Rowan."

"The yard falls to the race," said Kiva. She had her still door open under her right heel, on the track, by its corners. "East. A hand in four. It's steep. Anything that runs, runs into the race."

"Pryce."

"I'll want a gate between them and the house. And I'll want to turn them, if I can, so that their backs are to the race. It's not a skin; it's a pack. But I'd not have a heap run into that water."

"Talley."

"Three knots. They're close together. I'll cut the near one first, the one by the house." Jude scratched his jaw. "On your *and.*"

"On the wheel's *and,*" said Perrin. "It's theirs now. I'll count it." He took a breath that Kiva heard. "Finch, you're on the rail with the cords and you don't light. Witness."

"Behind you," said Vane, from somewhere up the field.

"*Go,*" said Perrin.

---

Anwen went through the gate first and brought her wall up between the three husks and the mill house door, silver, waist high, across the cobbles. The husks did not turn to it. They faced the wheel and stamped.

Kiva went to her ground. She had chosen it from the field: a place on the cobbles west of the husks, on the uphill side, between them and the house, where a rung that fell would run east down the fall toward her cloth before it got to the race. She tested it, clean, and planted, and stood with her cloth on her shoulder.

"*Creak,*" said Perrin, at the gate. "*And. Creak. And.*"

Jude went in.

He walked toward the near husk on his bad leg with his head on one side, into the breath, with the frost going up his boots. He stood a pace from it. He lifted his arm.

"*Creak—*"

The wheel creaked. The husks stamped.

"*—and,*" said Perrin.

Jude cut.

It was a good cut. It came down on the slap, after the stamp, before the lean, out of time with the husks and in time with nothing; and the near husk fell. It went down onto the cobbles in a heap like poured sand, and Kiva's cloth went over it at the first cast, and she was on her knees on the hem.

"*Dark! One!*"

The other two stopped dead, with their feet up.

*Creak,* said the wheel.

They stamped.

They had not lost it. Kiva felt it go through the canvas and the cobbles, slow, *pushing;* and she saw the two of them come down on the creak together, as though nothing had happened, as though the near one had never been. They had not had to find their count. It was turning in front of them, eighteen feet high, wet and black, and it gave it back to them on every stroke.

"*They've got it,*" said Perrin. His voice cracked. "They didn't lose it. They've— It's the wheel's, they can't lose it, it's *there—*"

Jude cut the second on the next slap.

The knot closed. It closed from the bottom up, as water closes behind an oar, and the third husk leaned in on it, and the two of them stood together, one broad thing with six legs, and stepped onto Jude.

He went back. He went back on the bad leg and it gave, and he went down on one knee on the cobbles in the breath of two husks; and the frost went over his shoulder and down his arm, and he made a sound through his teeth.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen.

Her wall came off the house door and swung across the yard between Jude and the two husks, as she had swung it in the Pit, from the far end; and it stood with the two of them leaning on it, stamping on the creak; and Jude was on the other side of it, on his knee, with his left arm white to the elbow.

"*Talley,*" said Perrin.

"*It's a breath. It's not taken. I can feel my hand.*" Jude got up. He got up badly, and stood. "I can't cut them. Not with that turning. It gives it back to them every stroke."

Kiva was on her knees on the hem with a sealed Handful under her canvas and nobody to haul the corners, and she was looking at the wheel.

It was not hard to see. It had been in front of her since the field. It was eighteen feet of black wet oak, turning on its axle at the end of the mill, and above it, where the race came along the hillside from the beck, there was a wooden channel on stilts that carried the water out over the top of the wheel and let it fall into the buckets; and at the head of the channel, twenty paces up the bank, there was a hatch. A square board of oak in a frame, with an iron bar through a ring at its top, to let the water into the channel or shut it out.

She had seen a bar like it before. She had seen two boys put their whole weight on one, in the dark, under a hill, while a string stood in a drain.

"Stop the wheel," she said.

Perrin turned his head.

"Stop the wheel. They're keeping its time. Take its time away from them." She heard her voice go up and brought it down. "There's a hatch. At the head of the race, up the bank. Shut the hatch and the water stops, and the wheel runs down, and they'll have to find their own count again. And when they're finding it, they've three counts. Like the Pit."

"The miller said he'd not stop it."

"The miller's in the house with his children behind a window," said Kiva. "It's not his to say. It's yours. It says so on your paper."

Perrin looked at the hatch, twenty paces up the bank. He looked at Anwen at the end of her wall, with two husks leaning on it and her heels going back an inch in the cobbles. He looked at Jude with his white arm.

"*Two to the bar,*" he said. "Finch. With me."

"I can't light."

"You can pull. It's a bar. It wants weight, not a door." He was already going. "Pryce. The count's yours. I'm off it. *Yours.*"

There was no gap. Kiva heard it. Anwen had been counting under her breath behind him since the gate, off the husks' feet, as she had counted behind him on a frozen yard with twelve children stamping; and she said it at once, without looking round, with her eyes on the husks and her hands on her wall.

"*Mine. Creak. And. Creak.*"

Perrin went up the bank at a run with Pru behind him, and Kiva heard his boots slip on the frozen turf and catch. He got to the hatch. He put both hands on the bar, and Pru put hers beside them, and they leaned.

The bar did not move.

"It's frozen," said Pru. "It's frozen in its ring."

Perrin planted.

Kiva felt it from forty paces, through the cobbles, through her own planted heel: a still door going down into a hillside like a post going into soft ground, wide, all of it. He had his feet set either side of the hatch frame and his whole weight on the bar, and his still door open under him so that the hill took him and gave him back; and he pulled.

The ice in the ring cracked like a shot.

The hatch went down. It went down into its frame with a groan of wet oak, and the water that had been pouring out along the channel above the wheel stopped pouring, and went back into the beck over the hatch's top in a white sheet, and the channel above the wheel ran thin, and thinner, and was a trickle.

The wheel went on turning.

It had eighteen feet of wet oak and a full set of buckets on it, and it went on turning on its own weight, slower, the creak longer, the slap later, and slower.

*Creak.* The two husks stamped.

*Slap.* They leaned.

*Creak.* It was late. They stamped on the place where it ought to have been, and it came after them, a long beat late; and they stamped again on the creak, and they were late now, and the wheel was slower still.

"*They're losing it,*" said Anwen. "Rowan. They're losing it."

"*Count them,*" said Kiva. "*Not the wheel. Them.*"

The wheel stopped.

It stopped with a bucket at the top, dripping, and a long groan from its axle, and stood. And the yard was suddenly very quiet: only the beck, going over the hatch in its white sheet, and the two husks on the cobbles, with their feet up, quite still, not knowing which foot was meant to be down.

*One,* said Kiva's lips, without her meaning them to. *Two.*

They stamped.

It was not together. The near one stamped, heavy, and the far one a half beat after, as two files of juniors had stamped on a frozen yard when one file sat down. They were not keeping the wheel's count. They were not keeping any count. They were each trying to find one, and finding different ones, and treading on each other's heels.

"*Nearest,*" said Anwen. "*The near one. One. And—*"

"*Talley,*" said Kiva.

Jude was round the end of the wall. He had come round it while she spoke, on his bad leg, with his white arm hanging and his good one up, and he was a pace from the near husk in its breath.

"*—and,*" said Anwen.

He cut.

The near husk fell. It went down in a heap on the cobbles, a yard from the race's edge, and began to run.

It ran east, down the fall of the yard, as Kiva had said that anything would; and it ran fast, open and loose, toward the stone lip of the race, a yard off, and the black water below it.

Kiva's second cloth was on her shoulder. She threw.

It was not a good cast. She threw it from her knees, with her right arm, past Anwen's wall and Jude's legs, at a heap that was moving, and it went out crooked and came down across the heap's leading edge and the race's lip together, half on the cobbles and half hanging over the water.

"*Dark! One!*"

Some of it went in. She saw it go: a grey tongue of ash running off the cobbles under the cloth's edge and over the lip and down into the race, where the water was still moving, slow, back toward the beck; and it went into the water like flour into a bowl and was gone.

The rest lay under the canvas, on the stone, on the lip.

The last husk was alone.

It stood on the cobbles by itself, with nothing to keep time with, no pack and no wheel; and Kiva felt it through the canvas under her hand as she had felt the pack on the road go to a count of two. It was searching. It stamped, alone, and leaned, and turned on its three thin legs toward the mill house, where there was a light in the window and a child's face that had come back to it.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen.

She walked her wall. She walked it from the far end, round, in front of the husk, between it and the house, so that it walked into the silver and leaned there; and she kept walking, and it followed the wall round as a cow follows a fence, until its back was to the house and its face was to the race and the stopped black wheel.

"*Stopped,*" said Anwen.

"*Two,*" said Kiva. She could feel it now. It had found something. "It's on a two. It's walking on a two."

"*One,*" said Anwen. "*And. Two—*"

Jude cut it on the *and.* It fell where it stood, on the uphill side, away from the race, onto the cobbles of the yard, and lay there, and did not run, because the fall of the yard was toward the race and it had come down with its back to the house and its heap behind it, uphill; and it leaned a hair toward the wheel, and settled.

There was no third cloth.

Kiva got up off the hem of the second, where it hung over the race's lip, and went across the cobbles to the last heap, and knelt beside it, and put her right hand flat on the stone next to it, and had nothing to throw.

"*Finch!*"

Pru came down the bank at a run with her own sheet off her shoulder, Mrs. Pryce's grey double having been left at the hut, and threw it over the heap from three paces, flat, at the first cast.

"*Dark! One!*" said Pru. "I'm not lighting. I'm only saying it."

---

It took the best part of an hour to seal the three bags and get Jude's arm into the mill house kitchen, and in that hour Kiva found out what a moving pack had cost them.

The first bag was clean. Anwen and Jude hauled the corners, Jude with one hand, and Pru tied, three turns, the little finger across, and sealed at forty-seven.

The second, on the race's lip, was not. When they drew the corners in, the canvas that had hung over the water came up wet and grey and frozen stiff where the husk's breath had gone over it as it fell, and the neck would not come up straight. Pru tied it at sixty-three, with her teeth set, and said that she would have it written that it was the cloth and not her.

"It's the cloth," said Vane. He had come down into the yard at last with his scale. "That sheet's frosted to the weave. It'll not draw again." He held up the stiff grey corner of it to the light. "I'd not throw that on anything. I'd not lie on it."

It was Pru's sheet. The one with her own name on the pole.

She looked at it. She did not say anything for a moment, and then she said, quite steadily, "It's a sheet. It was always going to be a sheet."

The third bag was clean. Pru tied it on her own cloth at fifty-one.

Vane combed them on the mill's doorstep with the comb from Pru's bag. He held it over each neck in turn, with his head on one side, and Kiva stood beside him with her hands in her coat and listened.

On each the third reed sang, clear and thin.

On each the seventh sang under it. It was very faint, a high thread of a note, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass in another room. But it sang.

"*Ward,*" said Vane. "*Full. Trace.*" He said it three times. "Clean, the three. They'll count. I'll weigh them at the hut."

Kiva had known that they would sing. She had known it since the field, since the moment she saw three husks in a yard keeping a wheel's time; she had known it at the hut, at the map, when Vane said *Garside's mill,* and she had known it on the tram in the first month, when Kellan said that it had all had the hair since the thaw. She did not mind it. It was what the pack was. They had come for the pack because it was going to hurt somebody, and they had killed it, all three, and there was a child at a mill house window with a woman's hand on her shoulder who was going to go to bed that night in a house that nothing was standing outside.

They had killed well. She knew it, and she let herself know it, standing on that doorstep with the frost on her boots. A pack that kept a wheel's time had been broken by stopping the wheel, and a count had gone from the captain to the Hold without a gap, and a wall had walked a thing round so that it fell uphill; and every one of those things had been a word on a sheet in a kitchen a fortnight ago, and none of them had been hers.

The miller came out of his door while Vane was writing. He was a big grey man in a leather apron with his sleeves rolled, and he looked at the stopped wheel and the hatch shut at the head of his race and the white sheet of water going over it back into the beck, and then he looked at Perrin.

"You shut my hatch," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"I've cloth in the stocks for a merchant at the Sag. I told the Line's lad that I'd not stop the wheel unless somebody paid for the cloth."

"I know you did," said Perrin. He had gone red; he did not stop. "They were keeping its time. We couldn't break them while it turned. I shut it. Under my name." He took the license out of his coat, folded, and held it out, as though the miller might want to read it. "That's my name. If you want to send to anybody about the cloth, you'll send to me. T7. Cade."

The miller looked at the paper. He did not take it. He looked at the bag on his doorstep, and at Jude, sitting on his own kitchen bench through the open door with his white arm in Mrs. Garside's lap and a bowl of warm water, and at the child's face at the window.

"I'll open my hatch when you've gone," he said. "I'll not send to anybody." He went back in. At the door he stopped. "You might have knocked."

"I'd no time to knock."

"No," said the miller. "I dare say you hadn't."

---

They went up onto the moor at one o'clock, the five of them and Vane, with two sheets and a frosted one rolled on Pru's back because she would not leave it, and Jude with his left arm strapped across his chest by Mrs. Garside in a linen sling.

"I can cut with the right," he said. "It's the right I cut with."

"You'll cut if you're asked," said Perrin.

"I know I will. I'm telling you that I can."

The moor was not like the gill. Kiva had never been anywhere like it. Once they were up over the lip of the Edge, by the cleft behind the hut, it went away in front of them in every direction, brown and grey and flat and enormous, under a sky that was the same color all the way round, with no tree on it and no wall and no house; and the wind came across it without anything to stop it, from the north, and went through her coat as though she had not got one on. There were patches of old snow in the hollows. There were sheep, far off, that lifted their heads and watched them go by and put them down again.

There was nothing that kept time.

She tested it. She did it every half mile, without stopping, without saying so: a hand flat on a stone, or on the frozen peat at the side of the track, light, the two fingers on it and the eight held off. Nothing. Nothing. A slow push once, which she thought was her own heart, since she had been climbing, and Pru, who had seen her do it, came up beside her and took her wrist without being asked and said *eighty-eight,* and it was.

Then nothing again.

The kiln was where Vane's finger had put it on the map. It was a low round tower of rough stone on the side of a slight rise, a little taller than a man, with a black arched mouth at its foot facing south, toward the Sag, and a heap of old white spoil beside it that the snow had got into. There was a spring below it, in a cut in the peat, with a stone trough.

The thing was sitting in the kiln's mouth.

Kiva saw it from three hundred paces, a grey shape in the black arch, low; and then from a hundred, and it was a husk, sitting down. She had never seen a husk sit. It had folded its three thin legs under it like a cow in a field, and its knot was low, nearly at the ground, and the grey column of it went up into the arch's dark and was lost there. There was no frost round it. That was the first thing that she noticed, and the thing that she remembered afterward: the peat in front of the kiln's mouth was not white. Its breath, if it had one, was not going anywhere.

It did not move as they came. It did not turn whatever it had to turn toward them.

They stopped at fifty paces. Nobody said anything. The wind went across the moor and moved the dead grass round the spoil heap, and that was the only sound.

"Rowan," said Perrin. He said it very quietly, as though the thing might hear.

Kiva went forward alone.

Nobody told her not to. She went to twenty paces, and knelt on the peat, and put her left hand flat on a stone that stuck up out of the ground there, grey and lichened, with the first two fingers on it and the other eight held off, so light that she could hardly have said that she was touching it at all.

She waited.

*Nothing.*

She waited through a slow thirty. She did not count it; she had given that up. She knelt with two dead fingers on a stone on a moor and listened with them as she had listened on a rail and a flagstone and a surface plate and a wharf, and there was nothing. There was a stone. There was the cold, which her thumb told her about. There was not the least push, not now, not after a long nothing, not at all.

She held out her right wrist behind her without looking round, and somebody took it. It was Pru. She knew the fingers.

"Seventy," said Pru, after a moment. "Seventy-two. You're quite calm."

"Then it's not me," said Kiva. "And it's nothing. There's nothing here keeping time. Not the stone. Not that."

She had never in her life put her fingers on anything, since the second month of the autumn, and had them say nothing, and believed them.

She got up and went back to the others.

"It's quiet," she said.

They all looked at the kiln.

"Quiet," said Jude. He was standing a little apart, with his strapped arm across his chest and his good one hanging. "Then I'll ask it, since I said I would, and since it's the last time."

"Ask," said Perrin.

"It's sat there five days. It's not touched a sheep. It went up here away from every wheel and lamp and drain on this ground, and it's sat down." Jude did not take his eyes off the black arch. "If I cut it, and it's plain, it's for a hand. I'll be glad. If I cut it, and it isn't, it's for the book, an eighth of it, and it's sat here five days harming nothing, and I've killed it for a reading." He scratched his jaw with his good hand. "I'm not saying don't. I'm asking what it's for. I'd like to hear somebody say it before I go up there."

Nobody answered him for a moment. The wind went across the moor.

"It's on Line ground," said Vane. He had come up behind them, with his scale. He said it quite gently, for him, in his outdoor voice. "It came off a tear. It's sat five days. It'll sit till the thaw, and then it'll come down, since they always come down; and when it comes down it'll go to the Sag, where there's two hundred houses and a pump. I've seen it. I'd not say that it's harming nothing. I'd say it's waiting."

"You don't know that," said Jude.

"No. I've thirty years of them coming down. I've never seen one go up." Vane looked at the kiln. "I'd take it."

"It's for a hand," said Kiva.

She heard herself say it, and heard how much she wanted it to be true, and said the rest because it was also true.

"If it's plain, it's for a hand. That's why we came up. And if it isn't, it's still a loose thing on Line ground over a valley with a mill in it, and Vane's right, and we'd take it anyway; we'd just not have walked two miles into a wind for it." She looked at Jude. "I can't tell you which before you cut. I've two fingers that say nothing's keeping time, and I've never had them say that before, and I don't know what it means. I'd like it to mean plain. I'd like it so much that I'm not to be trusted about it."

Jude looked at her for a long time.

"That's an answer," he said. "I'd want it said that it's an answer."

He went up to the kiln.

He went alone, slowly, with his head on one side, on his bad leg, with his strapped arm across his chest; and the others came after him to ten paces and stopped. Kiva went to her ground. She tested it, under her heel, by its corners: peat, frozen, solid, and under the peat, a long way down, rock. No room. It fell south, a little, toward the Sag. She planted, a pace south of the kiln's mouth and two to the side, and took the cloth off her shoulder.

Perrin did not count. There was nothing to count.

Jude stood in front of the kiln's black mouth, a pace from the grey thing sitting in it. It did not move. He looked down at it for a moment, and Kiva could not see his face.

Then he lit, and cut.

It came down out of his right shoulder, heavy and straight, into the low knot where the thing sat; and the thing did not stand, and did not lean, and did not close. It came apart. The woman turned round.

She might have been fifty. Her hair was quite white and cut short under a plain cap, and her face was long and pale and very calm, with deep lines at the eyes; and her eyes were grey, and they came to rest on Kiva and stayed there, without searching, as a hand rests on a table.

"Miss Rowan," said Clemency Thrale. "Mr. Cade. Sit down. I'll stand, if you'll let me. I've been sitting with your aunt's letter for two days and I find I've no more use for chairs."

Kiva sat. Her knees had decided, as Anwen's had at a kitchen table.

"You came for a hand," said the Master.

"Yes."

"You wrote that it was not for a year. You wrote it in the third line, and you said that you'd want it said first, and you didn't say it first; you put the hand first." The grey eyes did not move. "I'd like to know why."

Kiva had not expected that. She thought about it, honestly, since she had been asked, in a room where she could not have done anything else.

"Because the hand's why we're here," she said. "The year's why we might not be. I'd sooner you knew what we wanted before you knew what we didn't."

"That's a fair order." Thrale's hands stayed folded at her waist. "I'll say what we are, once, and then I'll say what I'll do, and then you may say no to either. That's the rule of this house. It's the only rule we have that isn't about doors." She looked at the window, at the one light on the far bank. "We keep one door each, for life, and every other shut. We keep no clock and no cell and no pump. We've stepped nothing; we never have. There's plain ash in our cellar that was tinned before the Halden patent was sealed, and some that was tinned before there was a Halden, and it's never been near a thing that kept time. There are forty-one jars of it. There have been forty-one jars since I was made Master, and before that there were forty-four." She paused. "I can hold a great deal, Miss Rowan, and for a very long time. I can't make a jar more than it is. Nobody can. When you go down to the ferry in the morning, there'll be forty."

Kiva did not say anything.

"We don't sell," said the Master. "We've never sold. We've been offered a great deal this winter, by people I'd not have expected to write to us, and we've sold nothing, and we won't. But we've always made good a hand." She looked back at Kiva. "Your aunt's letter says that in the tenth month your team's tin of plain eased her reading hand. One dram and two-tenths, out of two, for a hand that was starved between the cells of a bank on a hill, so that she might read again."

"Yes."

"Then your tin eased a hand. We'll put back what it gave. One dram and two-tenths of plain ward-trace, from our cellar into your tin, so that your tin's what it was before it did a kindness." Thrale's voice did not change. "Not for your team. Not for a floor or a crossing or a bag or a gate. Not for a bank. Not to be sold, whatever you're offered for it; and you will be offered a great deal. For a hand. Whoever's hand is hurt next, in your four or out of it, if it's a hand that plain will ease. It'll be written so in our book and in yours, in your hand and your captain's, and if it's ever spent on anything else, I'd want to be told."

Perrin had gone very red.

"That's binding," he said. "Ma'am. On us. On the tin."

"It's binding. That's why I'm saying it to you, and not only to her. It's under your name, I'm told." The grey eyes went to him for the first time. "You may say no. I'd think none the worse. A tin that's bound is a tin you can't use when you'd most like to, and I'd not pretend otherwise."

Perrin looked at Kiva. Kiva looked back, and did not help him, as she had not helped him on a frozen yard; and she saw him see that she was not going to, and saw him decide.

"Yes," he said. "Under my name. For a hand."

"Miss Rowan?"

"Yes," said Kiva. "For a hand."

"Then it's done." Thrale did not move. "The sister will bring it to you in the morning, in your own tin, under our seal and yours, and you'll both sign. Tonight you'll sleep under this roof, and you'll keep our rule while you do, which is no door lit and no clock wound, and you'll find that it's very easy, since there's nothing here that wants either." She paused. "That's all that's asked. A night. Not a year."

Kiva sat in the chair with her doors lying down inside her like sleeping dogs, and found that she could not think of a single thing to say.

"Your aunt answered two of my six questions," said the Master. "She answered them shortly, and I think truly. She wrote that the other four were yours, if you ever chose." The grey eyes rested on her, quite still. "I'll not ask them. Not tonight, and not by letter, and not if you come back. If you ever want them, the latch is on your side of the door. It always was."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I've only said what's so." For the first time something moved in that long pale face, at the corner of the mouth, and went again. "I'll say one thing more, since you've a long road and I've a short memory for what I've decided not to say. Your sister at the gate wrote, in the summer, that you counted when you read. I've thought about it since. I've not decided what I think. I'd not have you go south believing that I'd decided."

"Nor have I," said Kiva.

"No," said Clemency Thrale. "I didn't suppose that you had."

She turned back to the window.

"I've not been past that gate in eleven years," she said, to the glass. "The Master keeps the house. When she goes out, the house has no Master, and it's never been without one long. I've gone out twice in my life." She was quiet a moment. "I'd like to see the sea again before I'm very much older. I don't expect to. Good night, Miss Rowan. Mr. Cade."

---

Kiva slept that night in a narrow white room off the stone passage, under a grey blanket, with Pru in the other bed and the rain on the window; and she slept as she had not slept since she was small, without waking once, without dreaming of anything at all.

In the morning the sister brought the tin.

It was Seven's own tin, which Anwen had carried down from Meridian in the team's box with the wax still on it, and the sister had taken it from Anwen at the door last night and had it all night in the house. It had a second seal now beside the first, grey wax, with a mark on it like two hands laid flat side by side; and inside it, the sister said, was what had been put back.

She held a comb over it, in the courtyard, in the grey light, before anybody signed. She did it without being asked.

The third reed sang.

The seventh did not.

It did not sing at all. It was not that it sang faint. It was still, a thin bar of metal in a row of thin bars of metal, with the third ringing beside it like a spoon on a glass; and Kiva stood on the wet flags with her hands at her sides and listened to it not sing, and it was the first time since the thaw that she had heard a comb held over ash and heard nothing on the seventh but nothing.

Aske had come out behind them with his bag. He stood beside her and listened as well, and he did not say anything, and she saw that his eyes had gone bright behind his spectacles, and that he was not going to remark on it.

"*Sealed, medical, W, plain,*" said Jude, at the table by the door, writing in the team book. "*Eight-tenths, off a husk on the Sag in the fourth month, 2680. One and two-tenths, given at the house of the Even Hand on Friday the 27th of the second month, to make good what eased M. Rowan's hand. Two drams.*" He stopped writing. "*For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.* I've put that under both lines. I'd want it under both."

"Both," said Perrin, and signed, under his name, and gave Kiva the pen.

She signed. *K. Rowan, catcher, T7. For a hand.*

The sister signed the Order's book, at the same table, with the same pen, and blotted it, and shut it; and then she looked at Kiva across the table, quite calmly, for a long moment, as she had looked at her across a reading room.

"Your lips didn't move," she said. "Last night, at supper. I watched."

"There was nothing to read."

"No," said the sister. "I know."

---

The ferry went at eight.

It was a flat broad boat with a rail round it, worked by a cable and a windlass, and it took the carrier's cart and his horse and the six of them and their sheets and poles and the team's box, with room over; and the ferryman wound the windlass, slowly, and the bank with the slip and the grey house on its bluff went away behind them into the morning, and the brown water went by under the boat's flat bottom with a sound like somebody shushing a child.

Kiva was hot by the middle of the river.

She did not notice it at first. She was standing at the rail with her hands on it, looking south, down the long valley toward the haze where the river went, and the coast; and she thought that it was the sun, which had come out over the Sag behind them and was warm on her back for the first time in weeks. Then she found that her hands on the rail were hot, both of them, the eight fingers that reported and the two that did not, and that the wind off the water, which was cold, was not cooling them.

She took them off the rail. She put the left one inside her coat against her ribs, where the disk was, and the disk was cold, and her ribs were not.

"Rowan," said Pru, beside her. "You've gone red."

"It's the sun."

"It's not the sun. You've gone red from the collar." Pru took her wrist without asking. She looked at the far bank, since there was no clock, and counted under her breath; and Kiva saw her face change.

"A hundred and twelve," said Pru. "You're not frightened. You're not *doing* anything." She did not let go. "Aske."

Aske came along the rail. He took the other wrist and held it for a minute by his watch, with his face quite still, and then he put the back of his hand against her cheek, as her aunt used to when she was small.

"Fever," he said. "Sit down. Here, on the box. Talley. Your coat."

She sat down on the team's box, against the rail, in Jude's coat. The far bank came nearer, very slowly, on the cable. She heard Perrin ask whether it was the hand, and Aske say that he did not think it was the hand; and Anwen say something that she could not hear; and the windlass creak, round and round, slow, and the water shush under the flat bottom of the boat.

Then the roof went.

---

It was not a roof. It was not the sky over a river valley in the second month, grey and white with the sun coming through it. It was nothing.

She had known it would be. She knew it as she knew the order of her own doors. It came as it had come when she was thirteen and on Classification Day, all at once and from every side, and there was nothing over her at all; and the nothing was not empty, and it was not dark. It was enormous. It was paying attention.

There were roads. There were lights on them, going along them, a great many, unlike, from every side, small and far off and moving; and she was above them, as she always was, looking down.

She was not so high as she had been.

She did not know how she knew it. There was nothing to measure it by. But the lights on the roads were nearer than they had been, by a little; she could see that some were bright and some were dull and some were going out, and that some had gone white; and she had not been near enough to see that, before, she thought. She did not know what it meant. She did not want to know what it meant. She was lower, and the nothing over her was paying attention, and she could not have said to what.

She kept her mouth shut.

She found that she was doing it. She had a hand on her wrist and a voice somewhere counting, and something in her that had learned in a van, at a hem, with a cloth under her hands and two words owed and nothing else, had shut her teeth. She did not say anything. She did not say what she saw. She held it behind her teeth, as she had held *Hobday's proud* behind her teeth on a road in the frost, because there was nobody on that boat whose word it was, and it was not her place to say it.

And then there was a bank, and a boat bumping against a slip, and a horse being led off up a ramp, and the sky was a sky again, grey and white, with the sun coming through it.

---

She was on the river by noon, on a long low barge going south with the current, under a canvas awning, on a straw mattress that Aske had bought off the bargeman for a shilling. The fever stayed all that day and all that night, and broke on Saturday at about four in the morning, in a long sweat that soaked the mattress through.

Aske counted it. He sat beside her on an upturned bucket with his watch in his hand and took her wrist every hour, and wrote what he found on her card in pencil, and did not ask her anything at all.

At about two on Saturday morning, when it was worst, he said, without looking up from his watch, "Have you had one of these before?"

"Yes," said Kiva.

He wrote it.

"I'll not ask what," he said. "I'll count."

Pru held her other wrist through most of the night, on her knees on the barge's boards, and did not ask her anything either. Once, when the fever was high and Kiva's teeth were shut so hard that her jaw ached, Pru said very quietly, "You can say it, if you want. Whatever it is. I'll not tell anybody." And Kiva shook her head, once, on the wet straw; and Pru said, "All right," and went on counting, and did not ask again.

By Saturday noon she could sit up. By Saturday evening she could eat. The barge went on down the river, slow, between green banks that she did not know, with willows on them and cattle standing in the shallows and here and there a village with a church; and the river got wider, and browner, and slower, and the air began to smell of something that she had never smelled before and could not put a name to, flat and cold and clean, coming up from the south on the wind.

"Salt," said Jude.

He was sitting at the barge's bow with his strapped arm across his knees, looking south. He had been sitting there since noon.

"It's salt. The bargeman says. It comes up the river on a south wind, thirty miles." He did not turn round. "I'd want it said that I've never smelled it."

She wrote her book that evening, under the awning, by the bargeman's lantern, sitting up on the dried straw with Jude's coat round her shoulders.

It went to Orla on Sundays. It would go to Orla at Saltmere, at the gate, on a crate, on the first Sunday of the third month. It had lain on the kitchen table every night for Mara for a spring and a summer and an autumn and a winter, and it had never once had a page torn out of it, and it had never once had in it what she had seen when there was no roof.

She wrote on the left, where the things went that she had seen.

*Friday the 27th. The ferry below the house of the Even Hand. Fever, from the middle of the river. A hundred and twelve, by Finch. Aske counted it all night. Broke Saturday at four. I can sit up.*

*Cold, the disk. Before and after. I looked.*

On the right, where the reasons went, she wrote nothing for a long time.

Then she wrote one line, small.

*I kept my mouth shut. I'd learned how.*

She shut the book, and laid it on the straw beside her, under her hand. The barge went on down the river in the dark, with the lantern swinging at its bow and the salt coming up on the wind, and somewhere ahead of it, three days off, a gate that would open on Tuesday; and in the team's box under the awning, in a tin with two seals on it, there were two drams of plain ward-trace that were for a hand and for nothing else.

Perrin was asleep against the box, with a coil of tarred rope over his shoulder.

Before he slept, he had looked at the bank going by in the dark for a long time, and said, to nobody, "Sixteen."

It was the twenty-eighth. Nobody had told him to count it. He had counted it anyway.
