## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like

and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that

## Where we enter

It is the night of Sunday the 20th of the third month, 2681, at Saltmere. The National Measure has been read:
the Ropewalk School's first four won, Halden License second, and **Team Seven finished seventh of twenty-four
on 69, on the national license roll by one mark, warranted (as a roll four) for Wednesday**. The Charter
Board's finding named the dream (**the unroofed sky**, "for want of a better", no agreed meaning) and **warranted
a Barrel response at the Saltmere harbour pump house at the dawn slack on Wednesday the 23rd**, on the keeper's
leave: the Saltmere Company's officers; the Works' reader on the landing; the keeper's fitters on the cells; **the

noon on Wednesday: the dawn spring slack at 6.09 by the pump house clock, not before 6.07 nor after 6.12, lasting

inside will keep the bank's count, four and four, at fifty-seven to the minute or faster; nothing else in the
basin will keep a count. The Board has told nobody what the thing in the basin is, because nobody knows.

**The thing in the basin** stands up at each spring slack and grows each time (a hand high in the ninth month,
4' 1", 4' 6½"). It is keeping the harbour's time. It is the first Barrel-class expression on the page. The

## What this movement is for

This is the end of Book Two. It has to deliver a complete, satisfying national-scale ending with its three meanings
paid (the competition, the nation counting what it has, and the beat itself), and it must not be a bridge to Book
Three. The movement has three parts of unequal weight that you may divide as the story wants: **rehearsal** (Monday and
Tuesday, in developed scenes), **the Slack** (Wednesday dawn, the longest and most sustained set piece in the book), and
**after** (the rest of the month and the last of the third month, to Thursday the 1st of the fourth).

### Rehearsal, with one bad assumption found in time and one that is not

Developed scenes of people learning to do a thing together that has never been done at this scale. Build the
operation from parts the reader already owns: the handed crossing (catch, carry, plant, three people with unlike doors,
one caller who has been inside both gaps; it costs almost no ash and a great deal of declared trust; it cannot be done by
strangers); Hart's quiet; Mara's thirty-six counts; Coombe's drills; Ivey's second; Yeo's closing crews; Pike's far
edge; Carrow's lay and Dray's feet; the Graving. **The Graving** (a dry dock roofed with tiers; a floating gate keeps
the harbour out; with the gate shut and the sluices closed it is a basin of still water whatever the tide does outside)
becomes the purse that catches the ash when its gate is shut. Find, in rehearsal, one bad assumption in time to fix it
and let one survive to the dawn. Nobody is rehearsed past what the cohort can bear.

### The Slack: the operation as the map gives it (intent, not choreography)

At the dawn low-water slack of Wednesday the 23rd, a mixed cohort from the Measure's roll and three professional crews
take Saltmere's harbour bank of thirty-six cells **out of step**. The water is still for about twenty minutes. The thing
standing in the basin has to come off the water first.

- **Students** stand the wall, carry, cut on a foreman's slate, and bag. **Kiva calls three handed crossings in a row and
  tests every mark first.** Students close no tear.
- **When a link fails, she supplies it herself: one certified crossing, on a sanctioned mast, read by the office's
  reader.** It is the first decisive thing she has ever done that was entirely inside her certificate. **When a second
  link fails, she makes a second crossing in the same hour, against her healer's card, with the price said first.**
  (A third failing link is not offered: three doors stay three clean.) Place the crossings by the calendar and by
  Aske's card, which Aske says twice.
- **Professionals** close the tear: fitters and keepers under Mara Rowan, Jessamy Hart and the harbour keeper
  Peverell fill thirty-six cells on thirty-six counts. Mara does not read the bank herself and does not touch a cell
  until she decides to; whatever she decides, she decides on the page.
- **The Proprietor holds the harbour's pumps on his own pattern** so that the low town does not flood. The operation
  cannot finish while he holds it, because his pattern is the Step. **He lets go: of the bank, and, in writing, of his
  foundation's claim on "all ash exhibiting the Step, however produced."** His limit is visible: his left hand on the
  glass case, not lifted for more than a minute in seventy years. What it costs him to lift it must be shown and must
  be real. **Master Thrale holds a ward across the dock mouth and cannot move while she does.** Both limits are on the
  page; no Glory master does a student's or a fitter's work.
- **For some seconds two Glory fields from unlike traditions touch. Every lamp on the coast shows a point of light.**
  Three people who have dreamed (Kiva, and the two masters) are seen to count together. Do not explain it.
- **The bank comes out.** The harbour's pumps stand (the Proprietor's hold ends); the low town takes water. **A
  professional died on this coast earlier in the book (Penhale); nobody dies at the Slack.** Carrow and Dray stand on the
  wall and each uses a piece of the other's method. Anwen leads a mixed ward line. Rhea takes a count from her own second.
  Jude, Perrin, Pru, Kellan and Noa have work in it that is theirs. The prediction opens at noon; read what is written
  against what happened, honestly, including where it was wrong.

### After

- **What it does not settle is whether the Proprietor was right.** He has believed for seventy-seven years that

- **Kiva's cost:** the hand is gone to the wrist for three weeks and returns as before. What does not return is the

# Chapter 40 — Kiva: For Want of a Better

The Board's table stood on the floor of the Graving at two on Sunday, on the chalk of the pen's middle line, where the Commodore had lain on Saturday with his tarred hat over one eye.

It was the same long deal table from the Salt Hall, carried along the quay in the morning by the Board's carpenters and lowered eighty feet down the dock on a rope. The Clerk sat at the middle of it with the two Board members on either side. On the table in front of them, in a row, lay the things the Board had asked for. A grey box tied with tape, with a number on its end and a red card in its holder. A black calf book with a straw in it, from Ferren. A larger calf book with a straw in it, from the press behind the keeper's desk at the harbour pump house. Noa's roll of paper with its four columns, and his ledger, and a sheaf of yellow forms. A thin sheaf of cream paper in the Board's clerk's hand, which was everything that had been said at a long table on the Long Quay at nine that morning.

Mara sat on the first bench in front of the table with a single sheet folded in three in her lap, brown wax and black wax across its fold, and her reading hand flat on it.

The tiers went up round them on three sides to the iron and glass roof, and every place on every tier was full. Kiva had never seen so many people in one place. She had thought the Graving full on Friday for Carrow, and on Saturday for the reading. It had not been full. People sat on the steps between the tiers now, and stood along the lip at the top, eighty feet up, against the rail where she had laid her fingers last night, and leaned over it. The green was there, and the grey, and every circuit's colours. The Ropewalk's four sat together on the second tier with Lyle at the end. The Tarn sat in its knot. Meridian sat in the middle, all four of the first company and their reserves: Halden in black and gold, Old Four, Twenty-Eight, and Seven.

Seven was not on the tiers. Seven was on the second bench on the floor, behind Mara, because the Clerk had said so. Perrin sat at the end with his hand pressed to his side. Anwen sat next to Kiva on her left. Jude sat on her right with the book under his good arm, and Pru beyond him with her capped hands folded in her lap.

Kiva was warm. She had been warm since noon. It was not a fever yet. It was the thing that came before one, a heat in the hands and the backs of the knees and a lightness behind the eyes, and she knew it as well as she knew the order of her own doors. She had eaten, and drunk water, and sat in the shade of Quiller's shop with the door shut, as Mara had told her. It had not stopped. She had not expected it to.

Aske was at the end of the bench behind her, with his bag on his knees. He had held her wrist at half past one in the shop and said *ninety-four,* and written it, and not said anything else.

At two, by the clock on the Board's table, the Graving went quiet from the top down.

It went quiet as a field goes quiet when a hawk comes over. It began at the lip, eighty feet up, at the landward end, and came down the tiers row by row, and Kiva turned on the bench to see why, and felt her doors lie down.

The Master of the Order of the Even Hand was coming down the Graving's steps.

She came slowly, a step at a time, with her hands folded at her waist, in plain grey, with a grey cap, tall and thin and very upright. A sister walked a step behind her on either side. The tiers made room for her without being asked. She did not look at them. She looked at her feet on the stone steps, and once, halfway down, she stopped and stood quite still for a count of three with her eyes shut, and Kiva saw the town's count go through her, from her feet up, as cold goes up through a boot. Then she came on.

She did not come to the bench. She walked along the floor of the dock, past the Board's table, past Seven's bench, all the way to the seaward end, where the floating gate held the harbour out, and stood there with her back to its black timber and the sea breathing behind it.

"The Board will put you where you like, Master," said the Clerk.

"Here," said Clemency Thrale. "With the sea at my back. It keeps no time. It helps." Her voice came the length of the dock floor as though there were nothing in between. "I'll stand. I've an hour. I'd be glad if the Board would take me inside it. After it, I'll go back to the Mole."

"The Board will take you in it." The Clerk looked at the clock on her table. "It's two. You'll be gone by three."

"The Charter Board sits again," said the Clerk, "on the matter Miss Rowan put on its table on Friday night. It has before it a sealed page, a notebook, two keepers' books, the Proprietor's evidence taken this morning, and the returns. It'll hear them in that order, and then it'll hear the Master, and then it'll say what it can." She folded her hands. "The page first. Reader."

The Reader of the Compact Library stood up from the end of the first bench, with her two pairs of spectacles and her bright eyes, and came to stand beside Mara.

"Lavinia Arkwright," she said. "Reader, the Compact Library, Meridian. Since 2649." She took the folded sheet out of Mara's lap, without asking, and held it up so that the Board could see the fold. "I took this into my press on Monday the 25th of the sixth month, 2680, at eight minutes to two in the afternoon. It was shut when it came to me, under Mrs. Rowan's own wax and the Works' reconciling stamp. I put my wax across hers and wrote the hour through the join. It's been in my press since, in the dark, with the Library's settlement on the Annick papers, which is the safest place I have." She turned it to the tiers so that the nearest could see the black wax over the brown, and the figures across the join. "It's not been opened. My wax is whole. I've looked at it every hour for two days, and I'm satisfied."

"And when was it written?" said the heavy Board member.

"I don't know, sir. I've never read it. I can say when it was shut." She looked at Mara. "Mrs. Rowan can say when it was written."

"The 29th of the fifth month, 2680," said Mara. "At ten at night, at my kitchen table in Coper Street, in pencil, in my hand. In her words, as she said them, with a wet cloth on her chest, after a fever. The same night." Her voice was dry and exact. "I sealed it at my table on a Sunday night in the sixth month, and took it to the Library on the Monday. The Reader put her wax over mine before Master Annick's box was opened. Then my niece read the notebook, in the Library's room, with the page already in the press. The Reader saw her read it."

"I did," said the Reader. "Her lips counted. I wrote it down."

The Clerk looked at Mara.

"Open it, Mrs. Rowan."

The Reader gave the page back to Mara. She took a small knife out of her satchel, a bone-handled thing with a thin blade, and held it out handle first. Mara did not take it at once. She turned on the bench and looked at Kiva.

Kiva put her left hand on her aunt's right, over the knife's handle. Her hand was hot. Mara's was cold. Neither of them said anything. They put the blade under the black wax together, and the Reader's seal lifted off the brown with a small dry sound like a twig, and then under the brown, and Mara's own seal and the Works' stamp came away in two pieces and fell on the dock's stone between Mara's boots.

Mara unfolded the page.

It was pencil, in her own small exact print, on one side of the sheet. Kiva could see it from the bench. She had not seen it since the night it was written. She had not read it then. She had lain on her bed with the cloth on her chest while her aunt wrote it at the kitchen table, and Mara had sealed it on the Sunday without reading it over, because she said she knew what was on it.

"Read it," said the Clerk.

Mara read it.

She read it in her ordinary voice, the voice she used to read a fault report across a counter, dry and level and exact, and every word of it went up the stone walls of the Graving into the tiers and the iron and glass roof, and nobody in four thousand people made a sound.

*"The 29th of the fifth month, 2680. Ten at night. K.R., told at the kitchen table after a fever, in her words. Written by M.R."*

*"She was high. Above things, looking down. There were roads, from every side at once, and lights on them, a great many, coming. The lights were all unlike. She says they were people. She didn't know them. She knew what they were, because she knew what she was."*

*"There were notes. That's new. She tried to count them and lost her number at four."*

Somewhere high in the tiers, very small, somebody said *four* under their breath, and somebody else said *hush.*

At the end of the Board's table, Noa had not moved. Kiva saw his pencil come down on his ledger and write, small. She knew what he was writing. *N.B.* She knew he would not write anything after it.

*"The lights touched. When they touched, she couldn't tell where one stopped. There was one note, then, that had been a great many."*

*"She looked up. There was no sky. Not dark. Nothing. And in it, something enormous and close. Not heavy. Full. She says it was paying attention."*

*"Every lamp in the city answered."*

*"And under it, at the very end, something small. It ticked. Once. She says it's the word she'd use for a bag, and for a drum pen, and for this. I've written: the word she'd use for both."*

Mara lowered the page.

*"Sealed, M.R."* she read, from the foot. *"That's all there is."*

She folded it again, along its old folds, and held it in her lap under her reading hand, and did not look at anybody.

The Graving did not make the warm sound, or the long sound, or the sound it had made for Carrow. It did not make any sound at all for so long that Kiva could hear the sea against the floating gate, three hundred paces off, where the Master of the Order stood with her back to the timber. She could hear, under it, through the stone of the bench, the town's count, faint, four and four.

She sat with her hot hands in her lap and found she was not ashamed. She had thought, on the bench, that she might be. She had thought that hearing her own words read out in her aunt's voice, the words of a girl of seventeen with a wet cloth on her chest who did not know what any of it meant, would be like being stripped in front of a town. It was not. It was like hearing a fault report read: a thing that had happened, written down the same night by somebody who knew how to write things down, and kept in the dark so that it could be believed.

She thought it was the best page her aunt had ever written. She thought she would tell her so, sometime, when it would not make her angry.

"The notebook," said the Clerk. "Reader. Master Thrale."

The Reader had untied the grey box's tape. She took out a notebook with a cracked green cover, no bigger than a field book, and held it in both hands. Then she turned, not to the Board, but down the length of the dock floor, to the woman standing at the gate.

"Master," she said. "This is your Order's. It came to my Library by a theft eighty years ago, and the Order's been asking for it back for eighty years, and the settlement of 2611 says it's read in the room and not copied. I've brought it two hundred miles under my own custody because the Board asked." She held it a little higher. "I'll not read it aloud if you object. I'll take it home unread and write in my book that I was asked not to, and by whom. Nobody will think the worse of either of us."

Thrale stood with her hands folded and the sea behind her.

"She told nobody in her house," said the Master. "She wrote that. I've read it. Once, in your room, in 2670, when I was made Master, under your rule, with a Halden clerk by the window watching my lips." Her voice did not change. "She said they'd make it into doctrine by spring, if she told them. She was right. They would have. We would." She was quiet a moment. "Read it. In front of everybody, so that it belongs to nobody. And don't make it anything."

The Reader opened the notebook at a slip, and read.

She read it as she had read a night clerk's margin to Kiva in a reading room in the summer, in exactly the same voice, without looking up until she had done. *Fever again. Fourth time with this dream, so I will write it. Somebody should.* The roads. The lights, each a different light. *I know what they are, because I know what I am, and they are that.* The sky not torn. *As if someone had taken the roof off a house in the night and the house woke and found itself under the stars.* What came down, not crushing, full, like standing under a wave that does not break. *It is paying attention.*

*"Every lamp in Meridian answers at once. Some go out. Some go white. One turns a colour I have no word for."*

Kiva kept her face still. She had kept it still in the reading room when her lips counted. She kept it still now, on a bench in a dry dock with the whole town round her, and she did not look along the bench at Mara, and she did not look up the length of the dock at the gate.

*"I do not know if we gathered to hold it shut, or to break it open, or to take it, or only to stand there together when it came. I wake before I find out. Every time."*

*"The sky unroofed. That is the only way I can say it."*

The Reader shut the notebook.

At the gate, Thrale had not moved. But her folded hands had moved. One thumb crossed the other, and went back.

The keepers took less time, and they were worse.

The Reader read the Ferren page for Lumb, who was two hundred miles away being uncivil about it: a fever of three nights, entered because the needles did it too, and the roof off the house, and all four-and-twenty standing up together, looking.

Peverell read her house's page herself, standing, the long *s* and *something was attending* and the white lamps on the Long Quay, and sat down without a word.

The Board's thin clerk read the Proprietor's evidence from the cream sheets, every word that had been said at the long table on the Long Quay at nine, in a flat careful voice. The town had heard about the window at second hand for seventy-seven years. It had never heard the line that came before it. *I dreamed that night.*

And then the question, and Kiva's answer, in her own words, as he had asked.

*Some had gone white. On the ferry. I was lower than I'd been. I don't know what it means. I'll not have it said I saw more.*

And under it: *A thing she has no word for. Not put on the table.*

The Graving heard it. Kiva heard it hear. There was a sound from the tiers like wind in a field, a long way off, and then it stopped. Halden's counsel, at the end of the first bench, did not move, and did not write anything in his folder. Two benches back, Kellan sat with his hands in his lap and his face quite white, looking at the cream sheets in the thin clerk's hands.

"That's Mr. Halden's evidence," said the Clerk. "Under his own name. The returns, Mr. Bexley."

Noa read the returns. He read Westerley, and Tarnfoot, and Holloway, as he had read them on Friday, and the Graving was quiet for every one of them, and nobody laughed. Then he picked up one more yellow form, which was new.

"This came in at eight this morning," he said. His thin voice was very clear. "From the Holloway lamp office. I wired the warden myself on Friday night after the Salt Hall. I told him what had been said in the hall, and by whom, and under what name. I asked him to tell the lamp-man." He did not look up from the form. "I'd no business to. It's not a reader's place. I'd like it written that I did it, so that nobody thinks the warden thought of it."

"Read it, Mr. Bexley," said the Clerk.

He read it.

*HOLLOWAY LAMP OFFICE. THE NIGHT LAMP-MAN HAS ASKED THAT IT BE WRITTEN NOW. HE SAYS NO ROOF. NOTHING OVER. SOMETHING THERE ATTENDING. THE LAMPS ON THE RING WHITE AND OUT BOTH. HE SAYS IF THE GIRL CAN SAY IT TO A BOARD UNDER HER NAME HE CAN SAY IT TO HIS WARDEN UNDER HIS. HIS NAME IS ON THE FORM. HE ASKS IT NOT BE PRINTED.*

Noa put it down.

"His name's on the form," he said. "I'll not read it, because he's asked. The Board can see it."

Kiva did not hear anything for a moment after that. She was aware of Anwen's shoulder against hers on the left, quite hard. She was aware of Jude on her right, very still, with the book under his arm, and of his hand, which had come off the book and lay on the bench between them, flat, not touching. She was aware of the heat in her own hands, and of the page folded in Mara's lap. And she was aware of a man two hundred miles off who had lit lamps for a living and had a fever and had been laughed at once in a gallery. He had read in a wire that a girl had stood up, and he had stood up.

She had not done it for him. She had done it so that the thing would not live in one head. She had not thought, once, that it would get another head to say it.

The Clerk looked at the clock on the table. "It's twenty-five to three," she said. "Master."

Clemency Thrale did not come forward. She spoke from where she stood, with her back to the floating gate and the sea breathing behind its timber, and her voice came the length of the dock as though there were nothing in between.

"Clemency Thrale," she said. "Master of the Order of the Even Hand. Glory. One door, for life: a ward. I've kept it since I was nineteen, and every other door I have shut." Her folded hands did not move. "I've not been past my house's gate in eleven years. I came past it on Wednesday, for a cellar, and I find I'm to say a thing in a dock as well. I'll say it once."

The Graving was so quiet that Kiva could hear her own heart, which was going a little fast, and a little hot.

"I've dreamed it," said Thrale. "Three times. At nineteen, the night I took my door. In 2670, the night I was made Master, in my own house, with nothing in it that kept time. And on the 17th of the ninth month of last year, at my window over the river, at twenty past two in the afternoon, with a fever that came on me at the window and was gone by dark." She did not look at anybody. "No roof. Nothing over. Not empty. Something paying attention. I'll not describe it further. The Master Annick described it better, and so did a girl of seventeen, and so, I find, did a man I've been told for eleven years is my house's enemy."

On the bench, Kiva felt her doors, which had been lying down since two, lie flatter still, as dogs do when the person they do not need to watch sits down.

"I've told nobody," said Thrale. "Not my house. Not the sister at the gate, who's standing behind me and has just heard it with everybody else." She did not turn round. "I'd my reasons. They were the Master Annick's. A house that keeps one door each would make a dream into a rule by spring, and I've spent my life making sure it hasn't any rules but the ones it needs." She was quiet for a moment. "Mr. Halden reads it one way. I've heard his reading now, from the Board's clerk. He wants every lamp in the country white, keeping one time, so that whatever comes finds them lit."

She looked down the length of the dock, past the Board's table, toward the Long Quay, which could not be seen from the Graving's floor.

"I read it the other way," she said. "The Master Annick didn't say white was good. She said some went white and some went out, and one turned a colour she'd no word for, and she woke before she found out what any of it was for. I don't know what it's for either. I know that it asks. It pays attention. It comes in where there's a door open to it." Her voice did not rise. "I'd have one door, and one quiet thing, and let nothing in that asks. That's my house's reading. That's why we keep no clock. I don't say Mr. Halden's a fool. He was at a window and I wasn't. I say I'd sooner a house where nothing answers."

She stopped.

"Is that the Order's position, Master?" said the heavy Board member.

"It's mine. The Order hasn't got one. If it had, I'd have to resign." Something moved at the corner of her long mouth, and was gone. "That's all I'll say of the dream. Now the cellar, because that's what I came for, and I've a quarter of an hour."

"Thirty-six jars," said Thrale.

She did not move from the gate. "There were forty-one in my cellar when I was made Master," said Thrale. "There were forty-four before that. "My house has never sold," said Thrale. "Nor given to a bank. Nor to a floor, nor a pump, nor anything that keeps time. We make good a hand. "Keeper. I said on the quay you'd hear the price first. Here it is." She did not raise her voice. "Rowan," said Aske, behind her, very low. He had her wrist already. "A hundred and two."

"I know."

Mara stood. She still had the page folded under her reading hand.

"The Works hasn't got one either," she said. "The Works doesn't believe anything that hasn't happened. Mr. Rusk will tell you so." She did not look at Rusk. "I'll tell you mine, because you've asked. The Clerk of the Measure stood up.

She did not look at a paper.

"The Board records that a thing has been dreamed, with a fever, by people who never heard of each other. By the Master Annick of the Even Hand, between 2446 and 2450, in a notebook she showed nobody. At the end of the first bench, Peverell's small crabbed hands closed in her lap.

"By the keeper at Westerley," said the Clerk, "that night, in the morning, to his wife. By Mr. Ambrose Halden, that night and eleven times since. By the Master Thrale, three times. "The Board believes that page. It's had thirty years of pages in a great many rooms. It knows a shut one."

The Graving did not make a sound.

"The Board can't say what was dreamed," said the Clerk. "It's heard three readings of it this afternoon, and they don't agree, and none of the people who gave them say they're certain. It was a page that had only two lines on it. Kiva had seen it once, in the autumn, upside down, across the Chair's desk. It was headed *Witnessed,* because there had been no column for her, and there was still no column for her.

The Chair wrote a third line. She wrote it sitting, in her level hand, while the Graving stood up around her, and blotted it, and turned the ledger round on the table, and held it so that Kiva could read it from the bench.

*Sunday the 20th of the third month, 2681. Put it on the table herself, under her own name, before anybody asked. Y.C.*

Kiva read it twice.

Then Aske stood up behind her, and put his hand under her arm, and said *now,* and she went.

---

The fever came up along the Long Quay, in the white lamps, as it had come a week ago.

She had expected it. She found expecting it did not help. By the third bollard she was shaking, and by Quiller's door she could not have climbed the stair if Jude had not taken one arm and Perrin, against every card he had, the other, until Aske said *Cade* in a voice Kiva had never heard him use, and Anwen took Perrin's place without a word.

Aske had her on the bed by the north window inside a minute, with the candle lit and his watch out and Pru on her knees on the other side with her capped fingers on Kiva's wrist, very lightly, because she could not grip.

"A hundred and four," said Aske. "Going up." He put the back of his hand against her cheek, as her aunt used to. "Two of them. In one day. A man with his hand on a glass in the morning, and a woman with her back to the sea in the afternoon." He wrote it on the card, standing, against the window frame, and Kiva watched him write it and could not read it for the heat. "*The third this year. Two Glory masters inside six hours.* That's all I'll write. It's all I know."

"I knew," said Kiva. Her teeth were together. "On the quay. Before."

"I know you did. You sat in the far chair this morning and you stood up for her this afternoon anyway." He sat down on the upturned bucket. "I'll not say you shouldn't. I'll count."

She lay with her eyes open and her jaw shut and waited for the roof to go.

She knew how it would come, if it came. She had felt it come on a ferry with her hands on the rail. She lay with the candle burning and Aske counting and Pru's light fingers on her wrist, and the quay lamps in a row along the black water outside the window. The fever climbed through her like a tide coming in over flats. She waited for the nothing and the roads and the lights, and for whatever was paying attention to pay it to her.

It did not come.

There was the loft's ceiling, dark, with its beams. There was the candle. There was the count, under the floor and the chandlery and the quay, four and four, going on. There was Aske's voice, saying a number every few minutes, and Pru's breathing, and somewhere at the far end of the loft somebody turning over who was not asleep.

At a quarter to seven, by Aske's watch, with the fever at a hundred and four and her teeth together, she took her left hand out from under the blanket and laid the first two fingers on the iron rail of the bed. She did it on purpose. It was the hour.

*It keeps time.*

"Write it," she said. "Please. I can't."

Pru took the book off the sill and the pencil on its string. She wrote it, with her capped fingers, very badly, worse than Kiva's left hand. *Sunday the 20th. A quarter to seven. The bed rail, the loft. Yes. Written by Finch.*

The fever broke a little after eleven, in a long sweat that soaked the blanket through, as it had on the barge and on the night after the Counting House. Aske said, "There," and wrote it, and Pru sat back on her heels with her hands held up in front of her face.

"Nothing," said Kiva.

She said it aloud, as she had said it a week ago. Her voice was very hoarse.

Aske looked at her over his spectacles in the candlelight.

"Nothing came," he said. It was not a question.

"Nothing came." She shut her eyes. "That's all. I'd have told you if it had. I'd tell anybody now."

He did not say anything to that for a while. Then he wrote one more line on the card, small, and this time he turned it round and showed her, because she had asked nobody to keep anything from her any more, and he seemed to know it.

*Fever, the third. Nothing came. Said so.*

---

At ten o'clock, while the fever was at its height, the Mercury's boy had come up Quay Street with his bag, and Jude had gone down and bought the late sheet, and stood at the window with it under the candle for a long time, reading, with his good arm on the frame.

He did not read it to her. He folded it and laid it on the sill. She saw it there when she opened her eyes at midnight, with the fever gone and the blanket soaked and Aske asleep at last on his bucket with his chin on his chest.

The headline went across the top of the folded sheet in letters as big as her thumb, and the candle lay on it.

*THE UNROOFED SKY.*

And under it, smaller: *The Board Names What Nobody Can Say. Two Masters and a Catcher. The Order Gives Its Cellar to the Bank. The Board Warrants Wednesday.* And under that, in a column with a line round it: *"Some Go Out. Some Go White." "I'll Not Have It Said I Saw More."*

She looked at it for a long time.

It was a name. It was not hers. It had been a Glory master's phrase in a notebook nobody had been let read, and a clerk had borrowed it in a dock for want of a better, and now it was in letters as big as her thumb on a penny sheet that a boy with ink on his thumbs had sold up and down Quay Street. By the morning it would be in every town on the coast. By the end of the week it would be in Meridian, on Coper Street, in the shop.

Everybody in this city knew that she dreamed. Everybody would know by Wednesday. Nobody knew what it was. The Board had said so, and so had the Proprietor, and so had the Master, and so had she.

She found she did not mind it. She had thought she would, for four years. She had kept her mouth shut for four years so that she would never have to lie in a bed and look at a sheet like this. And now she was lying in a bed looking at a sheet like this, and it was only a name. It did not say what the thing was. It did not say what she was. It said that a thing had been said aloud, and that nobody owned it now, not her, not the Proprietor, not the Order, not the Board. It belonged to the lamp-man at Holloway as much as to anybody.

She put her hand inside her shirt and found the disk.

It was cold. It had been cold at the far end of the Proprietor's table that morning, and cold on the bench in the dock while the Master stood with her back to the sea, and cold through the fever. She had looked each time. She would write it in the morning, on the left, with her left hand. *Cold. Two masters. As before.* There was no crossing, so the fourth column was owed nothing. She would write it anyway.

At the far end of the loft somebody was still not asleep. She could see the shape of him at the south window, sitting up. Kellan. He had a card in his hand, a white one with a Halden mark, and he was looking at it in the light from the quay. She did not know what was on it. She would ask him in the morning. She thought he would tell her, and that it would come out as a sum.

Beside her bed, on the next one, three feet off, Selka Dray was lying on her back with her eyes open, looking at the ceiling. She had been there all evening. She had not said anything. Now, as Kiva turned her head, Dray turned hers.

"Wednesday," said Dray, very low. "If they ask the roll."

"Yes."

"I'll be there." Dray looked back at the ceiling. "On my feet. I'd sooner you heard it from me."

She shut her eyes.

Kiva lay with her hand on the cold disk and her eyes open.

Outside the window, under the white lamps on the Long Quay and the yellow ones in the low town, Saltmere went on keeping its count. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster. At the end of the Mole, in a swept hut on the sea side where nothing kept time, a woman in grey who had broken her house's only rule was lying awake with her hands folded, and two sisters were writing it in a book. On a cart at the seaward end of the Graving, under a grey cloth, thirty-six jars stood in their straw, given. On the second floor of a pump house a keeper had written a line in her great-grandfather's book and gone to bed for the first time since Tuesday. In a tall grey house on the Long Quay, every window lit, a man of a hundred and three sat with his left hand flat on a glass case and, Kiva thought, his right over his eyes, where nobody could see him.

In the Board's press, in a black iron box with three locks, there was a paper with a seal on it and the hour, which would be opened at noon on Wednesday. At dawn on Wednesday, if two people were right, the basin would go still at nine minutes past six. There were three days.

And after that there was Rhea Sorn's last night on the van, and the last of the third month, and a card that had not been written yet, and either of them might say no.

The Measure was read. Seven was seventh. The unroofed sky had a name and no meaning.

She slept at last with her hand on the disk, and dreamed of nothing at all.

## 1. Book promise

Kiva Rowan ended her first collegiate season seated, unclassified, with eight fingers that
report and a company's card that promises no seat. Book Two takes her two hundred miles to the
coast. She spends a winter with her hands dark, kneeling at a professional's hem with her
mouth shut. She trains for Riftspawn that no longer die by the book. She stands the National
Measure at Saltmere among twenty-four fours and schools that solved her problem a different
way. And she finds that the thing Meridian did once, by hand, to one bank on one hill, has to
be done on water that never lies still.

The title carries three meanings, and the book pays all three: the competition; the nation
counting what it has (keepers' books, banks, fevers, plain ash); and **the beat itself**, the
count of four that more and more of the country's ash is keeping.

## 2. The argument

Book One asked why the post is crooked and answered: keep the reason with the method. Book
Two asks what happens when a method has to **travel.** Mara's eleven sheets reach the coast and
fail there, because a harbour is not a storm drain. Kiva's three-door sequence cannot travel
at all; it lives in one damaged hand. What can travel is the part she has been made to learn
all winter: say what the piece is, who has it, and when it changes hands.

Her earned capability in this book is not a fourth three-door. It is the **handed crossing**
(working term): catch, carry, and plant done by three people with three unlike doors, with
one caller who has been inside both gaps and can say when the load passes. It costs almost no
ash. It costs trust, rehearsal, and a count that is not hers.

## 3. Complete ending

**The Measure is read in full.** No board is struck. The Ropewalk School's first four wins the
National Measure. Halden License is second. **Team Seven finishes seventh of twenty-four and
makes the national license roll, which takes eight, by one mark.** Seven wins one board
outright, the Carry, on Perrin Cade's back. Selka Dray beats Kiva in a lane, on a declared
count, with a needle on her, exactly as promised; Seven wins the lane on the other three.

Kiva loses that exchange by choice. One crossing in seven days is all her card allows, and by
then she knows what the week's crossing is for. She fights Dray on one door and says why
beforehand, to Dray's face.

**The dream becomes a public fact.** Before the Charter Board, Kiva has Mara's sealed page
opened: timed and sealed before she ever read Annick, it proves that her account was her own.
Beside it lie Annick's entry, the Ferren keeper's line of 2604, the Saltmere harbour keeper's
line for the same night, and the first returns of a national reading of remarks columns. Two
living Glory masters then say under their own names that they have dreamed it: Clemency
Thrale, Master of the Order of the Even Hand, and Ambrose Halden, the Proprietor, who was
alive on the night in 2604. A Board clerk borrows Annick's phrase, and the papers print **the
Unroofed Sky.** Nobody can say what it means. The two masters read it in opposite ways.

**The Slack.** At the dawn low-water slack of Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, a mixed
cohort from the Measure's roll and three professional crews take Saltmere's harbour bank of
thirty-six cells out of step. The water has to be still, and harbour water is still for about
twenty minutes. The thing standing in the basin has to come off the water first. It is the
first Barrel-class expression on the page, and it is keeping the harbour's time.

- **Students** stand the wall, carry, cut on a foreman's slate, and bag. Kiva calls three
  handed crossings in a row and tests every mark first.
- **When a link fails,** she supplies it herself: one certified crossing, on a sanctioned mast,

  entirely inside her certificate. **When a second link fails,** she makes a second crossing in

dearer, deliberately uneven grid. It is a real national answer and it costs light, money, and
an industrial house its oldest property.

**What it does not settle** is whether the Proprietor was right. He has believed for
seventy-seven years that stepped lamps are the ones that go white instead of out.

**Kiva's cost.** The hand is gone to the wrist for three weeks and returns as before. What

answered. The disk is cold. Kiva writes that down.

# Kindling 2680, Book Two — universe bible

> Built by TAC

Status: **planning.** This file does not replace
`books/book-2680-opening/UNIVERSE_BIBLE.md` or `universe/UNIVERSE_BIBLE.md`; read both. It
restates the rules Book Two leans on and adds only what Book Two needs. Three labels are used

- `PLANNED` — intended for Book Two. Not canon until surviving prose says it.

## 1. Rules that do not move (`ESTABLISHED`)

- One source, three traditions: Ember, rift-granted magic (the hand door: Ward, Sustain), and
  internal cultivation (the still door). The soul fills. Ash is ownerless, finite fuel.
- The rank ladder: Kindled, Ember, Flame, Fire, Torch, Blaze, Glory. Rank and breadth are one
  axis; skill and control are another. Hold, Draw, Call, yield, grade, resonance, license,
  and standing are separate measures, and none pays for another.
- **Draw** is ash spent on a piece of work over the book expectation for it: 1.0 is standard,
  lower is better.
- A kill does not raise rank. Ash funds the next attempt. Learning is what makes it survivable.
- Ash is recorded by **yield** (Handful, Flask, Barrel, Well), **grade**, and **resonance.**
  The comb's third reed sings for ward-trace (W). The seventh (I) is cut to the Step.
- Glory stops ordinary aging, is rare, and cannot be granted, bought, or stolen. It widens
  what one person can hold: throughput, duration, precision. It never makes ash free.
- Riftspawn expressions echo worked powers with nobody home to direct them. Spawn anatomy
  as trained: breath; posts or load paths; knots; a cord; a hem; the tear; closure.
- Students close no tear. Licensed crews do. Radiation work and licenses not held are not
  taken over in a crisis.
- Classification is physical: rigs, masts, pens, tapes, projected tables, human rulings. No
  private overlay.
- **Doors, for Kiva.** A competition is all doors open and fighting. A stack is two open
  together and agreeing: soft, cheap, and open to anything near; forbidden to her. A sequence
  shuts each door before the next opens, and each gap costs about a quarter of a dram with
  nothing open. A crossing is one gap. Three doors are two gaps.
- **The disk** does not teach, speak, map, choose, open a door, or answer. Two bearers have
  written *warm, once,* after a first crossing, and *cold* ever after.
- **The dream** is a fever, not a prophecy that can be consulted. It cannot be summoned. Each
  recurrence has a bodily cost. Triggers on record: fever, ash overload, filter instability,
  close contact with Glory.
- **The Step** (Halden's foundation patent, 2391): a row of cells salted late with one pinch
  of ash already made to keep a beat thereafter beats as one and can be driven by one
  operator. Used in Halden's yards, refineries, lift yards, and big tram sheds. Pump houses
  and tenement boxes were never meant to be stepped. The claim covers "all ash exhibiting the
  Step, however produced." The caution: "Not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under
  load." Before the patent the Wren House called it *counting ash.*
- Shown, not explained, in Book One: stepped pumps make water rise and fall on a count of four
  and four; a cell joined to a stepped bank by nothing but that water goes into step; a tear
  wants "the thing that keeps time" and not the bright thing; a string with an arm in the
  water is itself a pump; cut cords spill open ash that can cook a bank.

## 2. The country (`PLANNED`, built on `ESTABLISHED` pieces)

- **The Charter Board** (`ESTABLISHED` as a name) licenses rift companies nationally, keeps the
  national roll, and runs the National Measure. It can ask a nation to look at its banks. A
  Circuit cannot.
- **Six circuits** send their first company of four fours each: **twenty-four fours.** Meridian
  sends Halden License, Old Four, Team Twenty-Eight, and Team Seven. Saltmere hosts. The other
  four circuits are named as the lanes need them.
- **The National Measure,** Monday the 14th to Saturday the 19th of the third month, 2681.
  **Four boards,** as at the Regional, each marked by place, twenty-four down to one, and
  added. The Hunt stands where the Regional's Call stood:
  1. **The Bench** — banks of cells with a fault planted; find, quench, re-tune, restart.
  2. **The Carry** — rescue and extraction against a clock.
  3. **The Hunt** — a licensed live hunt on tidal ground under company officers, marked on
     clean yield after shares, less every remark. Students close nothing.
  4. **The Lane** — the declared-role lane, as at home: three for the litter over a team's own

  Barrel-class responses under a company officer. Placement therefore decides who is sent for.

## 3. Saltmere (`PLANNED`)

- A harbour city. The Saltmere Company's green is `ESTABLISHED`, as is its harbour bank of
  thirty-six cells, in step since twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month, 2680.
- **The Ropewalk School** leads the host circuit. It teaches two-door candidates to open both
  doors together and twist them to agree: **a lay,** *laid* doors. It is what Meridian calls a
  stack. It is cheap, quick, and soft, it wins lanes, and its circuit has a column for it. It

- **The harbour.** Pumps keep the low town dry. The harbour's water moves twice a day and is
  still only at slack, for about twenty minutes. The Ferren method needs still water. Mara's
  sheets were tried at Saltmere in the winter and failed for that reason.
- **Many in Saltmere like the stepped bank.** It has not dropped a stroke. One operator can
  drive it. That is a fair position held by sensible people.

## 4. Spawn in a national season (`PLANNED`)

All new behavior obeys trained anatomy and combines or redirects it. None is a bigger Flask.
Working descriptions follow; the crews on the page give the names, as Book One's did.

- **Packs.** Husks that keep a count among themselves and move on it. Cutting one out of time
  breaks the pack for a few counts. Cutting in time does nothing.
- **A folding hem.** A hem that folds from its far edge, as a catcher's ward does, and so
  comes off a purse sheet.
- **An entering form.** A cord that goes to an open stack as a string goes to water that
  keeps time. A sequenced door gives it nothing to enter. A laid door is an open door.
- **Coast forms.** Things that stand in tidal creeks and work the flood and the ebb, where no
  sheet can be laid and ash goes into the water.
- **The first Barrel.** The expression in the harbour basin, grown at each spring tide. Its
  shape is the author's. It is not the Pit's square and not Stopgate's string abreast.
- **Yield** is higher on average and nearly all of it reads I above trace. Rich ash that
  cannot go on a floor, into a pump house, or into a medical tin.

## 5. Ash in a national season (`PLANNED`)

- **Plain ash is disappearing.** Natural yield carried the Step in trace by the spring of 2680
  (`ESTABLISHED`: "it's all had the hair since the thaw"). By the winter no fresh kill reads
  plain, in Meridian or in the hills.
- **Plain ash is what un-stepping needs:** a jar a cell, of ash that "has never heard a beat."
  Old stock is finite: reference shelves, wardens' winter stores, sealed medical reserves, and
  the cellars of an Order that never stepped anything.
- So the price of ash turns over. The richest red bags are close to worthless for safe work,
  and eight-tenths of a dram of plain ward-trace is worth an argument.
- A healer can ease a **starved** hand with plain ward-trace and cannot ease one that is
  **turned out** (`ESTABLISHED`). Stepped ward-trace is no use for it.

## 6. The handed crossing (`PLANNED`; the working term may be replaced on the page)

- **What it is.** Catch, carry, and plant, each done by a different person through one door,
  with a caller. A hand door lifts and folds. An Ember door takes the folded load across. A
  still door plants it. Between people there is no quarter-dram gap, because nobody shuts and
  opens.
- **What it costs instead.** For an instant at each pass the load belongs to nobody. A pass
  called half a count wrong drops it or nips someone (`ESTABLISHED`: Pru's fingers). Each
  holder must take a count that is not theirs. It needs rehearsal with those exact people.
- **Why it was not already done.** Schools teach one door and declared roles; a pass between
  unlike doors belongs to nobody's curriculum. The Home Line had a word for it once. A
  single-door fighter cannot feel the far side of a gap. Somebody who has been through both
  can call it.
- **Limits.** It is slower to set than a three-door sequence. It fails if any one of three
  people is hurt, late, or unwilling. It cannot be done by strangers. It does not close a
  tear, kill anything, or mend a bank.
- **Kiva's part** is the call, the ground test, and the written columns. Under her certificate
  she may also stand as one link by making one declared crossing on a sanctioned mast.

## 7. What Book Two establishes about the Step, the filter, and the dream (`PLANNED`)

Each line is to be earned by evidence on the page and said by somebody who can be wrong.

1. **The Step is a mark.** It is what rising pressure from beyond the filter leaves in ash.
   Halden did not invent the beat in 2391; the foundation found counting ash, learned to

2. **Tears come to it.** A stepped bank is a worked pattern keeping time at scale, and spawn
   expressions echo worked patterns. That is why a hold stood, and why a string went to the
   drain.
3. **People and spawn are answering one pressure.** Noa's columns get a fourth, for ash, and
   it rises with the other three.
4. **It is quickening.** Old Halden bench books give the Step a slower count than today's
   four and four. (The figures are the author's, after reading the patent scene in Book One.)
5. **It is not uniform.** The notch that follows Kiva's cliff by eleven seconds at Meridian
   follows it sooner on the coast. Nobody can say what that distance is a distance to.
6. **The dream is shared.** Annick (Glory, 2431; her entry undated); the Ferren keeper and the
   Saltmere keeper on one night in 2604; Kiva in 2676, proved independent by a sealed and timed
   page; Ambrose Halden in 2604 and since; Clemency Thrale; and returns from other cities'
   remarks columns.
7. **A T-0 can be local and can be wide.** When two Glory fields from unlike traditions touch
   at the Slack, every lamp on the coast shows a point of light, and ports overseas log the
   same minute.
8. **The readings disagree.** Halden: step the country, so that its lamps go white and not
   out. The Even Hand: one door, one quiet thing, and let nothing in that asks. The Works and
   the Line: whatever is coming, do not be one engine tonight. Book Two acts on the third and
   does not disprove the first.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.

  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
