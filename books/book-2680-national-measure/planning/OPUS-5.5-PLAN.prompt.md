A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that

- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,

- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —

- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult

- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a

# Kindling 2680 — series map

Status: Book One complete and accepted; Books Two and Three remain horizon plans.

## Series promise

A generation born into mature door schools discovers that its institutions preserved
the founders' methods but simplified away the reason those methods worked. A
three-door protagonist must rebuild connection between divided traditions before a
failing filter makes that division fatal. The visible ladder runs from adolescent
Kindling, through collegiate competition, into professional rift companies that
defend—and power—the rebuilt world.

## Repeating pleasures

- Collegiate teams, visible practice, measurable correction, rematches, standings,
  recruitment, and earned rank change.
- Discipline and military-school training: students drill against one another, learn
  spawn anatomy and kill mechanics, graduate through live-rift licenses, and carry
  every injury, correction, and recovered ash share into the next threshold.
- Different schools solving the same obstacle in incompatible-looking ways.
- Friendly loyalty, rivalry, humor, and mentors with private stakes.
- Historical records that change a present choice rather than delivering lore for
  its own sake.
- Rift-response set pieces where teamwork and terrain matter more than raw output.
- Professional rift fighting where rescue, closure, infrastructure, and clean ash
  recovery matter more than kill count.
- The earned-ash loop: train, fight, kill, contain, recover, grade, divide, invest,
  and return stronger. The soul must still learn; ash buys the next attempt rather
  than acting as automatic experience.
- Ashcraft: magic supplies pattern, ash supplies finite fuel, and Glory can hold
  industrial-scale patterns without making energy free.
- The tension between ash-power industrial houses and near-monastic school orders,
  with good and dangerous people on both sides.
- The recurring fever dream called **the Unroofed Sky**: Glory users converge, the
  planetary filter vanishes, and pure Glory presence reaches every person, rift,
  ash cell, and reactor lattice at once.
- The phrase **why the post is crooked** as both a literal curriculum question and
  the series' argument against empty tradition.

- The soul is what fills. Ash is ownerless fuel left by Riftspawn; it is finite.
- Kindled → Ember → Flame → Fire → Torch → Blaze → Glory remains the human rank
  ladder. Skill/control and rank/breadth remain separate axes.
- Glory stops ordinary aging through the shedding mechanism already recorded, but
  reaching it is rare and cannot be granted, bought, or stolen.
- Glory also allows a master to hold larger, longer, and more intricate ashcraft
  patterns. It improves throughput, stability, duration, and precision; it does not

## Combat progression, Riftspawn, and earned ash (`LOCKED 2680`)

The visible progression engine joins student combat, field service, and the ash
economy. It should be experienced repeatedly on the page rather than explained once.

1. **Train:** discipline schools teach body mechanics, door control, team roles,
   spawn anatomy, terrain, containment, quench procedure, casualty extraction, and
   ash recovery. Several Book One chapters must show students learning specifically
   how to kill Riftspawn, not merely how to duel other students.
2. **Contest:** school teams fight one another in Measures, brackets, and mixed-role
   lanes. A human opponent adapts, remembers, and exposes habits that a spawn does not.
3. **Hunt or respond:** students graduate through licenses from contained Handful
   releases to supervised live rifts. Riftspawn are dangerous animals or expressions,
   not training dummies. A clean kill still leaves containment and rescue work.
4. **Recover:** defeated Riftspawn dissolve into finite ownerless ash. The team must
   catch, quarantine, grade, and reconcile it before it can be used. Contaminated or
   badly captured ash can poison a cell, attract another rift, or injure an operator.
5. **Invest:** the public authority takes its civic and casualty shares; the school,
   team, and licensed fighters receive recorded yield shares. Earned ash pays for
   training cells, equipment, travel, medical reserve, and harder sanctioned entries.
6. **Advance:** rank is not an automatic reward for a kill. The soul grows through
   repeated owned exertion, correction, recovery, and reproducible control. Ash lets a
   student attempt more difficult work, but cannot purchase judgment or replace what
   the soul has learned.

This produces two linked scoreboards: personal capability (rank, Hold, Draw, control,
and Call) and team yield (clean ash recovered after rescue, closure, and public shares).
Kill count alone is an immature metric and a persistent source of institutional abuse,
but kills and usable ash must remain materially important to students and companies.

**Draw** is efficiency: ash spent on a defined piece of work divided by the governing
book or bench expectation. `1.0` is standard and lower is more efficient. The expected
cost is task-specific; the accepted book figure for a contested four-minute hold is
twelve hundredths of a dram, so eleven hundredths and a hair records as `0.93`.

Recovered ash has three separate measurements. **Yield** records quantity on the
Handful -> Flask -> Barrel -> Well ladder. **Grade** records purity and stability;
fraudulently stamped ash can kill an operator even when the quantity is correct.
**Resonance** records the defeated spawn's expression and therefore which training,
medical, containment, transit, or industrial patterns can use the ash efficiently.
A rare yield is valuable because of what it can safely fund, not because it grants the
killer a packaged new power. Student teams carry metered combat ash for permitted
techniques and equipment, so decisions about spending or saving an earned share have
visible consequences in the next match and the next rift.

### Filter escalation

As the planetary filter thins, more Glory pressure reaches the world. Prepared humans
can hold stronger, stranger, and more blended expressions, so each generation appears
more naturally gifted. The same pressure also gives rifts more available pattern:
spawn arrive more often, express greater variety, coordinate or adapt in unfamiliar
ways, and reach higher yield classes. Early institutions read the two trends
separately—an exceptional collegiate cohort and a bad rift season. The series gradually
proves they are one escalation. More ambient Glory creates opportunity, not free power:
untrained people overload, cells drift, doors interfere, and stronger spawn kill anyone
who mistakes new capacity for mastery.

## The Unroofed Sky (`LOCKED SERIES PROPHECY`)

The series uses a recurring fever dream, not a reliable spoken prophecy. Across
centuries, a small number of high-rank practitioners have recorded overlapping
fragments. Later schools call the collected pattern **the Unroofed Sky**.

- The dream shows Glory-level people arriving from different directions and
  traditions until their fields touch. Whether they gather to prevent the break,
  cause it, claim it, or survive it is not revealed.
- Above them, the filter does not crack wider. It disappears. The planet stands in
  unmediated **Glory presence**: immense, intelligent-feeling pressure and possibility,
  but not a speaking god and not automatically hostile.
- Every ash cell, reactor lattice, living door, and rift responds at once. Some
  systems brighten, some fail, some change category. The dream never supplies a
  technical solution.
- Kiva's three-door Kindling includes the clearest known version, apparently carried
  or amplified by the Satori disk. The disk may preserve earlier contact, respond to
  future resonance, or both. Book One does not settle which.
- The vision recurs in pieces as Kiva advances. She cannot summon it for answers.
  Physical fever, ash overload, filter instability, or close contact with Glory can
  trigger it, and each recurrence has a bodily cost.
- Independent Glory masters and archival accounts eventually prove Kiva is not the
  only dreamer. Industrial houses, school orders, professional companies, nations,
  and religious movements interpret the pattern differently.
- Book Two reveals the shared record and makes the dream a national/international
  political fact. Book Three brings the Glory users together and fulfills the fixed

  characters' choices determine what that collision becomes.

The fixed ending is the loss of the filter, not a predetermined winner, savior,
battle, or moral interpretation. Do not use the dream to excuse unearned power or

## Ashcraft and electrical power (`LOCKED 2680`)

Centuries of work have turned ash from carried healer fuel into the rebuilt world's
principal finite energy commodity.

- **Ash induction:** refined ash sits in a warded cell. A trained operator impresses
  a stable magical pattern on it; the induction assembly converts the controlled
  release into ordinary electrical current. Once tuned, a modern cell runs without
  continuous soul expenditure, but never without finite fuel, maintenance, metering,
  or risk.
- **Magic supplies form; ash supplies fuel.** A soul cannot be replaced by ash because
  ash has no owner, judgment, or intent. Ash cannot safely do complex work without a
  pattern held by a person or preserved in a carefully built device.
- **Traditions remain distinct:** Edge-derived work defines conductive paths and
  precise release; Ward-derived work contains reaction and redirects force;
  cultivated/internal work holds resonance, distributes load, and prevents collapse.
  Composite devices normally require teams or inherited patterns. Three-door access
  gives Kiva possibility, not instant mastery.
- **Uses:** lighting, water and waste pumps, heating, refrigeration, communications,
  medical machinery, tools, transit, station gates, arenas, and rift sensing.
  Critical services retain mechanical and hardened backups.
- **Failure modes:** contaminated ash, false grading, pattern drift, runaway heat,
  operator backwash, grid damage, and rift attraction. Every serious device has a
  meter, a shutdown path, and a responsible ledger.
- **Glory scale:** a Glory master can stabilize industrial patterns that lesser
  practitioners can only maintain in teams or for short intervals. This has produced
  powerful industrial houses—titans whose refineries, grids, transit works, and
  professional companies shape governments—as well as near-monastic school orders
  devoted to perfecting one discipline across a lifetime.
- Industry and devotion are not clean moral opposites. Some houses exploit schools;
  some fund public infrastructure; some orders hoard knowledge; some resist monopoly;
  and a few Glory masters belong credibly to both worlds.
- **Geographic ladder:** Book One remains regional around Meridian. Book Two widens
  to national circuits, intercity grids, and company charters. Book Three becomes
  international as rival grids, schools, Glory masters, and rift companies must
  cooperate under accelerating filter failure.

## Energy revolution after Meridian (`LOCKED 2680 HISTORY`)

The decisive technological leap occurred during the first century after the first
Home Line, then compounded for more than four hundred years.

1. **Ash as emergency fuel:** Meridian-era practitioners used ash mainly for healing,
   training, lamps, and survival work. Its finite nature and dangerous grading were
   understood before its industrial potential.
2. **Radiation return:** Sustain-trained Kindled learned to remove active radiation
   burden from living workers while medicine treated exposure and fixed damage.
   They could enter zones ordinary crews could not, recover records and components,
   and work in short, measured rotations. This never made radiation harmless or
   reversed tissue already destroyed.
3. **Nuclear recovery:** engineers combined ordinary shielding, remote machinery,
   disciplined rotations, ash containment patterns, and Glory-scale oversight to
   reopen surviving nuclear sites and build safer compact reactors. Ward does not
   move radiation; the breakthrough is containment, control, monitoring, and safe
   human access—not a quiet rewrite of the old power rules.
4. **Tamar Vey's question:** an itinerant Home Line teacher and storyteller visits
   an early recovery workshop whose engineers keep trying to make ash burn longer.
   With an ordinary bowl, a conductor, a measured pinch of ash, and a short-held
   pattern, Tamar demonstrates that ash can preserve an instruction after the
   operator releases it. The lesson is conceptual: the reactor's fire already knows
   how to make heat; perhaps ash should hold the rules around the fire. Tamar does
   not design the reactor or hand civilization a finished device. Engineers spend
   years turning that question into safe, reproducible work.
5. **Ash-nuclear synthesis:** ash patterns stabilize reaction geometry, manage heat
   and load, and improve electrical conversion. Nuclear fuel supplies dependable
   baseline energy; ash supplies control, high-density response, and compactness.
   Both fuels remain finite. Waste and failure still matter.
6. **Distributed power:** licensed Glory-impressed patterns allow factory-built
   small reactors and ash cells to run homes, buildings, farms, hospitals, and
   workshops without a Glory master standing beside each one. Local technicians
   maintain them; public ledgers track fuel and failures.
7. **Displacement:** within roughly a century of Meridian, ash induction and compact
   nuclear-ash systems displaced fossil fuels and large solar fields as primary
   energy. Legacy wind, water, solar, combustion, and mechanical systems remain as
   local backups rather than vanishing from knowledge.
8. **Flight and advanced equipment:** ash-electric lift craft, personal lift rigs for
   suitably trained Kindled, powered armor/tools, precision medicine, climate control,
   high-speed fabrication, and hardened communications become ordinary enough to
   reshape cities. Personal flight remains skill- and fuel-limited, not universal.
9. **Far-future society:** four more centuries of refinement make 2680 materially
   beyond most pre-Fall dreams. Its abundance is real but uneven. Grid access, clean
   ash, reactor licensing, professional protection, and Glory-pattern ownership are
   central political and class questions.

Tamar's role is remembered unevenly. Some accounts make him an inventor; others call
him a fraud who happened to ask the right question. Canonically he is mortal, human,
original to this world, and catalytic rather than omniscient. The science belongs to
the generations who test, fail, record, and build after him.

## Ash-electric civilization and Glory

- Refined ash is the dominant finite energy source. **Ash induction** converts a
  controlled ash reaction into ordinary current for lights, pumps, heat,
  communications, medicine, workshops, transit, arenas, and rift sensing.
- Ash does not create free energy. Cells are graded, metered, consumed, reconciled,
  and vulnerable to contamination or runaway.
- **Ashcraft** blends magic and ash: a trained soul supplies pattern, intention,
  and control; ownerless ash supplies most of the fuel. Different traditions shape,
  contain, conduct, or sustain the reaction differently.
- Modern cells continue running after a Kindled operator starts and tunes them, but
  still require maintenance, replacement ash, and emergency shutdown.
- At **Glory**, a rare master can impress stable patterns at civic or industrial
  scale. Glory does not make ash infinite; it expands safe throughput, duration,
  precision, and the complexity of blended systems.
- That capability produces ash-power industrial dynasties as well as school orders
  whose masters pursue near-monastic devotion to one tradition. Some orders reject
  corporate ownership; some industry houses sponsor them; some Glory masters move
  between both worlds.
- Pro rift companies feed the ash grid. This creates prestige and public service,
  but also incentives to overhunt, hide risk, manipulate ash grades, or protect
  wealthy districts first.
- A sanctioned kill produces a recorded yield. Civic power, casualty reserve, school,
  and team shares are removed in that order. Student shares fund training cells,
  metered combat techniques, equipment, travel, and medical reserve; they are not
  unmetered personal treasure.
- Ash is recorded by **yield** (Handful, Flask, Barrel, Well), **grade** (purity and
  stability), and **resonance** (which patterns can use that spawn expression safely).
  A large dirty yield may be worth less than a small stable one.
- Killing does not grant rank automatically. Real combat can deepen capacity because
  the practitioner works at the edge of owned control and must later reproduce the
  lesson. Ash funds the next threshold; training makes the threshold survivable.
- Filter thinning increases both human potential and Riftspawn pressure. The opening

## 5. The grid and the string (shown, not explained)

**What the page shows about the Step.**
1. The three Ferren pumps, on a bank in step, take their stroke together. Since the 17th of the
   ninth month the Ferren main, the storm drain for that side of the city, rises and falls on a
   count of four and four: two and a half fingers at the foot of Stopgate, a hair under the
   School's wall, and growing at the top of the hill.
2. The drums fell in in the order the main reaches them, two days apart: 19, 20, 21, then
   **25, (26, 27 dry), 28, 29.** Drums 22, 23 and 24 on the Kiln loop, where the copper leaves
   the drain, never fell in.
3. Two quenched drums did not stop it. Filled fresh, they were in step inside sixty counts.
4. In a lead-lined trough, a single plain cell on a stool, joined to a stepped bench bank by
   nothing but twelve paces of rising and falling water, leaned for an hour and went in.
5. **Nobody says that water can step a cell.** Mara: "the water in a box." Adlam's patent does
   not mention water. Why a tear's hold put the bank into step in the first place is not
   touched.

**The drill, the 2nd** (the Halden license yard; Birch's slate; a contained six-knot string):
1. The string ignored a lit gate twelve paces off, walked to the far end of the trough, and
   put two cords into the water. "It doesn't want the bright thing. It wants the thing that
   keeps time."
2. Kellan cut the top rung on Yarrow's word, with the cloth by the book hard against the
   trough. The open ash ran across the canvas and up the cord into the water. Yarrow bagged
   under thirty. **All six bench cells cooked together inside a breath;** the pump stopped.
3. **With the pump dead the water went on rising and falling. The string was pumping it.**
4. A fresh can on the stool went in at fifty-eight counts.
5. A boat-hook under the cord frosted from end to end and was taken; the fitter's left glove
   cracked.
6. Pru's ward, for one tap on the slack, folded before the haul, lifted the cord. Called half a
   count late by Kiva, it nipped Pru to the first joint. Called by Brask, it lifted clean, and
   **the water missed a beat and the cell on the stool fell out for a count.**
7. With the bung pulled and the trough empty, the cords hung ten counts and came apart into
   loose ash; the string read again and went to the gate.

**The string of the 5th.** Nobody on the page gives it a new name. The page calls it "a string
laid on its side" and "a string abreast"; Brask calls it a hold with nobody keeping it; Vane's
slate says "a string of twelve, abreast, every knot reading." It is never called a Barrel, and
no yield class is given.
1. The tear lies in the surface of the road over a shallow drain that is already keeping time,
   and runs on uphill toward ground that is not yet in step.
2. Knots rise through it side by side, eight paces apart, each on two legs, each reading,
   joined by a cord from which a frost hem hangs to the setts and stands above.
3. It does not turn to a lit gate. A head turns to a lit cell box in a house, and the cord
   bellies toward it.
4. Cut at the bottom end, the cord whips back to the next knot, and a new knot comes up at the
   top: "It's being paid out." Uncut, it pays out all the same at the drain's own pace until
   it runs out of road; a cut only lets the next knot out at once (the ninth and tenth rose on
   Rhea Sorn's two cuts; the eleventh and twelfth rose with nobody cutting). Twelve rose; ten
   stood when they were cut.
5. Where the drain dives, the tear stops. It then reaches with an arm to the nearest open
   water.
6. With its arm in the main it hauls and eases the main; the water goes on moving with the
   pumps stopped.
7. Taken off the water and planted in dry ground, with the sluice shut behind it and the bank
   dead, it had nothing to drive. Cut on one word with the corner given to the ground, the hem
   lay down. The tear, no longer held, gave three husks and one ordinary string and was closed
   by the Home Line in under an hour.
8. Its ash is loud. What came up after the hem was down "reads as this year's ash reads, and no
   louder."

**How the bank was taken out of step** (the method the page shows): nothing in the water that
keeps time; the pumps stopped; every cell quenched on one word; fresh cans, each salted with a
pinch of plain ash that "has never heard a beat"; a card under each handle with its own count,
"not two alike in this room"; a reader standing between each cell and the next for a slow ten;
a cell that falls back in is quenched and filled again; each pump restarted on its own eight
cells. Twenty-six minutes. It needs the main to be still, which on the night needed the arm
off the water and the sluice down.

**Instrument evidence.**
- **The notch and the lamps came apart.** The notch on Kiva's pen fell eleven seconds after her
  second cliff, as on both earlier occasions. The lamps (every window in the School, six
  hundred on the hill, and twenty-four dead needles at the pump house, which lifted "by a hair"
  with nothing in a single can) jumped about five minutes later, at *back,* when the hem came
  down. At the Pit the two fell in the same instant. Noa: "One of them keeps time with you. The
  other keeps time with that… I don't know what either is." Whether other masts showed the
  notch on the 5th is not on the page.
- **Noa's three columns.** People: median Hold 6.1 against forty years at 5.3 to 5.5; three
  Fire readings at seventeen; Stonehand's plate 340 for thirty-one years, then 350, 355, 362,
  380 since the thaw. The other thing: tears on the district board, fourteen against a usual
  four or five; skins, none in sixty years and nine this year; a Flask that walked a bus; a hold
  stood by husks; a string abreast. Both rise, and faster since the fifth month. Saltmere
  (Yeo's wire): a harbour bank of thirty-six cells in step from twenty past two on the 17th of
  the ninth month and not out; tears on the coast board nineteen against seven; skins eleven;
  the Saltmere school's plate beaten four times. **A line Noa will not vouch for:** a ship's
  engineer "off a hull from over the water" says that his own port's bank did the same at the
  same hour. "Not entered as fact."
- Nobody on the page says why. "It's two columns." Sent to the Charter Board under the Chair's
  seal, Adlam second, with a line of Yeo's and Lumb's notion of asking who else has a keeper
  with a book.

I'd not have seated you on the other."

"It's the same night."

"It isn't the same thing. I've been told that you're particular about that."

"The third line's hers," said Marten. He said it to the board, apologetically, as he said most things.

It was in pencil, fair-copied by a clerk. *On the request of S. Dray, blade, Team Twenty-Eight: that at the National Measure any candidate with more than one door be read from a sanctioned mast, by a reader of this office, as at the Regional. Granted, and sent to Saltmere under seal. She states that she has objected to the candidate twice and has not changed her mind. She states that she called a count for her on the 5th because she was the one who could. Both are entered.*

"I said that I'd write it," said Kiva. "You said that you'd take my word."

"I did. Then I thought that a thing like that ought to be in a hand that isn't yours." Dray looked up at her. The grey eyes were as sunk as ever, and quite steady. "Twenty-Eight goes to Saltmere. So does Seven. I shall be on my feet by the third month. I mean to beat you in a lane there, on a declared count, with a needle on you, in front of whoever they've got instead of eleven hundred people."

"I'd expect nothing else."

"That's a Halden man's phrase."

"I know. I've been keeping it."

Something happened to Selka Dray's mouth. It was not a smile. It was the place where one would have been, on somebody else.

"Push," she said, to Marten. "I've a healer at eleven. He says that I'm to bend it, and I intend to argue."

---

Rhea Sorn sent a boy with a card that evening. It was four words long. *Yard. Six. Bring Finch.*

There was no van out. There was only the hall yard, and the hooded lamp, and a tall woman sitting on an upturned tub with her elbows on her knees. Pike stood behind her with his hands at his back.

"I told you on the cinders that I wasn't offering you anything," said Rhea. "I wasn't. You were seventeen, with a hand in a cloth, and your team had my name on its entry." She did not get up. "You're seventeen still. The brackets came off this morning. So I've nothing on you any longer but what I choose to put, and I've thought for a week about how little that ought to be."

She held out a card, slate blue, with a good deal more than twelve words on it.

Kiva took it in her right hand, and read it under the lamp.

*Rowan, K. Catcher's second, to Pike. The night van, two nights in the month, from the first of the new year, when her healer's card is up. Dark hands. No door on my floor that is not on her certificate. Finch with her, for the cord, if Finch will. If T7 is called on a night of mine, she is T7's and I go short. Until the last of the third month. Then we look again, and either of us may say no. No seat is promised. R.S.*

It was a small thing. It was an edge of a wall and a lamp, written out long.

"That's all of it," said Rhea. "I've put the ends on it on purpose. I burned a card once with two words on it about you. One of the two was *mine.* I'd had no business writing it, and I knew it the same night." The pale eyes came up. "This one's got *either of us* on it. I'd like you to notice that I can learn."

"What's a catcher's second?"

"She kneels at my other hem," said Pike. "She sets nothing. She calls nothing. She watches a man who's been thought a great hand for six-and-twenty years, and she tells him afterward what he did that he didn't say aloud." He nearly smiled. "I'm told there's a deal of it. I've a first-year to thank for the notion."

"And Pru?"

"Ask her. I did, an hour ago, at her mother's door."

Pru was standing a pace behind Kiva's shoulder with her hands in her sleeves.

"I said I'd have to think," she said. "Then I said yes so quickly that I bit my tongue. Then I said I'd only come if I was asked by you as well, since I'm not your cord. I'm a catcher with a sheet. I've got a red tag in a cold room." She went scarlet. "That came out bigger than I meant."

"It came out the right size," said Kiva.

She looked at the card again.

She had wanted a thing like this since she sat on the Measure Hall steps in the spring and watched a boy with steady eyes shake hands under three striped awnings. She had wanted it on ordinary terms. She had thought that when it came she would know what to say.

*What's behind it?* That was Anwen's question. She found that she had it ready, and that it was a better thing to have ready than an answer.

"Two things," she said. "Before I say. I'd like the *before* column read out, each night, on the slate, as you read it on the fifth. Whatever's in it. To all of us, not to me."

"That's on the slate already. It's ruled. I'd look a fool rubbing it out."

"And I can't say yes for the four. If Seven wants me those nights for a thing that isn't a call, a floor, or a lane before Saltmere, they've the first word. I'd have to ask them. I don't know what they'll say."

Rhea looked at her for some time.

"I had a speech for this," she said. "In case you said yes at once. It was about not being grateful." She got up off the tub, stiffly. "I shan't want it. Take the card home. Ask your four. Bring it back signed or bring it back torn, and tell me which on the first of the month." She put her hands in her pockets. "I'll say one thing, since I'm told that I'm allowed a piece of advice a season. You'll hate being a second. You'll kneel at a hem and see every single thing that you'd do differently, and you're not to open your mouth until the bag's sealed."

"I've been a corner. For Pru."

"I heard. I heard that you were corrected eleven times."

"Fourteen, by the end."

"Good," said Rhea Sorn. "That's about the right number."

---

Four of them signed the book on Saturday the 15th, at the Rowan kitchen table, and Kellan read the whole of it, since it was the last of the season.

Most of it they had heard before: seven-tenths of the call drams spent, Anwen's forty-one hundredths on a wall the most of that and Kiva's share none; the three-door line, spent whole on the 5th; the four drams lent and come home on the 4th.

"*Credited from Stopgate, by the foreman's pot: 9.0,*" he said, when he came to what was new. "*To Talley, J., Kill, by name, a head's share: 1.8. To the book: 7.2.*"

"That's seven and eight-tenths with my name on it," said Jude. He was by the dresser, as ever. "In one autumn. A draft board will think I've been robbing a van."

"*Held: 26.53. Sealed, medical, W: 0.8. Opened once; see the 6th.*" Kellan blotted it. "That adds. I've done it three ways."

"It can't be made up," said Anwen. "The reserve. I asked at the ash office. There isn't a pinch of plain ward-trace to be bought in Meridian; it's all had the hair since the thaw. So it's eight-tenths, and it stays eight-tenths until somebody kills something quiet." She did not sound sorry. "I'd sooner have it short and know what it was for."

"I've a line," said Jude.

They looked at him. He had never proposed one. He was on the book by name, for a share, and had been careful since the seventh month to ask for nothing else.

"Stopgate. The top of it. There are four streets on boxes off drum twenty-nine, for a month, since a plank split. It was nobody's plank. It was two knots down the hill from mine." He scratched his jaw. "A dram, at par, to the wardens' account. I asked last week what ash was for, and a man told me. I'd like to have done it once on purpose."

"That was my line," said Perrin. "In the seventh month. I'm not jealous. I'm flattered. It's a new feeling and I don't trust it."

Anwen wrote it. *Returned at par to the wardens of Stopgate, for the boxes on drum twenty-nine: 1.0. Held: 25.53.*

"Saltmere," said Kellan. "The office hasn't posted a warrant for it. Nobody from this Circuit has been sent to the coast in eleven years. It was two a head to a regional qualifier, and it'll not be less." He did the sum aloud, since he could not help it. "Five heads. Ten, at the least. We've five-and-twenty. I've never before had a figure come out with something over."

"Five?" said Jude.

"If you'll stand. By name. On your three things."

"I'll stand. I'd want to see the sea." The chipped tooth showed. "And Renn's feet on a strange floor. I've a notion they'll fall in love again."

"They're cured."

"Nobody's cured. Ask Aske."

Kiva had the card in her coat, where she had carried it since six on Friday.

She laid it on the table, on the green cover, and read it to them. She read every word, down to *No seat is promised.* Then she told them the two things that she had asked for in the yard, and what she would do if they said no.

"Six nights," said Kellan. "Two in a month, for three months. It costs the book nothing. If we want a floor on one of them, she's ours; it's written." He turned the card over, to see whether there was anything on the back. "I can't find the catch. I've looked for it as I'd look for one of Adlam's."

"I mind," said Perrin.

He had his hands flat on the table. He was strapped to the armpits again, and sat like a man in a barrel.

"I mind that it's Sorn, who's had us in brackets since the fifth month. I mind that it's you, again, with a card. I've looked under both of those, and there's nothing there that would bear weight." He drew what breath he could. "I've a place in my Hall's second team from the new year, in my own colors. I've a figure cut in a stone. So I'm signing, and I'd like it noted that I minded properly, and at length, and that it was only habit."

"What's behind it?" said Anwen.

"A hem. Pike. And my mouth shut until the bag's sealed."

"For three months."

"Yes."

Anwen considered that for some time.

"I'd pay to watch," she said. She signed the back of a chartered company's card, in brown ink, which nobody at the ash office had a rule for. The others signed under her.

Mara came up the shop stair as they were blotting it. She had eleven sheets under her arm, tied with tape, and she carried them in her left hand.

"That's for the coast boat on Monday," she said. "*How the Ferren bank was taken out of step, with what was got wrong first.* I've put Rusk's firebreak in it, by his leave. He said that if I left it out, he'd come and write it in himself." She laid the bundle on the dresser and put the brass weight on it. Then she looked at the table, and the book, and four names on a slate-blue card. "I'm told that nobody's to ask me whether she ought. I notice that nobody has."

"I asked them," said Kiva.

"So I heard. Through a floor." Something happened at the corner of Mara's mouth. "It took a good deal longer than telling them would have."

---

Orla Dane had moved her crate.

It was not far. It was a pace to the north, out of the line of the south row. A watch officer had pointed out, quite civilly, that she had been sitting on a casualty road for one-and-twenty years, and she had not been able to find an answer to it.

It was Sunday evening. The yard was empty. She had the lamp on its hook.

There was a bar on the bottom wall now. It hung on two iron hooks that a fitter had let into the brick, at the height of a man's shoulder, an ash pole as thick as a wrist. Under it stood the post, black and split and banded. At the post's foot, where a cross had been scratched for her by a girl's heel all summer, lay a square of iron with a ring in it.

*You're no help,* she had said to that post. She had said it for years.

She took the card out of her coat again.

It had come on Friday, by the coast boat. It was a plain card, of the cheap sort that pump-fitters used for their accounts, and it had four lines on it in a round, rather childish hand that she would have known in the dark.

*Your letter was four lines. So is this.*

*I am a pump-fitter. Our harbour bank went into step on the 17th, at twenty past two. I knew what it was before anybody told me. I knew it with the hand, the bad one.*

*Tell the one who writes everything down that I should like to read it.*

*J.H.*

She had not written to be answered. She had said so, to a Placement Chair, across a desk, and had been rather proud of the sentence.

She found that it had been a lie of the tidying kind. She had wanted this for twenty years. She had only arranged matters so that she should not have to know it.

He had not said that he forgave her. She examined the card for that, as a wall-holder goes over a wall for a thin place, and it was not there. He had said that he fitted pumps. He had said that the hand was bad. And he had asked for something, plainly, of a girl whom he had never seen, since she had a thing that he wanted.

*I did not know to teach you that.*

It seemed that somebody had taught him to ask.

There were feet in the lane. There were a good many, and they were small.

She put the card away.

---

They had sent the juniors. There were fourteen of them, in their grey smocks, in a huddle by the first shed, and with them a bursar's clerk holding a proof sheet as though it might go off.

Kiva had come by the side gate with Anwen. She had not known that there would be children.

"The tenth edition," said Orla. She did not get up. "They've set the lane. Seven columns. They've put the seventh back, in the same type as the other six, and I'm told that it cost the printer a night's sleep to make it fit." She took the sheet from the clerk and held it out. "The bursar wants to know whether anything should be added. He asked me. I said that I wasn't the one who'd found it out twice."

Kiva took it in her right hand. Anwen, without being asked, held the lamp.

It was all there. *The lane with the crooked posts. At Meridian, under the south wall, on the line of the hill drain.* It had the nine posts, and the sluice, and the bar. *Call the turn before the eighth, aloud, and say who has the bar.* It had a collarbone, and a cellar in the Hythe.

And at the right, in print, with two initials that nobody in Meridian could put a face to:

*Why: because the road isn't straight. S.V.*

She had thought about that line a good deal in ten days. She had thought that when she understood it she would have something to say.

She did. It was about a plan that had been tidied by a helpful clerk, and a post that had been aimed and called crooked, and a girl who had gone east in the Pit and had nearly gone east again. It was about there being room on a bar for two. It had a shine on it that one could have shaved by.

She did not write any of it.

"There's a pencil in my coat," she said. "The left pocket. I can't get it out with this hand; the pocket's got a button."

"I know which pocket," said Anwen. She got it out.

Orla made room. Kiva laid the proof on the crate beside her, and held it flat with the heel of her left hand, and the last two fingers. The first two lay on the paper, and told her that there was paper.

She did not touch the last column. It had been right for five hundred years. It had only wanted the other six.

She wrote in the fourth, where it said *Went wrong,* under the two who had come to the bar unnamed.

*2680. The mark was called east, since east had served once before. East was the drain. The step was tried first, and it said so.*

She passed over the fifth, where the collarbone was. And in the sixth, under *Second try:*

*Called north, aloud, before. Said back by two. Three others had the corner after six. Two had the bar. One had the count, from a chair. K.R.*

She read it over. It did not look like wisdom. It looked like a meter reading.

"You've not put what it cost," said Orla.

"It's in Vane's ledger. And Aske's card. And the green book." She gave the pencil back to Anwen. "I'd be putting it in a fourth place so as to have it where students could see."

"That's a reason not to."

"It's the reason I've got."

Orla looked at the two lines for some time. Her crooked face did something complicated, and settled.

"I asked you in the spring to show me six," she said. "I was rather pleased with it. I'd no notion what I was asking for." She folded the proof along its crease and gave it to the clerk. "That goes as it is. Tell the bursar that there's a hand in two of his columns that isn't a founder's, and that if he tidies it, I shall come up his stair."

The clerk fled.

The juniors had been edging down the lane. They had got as far as the fourth post, and Kiva went down the lane to them. The sandy one with the plait was in front, and she had her eyes on the bar.

"Miss Dane says that we're to run it," she said. "Dark. From the sheds. She says that it's in the manual again." She looked at Kiva. "You said that you didn't know why it was crooked. I asked you. You said that you'd wondered for four years."

"I had."

"Do you know now?"

It was the plainest question that Kiva had been asked since a watch officer had held up a slate.

She could have told her. It would have taken about a minute. The child would have nodded, and gone round the post by her little sideways step, exactly as before, with a sentence in her head in place of a blank.

*Then they should go and find out,* somebody had once said, of students who laughed. Kiva had never heard it. She had only been walking toward it since she was thirteen.

"I know what it's for," she said. "I'd sooner you told me. Run it. From the sheds, in the dark. There are two things to say aloud before you reach the eighth post, and I'll give you the first. *Turn.* And which way."

"What's the second?"

"Who has the bar."

"*I* haven't got the bar."

"No. So you'll have to say who has. Before you get there. That's the whole of it."

The child thought about that. She looked along the fourteen of them.

"There's got to be two," she said slowly. "It's too long for one. Somebody'd have to be at the other end." Her face cleared. "Bettony. You're tall. You're the bar with me. Say it back."

"I'm the bar with you," said a long thin girl, in a voice of doom.

They went up to the sheds in a body, arguing about it.

Kiva walked back along the lane to the first shed, and stood under the lamp, where Orla had sat for twenty-one years. Anwen came with her. Neither of them stood at the post.

"You could have told her," said Anwen.

"I had it ready."

"I know. I watched you put it in your pocket." She was not smiling. She had the look that she wore when a wall had been gone over and had no thin place in it. She put the pencil back in the left pocket, and did up the button, without comment, as one does up a child's. "I told Mace on Thursday. About coming off a join. He'd it on his board already. He'd written, *Said so as she went.* He's left my name where it was."

"I knew that he would."

"You didn't. You hoped. They aren't the same."

"No."

The lamp hissed. At the far end of the lane, somebody small was counting three, aloud, for courage.

Kiva looked down at her left wrist. The cuff had come undone again. A button wanted an edge found, and a hole, and one thread told from two.

"Anwen," she said. "My cuff."

She said it before she had tried. It was not hard to say. That was what surprised her. She had hated it for a fortnight, and Pike had promised her a year.

"Give it here," said Anwen.

They came.

They came out of the dark at the sheds' end in a pounding, uneven line, fourteen pairs of boots on packed dirt, with nothing lit. They went by the lamp like a flock of starlings. A small, breathless, sandy voice went up ahead of them.

"*Turn! Left! Bar's me and Bettony!*"

"*Bar's me!*" That was the voice of doom. "*Say it back!*"

"*Turn left! Bar's them!*" said twelve others, more or less together.

Not one of them struck the post.

Two of them reached up to the wall. Kiva heard the ash pole come off its hooks, and a great deal of advice. She heard it go home in the socket. She did not hear it turn. They had been told that a gate was not to be shut on a dry night, and for once in the history of that School a junior had believed a thing because she had been given the reason with it.

"That's the first time that lane's been run to the bar since 2390," said Orla. She had her palm flat on the dirt beside her crate, as a woman feels for a tram. "I've sat here one-and-twenty years. I never thought to hear it."

Kiva did not answer.

She was looking over the bottom wall. It was a clear night, with a frost coming. Beyond thirty feet of brick the long hill went down, straight as a rule, with a lamp at every twentieth pace, to the Brickfields road and the river and the dark.

There was a card in her coat from a company, with *either of us* on the front of it and four names on the back. There was a line on a board about a Measure at Saltmere, in the third month. There was a pump-fitter on the south coast, though she did not yet know it, who knew a thing by his bad hand, and wanted to read what she had written down. Somewhere beyond all of them, a keeper whom she had never heard of had a book with a remarks column.

It was two hundred miles to the coast. She had seen the map on the wall of the watch room. The road went round the Sag, and doubled at a ferry, and followed a river that had never in its life gone where a clerk would have drawn it.

Down the lane the juniors had found that the bar would lift two of them off the ground if four more sat on the other end.

"Your cuff's done," said Anwen. "You may have your arm back."

Kiva took it back.

She looked once at the post. It stood a pace out from its row, black against the brick, with a bar through it and six children hanging from the bar. It leaned to the left, as it always had.

It was not listening for anything. She could see that now. It had only ever been pointing at the place where the road turned, for as long as there had been somebody to say so aloud.
