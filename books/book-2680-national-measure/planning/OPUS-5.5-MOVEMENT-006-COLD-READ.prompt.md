# Chapter 41 — Kiva: A Full Bucket

At a quarter to seven on Monday morning Kiva laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron latch of the loft's north window, because it was the nearest thing and she could not yet stand up for long enough to reach anything else.

*It keeps time.*

It came up through the latch from the frame, and through the frame from the wall, and through the wall from the chandlery and the quay and the black water under the quay. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster. It was the same answer it had given every morning since Hart had taught her to ask, and she found she was glad of it, the way a person is glad to find the stair where she left it in the dark.

She wrote it in her book with her left hand, badly. *Monday the 21st. A quarter to seven. The north window latch. Yes.*

Her body felt as though somebody had wrung it out and hung it over a rail. The blanket under her had dried stiff in the night. Her mouth tasted of copper and her knees did not belong to her yet. That was the fever's bill, and she knew it as well as she knew Aske's handwriting: a day like this one after every fever, with the world a little too bright and too far away, and then it would be over and she would be hungry.

At the south end of the loft Kellan was sitting on the edge of his bed in his shirt with the white card in his hand.

He had been sitting like that at midnight. She did not think he had moved much since. Halden License's beds were round him in a row, and Pettit was asleep on his back with his mouth open, and Yarrow had one arm over his face. Kellan was the only one awake at that end. He was holding the card by its edges as he held a ruled sheet he did not want to smudge.

She got up. It took two tries. She went down the loft between the beds with one hand on the iron bedsteads, and Old Four's stove ticked as she passed it, and Selka Dray, who was lying with her eyes open, watched her go and said nothing.

Kellan looked up when she sat down on Yarrow's trunk opposite him.

"You're a bad colour," he said.

"I'm told."

"Talley said a hundred and four." He turned the card over once in his fingers and back. "Aske said it to the stove, at about one, as though the stove had asked. Then he went to sleep sitting up."

"It broke at eleven." She looked at the card. It was thick and white, the size of a man's palm, with a small embossed mark in the corner that she had seen on the Counting House's door and on a glass case on a polished table. "You don't have to tell me."

"I know I don't." He did not move for a moment. Then he held it out to her, the way he had once held out a sheet of figures across a table at home, without comment, so that she could read it for herself.

The hand was large and upright and old.

*Mr. Renn. On Wednesday at the dawn slack, if Mrs. Rowan and the keeper will have it, I mean to hold the harbour's pumps on my own pattern while the cells are dark, so that the low town is not wet for longer than it must be. I should like a clerk beside my chair who will tell me the time and the count aloud, and will not tell me what I'd like to hear. This is not the foundation's request. It is mine. If you say no, say it to the man who brings this, and I shall think none the worse of you. A. Halden.*

Kiva read it twice and gave it back.

"He's offered the pumps," she said.

"He wrote to the keeper last night. A man went along the quay at ten with the letter and came back for this one at eleven." Kellan laid the card face down on his knee. "Six pumps. He'd hold them from the time the cells are quenched to the time the new cans are salted. Mrs. Rowan's sheet says that's six minutes, if nothing goes wrong. Six pumps for six minutes at fifty-seven strokes a minute is two thousand and fifty-two strokes." He looked at the back of the card. "He wants somebody to count them."

"Will you?"

"I've been sitting here working out whether I'd be counting for him or for the low town," said Kellan. "It comes out the same number both ways. That's what I can't get past." He was quiet. "He's a hundred and three. He's had his hand on that glass since 2611. That's seventy years, near enough, and something over twenty-five thousand days, and on every one of them he's had somebody beside him to tell him what he wanted to hear. Counsel. The clerks. His own house." He turned the card over again, face up. "He's asked for one morning of somebody who won't. I think that's the most honest sum anybody in that house has written in my lifetime."

"His pattern's the Step," said Kiva.

"I know it is. So will your aunt. She'll have said so before the letter was finished being read." Kellan put the card in his shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket over it. "That's her sum. Mine's the one on the card." He looked at her properly then, with his narrow careful face. "I'll go. I'll stand where he puts me. I'll tell him the count, and the time, and if he does something stupid I'll tell him that too, in front of his counsel." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's the first job I've ever been offered that's exactly what I'm for."

Behind her, Dray had turned her head on her pillow.

"Renn," she said, quite flatly, from three beds off. "If you're going down there, take a watch that isn't his."

Kellan looked past Kiva at her.

"I've my mother's," he said. "It loses four seconds a day and I know which four."

Dray looked at the ceiling again. "Good," she said.

Aske had the hand at a quarter past seven, at the north window, with the light coming in grey off the harbour and his watch open on the sill.

He held it for a long time, palm down and then palm up, and pressed the backs of the fingers, and the heel, and the place on the inside of the wrist where the cold had always begun.

"Warm," he said. "Eight mornings." He wrote it. Then he put two fingers on the side of her neck instead, under the jaw, and counted against his watch, and wrote that too. "Ninety. You're a day behind yourself and you'll be a day behind yourself till tomorrow. Eat whatever Quiller puts in front of you and then eat it again."

"I'm going to put my name down this morning," said Kiva. "At the Board's tent. For Wednesday."

He did not look up from the card.

"I know you are. You kept the crossing for it. You told me at this window on Thursday." He capped his pen. "I said the price twice last week, for the seventh. You've heard it. Past the elbow, five nights, and I'll not be surprised by six. I'll not say it a third time, because nothing's moved it."

"No."

"Here's the thing I haven't said." He took his spectacles off and folded them, which he did not often do, and held them in his hand. "Nobody's asked me, and I'd rather say it now than at six on Wednesday with the water running. If you make your crossing, and something goes wrong after it, and you make another in the same hour, against my card." He did not raise his voice. "I don't know what it costs. Nobody does; it's never been done. I'll tell you what I think. I think the hand goes. Not cold up the arm, the way it's gone after every one of these. Gone. The hand itself, to the wrist, the way your two fingers went and didn't come back, except I think the rest of it comes back, in weeks and not in nights. Three, if I'm guessing well. And then I think your arm's told me something about itself it can't untell, and I'll not sign one in seven after that. I'd want a fortnight between, for the rest of the year at the least."

Kiva sat very still on the edge of the bed.

"That's a guess," said Aske. "It's an honest one. It's the price as near as I can say it. I'll say it again on Wednesday, at the time, if it comes to that, because I'll not have you do it on a guess you heard two days before with a fever in you." He held up one finger. "And a rule, which isn't a price. If you've crossed, and shut, and you mean to light anything again after, you're dark for ten counts first. Nothing open. I'll not have a crossing's gap and a fresh lighting run together on a reader's tape where nobody can tell one from the other, least of all your arm." He put his spectacles back on. "If it doesn't come to that, I'll have said it twice for nothing, and you know what I think about that."

"Twice for nothing's cheaper," said Kiva.

"Than once too late." He wrote one more line on the card, and turned it, because she had asked nobody to keep anything from her any more. *Price of a second in the hour said, Monday, before. A guess. The hand to the wrist; weeks; then one in fourteen.* "There. It's written before it's wanted. That's all a card's for."

---

The Board's tent at the timber pond had its flaps tied back at nine, and there was a table across its mouth with the warrant on it, and a queue.

It was not a long queue. That was the first thing Kiva noticed, coming along the boards between Anwen and Jude with her legs not yet quite her own. On Saturday night ninety-six people had stood in rows on the Graving's floor while the Measure was read. This morning there were perhaps forty in the queue, and they were not standing as they had stood on the floor, in fours, by their colours. They were standing as people stand outside a door they have not been through before.

The warrant was a single sheet in the Clerk's own hand. It lay under a brass weight. Kiva read it over Anwen's shoulder while they waited.

*A Barrel response at the harbour pump house, Saltmere, at the dawn slack on Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, 2681, on the keeper's leave. Under the Saltmere Company's officers. Any four on the national license roll may stand in it, by name, on the Board's warrant and the Board's ash, from Monday. Those who stand will stand where the officer puts them and will close nothing. No four is obliged. A four that does not stand loses nothing by it, and the Board will write that it did not, so that nobody may say otherwise.*

Under it there were already names. The Ropewalk School, first four, in Carrow's round firm hand: *Carrow. Lyle. Tolver. Treen.* Old Four, under Brask's: four names. Halden License, under Pettit's slow careful one: *Pettit. Yarrow. Hesketh. Renn, by the Proprietor's leave, at the pump house; not on the mud.* Holloway's second four had signed at eight, the clerk said, before anybody else was up. All four of them.

"Holloway," said Jude, reading it.

"The lamp-man's town," said Anwen.

Westerley's first four had come and gone. They had not signed. Their captain had written a line under the warrant instead, in pencil, and initialled it: *Westerley first: not standing. We've a keeper at home who'd want us there, and we'll go to him.* Holloway's first four had sent their captain at eight with a note that said only *Not standing. With respect.* The Board's clerk at the table had copied both lines into the ledger, and under each had written, in a small neat hand: *Loses nothing by it.*

Nobody in the queue looked at those lines for long. Kiva saw that too, and was glad.

When Seven came to the table, Perrin signed first, because he was the captain. He wrote *Cade, P., captain,* and then stopped with the pen over the paper and looked at it for a moment.

"I can't stand on the mud," he said to the clerk at the table. "My healer's card. No plant, no lift. Till the 30th."

"Then write what you'll do," said the clerk, not unkindly.

Perrin wrote: *To declare for the four, and keep the brake. Not on the mud.* He handed the pen to Anwen. She signed without saying anything, and so did Jude, who wrote *Talley* with his left hand for the first time in a week, because Aske had said Monday, and it was Monday. Pru went scarlet at the table, for no reason anybody could see, and wrote *Finch* very small.

Kiva signed last.

She wrote *Rowan, K., catcher,* and then, because it was true and because she had put a thing on a table under her own name once already this week and found that she could, she wrote under it: *Caller, if the officer will have me. One crossing kept, as certified.*

The clerk read it and blotted it.

"The officer's at the pump house at half past ten," he said. "With the keeper and the Works' reader and the Marshal. Your captain's asked for. So are you."

Behind them in the queue somebody had come along the boards without a stick.

Selka Dray signed under Twenty-Eight's name, after Marten and Sedge and Bannock. She wrote her own name, standing, with her weight on the healed leg, and she did not write anything after it, because Dray never had. Then she turned round and found Kiva still beside the table.

"Wednesday," said Dray.

"Yes."

"I'm on my feet." Dray's face was a shut door, as it always was. "That's all I'm here to say. I've said it."

She went away along the boards toward the loft. Carrow, who had been watching from the tent pole with her arms folded, watched her go, and then looked at Kiva, and lifted one hand very slightly, palm out, the way Brask had once lifted a hand to say that a call had been fair.

---

They did not meet in the keeper's room. There were too many of them for it. Peverell had set a trestle table on the floor of the pump hall instead, between the third pump and the fourth, under the two long galleries where the thirty-six cells stood in their tall glass cases with their lights turning together.

It was the loudest room Kiva had ever sat down in. The six iron pumps went up and down on either side of the table, slow and enormous, each one as tall as a cart standing on end, and the whole floor shook with them, four and four. Every voice at the table had to be lifted a little to be heard over the stroke. It was the strangest place in the world, Kiva thought, to make a plan for stopping a thing: in the middle of it, with your feet on it.

Yeo sat at the head, because it was her warrant. Mara sat at her right with her reading hand flat on a sheaf of fault paper. Peverell sat at her left in her apron with her spectacles on their cord. Hart stood behind the keeper's chair with his good hand on its back and his bad one in his jacket. Rusk had a stool at the far end, with his thumbs in his waistcoat and his eyes half shut. Coombe sat on a crate with her rope in her lap. Rhea Sorn stood by the fifth pump in her slate blue with her own slate on her belt, because the officer had asked Graywater for an officer and Graywater had sent her. Noa had his ledger. And beside Noa, very upright on a borrowed stool, sat a sister of the Order of the Even Hand in plain grey, with her hands in her sleeves: the one who had stood at the Order's gate in the second month and looked at Kiva as though she were a parcel that had come to the wrong house.

The captains sat along one side. Carrow, Pettit, Brask, Marten, a tall freckled girl from Holloway's second four called Bolitho, who looked at the pumps as though she had never seen one so big, and Perrin, at the end, with his hand pressed to his side. Kiva sat beside Perrin. She had been asked for.

"I'll say what it is first," said Yeo, "and then the keeper will say what her house will do, and then the Works' reader will say what the bank needs, and then Mr. Hart will tell us when the water's still. And then we'll find out what we've got wrong." She looked round the table with her flat brisk face. "There'll be something. There always is. I'd rather find it on Monday."

"Say it so a child could say it back to you," said Coombe, from her crate. "Before you say anything else. If you can't, you don't know it yet."

Yeo looked at Mara.

Mara did not look at anybody. She looked at her own hand on the fault paper, as she looked at a fault report before she read it across a counter.

"It's a bucket with no bottom," she said, "that holds water anyway, because the water's keeping the town's time." Her dry voice went across the table under the pumps' stroke without having to be lifted. "If it spills in the harbour, the time goes back into the harbour, and the bank can't come out. If we cut it where it stands, there's a Barrel of open ash in the water the pumps draw from, and that can cook a bank. So we pick it up full, and we carry it out of the harbour without spilling it, and we empty it in the sink." She lifted her eyes. "The sink's the Graving. Then we take the bank out, while the basin's still."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The pumps went up and down.

"I could say that back," said Perrin. "I'd want to know what the bucket weighs."

"So would I," said Brask. He was the big mild captain of Old Four, who read things the way other people read the labels on jars, and he had been looking up at the galleries while Mara spoke, at the cells. "It's not a bucket, though. I've watched it twice from the wall. It's a cask. It's got staves up the outside, at the springs, grey ones, and a chime round the top, and no head and no bottom." He scratched his cheek. "The water's its head. And the head's keeping time."

Hart, behind the keeper's chair, looked at Brask for a long moment.

"That's better than anything in my book," he said. "Write it down, somebody."

Noa wrote it down.

Then they went round the table, and it took an hour and a half, and Kiva would remember afterward that almost all of it was clocks.

Hart had the first. He had his book of slacks open on the table under his good hand, and his watch on the inside of his wrist, and he spoke as he spoke to fitters, plainly, one figure at a time. The spring dawn slack had held its hour for a fortnight while the sea gate's table walked. It would come on Wednesday at nine minutes past six by the pump house clock, if the sealed page was right, and it would last about twenty minutes. Inside that twenty minutes the basin would be still, every part of it, except the one thing standing in it. When the slack ended, his hand would ache, and the flood would come in under the sea gate, and the thing would lie down into the mud if it was still there to lie down.

"And give the count back," said Mara. "That's the winter. Six cells went back in, on the second day, at the end of the slack. I've had it wrong for four months. It wasn't the water moving. It was that, lying down, and giving back what it held." She did not look at Peverell's fitters' names on her fault paper. "If it lies down in the basin while my thirty-six are filling, it's the winter again."

"So it comes off first," said Yeo.

"So it comes off first."

Peverell had the second clock. Her pumps would run on the bank as they always ran, until the thing was out of the basin and in the Graving and the Graving was shut. Then, on Mrs. Rowan's word, the thirty-six cells would be quenched together, and the pumps would lose their drive. The old cans would be drawn. The fresh cans would be set. Each would be salted with a jar of plain and given a card with its own count, not two alike in the house. Then she would start each pump herself, one at a time, on its own six cells.

"Six," said Rusk, from the end of the table, without opening his eyes.

Peverell turned her head.

"You said eight in the Salt Hall, Mrs. Peverell," said Rusk. "On Friday. *Each on its own eight cells.* I wrote it in my book because it was wrong. Thirty-six cells over six pumps is six. Eight's the Ferren figure. Twenty-four cells, three pumps."

Peverell looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment. Then she took her pencil out of her apron and drew a line through something on her own sheet and wrote above it.

"I read it off the Ferren sheet," she said. "I said it in front of the Board. I'll say it was wrong in front of the Board on Wednesday, in seven columns." She looked at Rusk. "Thank you, Mr. Rusk. You might have opened your eyes."

"I'd not have heard it better," said Rusk.

It was a small thing. Kiva found that it was the thing she liked best in the whole morning. A keeper had read a number off a sheet that had been written for a different house, and said it in a hall, and a man who did not believe anything that had not happened had written it down because it was wrong, and on Monday it was six. It was exactly what Mara had meant by *what was got wrong first.*

Then the keeper took a letter out of her apron and laid it on the table.

"From the Long Quay," she said. "Last night at ten. It's to me, because it's my house, and to Mrs. Rowan, because it's her method. I'll read it, because the officer should hear it and so should the captains."

She read it. It was the Proprietor's offer, as Kellan had said it in the loft: the six pumps held on his own pattern from the quench to the salt, so that the low town was wet for minutes and not for a quarter of an hour. He would be carried to the outfall walk. He would keep his hand where he always kept it. He would let go on Mrs. Rowan's word, and not before, and not after.

"His pattern's the Step," said Mara.

She said it at once, as Kiva had known she would.

"It is," said Peverell. "He says so, three lines down. *Mrs. Rowan will say that my pattern is the Step, and she will be right.*" Peverell turned the letter round on the table so that Mara could read the line. "He says that while the cells are dark and the old cans coming out, there's nothing in the house for his count to get into. He says that when you say *salt*, he'll let go, and the pumps will stand for as long as it takes your thirty-six to fill and count, and then I'll start them on their own. Four or five minutes standing, he reckons, and not eleven. And not fourteen."

"Fourteen," said Yeo.

"The carry's three minutes, if it goes," said Peverell. "Maybe more. My pumps run through the carry. Then the quench, the drawing, the setting. If I'd no help, from the quench to the last pump started is near fourteen minutes. That's two foot in Ropery Row on a spring, Mrs. Yeo, not one. That's the cellars to the stair-head." She did not look at anybody. "Penhale's mother's in Ropery Row."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The pumps went up and down.

"Then he'll have to let go," said Mara, "when I say. Not a stroke after." She put her reading hand flat on the letter. "I'll take it. On that condition, written, in his hand and mine. If he holds one stroke into the salt, the cans hear it and the cells go back in, and it's all for nothing, and the low town's dry for nothing."

"The Works would like to see it done," said Rusk, "before it's done."

Mara turned her head.

"I don't believe anything that hasn't happened," said Rusk mildly. "You know that. Ask him to come and hold one pump for one minute tomorrow, with its cells lit under it, and let go of it on your word, in front of the keeper and me. If he does it, I'll believe he can do it on Wednesday. If he won't come, I'll believe that too."

"He'll come," said Peverell. "He said he'd come Tuesday if it was wanted. It's in the letter. Line nine."

"Then it's wanted," said Rusk, and shut his eyes again.

The sister of the Order had not moved while they talked about the pumps. When they turned to the Graving, she took her hands out of her sleeves.

"The Master will stand across the mouth," she said. Her voice was low and very clear, the way Thrale's was, and it did not need lifting either. "She's said so. When the thing's inside and the gate can't yet be floated back, she'll hold a ward across the dock mouth, and nothing that keeps time will come out past her, and nothing will go in. She can't hold it and move. She can't hold it long. She can be in this town an hour at a time." She looked along the table. "And she'll lay down every door within forty paces of her, without meaning to, as she always has. You'll want to know that before you put anybody's door near her."

"Forty," said Coombe.

"On the quay, on Saturday, I paced it myself after." The sister turned her eyes to Kiva for the first time. "Miss Rowan's doors lay down at forty. In our house they lay down at less. The Master says that in our house she'd nothing to hold against."

"And here she'll have the whole harbour," said Kiva.

The sister looked at her, and did not say yes or no.

"Then the plant's past forty," said Yeo. She drew it on the table with her finger, in the dust from the pumps, and they all leaned in to see. The basin, with the pump house at its head. The ring, ten paces out from the outfalls. The Graving lying along the west side, with its mouth opening onto the basin at the south-west corner, fifty paces from the ring across the mud. "The gate's floated out tomorrow on the noon high water. It can't go back in till there's a flood under it to float it, an hour after the slack, maybe more. The Master holds the mouth till it's back. The thing goes in past her, and down the dock floor, and it's planted forty-five paces in, where her stillness can't reach whoever's planting it. And it's cut there, on an officer's slate." She looked up at Rhea. "Sorn, it's yours, the cut. If you'll have it."

"I'll have it," said Rhea.

"Fifty paces across the mud," said Brask. "And forty-five in. Carrying a cask full of water with no bottom. Who carries it?"

They all looked at Kiva.

She had known they would. She had thought about it since a ferry and a fever and a creek with a man's boot sticking out from under a fold, and she had known since Thursday what she would say, and she found now that saying it at a table in the middle of six iron pumps was not the same as knowing it on a bench.

"Nobody carries it far," she said. "That's the whole of it. Nobody holds it long enough for it to fold on them." She put her left hand on the table, flat, the way Anwen put her hands on a card. "It's the pass. Catch, carry, plant. Three people with unlike doors, and somebody to call the pass who's been inside both gaps. We did it on a yard in the second month. We did it at Mallow Creek with a sleeve ten paces long, and it held. This is bigger. So it's not three people. It's three planks. Wards together for the catch. Embers together for the carry. Still doors together for the plant." She looked at Yeo's lines in the dust. "And it's not one pass. A carry can't go the whole way with a thing like that on its fists. On one pair of fists it'll start to unfold by twenty paces. On two, with a count, by fifty, maybe. So it's three passes, and none of them longer than fifty. Off the water to a mark on the mud. From the mud to the sill. From the sill to the plant, past the Master's forty."

"Three in a row," said Coombe.

"Three in a row. I call them. I test every mark first, with my fingers, before anything's planted on it. If my fingers say a mark keeps time, it's not a mark." Kiva kept her voice level. "And I carry an Ember lit, declared, at every carry, behind the plank, as the reserve. If a plant fails, and the load's on nobody, I take it on my Ember, and I cross to still, and I plant it myself. Ember to still. It's on my certificate. One crossing. I've kept it for this."

"And if two fail?" said Rhea.

Kiva looked at her across the pump hall.

"Then I've a healer who said the price this morning," she said, "before anybody asked him. And I'll hear it again before I do anything about it."

Rhea held her eyes for a moment. Then she nodded, once, and wrote something on her slate.

"Students close nothing," said Coombe. "Say who closes the tear."

"Mine," said Yeo. "When the thing's lifted off it, there'll be whatever it's been standing on, open in the mud. Ivey's crew closes it. Students on the basin wall to cut whatever comes up out of it, on my slate, and not one stroke without my word." She looked at Carrow. "That's the wall, Miss Carrow. You asked for it."

"I did," said Carrow.

"And who has your far edge?" said Coombe.

She said it to the whole table. She had said it on a causeway at the Hunt to every catcher in the draw, and the printed page with Penhale's name at its head was in every officer's pocket in Saltmere now; Kiva could see the corner of Yeo's copy at the top of her coat.

There was a short silence.

"Every plank names it before the pass," said Kiva. "Out loud. Who's watching the far side of the load while the rest of them watch the near side. I'll not call a pass till I've heard it." She found she was looking at Rhea again. "A second's place. The far edge is the second's."

"Then it's written," said Yeo, and Noa wrote it.

They went on. They went through the planks name by name, who had which door and who had rehearsed with whom, and Kiva saw, as the names went down on Noa's sheet, that most of them had never stood in a pass in their lives and that some of them had never stood beside each other on any floor. The Ropewalk's four had beaten Seven twice. Old Four had protested Seven's Carry. Twenty-Eight had beaten Seven in a lane on Saturday, two hours before the Measure was read. Halden License had beaten everybody, and was standing without its blade. And they were going to put their hands under one thing together at nine minutes past six on Wednesday and let go of it on a word.

"It can't be done by strangers," said Perrin. He said it quietly, to the table, with his hand pressed to his side. "I'd want that known. It's on Rowan's sheet. It's the first line under the limits."

"Then they'd better stop being strangers," said Coombe. "By Wednesday." She stood up off her crate with her rope in her hands. "The Graving floor at two. All of them. Bring whatever's nearest to a cask full of water that you can lift with three wards. I'll bring the rope."

---

What was nearest to a cask full of water, it turned out, was a cask full of water.

The Company's cooper sent it down from the yard behind the Long Quay on a dray: a brewer's tun with eight iron hoops, as wide across as a big man is tall, standing on end with no head in it. They lowered it down the Graving's ramp on rollers and stood it on the stone of the dock floor at the seaward end, on the chalk where the lane pen had been, and the Company's men filled it from the fire main with a canvas hose until the water stood in it level with the top hoop. Then they nailed a plank across its bottom from the outside, because a tun with no head and no bottom would not have held a cupful.

"It's not right," said Brask, looking at it. "It's got a bottom."

"It's got weight," said Coombe. "That's what it's got. It'll not fold to the nearest thing, and it'll not keep time, and it'll not frost your hands. It'll be heavy and it'll slop. That's all I'm asking of it."

They began at a quarter past two, with the tiers empty above them and the iron and glass roof letting the grey light down on the wet stone, and for the first hour it went very badly.

It went badly in a way Kiva had not foreseen, and it took her most of that hour to see why.

The catch was not the trouble. Four wards came up together under the tun's foot, Anwen's and Edda's and Hesketh's and Sedge's, silver and low, and lifted it an inch off the stone, and held. That was good. The carry was not the trouble either. Jude and Gower stood behind the wards with their edges lit on their right fists, and when the word came they took the weight on the bars and walked it, slow and steady, ten paces down the floor, with the water slopping at the top hoop. And the plant was not the trouble. Brask and Tolver were still doors, and they stood square on the chalk with their hands open, and when the word came their doors went down into the stone and the tun came down onto them and stood.

The trouble was the word.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva, the first time, short, with the swallow, the way she had said it at Mallow Creek.

Anwen's wards opened. Edda's opened. Hesketh's opened, half a count late. And Sedge's did not open at all. It held on under the tun's foot, alone, a quarter count and then a half, and the tun tipped on it, and slopped a bucket of the Company's water over Sedge's boots and Jude's, and Gower swore.

"Again," said Coombe, from her step on the bottom tier.

They did it again. This time Sedge opened on the word and Hesketh held. The third time, Hesketh and Sedge both opened and Edda held, because she had been watching the other two and not listening.

On the next pass it was the carry. Treen and Pru and Marten and Yarrow, the catchers' plank, caught the tun from Brask and Tolver's plant, and Kiva called the pass to the Pascoes, who were brother and sister from Holloway, both Embers, both tall and fair and both very silent. And Treen held. Treen held on under the tun a full count after the word, with her ward low at her hip and her quick dark face set, and the Pascoes' edges were under it as well, and for a full count the tun belonged to two planks at once. It did not slop. It went perfectly steady. Treen looked up, pleased.

"That's a stack," said Kiva. "Between people. It's two planks holding one thing, agreeing. It's the thing we can't do."

"It held," said Treen.

"It held because it's a tun with a plank nailed under it." Kiva heard her own voice and made it plainer. "The thing in the basin goes to the nearest thing. If two of you have it at once, it goes to whichever's nearer, and that's the one it folds on. That's Penhale. That's the whole of Coombe's page." She did not look at Coombe. "The pass only works because for a quarter of a count nobody has it at all. There's nobody for it to go to."

Treen did not answer. But she had gone, Kiva saw, a little pale under the wind-brown.

"She's laid," said Carrow, from behind the plank, quietly. She had come down off the wall's place where Coombe had put her and stood with her arms folded, watching. "She's not laid now, she's one door. But she was laid for four years, and so was I, and so were Lyle and Tolver. That's what laid is. You hold till the other agrees. You never let go of your half till the other half has it." She looked at Kiva. "You're asking us to let go first. Into nothing."

"Yes."

"Every one of us has been taught since we were twelve that that's how a load gets dropped."

"I know." Kiva had not known, until Carrow said it. She found that she did now, all at once, and that it explained the whole of the last hour. It was not that they were slow. It was that their hands were good, and had been taught well, and would not do the one thing she was asking them to do. "It's how a load gets dropped, if there's nobody waiting. There's always somebody waiting. That's what the call's for."

Coombe got up off her step.

She came across the wet stone with her rope in her hands and her grey Board coat dark at the hem, and stopped in front of Treen, and held out the rope's end.

"Hold that," she said.

Treen held it.

"Now let go of it," said Coombe, "into the air. Not into my hand. Not down. Out." She held her own hand a foot below the rope's end, open, waiting. "I'll have it. You'll not see me take it. You'll have let go before I do."

Treen let go. The rope's end dropped half a hand through the air and landed in Coombe's palm, and Coombe closed her fingers on it.

"Again," said Coombe.

They did it for twenty minutes, all of them, wards and Embers and still doors alike, in pairs across the dock floor in the grey light, letting go of a rope's end into the air above an open hand and not looking to see if it was caught. Kiva did it with Pru, and then with Hesketh, and then with the elder Pascoe, who let go of it very carefully as though it might break. It was absurd. It was the most useful twenty minutes of the day. By the end of it, when Kiva called *nobody* over the tun, every ward on the plank opened on the end of the word, together, and the tun hung on nothing for the space of a quarter of a count before the Embers had it, and it did not slop at all.

"There," said Coombe. "That's strangers stopping."

At half past four they ran it end to end three times, on the dry floor, with the tun, from one chalk mark to the next and the next and the next. On the third run Kiva found that she was not saying *nobody* the way she had said it at Mallow, short, with the swallow, to two people who had rehearsed it with her on a yard. She was saying it louder, and longer, and from her stomach, so that a girl from Holloway twelve paces off with her back half turned would hear it at the same instant as Anwen at her elbow. It was a different word when eighteen people had to hear it at once. It had to be a word that belonged to nobody's ear in particular.

She stood by the tun at the end of the third run, with the water slopping quietly against its top hoop and the grey light coming down through the glass, and understood that she had been calling the pass for four months as though it were hers.

It had never been hers. It had been Jude's name for it, and Pru's fold, and Perrin's plant, and the Line's old word. She had only been the one who could hear both gaps. Now there were eighteen people and a tun and a word, and the word had to be something any of them could have called if they had stood where she stood. She thought that if she did it right on Wednesday, somebody else would be able to stand there next time.

She had not thought of that before. She wrote it in her book that night, on the right, left-handed, under the day's line. *The call's not mine. It wants to be a word anybody can hear.* Then, after a while, under that: *Somebody else will have to call it. Teach it so they can.*

At five Coombe took her rope and went up the steps.

"Tomorrow at the slack," she said over her shoulder, "you'll all stand on the basin wall and watch the real one come up, and not touch it, and not light a door, and not say a word. Then you'll come back here and find out what this tun was lying to you about." She stopped on the third tier and looked down at them, eighteen strangers and a cask, on the wet floor of the Graving. "It'll be lying about something. It's a tun."

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-42.md

# Chapter 42 — Kiva: The Nearest Thing That Keeps Time

The cohort stood along the top of the basin wall at five to six on Tuesday morning, twenty-four of them in a row with their hands in their pockets, and not one of them had a door lit.

Coombe had walked the row at half past five and looked at every pair of hands. She had not said anything. She had not needed to. Kiva, at the end of the row by the pump house corner, had watched the Ropewalk's four put their fists in their coats as the Marshal came level with them, and the Pascoes do the same, and Dray, who never put her hands anywhere she had not decided to, fold her arms.

Below the wall the basin had drained to the bottom of the spring ebb. The mud lay grey and glistening from the outfalls to the far side, where the Graving's floating gate stood shut in its mouth, black timber and iron, with a hand of brown water lying against its foot. Ten paces out from the outfalls there was the place that lay a little darker. The outfalls poured into the hand of water below them, six iron mouths in a row along the pump house's foot, four great gulps and four, and the sound of it came up through the stone of the wall into Kiva's boots.

She was not on the wall for long. At a minute to six Hart came out onto the iron landing below the gallery and looked up at her and lifted his good hand, and she went down the ladder.

He had his book of slacks open on the landing's rail and his watch on the inside of his wrist. His bad hand was out of his jacket. He laid it flat on the grating, on the iron bars furred with weed, and did not look at it.

"Lay them on," he said. "Same as the 9th. Light."

She knelt and laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the bar beside his.

*It keeps time.* At once, and loud. The outfalls, the pumps behind the wall, the whole basin carrying it, four and four, into her wrist.

"Seventy-eight," said Pru, from behind her. Pru had come down the ladder after her without being asked, and was crouched on the landing with two fingers on Kiva's right wrist and her own capped hands steadier than they had been on Saturday. "You're not calm. That's the ladder."

"It's the ladder," said Kiva.

On the gallery above them, at the rail, there were four people. Mara with her watch open in her hand. Peverell in her apron. Yeo in green with gold at her collar. Coombe, with her rope. Nobody on the gallery said anything. They were waiting, as everybody on the wall was waiting, for a man with a ruined hand to say one word.

"Quiet," said Hart.

It was nine minutes past six by the watch on his wrist. Kiva felt it go under her fingers as she had felt it go on the 9th: not a stopping, but an absence, as though a hand that had been pressing on her wrist for a fortnight had been lifted off. The outfalls beside her went on pouring. The pumps went on going up and down. And under her two fingers, on the grating a yard from all of it, there was nothing keeping time at all.

Ten paces out, the ring came up out of the mud.

She had seen it twice. It did not help. It came up as it had come up on the 9th, all round at once, grey as ash with a rim of white frost along its lip and the mud sliding off it in sheets. She saw the staves this time, because Brask had told her to look for them: long grey ridges going up its outside from the mud to the chime, a little proud of the rest, like the staves of a cask. She saw the water stand up inside it, brown and flat and held, higher than the mud and higher than the water round its foot, with nothing under it but the mud and nothing holding it but the ring.

It rose, and stopped.

"Four foot ten," said Hart, sighting across his brass level at the staff. "And a half."

The surface of the held water trembled. It trembled in rings from the middle outward, four times, and stopped. Four times, and stopped. On the wall above, twenty-four people who would have to lift it tomorrow stood with their hands in their pockets and did not make a sound.

Kiva looked at the rings on the water. She looked at them for a long time, the way Rhea had taught her to look at a hem, the near edge and then the far edge and then the near again.

They were not even.

She had thought, on the 9th, that they went out from the middle to the lip all round alike, like rings from a stone dropped in a pond. They did not. They went out from a place a little off the middle, toward her, toward the pump house wall, and the rings were closer together on the side nearest the outfalls than on the side nearest the Graving. The whole held water leaned, very slightly, the way a dog on a lead leans toward the person it wants.

"It's not looking at you," said somebody from the wall above, flatly.

Kiva looked up.

It was Dray. She had not moved from her place in the row. She had her arms folded and her eyes on the ring, and her face was doing what it always did, which was nothing.

"It's looking at the pipes," said Dray.

Hart did not take his hand off the grating. But he turned his head and looked at the outfalls beside him, six iron mouths pouring into the hand of water on the count, and then out at the ring, and then at Kiva.

"Say it," he said.

"It leans," said Kiva. She kept her fingers on the bar. "The water in it. It's closer on this side. It's leaning at the outfalls." She heard her own voice go flat as Dray's had, because she was frightened and did not want it to show. "On Monday we said, *be the nearest thing.* That's Mallow. That's what Pru did with her ward. It came to her because she was nearest." She looked at the ring. "It's ten paces to the outfalls and it's leaning at them over a whole basin of mud that's nearer. It's not going to the nearest thing. It's going to the nearest thing that keeps time."

Nobody said anything on the gallery.

"The stow at Mallow kept time," said Kiva. "It was a coast form on a post. It wanted the line, because the line was a person and a person keeps time. Its own. A heart." She was working it out as she said it, and she could feel Pru's fingers on her wrist and hear Pru not saying the number. "This doesn't want a heart. It's keeping the bank's count. It wants the thing that keeps the bank's count. If Anwen's line stands at its foot tomorrow, still, with their wards up, it'll go past them to the pipes. Like a dog past a stranger."

"Unless they keep its count," said Hart.

He said it very quietly, with his bad hand on the grating, to nobody in particular.

On the gallery Coombe had stopped tying the knot in her rope.

"Yeo," she said.

Yeo had her watch out. "Twelve past," she said. "Eighteen minutes, Mr. Hart?"

"About."

"Then you've eighteen minutes." Yeo looked down over the rail at the landing. "One door. On the landing, on the iron, not on the mud. For a count of forty and no more. On my word." She looked along the wall. "Pryce."

Anwen came down the ladder.

She came down it fast, which Anwen never did, and stood on the landing beside the grating with her face quite white and her hands at her sides. The ring stood ten paces out from her across the mud, grey and staved, with its held water leaning toward the iron at her back.

"Light it," said Kiva. "Low. The way you'd stand a wall. Don't do anything with it."

Anwen's ward came up on the landing in front of her: a wall, silver and steady and as wide as she was, low, at her knees, facing the ring across the mud.

The held water went on leaning at the outfalls. Kiva watched the rings. They did not change by a hair.

"Nothing," said Hart.

"Nothing," said Kiva. "It doesn't see her." She looked up at the wall. "Dray."

Dray unfolded her arms.

"I can't call it," said Kiva. "My fingers say a thing keeps time. They never say what time. I need somebody else's count, named before. That's on my list. It's been on my list since the autumn." She held Dray's eyes up the height of the wall. "You called it for me once. Call it now. Its count. Out loud, so Anwen can hear it."

For a moment Dray did not move at all. Then she took her watch out of her coat with her left hand, and opened it, and looked at the ring over the top of it.

"*One,*" said Dray. Her voice was flat and carried like a dropped plank. "*Two. Three. Four.*" A pause. "*Rest.*" She did not look at her watch again; she was looking at the water. "*One. Two. Three. Four. Rest.*"

"Lean on it," said Kiva to Anwen. "On the beat. Don't hold still. Lean your wall on one and come off it on rest, the way you taught Lyle to lean from the floor. Keep the count."

Anwen did not answer. Her face had gone very still. She leaned.

Her wall moved. It moved a hand's breadth toward the ring on *one*, and held, and on *two* and *three* and *four* it pressed, a little more each time, as a hand presses on a door that might open; and on *rest* it came off. And on the next *one* it leaned again.

It took four counts.

On the fifth, the rings in the held water moved. They moved off the side nearest the outfalls, the way a cloud shadow moves off a field, and came round until they were closest together on the side toward the landing. Toward Anwen. The whole held water leaned at her across ten paces of mud, as it had leaned at six iron mouths a moment before, and stayed leaning, four and four, while Dray counted on the wall above and Anwen's wall pressed and came off and pressed.

"*Thirty-eight,*" said Yeo, on the gallery. "*Thirty-nine. Forty.* Shut it."

Anwen shut. Her wall went out like a lamp.

The held water swung back to the pipes. It took four counts to do it, as it had taken four to come round, and then it was leaning at the outfalls again as though nothing had happened.

Anwen sat down on the landing. She sat down quite suddenly, with her back against the masonry and her hands flat on the wet iron on either side of her, and looked at the ring.

"It looked at me," she said. Her voice was very small for Anwen's. "It turned round and looked at me."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"That's what we're going to be." Anwen did not take her eyes off it. "Tomorrow. The whole line. The thing it wants." She swallowed. "I want it said that I'd not known that, on Monday, when I put my name down."

"Nobody knew it on Monday," said Hart.

"Write it, somebody," said Coombe from the gallery. Her voice was not quite as flat as usual. "*The plank keeps the thing's count, on a called count, or it's nothing to the thing. And it makes itself the thing the thing wants. Said before.*" She began, at last, to tie the knot in her rope. "That's what the tun was lying about. A tun doesn't want anything."

At thirty-one minutes past six Hart said "*Ache,*" and the ring went down into the mud, and the held water came over its lip on all sides in a long white sheet, frosting, and spread across the mud and was gone. Kiva heard the flood begin a long way off under the sea gate, like breath.

She looked up at the wall. Dray had shut her watch and put it away, and folded her arms again.

"Tomorrow," said Kiva. "Every pass. From the wall. Will you call it?"

Dray looked down at her for a long moment, with her shut face and her healed leg under her, in a row of people she had beaten in lanes and been beaten by.

"I'll call it," said Dray. "On my feet." She paused. "Not because I've changed my mind about you. Because you can't hear it and I can, and that's the job." She turned away along the wall. "I'd want my name on whatever gets written. So it's mine if it's wrong."

Anwen Pryce had her ward line on the Graving floor by ten, the four of them in a row on the wet stone, and for the first time since the autumn she did not know what she was doing.

She knew what a wall was for. A wall was for standing in the way. She had been the best student wall in Meridian since she was fifteen, and she had stood in the way of litters and blades and a ladder and a lodger, and once, on a hill, of a dead man's weight coming down through her arms. A wall was still. That was the whole of it. You put it where it was wanted and you held it there, and your own count went on inside you, slow and private, like a clock in another room.

And now she had to lean it on somebody else's.

"*One,*" said Dray, from the step of the bottom tier, where Coombe had put her. "*Two. Three. Four. Rest.*"

Anwen leaned. Edda leaned beside her, small and stern, and Hesketh beyond Edda, and Sedge at the end, and the four walls went forward a hand's breadth together toward the tun, and pressed, and came off on the rest. On the next *one* Hesketh was late. On the one after that Sedge was early, because she had been listening to Hesketh being late and trying to make it up.

"Don't listen to each other," said Anwen. "Listen to her."

"I can't not listen to the person next to me," said Sedge. She was a tall fair girl from Twenty-Eight with a wall like a sheet of tin, and she had beaten Anwen's wall twice in the Lane and been beaten by it once. "That's what a line is."

"Not this one." Anwen did not know how she knew it. She found she did. "This one's four people listening to the same voice. If we listen to each other we'll all be wrong together, a little, and it'll go to whichever of us is least wrong." She looked along the line. "It'll go to one of us. And that one's the one it folds on."

Nobody said anything to that. They leaned again on Dray's *one*, and this time they were together, and on the next they were together again.

The tun did not care. It stood on its plank on the chalk with the water slopping at its top hoop and did not lean at anybody. That was the trouble with it. They could practise the count until they were sick of Dray's voice, and the tun would never tell them whether they had it right. Only the thing in the basin would do that, tomorrow, at nine minutes past six, with the whole town on the wall.

She thought of the water in the ring swinging round to look at her. She had not been frightened of much in her life. She had been frightened then, sitting on the iron with her hands flat on it, more than she had been at the Pit or on the hill or under the lodger at the Hunt, because a lodger only wanted in, and this had wanted *her.* It had wanted the count she was keeping. It had turned the whole of its held water round, across ten paces of mud, the way a sunflower turns.

She had not told Kiva that. She would not. Kiva had a crossing to keep and a word to call and a hand that her healer had said the price of twice, and she did not need Anwen's fright as well.

"Again," said Anwen, and they leaned.

Across the dock floor, at the chalk mark Coombe had drawn forty-five paces up from the seaward end, the third still plank was standing in the way of nothing. Pettit, square and sleepy, with his hands open. And beside him, Maud Lyle.

Anwen watched her between counts.

Lyle stood the way she had always stood, very straight, with her chin up and her feet a little apart. She had been the Ropewalk's laid ward once, ward and stance together, the best two-door Hold on the coast; and then a lodger had gone into her at the left ankle on Skerry Pill, eight days ago, and taken the ward with it, and she had a stance and nothing else. Anwen had taught her to lean from the floor on the Thursday after. She had learned it in an afternoon. She had leaned on Seven's litter in the Lane on Friday and very nearly cost them a carry.

She was not leaning now. She was standing on the chalk with her stance open and her hands at her sides, and every time Dray said *one*, and the four walls went forward toward the tun across the floor, Lyle's left foot moved. It moved an inch back. On *rest* it came forward again.

She did not know she was doing it. Anwen was nearly sure of that.

At eleven Coombe called a rest, and the line shut, and Anwen walked across the wet stone to the third chalk mark with her hands in her sleeves.

"You're moving your foot," she said.

Lyle looked at her. She had a long pale face and very dark eyes, and she had looked at Anwen like that on Thursday on the Ropewalk's floor when Anwen walked her wall away and left her leaning on nothing.

"I know," said Lyle.

"The left one."

"I know which one." Lyle's voice was perfectly level. "It's where it went in. At the Hunt. It's not hurt. There's nothing wrong with it. It just goes back when something comes toward me that wants a door." She looked at the tun across the floor. "It doesn't even want anything, that. It's a barrel. And my foot goes back."

Anwen stood beside her and did not say anything for a moment.

"Do you want out?" she said. "You can. Nobody would say a word. I'd see to it."

"No." Lyle did not hesitate. "I want to be asked. Tomorrow. Before." She turned her dark eyes on Anwen. "Your catcher says you say it before or it's no good. I've heard her. I'll stand on this mark tomorrow, and if my foot goes back further than an inch I'll say so before anybody's put a cask on me, and they can put somebody else there." She was quiet a moment. "That's more than anybody did for me on Skerry Pill."

Anwen thought about that. She thought about it all the way back across the dock floor to her line, and while Dray took them through the count again, and through two more passes with the tun.

At noon, when Coombe let them go, she found Kiva on the steps of the bottom tier with her book on her knee and her bad right hand curled against her coat, writing left-handed. Anwen sat down beside her on the cold stone. She had been working out how to say it since eleven.

"Lyle's frightened," she said. "Her left foot goes back. It's where the lodger went in. She says she'll say it before, if it's too much, on the mark. She asked to be asked." She looked at her own hands in her lap. "I want it said I asked her if she wanted out, and she said no, and I believed her. And I'm telling you, because you're calling it, and you'd want to know whose foot moves."

Kiva had stopped writing.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's not a favour. It's the far edge." Anwen looked across the floor at Lyle, standing by herself at the third mark. "I'm the only one on the floor who's watched her lean. That makes me hers."

Kiva wrote something in the book, small, and turned it so that Anwen could see. *M3: Lyle. Asked to be asked, before. Pryce has her far edge.*

Anwen read it. She did not go red, because she never did.

"That's what you'll be doing," she said after a while. "Wherever it is you end up. This. Writing down whose foot moves." She stood up. "I'll be on Mace's line. Doing it for a van." She brushed the wet off her skirt. "I'd not have thought, a year ago, that we'd be doing the same thing in two places. I'd have thought one of us would be doing it for the other."

She went down the steps before Kiva could answer, back toward her line.

The Graving's gate went out at twenty past twelve, on the top of the noon high water, and half Saltmere came down to the Long Quay to watch it go.

Kiva watched it from the lip at the landward end, three hundred paces off, with Jude beside her. It was a strange thing to see. All the Measure the gate had stood in the dock's mouth as though it had been built there, black timber and iron, holding the harbour out. Now the Company's men had opened the sluices in it, and let the harbour in until the dock was full and the water on both sides of the gate stood level, and pumped the gate's own hollow belly dry. And the gate came up off its sill. It came up a foot, two feet, with water pouring off its ironwork, and floated. Two Company boats took lines on it and towed it out of the mouth sideways, very gently, as two men walk a wardrobe through a door, and laid it against the outer quay and made it fast.

The dock lay open to the basin. Where the gate had been there was nothing but brown water, level from the harbour into the Graving and up the dock to the foot of the tiers, with the tiers reflected in it upside down.

"That's the sink," said Jude.

"That's the sink."

"It looks like the sea came in and sat down." He was quiet a moment. "Tomorrow at six it'll be mud, at the mouth. And a hand of water on the floor. And it'll stay open till the flood floats that back in." He looked at the gate lying against the outer quay with the Company's boats round it. "And the Master'll stand across the hole in the meantime. On her own."

"That's the Master's."

"I know whose it is." Jude scratched his jaw with his right hand. His left was out of its sling for the second day and he held it a little away from his body, as though it belonged to somebody who had lent it to him. "I asked Yeo what the ash is for, when it's all in there. The Barrel. When it's cut, and it's on the floor of the dock in the water."

"What did she say?"

"She said it'll all be red. It's been keeping the town's time for six months. It'll sing on the seventh like a kettle." He looked at the open dock. "She said it's for nothing. It goes in the Board's press as evidence, and stays there. She said that's what most ash is for, this year." He was quiet. "I didn't like the answer."

The Proprietor came along the Long Quay at three o'clock, carried, with his left hand flat on his glass case, and the town stopped to watch him come.

Kiva had known he would be carried. She had read his letter on the trestle in the pump hall. It was another thing to see it. She was standing at the far end of the outfall walk, at the corner where it met the basin wall, a long way off, because Mara had told her to stand there and she had not needed telling. And down the Long Quay toward her, between the white lamps on their fluted posts, came a slow small procession that the whole quay drew back from.

Four men in Halden's livery carried a chair on two long poles, as a sedan was carried in old pictures. In the chair sat a lean man in a dark coat with grey at his temples, very upright, a man of fifty-five by his face. Beside the chair, keeping pace with it exactly, two more men carried a stand between them: an iron stand with a glass case on it, brass-framed, at the height of the man's left hand where it lay on his knee. His left hand was not on his knee. It was flat on the glass. The two men with the stand walked as men walk carrying a full bowl, looking at nothing but the hand, so that it never moved by a hair from where it lay.

Adlam walked behind, with his hat in his hand. Halden's counsel walked behind Adlam. And beside the chair, on the side away from the case, Kellan walked in his black and gold with his mother's watch open in his palm.

Nobody on the Long Quay said anything. The porters stopped with their barrows. A woman selling hot things at a brazier stopped with her ladle in the air. The Mercury's young man was there, with all three of his pencils, and did not write. They watched the Proprietor of the Halden foundation go by in a chair, at walking pace, a hundred and three years old, with his hand on a glass case that two men carried beside him like a sacrament, and Kiva thought that most of them had never seen him before in their lives. He had been a lit house on the quay. Now he was a man.

On the fourth bollard from the outfall walk, Kiva's heart began to slow.

She felt it come down, as it had come down on the Counting House's stair. Her Ember turned over under her breastbone, once, as a dog turns over in its sleep, and lay still. Her still door did not lie still. It wanted to beat. It wanted to go four and four with the thing coming along the quay, and she held it, and kept her left hand in her coat pocket, and did not touch the rail.

The chair came to the foot of the iron stair. The four men set it down on the stone of the outfall walk, beside the middle outfall, gently. The two with the stand set the stand down beside it without lifting the glass a hair. Then they all stepped back.

Mara came down the stair from the gallery. Rusk came after her, and Peverell. They stopped on the walk at a little distance from the chair. Mara had her watch in her hand.

"Mr. Halden," she said.

"Mrs. Rowan." His voice was pleasant and dry, and carried along the walk to where Kiva stood. "I'm told the Works would like to see it done before it's done. I'd want the same, in your place." He looked up at the pump house wall, at the six iron mouths along its foot, pouring into the brown water on the ebb. "Which?"

"The third," said Peverell. "Its six are lit. My fitters have their hands on all six. If anything goes into them that isn't theirs, they'll say *town,* out loud, and I'll hear it from here."

"They'll say it," said the Proprietor. "It'll be true. It's the same count." He took his right hand off his knee and laid it flat on the iron flange of the third rising main, where it came out through the wall to the outfall, as he might have laid it on a table. "Tell me when."

Mara looked at her watch. Kiva saw her face, from the end of the walk, and knew it: the face she wore over a fault report she did not like.

"Take it," said Mara.

Nothing seemed to happen.

The third outfall went on pouring into the basin, four great gulps and four, exactly as the other five did. The pump behind the wall went on going up and down. But on the gallery above, through the open door, somebody said "*Town,*" quite loudly, and then somebody else, and then four more voices, one after another, *town, town, town, town.* The six fitters on the third pump's cells.

"It's not them driving it," called Peverell's fitter with the burn scar, from the doorway, sounding surprised. "Mrs. Rowan. It's driving them. Same count. But it's coming the other way."

"Yes," said the Proprietor. "That's what my pattern is. It comes the other way." He had not moved at all. His left hand lay flat on the glass and his right on the iron, and his face was as it had been on the quay. "I've held a yard of four hundred cells like this, madam, for a fortnight, when its drive failed. I've never held a pump. It's a great deal lighter."

"Let go," said Mara.

He let go.

He took his right hand off the flange and laid it back on his knee, and on the gallery the six voices said *mine,* one after another, the cells taking their pump back, and the third outfall poured on into the basin exactly as before. It had taken perhaps a minute. Mara looked at her watch.

"Fifty-eight seconds," she said.

"I'd have given you a round minute, if you'd asked." The Proprietor looked at Rusk. "Well, Mr. Rusk?"

"I believe it," said Rusk. "It's happened."

The Proprietor laughed, short and real, as he had laughed at his own table.

It was Kellan who spoke next. He had not moved from his place by the chair. He was looking at his mother's watch, and he did not look up from it.

"Sir," he said. "That pump's cells were lit. When you let go, they took it back. Tomorrow the cells will be dark when you take the pumps. All six. There'll be nothing under them to take it back." He looked up. "What happens when you let go of a pump that's only got you?"

The Proprietor turned his head and looked at him for a long moment.

"Then it stops, Mr. Renn," he said. "And it's mine to let go of. That's simpler, not harder." He laid his right hand flat on his knee. "Write down that you asked. I'd want it in your book that you asked before."

Kellan wrote it down.

From the far end of the walk, with her heart going slow and heavy and her still door wanting to march, Kiva watched the Proprietor's four men take up the poles again, and the two men take up the stand with the glass between them, so carefully that his hand did not move. She watched them carry him back along the Long Quay between the white lamps. He did not look round at her. She had not thought he would. He had seen her at the end of the walk when he came, and she had seen him see her, and that was all.

She thought about Kellan's question all the way along the outfall walk to the low town. It was a good question. It had been asked before, and written down. The answer had been plain and sensible and from a man who had held four hundred cells for a fortnight.

She could not have said why she did not like it.

At the evening low water, with the sun going down red behind the hill and the Graving open to the basin, they ran the whole route on the mud with the tun.

It was the first time anybody had walked it. The basin had drained to the bottom of the ebb, and there was no ring, because Hart said it was the dawn it wanted, at the springs, and never the evening. There was only the mud, grey and soft and shining, from the outfalls across the basin to the Graving's open mouth, where the stone sill showed above a hand of water and the dock floor went away inside it, wet and level, toward the tiers.

Kiva walked it first, alone, with Coombe on the wall above.

She walked from the place ten paces out from the outfalls where the ring would come up. She walked twenty-five paces west across the mud, counting her strides, and stopped where Coombe's man had driven a stake, and that was the first mark. She walked twenty-five more to the sill of the dock mouth, which was stone, a long granite step as wide as the mouth with the iron of the gate's seating set into it, and that was the second. Then she walked in over the sill onto the dock floor and up it, between the walls of the dock rising on either side of her into the tiers, and counted forty-five paces.

At forty, the sister of the Order was standing on the dock floor with her hands in her sleeves.

"Here," said the sister. "That's where your doors lay down on the quay, on Saturday. I paced it after, to the bollard." She looked up the dock. "The Master will be on the sill. Forty from her is here. Go on five."

Kiva went on five. Noa was there, with chalk, and drew the third mark on the stone in a long white line across the floor. Behind him, ten paces off against the dock's south wall, the Board's lane mast had been moved down from the pen in the afternoon and set up on its feet, forty feet of black iron with its drum at the top and its eight pens hanging on their wires.

"It'll read the sill and this line both," said Noa. "And everything between. I'll be on the desk. Polglase has the travelling mast on the basin wall above your first mark, for the mud." He looked at her. "The Clerk's sanctioned both, in writing, for the warrant. If you cross anywhere from the ring to here, it's read."

"I know."

"I'd like you to know I'll be reading," said Noa. "And not watching. Those aren't the same." He went back to his desk.

Kiva knelt on the chalk line and laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the stone.

*It keeps time.*

Of course it did. It was the evening low and not the slack, and the pumps were pouring into the basin a hundred paces off, and the dock was open to the basin, and the whole of it, mud and water and stone, was carrying the town's count. She had known it would. She went back down the dock floor and tried the sill, and the stake on the mud, and the place where the ring would be. They all said the same thing.

She stood on the mud by the place where the ring would rise with her fingers still cold from the stake, and understood that she could not test the marks tonight. Nor could she have tested them this morning, because this morning she had been on the landing, and the marks had not been driven. The only time any mark on this route would ever be quiet, and say *nothing* under her fingers, was in the twenty minutes of the dawn slack tomorrow, when the count had gone out of the whole basin into the one thing standing in it.

So she would test every mark tomorrow, in the minute before its pass. With the ring up, and the planks waiting on her. She would kneel and lay her two fingers on a stake while a cask of held water stood on somebody's trays and twenty-four people and the town watched from the wall, and if the stake said *keeps time,* there would be no mark there, and no plant, and she would have to say so.

She told Coombe, on the wall.

"Then you'll test it tomorrow," said Coombe. "In the minute. And if it's wrong, you'll say it's wrong, in the minute, and we'll plant somewhere else." She did not look up from her rope. "That's what testing is. If you could do it the night before, it'd be called guessing."

They ran it three times with the tun, end to end, from the ring's place to the chalk forty-five paces inside the dock, with Dray on the wall calling the count for planks that kept it on a cask that did not care, and the light going red and then grey over the hill. On the third run nobody held on after *nobody,* and nobody let go before it. At the sill, as the Pascoes walked the tun the last paces in over the granite, a hoop slopped a bucket of water over the far side onto Bannock's boots, and Pru, kneeling at the catchers' end, said "*Far edge,*" two words, once, and Wace stepped back, and Bannock did not. He stood there, very large and calm, with the Company's water running off his boots, and looked down at them.

"It's water," said Bannock.

"Tomorrow it won't be," said Pru, and went scarlet.

At eight, when Coombe let them go, a flat iron-wheeled cart came along the Long Quay from the direction of the Board's tent, drawn by a sister of the Order with another sister walking beside it. It had a grey cloth over it. Kiva knew what was under the cloth before she saw it. The thirty-six jars had been combed at the gate that afternoon, every one, under the Clerk's eye, on the third reed and the seventh. Every one had sung full on the third and been silent on the seventh. Now they were going back along the quay to a swept hut at the end of the Mole, where nothing kept time, to sleep one more night where they could not hear anything.

The sister walking beside the cart nodded to Kiva as it passed, and did not stop.

---

She did not sleep much.

She lay on the bed by the north window in the loft with her book under her pillow and the quay lamps in a row along the black water, and the town's count coming up through the iron of the bedstead, four and four, the way it had come up through every bed and bollard and rail she had touched since the boat came in under the sea gate three weeks ago. She had gone to sleep to it every night. She had woken to it. She had laid her fingers on it every morning at a quarter to seven and written *yes.*

If it went right tomorrow, she would never feel it again. Not this one. The bank would come out at half past six, and the pumps would start one at a time on their own uneven counts, and the town would stop keeping one time in everything. The bollards would go quiet. The gratings in the gutters would stop rattling four and four. Penhale's mother would stand in her kitchen in Ropery Row with her feet wet for five minutes and then dry again, on a count nobody had given her.

She found she was going to miss it. That surprised her. She lay and thought about it for a long time, and did not try to make it into anything.

At about midnight Pru, in the next bed, said into the dark, "Are you awake?"

"Yes."

"I'm going to call the far edge tomorrow. For the catchers' plank. Treen says I'm to, because I've done it on a creek and she's only done it in a lane." A pause. "I wanted you to know it's me. So you'd not have to look."

"I'd have looked."

"I know you would. That's why I said." Another pause. "Goodnight."

Two beds off, on the other side, Selka Dray turned over in her sleep and said a number, quite clearly, and was quiet again. Kiva lay and listened to the count in the bedstead, and did not know whose it was, and for the last night she did not mind.

Aske had the hand at a quarter to five, by candle, at the north window, with the loft dark behind him and twenty people dressing in it as quietly as twenty people can.

# Chapter 44 — Kiva: Three in a Row

The four walls went forward on *one* and did not come off on the rest.

They went in under the grey staves at the foot, where the mud had slid away from them, low and flat, the way a spade goes in under a sod. Anwen's went in first, because she was nearest, and Edda's beside it, and Hesketh's, and Sedge's at the end, four silver edges sliding under the chime of a cask as wide as a cart is long, side by side, until there was a floor of ward under the whole west half of it.

And it came to them.

Kiva saw it come. The held water leaned at the line, four feet eleven inches of brown water standing in the air with nothing under it, and the ring leaned after its water. It came off its foot. It came off the mud where it had stood since the autumn the way a cask is tipped off a cart onto a sledge, except that it did not tip. It slid. It slid a pace west, upright, with its held water level, off the dark place in the mud and onto the four walls, and stood on them.

The weight went into four girls' arms.

Kiva was at Anwen's left, a pace off, with her Ember lit at her side. She saw it go into Anwen. She saw Anwen's shoulders go down an inch and her elbows lock and her face go white to the mouth, and she saw the same thing happen to Edda beside her, and to Hesketh, and to Sedge, one after another along the line as the weight found them, like a wave going along a row of posts. Nobody made a sound. The cold came off the staves onto Kiva's face. Frost began to form along the silver edges of the walls where the grey sat on them.

"*Two,*" said Dray, on the wall. "*Three. Four.*"

The ring stood on the ward line and its held water trembled, four times, leaning at the four of them.

"*Rest,*" said Dray.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

She said it as she had learned to say it on the Graving floor on Monday, from her stomach, long, a word that belonged to nobody's ear in particular. Four walls opened. They opened together, on the end of the word, into the air, as eighteen strangers had let go of a rope's end above an open hand. And for a quarter of a count the cask stood on nothing.

It hung a hand's breadth above the frosted mud with nothing under it at all, grey and staved, with its brown water standing in it, belonging to nobody. Kiva had seen a sleeve hang like that at Mallow Creek. It had been the length of a room. This was the height of a man and as wide as a cart, and full, and it hung there in the grey morning in front of twenty-four people on a wall and the whole of Saltmere behind them, and did not fall.

Then Jude and Gower had it.

They came in under the chime on either side, north and south, with their bars lit on their right fists and laid flat, and the foot of the cask came down onto the two bars and stayed. Kiva saw the weight go into them as it had gone into the wards. Gower's knees bent and straightened. Jude's face did not change at all. He had his left arm held away from his body as he had held it all week, and his right fist under the chime, and his eyes on the held water.

"*Carrying,*" said Jude.

"*One,*" said Dray.

They walked.

It leaned at Jude and Gower as it had leaned at the wards, four and four, and as long as it leaned at them, they were the thing it wanted, and it rode.

It was open.

Kiva looked at his fist. The grey had come round his bar and his knuckles and his hand to the wrist and was going up his forearm, cold and thin and folding, and it was doing the same on his sister's, and they had carried it twenty-five paces through mud and a slurry and a stumble, and they were done.

She did not decide it. She had decided it on Monday, at a trestle, in a room full of pumps. She had decided it in a ferry's fever and on a bench in a dock. She had decided it on Thursday morning, at a window, when she told her healer what the week's crossing was for.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

The Pascoes opened their fists. The cask hung in the air over the slurry at the sill's foot, on nothing, grey and staved and full, with the frost on its south side where the sheet had gone over, and it belonged to nobody.

She took it.

She put her right fist under the chime with the bar lit along it, at the north side, where the elder Pascoe's had been, and it came down onto her bar. It came down onto her the way a wall comes down onto a man who has put his shoulder under it to hold it up: all of it, at once, everything a Barrel weighs and everything the town's count weighs inside it, onto one girl's right fist. Her knees went. They went to the mud and she let them go, because she could not have stopped them, and she knelt in the slurry at the foot of the sill with the cask riding on her bar a foot above her head and the held water leaning at her, four and four, and the grey beginning at once to come round her knuckles.

It was heavier than anything she had ever carried in any door. It was heavier than she had known an Ember could hold. It was heavier than the sleeve at Mallow Creek by so much that she understood, kneeling there, that she had never really known what a Barrel was. She had read the word in the Board's books for a year. This was what it meant.

She could hold it for a count. Perhaps two.

"*Wace,*" she said.

"*Ready,*" said Wace, behind her on the stone, with his door open.

She shut her Ember.

There is a moment in a crossing when nothing is open. Kiva had been through it six times on sanctioned masts and once in the Pit with a field direction and three times with three doors, and she knew it as she knew the stair at home in the dark. It was a quarter of a count, perhaps a little more, with every door she had shut and the ash in her going nowhere, and in it she was nobody's either. She was not a catcher or an Ember or a still door. She was a girl kneeling in mud with her hands empty.

The cask hung over her on nothing.

Then her still door opened.

It opened down through her knees into the slurry and through the slurry into the granite at the sill's foot, and it was slow and heavy and very sure, as it always was, the way a post goes into wet ground. She felt it find the stone. She felt Wace's beside it, a pace off, already there. And the cask came down onto the two of them together, onto her stillness and his, and stood. It stood on the sill of the Graving's open mouth, upright and full, with the held water trembling in it four and four over her head, and did not fold, because there was nothing in a still door for it to fold onto. There was only ground.

"*Planted,*" said Wace.

Kiva knelt in the slurry with her still door open and her hands on her knees and the cask standing on her.

Ten paces inside the dock, at his desk under the lane mast, Noa looked at his pens.

"*Cliff,*" he said. His thin reader's voice carried up the dock and out of the mouth to the basin wall as clearly as Dray's. "*Ember shut. Gap. Still open. A crossing, single, declared at six, by the captain's voice.*" He wrote. "*Eleven minutes past six and forty seconds. Read.*" He wrote again, and looked at the tape a moment longer. "*The notch at five.*"

On the basin wall, under the travelling mast, Perrin had his hand pressed hard to his side. Beside him Orla sat on her crate with her stick across her knees, and she did not move at all. The Chair, further along the wall, had opened her ledger against her arm and was writing.

Kiva did not see any of them. She was looking at her own left hand, lying on her knee in the slurry.

It was warm. It had been warm for ten mornings, and it was warm now. Nothing had happened to it yet. Aske had said the seventh went past the elbow and sulked five nights, and that it would begin within the hour, at the knuckle, and come up. It had not begun. She had crossed, on a sanctioned mast, read by the office's reader, declared by her captain's voice, inside her certificate, on her own word, with her sponsor sitting on a crate on the wall thirty paces away. It was the first thing she had ever done that mattered that was entirely inside the lines somebody else had written for her. She found that she did not feel anything about it at all, except that the cask was very heavy and her knees were wet.

"Rowan," said a voice at her shoulder.

It was Aske. He had come along the sill from its north end, where Bannock had sat down, with his bag in his hand. He did not touch her. He looked at her left hand on her knee.

"Warm," said Kiva. "Still."

"It'll not be warm by seven." He looked along the sill at Bannock, sitting on the granite with his white legs out in front of him and Wace's coat already over them. "He's burned. Hip to boot. Not taken. He'll stand by Friday." He looked back at her. "You'll be holding that till the third pass catches it from you."

"Yes."

"Then I'll be here." He set his bag down on the granite beside her and stood over it, an old man in a black coat on the sill of a dock with a cask of held water standing on a girl's stillness beside him. "That's all. I'm here."

They had run the whole length of the basin wall from the outfalls, along the top, the four of them, while the second pass walked across the mud below them. Anwen came down the steps first with her cap gone and her hair coming down at one side. Edda came behind her, small and stern, and then Hesketh, and Sedge last, breathing hard. They went past the cask on the dock floor side, inside the mouth, and stood in their line on the granite on the far side of it from Kiva, between the cask and the dock. Four walls came up at their knees, low and silver.

Behind them, on the dock floor, Jude and Gower were waiting with their bars unlit and their knuckles white.

"*First line,*" said Kiva, from the slurry. "*Far edge.*"

"*Pryce has it,*" said Anwen.

"*One,*" said Dray, from a long way off, on the basin, by the seam.

The four walls leaned. The held water came round from Kiva to them across the cask's width, the way it had come round on Tuesday on the landing and an hour ago at the ring. Kiva felt it go. She had been the thing it wanted for a minute and a half, kneeling under it with her stillness in the granite, and she felt it stop wanting her and turn, and it was like having a hand taken off the back of her neck.

"*Rest,*" said Dray.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

She shut her still door. Wace shut his. The cask came up off the sill on nothing for a quarter of a count, and Anwen's line took it on four walls laid flat, at the foot, on the dock side, and held.

Kiva was dark.

She knelt in the slurry with every door shut and her hands empty and her knees soaked through, and she was nobody's. The cask was on Anwen's line, a pace off across the granite. In a moment it would be on Jude's bar and Gower's, and they would walk it in, forty-five paces up the dock floor, the long pass, the one Kiva had said on Monday was nearly too long, to Noa's chalk line, where Pettit and Lyle were waiting with their doors open to plant it.

"*Nobody,*" she said, on the next rest, from her knees.

Anwen's line opened. Jude and Gower took it.

"*Carrying,*" said Jude.

They turned and began to walk it in. Kiva watched them go up the dock floor between the walls of the Graving, two figures with a grey cask riding between them on two bright bars, with the tiers rising on either side full of silent people and the lamps along every tier burning white, and forty-five paces of wet stone ahead of them to the chalk.

At the chalk, forty-five paces off, Maud Lyle's voice came down the dock.

"*Kiva.*"

It was not loud. It was very clear, and very steady, the voice of a girl who had decided to say a thing and was saying it.

"*I can't,*" said Lyle. "*I'm saying it before.*"

Kiva looked up the dock.

Lyle was standing on the chalk beside Pettit, very straight, with her chin up and her hands at her sides. Her left foot had gone back. It had gone back a yard, not an inch, and the rest of her had not followed it, so that she stood on the chalk line with one foot behind her like a girl about to run and holding herself from running by her whole will. Her face was white as paper. Her still door was open; Kiva could see its weight in the way she stood. But it was going back with her foot, into the stone behind the chalk and not under it, and it would not stand where a cask could come down on it.

"I'd stand if I could," said Lyle, down the dock, quite steadily. "It went into me there. At the ankle. It's coming and I can't keep my foot." She did not look away. "I'm sorry. I'm saying it now so it's not after."

Pettit, beside her, had not moved. He was a still door, square and sleepy, and he stood on the chalk with his door open, alone.

One still door could not plant it. A plank was two.

Jude and Gower had stopped. They stood on the dock floor six paces in from the sill with the cask riding between them on their bars, and they had heard her, and they were looking back at Kiva over their shoulders.

"Keep walking," said Kiva. "Walk it in. Slow. As far as you can."

"It's on my knuckles already," said Jude.

"I know. Walk it."

They walked.

She knew the sum. Everybody on the dock floor knew it. Two Embers, with a count, could carry it fifty paces before it folded on them, maybe. It had been on their knuckles at fifteen on the first pass. It was on them now at six. They would get thirty paces up the dock, perhaps thirty-five, and then they would have to say *nobody* or have it fold on them, and there would be nobody to say it to but Pettit alone on the chalk ten paces further on, and one still door could not hold it, and it would come down on the dock floor between them, held water and all. Inside the dock. Inside the mouth. Where the Master was not yet standing, because the Master would not come down to the sill until the plant was called, and the plant could not be called, because there was no plank.

The cask would spill on a dock floor open to the basin, with the slack going, and the held water would run down the floor to the mouth and out into the basin, and the count would go back into the harbour's water, and the bank would not come out.

Kiva knelt on the sill with every door shut.

She had made one crossing. It was the week's. It was on her certificate, and on her healer's card, one in seven days, and she had made it eleven minutes ago, and it was spent.

She looked at Aske.

He was standing over his bag on the granite a pace from her, an old man in a black coat. He had heard Lyle. He was looking at Kiva's left hand, lying warm on her knee. He did not look at her face. He did not say anything for the space of one breath.

"Ten," said Kiva. "You said. After a door, ten counts dark before any other. Count me ten."

Aske looked at her then.

"*One,*" said Aske.

He said it as he said a pulse, against his watch, flat and exact. He did not raise his voice.

"*The hand goes.* Two. *Not up the arm. The hand. To the wrist.* Three. *For weeks. Three of them, if I'm guessing well, and I've guessed well at everything else about that arm.* Four. *Then it comes back as it was, the two fingers as they are, nothing more.* Five. *And I'll not sign one in seven after. It'll be one in fourteen, for the year at the least, and maybe after.* Six. *I don't know what else. Nobody does. It's never been done.* Seven. *That's twice I've said it, Monday and now. I'll not be said to have said it once.* Eight. *Say you heard.*"

"Heard," said Kiva.

"*Nine,*" said Aske.

She looked up at the basin wall.

Perrin was there, under the travelling mast, beside Polglase's desk, thirty paces off across the mud, with Jude's book under his arm and his hand pressed to his side. He had heard Lyle; the whole basin had heard Lyle. He was looking at Kiva. She could not see his face at that distance. She could see that he was not moving.

He was the brake. He had kept it for Seven since the autumn. He called *off* when the thing in front of them was going wrong, and everybody stopped, and nobody argued with him, because that was what the brake was for. He could call it now. He could call *off* from the wall, and she would have to stay dark on the sill with her hands empty, and the cask would come down on the dock floor when Jude and Gower could not hold it any longer, and the bank would not come out, and it would be his word that stopped it and not hers, and her hand would be warm in the morning.

She waited for him to call it.

"*Ten,*" said Aske.

"*Rowan,*" said Perrin.

His voice came across the basin and in at the mouth and up the dock floor, as it had come across the Graving on the Carry from the foot of a ladder, saying *set.* It was not loud. It did not need to be.

"*Ember, and a crossing to still. The second in the hour. Against her card, with the price said first. Declared by Cade, captain.*" He stopped. Then, because he was Perrin and could not help it: "*I'd like it known it's mine as well.*"

"*Read,*" said Noa, from the dock floor, writing.

Kiva lit her Ember.

It came up under her breastbone as it always came, glad, as though it had been waiting by the door, and it did not know anything about cards. The bar came down her right fist. She got up off her knees out of the slurry onto the granite, and past Aske, who did not move, and down off the sill onto the dock floor, and ran.

She ran forty paces up the dock floor of the Graving between the walls, with the tiers going up on either side of her full of people who made no sound at all, and the lamps burning white along every tier, and her own boots loud on the wet stone, and ahead of her two figures and a grey cask, going slower and slower.

Coombe had said that a caller who runs arrives at her mark with her pulse in her fingers. It did not matter now. There was no mark to test. The mark was tested; she had tested it at eight minutes past with her two fingers on the chalk, and said *good,* and it was still good. She needed only to get there.

She caught them at thirty-four paces.

Jude was walking with his teeth shut and his right fist under the chime, and the grey had gone round his hand to the wrist and up his forearm under the sleeve. Gower was the same on the other side, and walking with his eyes shut, which Kiva had never seen anybody do while carrying. The held water leaned at them, four and four. It wanted them. It was folding onto them as fast as it could and they were walking it in anyway, a pace on each beat and a pace on the rest, because Dray's voice was still coming up the dock faintly from the basin, and they were keeping it.

"*Jude,*" said Kiva.

He opened his eyes. He looked at her bar, lit, beside his.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

They opened their fists. She did not see them do it. She was already under the chime on the north side with her bar laid flat, and the cask came off their two bars and hung on nothing for the space of a quarter of a count, and came down onto hers.

It came down onto her as it had come down at the sill.

It did not put her on her knees this time. She had known it would come, and she had set her feet on the stone before it came, and she took it standing, with her right fist under the chime and her arm bent and her whole back taking the weight. She could not lift it higher. She could not have lifted it an inch. She could carry it, perhaps, for a count or two, the way you can carry a thing too heavy for you as far as the next table if the table is near enough.

The table was eleven paces off. Pettit stood on the chalk with his door open and his hands at his sides, alone, watching her come, and Lyle a yard behind him with her face white.

Kiva walked.

She walked eleven paces up the dock floor with a Barrel on her fist. She did not know afterward how. She walked on Dray's count, which she could still hear, faint, from the basin, one and two and three and four, and on the rest. The grey came round her knuckles at the second pace and round her hand at the fifth, cold, a thin wrap folding to the nearest thing, and the nearest thing was her. At the eighth pace it was at her wrist. At the tenth it was past it.

At the eleventh she was on the chalk, beside Pettit's boots.

"*Pettit,*" she said.

"*Ready,*" said Pettit.

She shut her Ember.

Nothing was open. The cask hung over the chalk line on nothing, and so did she, a girl standing on a wet floor with her right fist empty and the frost still on it, and in the quarter of a count between the one door and the other she felt her left hand go.

It did not go cold. It had gone cold six times, after six crossings, from the knuckle up, slowly, over hours, and she knew that cold as she knew the order of her doors. This was not that. It went the way a lamp goes when somebody turns the key the whole way down. It was there, at the end of her arm, warm, the hand she had held out to Aske at a quarter to five; and then the light went out of it. From the tips of the fingers to the wrist, all at once, it was gone. There was a weight at the end of her arm, a little heavier than a hand, and nothing in it.

Her still door opened.

It went down through her boots into the chalk and the stone of the Graving's floor under it, slow and heavy and sure. Pettit's was there already, a pace off. The cask came down onto the two of them and stood.

"*Planted,*" said Pettit.

He said it quite politely, as Brask had, as Trewin had at Mallow Creek. Then he looked at her.

"Rowan," he said. "Your hand."

"I know."

Ten paces off, under the lane mast, Noa had his eyes on his pens.

"*Cliff,*" he said. His voice had gone higher than she had ever heard it. "*Ember shut. Gap. Still open. A crossing, single. The second inside the hour, declared by the captain's voice, the price said first.*" He wrote. "*Fourteen minutes past six and fifty seconds. Read.*"

He did not say anything about the notch. Kiva did not ask.

On the outer quay, high above the dock mouth, the Master of the Order of the Even Hand came down the steps.

Kiva did not see her come down. She felt it. She was standing on the chalk with her still door open into the stone and a cask standing on it, forty-five paces from the sill, and her doors felt the Master come down the steps one at a time, the way a dog in a yard feels a stranger come in at the gate long before it lifts its head. When the Master reached the sill, Kiva felt her stop there, and stand, and turn to face the basin with her back to the dock.

Then the ward came up.

Kiva saw it, because everybody saw it. It came up across the whole mouth of the Graving from wall to wall, from the granite of the sill to a man's height and then higher, grey, not silver, the grey of a winter sea under cloud. It was not a wall as Anwen's was a wall. It did not stand in front of anything. It hung across the dock mouth the way a held breath hangs in a chest. Through it, faintly, Kiva could see the basin beyond the mouth, and the mud, and the seam that Ivey's crew had nearly shut, and the pump house at the head of it all with its six outfalls pouring into the hand of water. She could not hear the outfalls any more. She could not hear Dray.

On the basin wall, Yeo's whistle went, three short notes.

It was for Mara. Kiva knew it was for Mara. It said the Master's ward was up and the sink was shut and the bank was the Works'. She did not look round at it.

Her still door wanted to lie down.

She felt it begin the moment the ward came up. It was not like Thrale on the quay on Saturday, at forty paces, when her doors had lain down like dogs and she had let them. It was a slow heavy pull, down and away, the way a tide pulls at a post, and her still door was holding a Barrel. If it lay down, the cask would have nothing under it but Pettit, and one still door could not hold it, and it would come down on the dock floor, held water and all.

"It wants to lie down," said Pettit, very calmly, a pace off. "My door. Does yours?"

"Yes."

"Forty-five paces," said Pettit. "The sister paced forty."

"She paced it on the quay. In the town." Kiva did not take her eyes off the held water. "Here she's got a ward up and the whole harbour on the other side of it." She set her feet. "Hold it. It's only wanting."

"It's wanting a good deal," said Pettit, but he held it.

Rhea Sorn came up beside her on the chalk.

She came from behind, where she had been standing with Pike since six, and she stopped at Kiva's right shoulder with her slate on her belt and her edge unlit. She did not look at Kiva's left hand. She looked at the cask standing on Kiva's still door, a pace from her face, with the held water trembling in it, four and four, and the grey staves with the frost along them, and the chime.

"It's to be cut," said Rhea. "On my slate. Out of time. In the rest." She did not raise her voice. "I can't hear Dray through that ward. Nobody in here can." She took her slate off her belt. "You've your door in the stone under it. You'll feel its count. Give it to me."

Kiva had never read a count through her still door in her life. She had felt the town's count through bollards and gratings and bed rails, with her two fingers, which could not tell her what time. But on the Counting House's stair, and on the Long Quay yesterday, thirty feet from a man with his hand on a glass, her still door had wanted to beat. It had known a count then, well enough to want to march to it. She had spent all her strength holding it shut. She had never once thought to ask it what it knew.

She asked it now.

It was there. It came up out of the stone under the cask into her still door and up through her boots, slow and heavy, four and four, as the town's count had come up through the iron of the bedstead in the loft every night for three weeks. The cask's count. The bank's. The harbour's. It had always been there, in the stone, under every still door she had ever opened in Saltmere. She had never listened.

"*One,*" said Kiva. "*Two. Three. Four.*"

Rhea lifted her hand.

"*Jude. Gower,*" said Rhea, without looking round. "*Edges. North and south. On the word.*"

Kiva heard them light behind her.

"*One,*" said Kiva. "*Two. Three. Four.*" She felt the rest come up out of the stone. "*Now.*"

"*Cut,*" said Rhea.

Two bars went into the cask from two sides at once, in the rest, through the grey staves above the foot. Kiva felt them go in. She felt the cask's count stop in the stone under her still door the way a heart stops under a hand, between one beat and the next, not on a beat. The staves went grey. They went soft. They went grey all the way round, from the cuts outward, the way frost goes over a field, and slumped.

And the held water came over the chime.

It came over all of it at once, on every side, four feet eleven inches of held water with nothing holding it any more, coming down the outside of the slumping staves in a white sheet, frosting as it came, onto the dock floor. It came toward Kiva's knees.

"*Far edge,*" said Pike.

Two words, once, from behind her left shoulder, where nobody had been watching but him.

Kiva shut her still door and stepped back. She stepped back and to her left, the way she had stepped off a line at Mallow Creek on Pru's word, and the white sheet came down onto the chalk where her boots had been and went past her on the right, a hand from her leg, and on down the dock floor. Pettit did not step back. He was a still door, and posts do not step, and the sheet went over his boots to the ankle and frosted them like salt, and he looked down at them and said "*Oh,*" very mildly, and shut.

The cask fell in on itself. There was no sound. It went down into the white water spreading over the dock floor as a sandcastle goes down when the tide reaches it, and was grey, and then was nothing but a long low drift of grey under the moving water, and the water ran on down the dock floor's slope toward the mouth.

It ran forty-five paces down the Graving, white and then brown and then grey, spreading from wall to wall, carrying the drift with it. Kiva watched it go. She watched it reach the sill, where Aske stood over his bag and Anwen's line stood shut on the granite and Bannock sat with Wace's coat over his legs. She watched it reach the Master's ward.

It stopped.

It came up against the grey across the dock mouth and lay there, against it, the way water lies against a gate, and did not go through. It spread back from the ward across the floor of the dock, a shallow still pool from wall to wall, grey with ash, and was still. Nothing in it trembled. Nothing in it kept any time at all.

The Master stood on the sill with her back to the dock and her hands folded at her waist and the held water of the Barrel lying against her ward at her heels, and did not move.

"*Sixteen minutes past,*" said Noa, at his desk.

Kiva sat down on the dock floor.

She did not decide to. Her knees decided, as they had decided on a bench on Sunday. She sat on the wet stone a pace from the chalk with the grey water lying shallow round her, and Pike sat down beside her without being asked, with his half-closed hands on his knees.

She lifted her left arm with her right hand and laid it in her lap.

The hand lay there at the end of it. It was her hand. It had the two dead fingers that had been dead since the autumn, and the scar across the heel from a can's lip when she was eleven, and the ink on the side of the first finger from writing left-handed all week. She looked at it for a long time. It did not tell her anything. It did not say *something's pushing.* It did not say *keeps time,* or *nothing.* It did not say it was cold. It lay in her lap like a thing she had been asked to hold for somebody.

"Gone?" said Pike.

"Gone."

He did not say anything to that. He sat beside her on the wet floor of the Graving, an old catcher with his hands closed halfway, and looked down the dock to the mouth, where the Master stood with her back to them and her ward across the opening and the flood not yet come.

"You were my second all winter," said Pike after a while. "I've been yours for an hour." His long brown face did the thing it did, very nearly. "It's a hard job. I'd not known."

"Two words, once."

"Two words, once," said Pike. "That's the easy part."

# Chapter 45 — Kiva: Let Go

Mara Rowan had her watch open in her left hand and her reading hand in her coat pocket when Yeo's whistle went three short notes, and she did not look up from the second hand.

"Fifteen minutes past," she said, to Hart, on his knees at the grating below her. "And forty seconds."

"Quiet," said Hart. His bad hand lay flat on the bars. "Still quiet."

She had not seen the dock. She had not seen anything of it after the cask went past the first stake and the second pass took it on toward the Graving's mouth, a grey shape riding across the mud between two fair heads. She had heard Noa's thin voice say *cliff* from the far side of the basin, eleven minutes past and forty seconds, and *read,* and she had kept her watch hand still. She had heard a girl's voice say *I can't* a long way off, and a healer's say *one,* and then *ten,* and Perrin Cade say that it was his as well. She had heard *cliff* again. She had kept her watch hand still. She had told her niece at half past five that she would not ask anybody to run and tell her anything, and she had not.

Now the whistle said the Master's ward was up and the sink was shut, and the rest of it was hers.

"*Quench,*" said Mara.

She did not raise her voice. Peverell was in the pump house doorway above the landing, where she could see the landing and the six pump levers inside both, and Peverell said it after her, inside, in her small dry keeper's voice: "*Quench.*" And thirty-six fitters on two galleries pulled thirty-six red handles together.

Mara heard it through the wall. It was a sound like a long breath let out. The thirty-six lights that had turned together four and four for six months went out in their tall glass cases all at once, and the pump house was dark.

The pumps did not stop.

She had known they would not. She had read the letter on the trestle on Monday and watched it done on Tuesday on one pump with its cells lit. She still felt it in her back teeth. Six iron pumps that had lost their drive in the same breath ought to have stopped on the stroke, and the outfalls below her ought to have stopped pouring. They missed one stroke. All six. Then they went on, up and down, four and four, on a count that came from a man in a chair at the foot of the iron stair with his right hand on the flange of the middle rising main and his left on a glass case.

Mara did not look at him. She could see him at the edge of her eye, a dark coat and a rug and a glass on an iron stand, and Kellan Renn beside him in black and gold, counting aloud under his breath. *Fifty-seven.* She looked at her watch.

"*Draw,*" said Mara.

"*Draw,*" said Peverell, inside.

That took the longest. She had timed it on the training cells in the keeper's room until she could hear it in her sleep: a minute and a half, if every fitter was quick and nobody fumbled a latch. Each fitter opened the slot at the foot of a dark case and drew the old can out, hot, into a box, and shut the box, and set it aside. Thirty-six old cans, stepped, every one of them, that had kept the town's time since twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month. They would go to the Board's press in their boxes as evidence, with the red tags on, and nobody would ever burn them in anything again.

She heard the boxes shut, one after another, along both galleries. *Thirty-three. Thirty-four.* A latch stuck on the north gallery; somebody swore and freed it. *Thirty-six.*

"Eighteen past," said Mara. "*Set.*"

Fresh cans went in. She heard them go home, thirty-six small iron sounds. Each fitter laid a hand flat on the glass of the case in front of them, palm down, fingers spread, as she had taught them at the keeper's table with three cells and a lamp. They had their jars at their feet in straw. They had their cards in their aprons, thirty-six cards in thirty-six different hands, each with its own count on it and not two alike in the house. Nobody salted. Nobody was to salt until she said.

"*Town or mine,*" said Mara.

"*Town,*" said a fitter on the south gallery, at once. Then another. Then all of them, along both galleries, one after another, not loudly, as people say a thing they have been told they will have to say. *Town. Town. Town.*

That was right. The fresh cans had nothing in them yet but fresh ash, and they were sitting in cases joined to pumps that were going four and four on a man's pattern. They would hear the pumps. They would say the town's count, because it was the only count in the house. It meant nothing yet. In a moment she would say *let go,* and the pumps would stand, and the house would go quiet, and every fresh can in it would be quiet too, for the minute it took to salt it with ash that had never heard a beat, and give it a card, and let it find its own.

She looked at her watch. Nineteen minutes past, and ten seconds.

She looked down the iron stair at the man in the chair.

"Mr. Halden," said Mara. "Let go."

Kellan Renn had his mother's watch open in his left palm and his right hand ready to count, and he saw the Proprietor take his right hand off the iron flange of the middle rising main at nineteen minutes past six and eleven seconds, exactly on the word, the way he had taken it off on Tuesday.

The pumps went on.

Kellan heard them. He heard them before he saw anything, because he had been counting their strokes aloud under his breath for three minutes, *fifty-seven* at the turn of every minute, and the count did not stop in his mouth when the hand came off the flange. It did not need to. Up and down, behind the wall at his back, four and four. The six outfalls went on pouring into the hand of water on the mud below the landing.

"*Town,*" said somebody inside, on the gallery. "*Still town.*"

The Proprietor did not move.

He sat in his chair at the foot of the stair with his right hand lifted an inch above his knee, where he had brought it, and his left hand flat on the glass, and his face exactly as it always was. Kellan saw that it was exactly as it always was. He had sat at that face's table four times. He had watched it say *I'm sure of my reading* to a Board. He knew it as well as he knew a column of figures, and he saw it now do nothing at all, which in that face meant that something had happened behind it that it had not expected.

"Sir," said Kellan.

"I've let go," said the Proprietor. His voice was quite pleasant. "My hand's off it."

"It's still going."

"I can hear it, Mr. Renn." The Proprietor looked down at his own right hand, an inch above his knee. Then he looked at his left, flat on the glass, where it had lain since 2611. Under it, in the brass-framed case, the old cell that the foundation called the first turned its light, small and yellow, four and four, as it had turned for two hundred and ninety years under one Proprietor's hand after another.

Kellan saw him understand it. He did not know how he saw it. Nothing in the face changed.

"It's one hold," said the Proprietor. "Not two." He said it quite slowly, to the glass. "I've held yards with my right hand and kept this with my left for seventy years, and I'd thought they were two things. They're not. It goes from here—" he did not lift the left hand; he looked at it— "through me, into whatever my other hand's on. On Tuesday there were lit cells under that pump to take it back. Today there's nothing under it but me." He was quiet a moment. "You asked me on Tuesday what happens when I let go of a pump that's only got me. I said it was simpler. I was wrong. I can't let go of the pumps without letting go of this."

On the landing above them, at the rail, Mrs. Rowan had not moved. She stood with her watch open and her face, Kellan thought, like a woman reading a fault report that had come out the way she had been afraid it would.

"*Nobody salts,*" she said, over her shoulder, to the doorway. Her voice was dry and exact and did not hurry. "Hold the jars. Hands stay on the glass. It's still town and it'll stay town till I say."

"*Nobody salts,*" said Peverell inside.

Then Mrs. Rowan looked down the stair again.

"Mr. Halden," she said. "Your clerk will tell you the time. I'll tell you the rest, once. The slack ends at half past. The salt and the cards and the count take four minutes, if my fitters are as good as I think, and the pumps three more to start. If you've let go by twenty-four minutes past, there's time. At twenty-four, if you've not, I'll call it off. I'll say *stand* and Peverell will put the old cans back, and the bank will go on as it's gone since the autumn, and the low town will be dry, and none of it will have been for anything, and it'll be on my name." She did not change her voice. "I'll not ask you twice. I'll not touch a cell to make it go faster. That's all I've got."

She looked back at her watch.

"Twenty past," said Kellan. He heard his own voice say it, quite level.

The Proprietor did not answer. He was looking at his left hand on the glass.

Kellan had read about that hand in his mother's newspaper when he was eight. Everybody in Meridian had. *The Proprietor's hand has not left the foundation cell since 2611.* He had thought it was a figure of speech, the way the Mercury said a thing had not been out of a man's mind. Then he had sat at the long table and seen that it was not. Adlam had told him, the first night, in the street afterward: once a year, on the first of the first month, the glass was taken off its frame and cleaned by two clerks with soft cloths, and for that, and only that, the hand came up. Under a minute. Adlam had held the watch on it every year for nine years. Fifty-one seconds was the longest.

"What happens to it," said Kellan, "if you lift it for longer?"

"I don't know." The Proprietor did not look up. "Nobody's ever lifted it for longer. Not me. Not the two who held it before me, that I was ever told." He was quiet. "I think it goes to its own count. I think the first stepped cell in this country, that's kept the foundation's time since 2391, goes to whatever it would have kept if nobody had ever salted it. I think that's the foundation's oldest property gone in a minute, and I'm the man who let it go." He breathed in. "And I think something else. I've kept that count in my own chest for seventy years, Mr. Renn. Fifty-seven. My heart's kept it. I don't know what I am if it stops."

"Twenty-one," said Kellan.

He found that he had his right hand on the old man's left wrist, the free one, with two fingers on the pulse, the way Talley had taught him on a boat because Finch had taught Talley. He did not remember putting it there. The pulse under his fingers was going fifty-seven to the minute, steady as a clock, four and four.

"You asked me," said Kellan, "to tell you what you wouldn't like to hear. On the card. Not counsel's. Yours." He did not take his eyes off his mother's watch. "Seventy years is twenty-five thousand days, and something. This is three minutes. If you lift at twenty-two, there's time. If you lift at twenty-four, there's not. If you don't lift, the low town's dry this morning, and the bank keeps your count, and the next time anything in this harbour falls into step it'll be because it was never got out, and every keeper in the country will know it was got as far as the salt and stopped because a man couldn't take his hand off a glass." He breathed. "That's the sum. It's the same number whichever way I do it. I've done it four ways since you said *one hold.*"

The Proprietor looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time, for perhaps four seconds, with his lean face and his grey temples and his eyes that had looked out of a window in 2604 at the lamps of the Long Quay going white. Kellan held the look. He did not know what was in it. He thought, afterward, that he had never in his life been looked at so carefully by anybody, and that it was the look of a man checking a sum.

"Twenty-two," said Kellan.

The Proprietor lifted his hand.

He lifted it off the glass slowly, an inch, the way a man lifts a dressing off a wound. The palm came away white. It came away the shape of the glass, flat, the fingers spread and stiff, as though it had been pressed in a book. On the glass where it had been there was a print of it, five fingers and a palm, in a faint fog that had been there so long it did not fade. He held the hand up an inch above the print. It shook.

Under the glass the first cell's light went on turning, four and four. Then it missed. It missed the way a man misses a step on a stair he has gone up every day of his life, and caught itself, and missed again. And then it was not turning four and four any more. It was turning slower, three and then two and then four, uneven, with a long pause, like a thing remembering how to walk.

The pumps stopped.

Kellan felt them stop through the stone of the walk, in the soles of his boots. Six iron pumps behind the wall at his back stopped on the down-stroke and stood. The six outfalls below the landing gave one last gulp each into the hand of water on the mud, and stopped pouring. For the first time since the boat came in under the sea gate, Kellan Renn stood in Saltmere and heard nothing going four and four anywhere at all.

Under his two fingers the old man's pulse stumbled.

It went fifty-seven. Then it went sixty-one, and forty-nine, and something fast and ragged that Kellan could not count, and the lean face went grey from the mouth outward, and the Proprietor's right hand closed hard on the arm of his chair. Adlam said something sharp behind them and started forward. Counsel said "*Sir*—"

"No," said the Proprietor. He did not look at either of them. "Leave it. Let it find something."

He was looking across the basin.

Kellan looked where he was looking. Across the drained basin, past the seam in the mud where Ivey's crew were getting up off their knees, past the first stake and the second and the long empty sill of the Graving's mouth, to the grey ward that hung across the mouth from wall to wall, and the tall thin figure in grey standing in front of it with her back to the dock and her hands folded.

Something went out of the old man.

Kellan felt it go through the two fingers on his wrist. It was not the pulse. It was what the pulse had been keeping. It went out of him the way heat goes out of an iron when it is set down on stone, all at once, and across the basin, and Kellan, who had never in his life felt anything that could not be written in a column, felt it go across the mud and the water and the seam, toward the Graving's mouth, toward the grey ward and the woman standing in front of it.

The Proprietor's lips were moving.

He was counting. Kellan could see it. He was not counting aloud, and his eyes were open and on the far side of the basin, and his lips were moving on a count, four of something, and a rest, and four.

Kellan looked down at his mother's watch.

*Twenty-two minutes past six,* he would write afterward, *and eleven seconds. His lips counted. Four, and a rest. I don't know what.*

Kiva was sitting on the dock floor forty-five paces in from the sill with her dead hand in her lap when it came across the basin.

She did not know what it was. She knew afterward, because the whole town knew afterward, that it was twenty-two minutes past six and that the Proprietor had lifted his hand. She did not know it then. She knew only that her doors, which had been lying down since the Master's ward came up, flat and quiet as dogs on a hearth, lifted their heads all at once. And she felt something come across the mud and in at the dock mouth, toward the ward, the way a wave comes across a bay toward a wall.

It was a count.

It was the town's count. It was the bank's, and the Regiment's, and the outfalls', four and four, the count that had come up into her fingers from every bollard and grating and bed rail in Saltmere for three weeks. But it was not in the stone any more. It was not in the water. It was coming across the basin by itself, loose, the way the held water had come down off the chime when the cask was cut, with nothing holding it.

It came to the Master's ward.

It did not go through. Kiva saw the grey across the dock mouth shiver from wall to wall, once, as a sheet shivers when a door is opened somewhere in the house. And she saw the Master, on the sill, with her back to the dock and her hands folded at her waist, go perfectly still. She had been still before. She went stiller. The ward did not move. The count lay against it, outside, the way the held water lay against it inside, and for a moment there were two things touching across that grey, and Kiva felt both of them, with her doors, from forty-five paces, at once.

Every lamp in the Graving went to a point.

There were three rows of them along the tiers on either side, white lamps in glass globes on iron brackets, lit since five, and all of them did it at once. The flame in each globe went small. It went small the way a flame goes when the wick is turned down, except that it did not go dim. It went to a single point of light in the middle of each globe, very small and very bright, as bright as the whole flame had been, brighter. It was not white. Kiva did not know what colour it was. It was not out. Every lamp on the tiers, three hundred of them, was a point, hanging in the middle of its glass like a star in a well.

Through the grey of the ward, faintly, she could see the Long Quay beyond the basin, and the white lamps on their fluted posts that the lamp-men had not yet put out. Every one of them was a point. Along the low town's edge, the yellow lamps in the windows of Ropery Row. Points. On the iron landing over the grating at the head of the basin, the oil lamp Peverell kept lit there. A point.

On the tiers, three thousand people drew one breath and did not let it out.

Kiva found that her lips were moving.

She was counting. She had not decided to. She could hear herself, under her breath, very low, on the dock floor: *one, two, three, four.* And a rest. *One, two, three, four.* She did not know what she was counting. It was not the town's count; that was outside the ward, lying against it. It was not the cask's; the cask was a drift of grey ash in a shallow pool. It was a count. She was keeping it. She could not stop.

Rhea was standing over her. Kiva did not know when Rhea had come. Rhea was looking down at her face, at her mouth, and Rhea's own face was doing nothing at all.

At the sill, forty-five paces off, the Master's sister stood at the Master's side, close enough to see her face. Kiva could not see the Master's face. She could see the sister's, turned toward it, white.

*One, two, three, four.*

Then the points went out.

They did not go out. The flames came back. Every lamp on the tiers was a lamp again, white, an ordinary flame in a glass globe, as though somebody had turned three hundred wicks up together. The lamps on the Long Quay were lamps. Peverell's oil lamp on the landing was yellow. And the count that had come across the basin and lain against the ward was not lying against it any more. It was not anywhere. Kiva felt for it with her doors and it was gone.

Her lips stopped.

"*Nine seconds,*" said Noa, at his desk under the lane mast, ten paces off. His voice was very small. "*By the mast clock. From twenty-two minutes past six and eleven seconds.*" He was looking at his tape. "*A hair. On every pen. Nobody on the mast.*" He did not say anything else, and he did not draw anything.

Kiva put her right hand inside her shirt and found the disk.

It was cold. It had been cold beside a Glory master at the far end of a polished table, and on a bench in a dock, and through two fevers, and through two crossings this morning. It was cold now. She held it for a moment against her chest, and it was a round flat cold thing on a cord, and nothing else.

"You were counting," said Rhea.

"Yes."

"Out loud. Low. Four, and a rest." Rhea did not take her eyes off Kiva's face. "I've written it." She touched the slate on her belt. "I'll not write what. I don't know what."

"Nor do I," said Kiva.

At the sill the Master had not moved. Her ward hung across the dock mouth, grey, from wall to wall. Her sister had taken a small book out of her sleeve and was writing in it, standing, very fast.

Mara Rowan heard the pumps stop, and saw the lamp on the landing come back yellow from a point, and did not let herself look at either.

"Twenty-two," she said, "and twenty seconds. *Salt.*"

"*Salt,*" said Peverell inside.

Thirty-six jars came up out of the straw on two galleries. Mara could not see them. She could hear them: thirty-six grey seals with two hands on them broken by thirty-six thumbs, and thirty-six pinches of ash that had never heard a beat going in at the tops of thirty-six fresh cans, in thirty-six dark cases, in a pump house where for the first time in six months nothing at all was keeping time. Not the pumps; they stood. Not the cells; they were dark. Not the man at the foot of the stair, who was sitting in his chair with his white hand held up off his glass and his face grey, and Kellan Renn's fingers on his wrist, and Adlam on one knee beside him, saying something low.

"*Cards,*" said Mara.

Thirty-six cards came out of thirty-six apron pockets. Each fitter laid their own under the handle of their own case. On each card, a count. Three and a quick. Five slow. Two and two and one. A hymn tune's first line. A girl's own pulse, taken at the keeper's table on Monday night and written down. Not two alike in the house. Mara had read every one of them herself at midnight and initialled it.

"*Town or mine,*" said Mara.

There was a silence on the galleries.

It went on for a long time. It went on for the length of three breaths. She stood at the landing's rail with her watch in her hand and her reading hand in her coat pocket, flat against her side, and did not take it out. It wanted to come out. It had wanted to come out since Sunday. It wanted to go up that stair and along the south gallery and lay itself flat on a dark case and *know,* the way it had known the Ferren bank on a hill in the autumn, the way it had gone white to the wrist knowing.

She had decided on a kitchen floor four years ago that she would never ask the girl anything about the dream, only write it down when it was told. She had decided in the pump hall a week ago that she would not touch a cell in this house. She found that it was the same decision, and that it was harder the second time, and that she was going to keep it.

"*Mine,*" said a fitter on the north gallery.

It was the heavy man with the burn scar on his wrist. Hart's mate. Mara knew his voice.

"*Mine,*" said another. "*Mine.*" Then they came along both galleries, one after another, not together, because they were not keeping time together any more: *mine,* and *mine,* in thirty different voices, some loud and some hardly louder than a breath. Mara counted them. She did not look at her watch while she counted. *Twenty-nine. Thirty. Thirty-one.* The man at the foot of the stair had shut his eyes. *Thirty-three. Thirty-four. Thirty-five.*

There was a silence.

"*Nineteen,*" said Mara. "*Case nineteen.*"

"I can't tell," said Loveday Hosken, from the south gallery, through the door. Her voice was very high and quite steady. "Mrs. Rowan. I can't tell. It's not town. It's not anything. It's so quiet I can't—" She stopped. "I can't feel my hand properly. It went when the pumps went. I've kept it on."

Mara did not move from the rail.

"Then say you can't tell," she said. "That's a word as well. Say it, and keep your hand where it is, and wait."

"I can't tell," said Loveday Hosken.

They waited. Mara stood on the landing and looked at the second hand of her watch go round. Hart had not taken his bad hand off the grating. Below her the outfalls stood dry, six iron mouths over a hand of still water, and the water did not move.

"*Mine,*" said Loveday Hosken. "*It's mine.* It's slow. It's—Mrs. Rowan, it's doing the thing on my card. It's doing my mother's hymn."

"Thirty-six," said Mara. "Twenty-five past." She took a breath and found that it went all the way down, for the first time since three. "Mrs. Peverell. Your pumps."

Peverell started them herself, one at a time, each on its own six cells.

Mara heard the first one go at twenty-six minutes past. It did not go four and four. It went in a long slow stroke and a short one, and a pause, and the first outfall below the landing coughed and poured into the hand of water on its own count, unlike anything. At twenty-seven the second went, faster, three and a rest. Then the third. Then the fourth, which was slow and heavy as a man walking uphill. Then the fifth. At twenty-nine minutes past six the sixth pump started on its own six cells, and six iron mouths below the landing poured into the basin on six different counts, and no two of them agreed, and the water under them went every way at once and did not keep any time at all.

"It sounds like a fair," said Hart, from his knees, after a while. He sounded astonished. "It sounds like six bands at a fair."

"It sounds," said Mara, "like a pump house."

She shut her watch. She stood at the rail for a moment longer with it closed in her hand. Then she put it in her pocket, and took her reading hand out of the other pocket, and went up the stair and in at the door and along the south gallery between the cases to case nineteen.

Every cell along the gallery was lit again, and every one turned its light on a count of its own, slow and quick and uneven, so that the long gallery looked like a street of windows at night with a different family behind each. Loveday Hosken was standing at nineteen with her right hand flat on the glass. Mara looked at the hand. It was white. It was white from the fingertips to the wrist, the way Mara's own had been white on a hill in the autumn, starved, every bit of warmth gone out of it into a cell that had needed it more.

"Take it off now," said Mara gently.

The girl took it off. It did not close.

"I kept it on," said Loveday Hosken. "You said. Hands stay on the glass."

"You did right. I'll have the healer for it." Mara looked at the case. It was not hers to touch. Every one of the thirty-six was somebody else's, and had been since the jars came along the quay at half past five in thirty-six pairs of arms. She had not touched a cell in this house in three weeks, and she had not needed to, and the bank was out, and the low town was taking water for seven minutes in Ropery Row on a count nobody had given it.

She laid her reading hand on case nineteen.

She did not read it for anything. It was done. She laid her hand flat on the glass, palm down, the fingers spread, because she had decided to, now that it was nobody's need, and she wanted to know. The light inside turned slow and yellow on a hymn's first line. Under her hand, through the glass, there was the cell. And under the cell there was nothing at all but more of itself.

"Mine," said Mara Rowan, very quietly, and took her hand away, and went to send for Aske.

Hart said *ache* at thirty-one minutes past six, and the flood came in under the sea gate.

Kiva heard it from the dock floor, faintly, through the Master's ward, like breath. She had heard it at the end of every slack since she came. She had never heard it come into a basin where nothing was keeping time. It sounded the same. She did not know why she had thought it would sound different.

She sat on the wet stone with Pike beside her and Rhea standing over them and her dead hand in her lap, and watched the flood come.

It came across the basin from the harbour, brown and quiet, over the mud and the first stake and the seam that Ivey's crew had shut, and up to the sill of the Graving's mouth, and against the Master's ward. It did not come through. It rose against the grey from the outside, a hand, a foot, the brown harbour water standing higher and higher against nothing that Kiva could see but grey, while inside the dock the shallow pool of the Barrel's held water lay still and grey against the other side of it and did not rise at all.

The Master did not move.

She stood on the sill with her back to the dock and her hands folded at her waist and the harbour rising against her ward at her feet, and she did not move, because she could not. Kiva had heard the sister say so on Monday at the trestle in the pump hall. *She can't hold it and move.* Now she watched it. The tall grey figure stood on the granite with the sea climbing the other side of her door, and did not shift her feet, or turn her head, or unfold her hands. Once Kiva saw her shoulders go up and down, slowly, as a person breathes who has been asked to carry a thing one more stair. That was all.

At twenty to seven the Company's two boats came out from the outer quay with the gate between them.

They brought it in on the flood the way they had taken it out on Tuesday, on lines, a great black wall of timber and iron floating level on the brown water, until it lay across the mouth of the Graving outside the Master's ward with a man's length of water between. Then the Company's men began to let the harbour into its hollow belly through its own sluices, to sink it onto its seating on the sill.

It went down very slowly.

At ten to seven the Master's hour was up. Kiva knew it, because the sister had told her so on the basin wall at ten to six, and she had done the sum then and seen that everybody had done it. Nobody on the dock floor said anything. On the tiers above, three thousand people who had seen every lamp they could see go to a point and come back did not say anything either. The gate went down a foot. The Master stood.

At five to seven the gate's foot touched the granite outside the ward, and the Company's men on it shouted to each other, short Company words, and let in more water, and it settled. The Master stepped back.

It was the first time she had moved since a quarter past six. She stepped back off the sill onto the dock floor, one pace, with her hands still folded and her ward still up, so that the gate could come home onto the seating where she had been standing. She stepped back as a person steps who does not trust the floor. Her sister put a hand under her elbow and she let it stay there.

At four minutes past seven the gate seated.

Kiva heard it. Everybody in the Graving heard it: one long deep sound of timber and iron coming down onto granite under the weight of the harbour, and stopping. The black gate stood in the dock's mouth as it had stood all through the Measure, holding the harbour out, with the flood standing brown against its far side and the Barrel's held water lying grey against its near side, in a shut dock, still.

The Master let go.

Kiva felt the ward go. It went out of the dock mouth the way a held breath goes out of a chest when there is no more need to hold it, and her doors, which had been lying down for forty-nine minutes, got up all at once, one after another, and stood, and shook themselves. She felt her Ember turn over under her breastbone. She felt her still door come up through her boots into the stone, wanting nothing. And she felt, through the stone of the dock floor, from a long way off, from the head of the basin, six counts at once, all different, none of them hers, none of them anybody's in particular, going on.

The Master of the Order of the Even Hand stood on the dock floor a pace inside the shut gate, grey to the lips, with her sister's hand under her elbow and her own hands at last unfolded at her sides. She did not fall. Kiva watched her not fall for a long time.

Then the second sister came down the steps from the outer quay with the Order's flat iron-wheeled cart, empty, that had carried thirty-six jars along the Long Quay on Saturday and back to the Mole and back again, and the two sisters helped the Master onto it. She sat on the cart's bare boards with her hands in her lap and her long grey face turned toward the shut gate.

Kiva got up off the dock floor. She did it badly, with one hand, and Pike helped her with his half-closed one, and she walked down the dock to the gate on legs that had knelt in slurry and carried a Barrel and did not want to walk anywhere. Her doors stood up as she went and did not lie down, because there was nothing to lie down for. The Master was only a woman on a cart.

She stopped at the cart's side.

The Master looked at her. Her grey eyes did not search. They looked at Kiva's face, and then down at the left hand hanging at the end of Kiva's arm, and up again.

"You counted," said Clemency Thrale. Her voice was very low and very tired and still came to Kiva as though there were nothing in between. "My sister says I did. Your officer will say you did. The man on the outfall walk will have a clerk who'll say he did." She was quiet for a moment. "I don't know what. Do you?"

"No."

"Then neither of us will say." The Master turned her face back to the gate. "Write that you counted, and that you don't know what. My sister's written it for me, in my words. That's all anybody should write." She shut her eyes. She opened her eyes and looked at the sister at the cart's handle. "Take me home. I've been in this town an hour and fourteen minutes, and I'd like to see the sea."

The sisters turned the cart round and pushed it up the long ramp at the dock's south side toward the outer quay with the Master sitting on it with her eyes shut, and the iron wheels made the only sound in the Graving.

Kellan Renn held the board on his knees at twenty-five minutes to seven, on the outfall walk at the foot of the iron stair, while the Proprietor wrote on it.

He held it because the Proprietor had asked him to, and because Adlam's hands were shaking and counsel's were not, and the Proprietor had looked at counsel's and then at Kellan's and said, "Mr. Renn. Would you." The board was a clerk's writing board from the keeper's room. On it was a sheet of the pump house's fault paper, because it was the nearest paper, and Peverell had brought it down the stair herself and laid it there without a word and gone back up to her pumps.

The Proprietor wrote with his right hand. It was steady. His left lay in his lap on the rug, palm up, white still, the shape of the glass, the fingers half curled and not closing. He had not put it back on the case. The case stood beside him on its iron stand with the print of his hand fogged on its glass, and under the glass the first cell turned its light on a count of its own, slow and uneven, three and a pause and two, like nothing the foundation had ever owned.

His pulse, under Kellan's fingers a quarter of an hour ago, had found something at last at twenty-six minutes past, when the first pump started. It had found sixty-four. It had stayed there. Aske, when he came, would say that it was a perfectly ordinary pulse for a man of fifty-five, and that he could not speak for a man of a hundred and three.

"*The Halden Foundation,*" wrote the Proprietor, "*relinquishes, from this day, its claim on all ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.*"

He stopped. He looked at it.

"Counsel will tell you I can't do that on fault paper," he said, not looking up. "Counsel will be right. He'll draw it up properly this afternoon, in nine pages, and I'll sign the nine pages, and they'll go to the Compact's court tonight with your figures." He touched the line with the end of the pen. "But this is the one I mean. I'd want it written where it happened, by the hand that's still working, on the paper this house writes its faults on."

"Why?" said Kellan.

He had not meant to ask. It was not his to ask.

The Proprietor looked across the basin. The flood was coming in over the mud, brown and quiet, past the seam and the first stake to the shut black gate of the Graving. Below the landing the six outfalls poured into it, each on its own count, every way at once.

"Because I felt it go," he said. "Out of me. Across that water. And it was never mine." He was quiet. "Your figures said so on Friday, in front of the Board. Adlam's books said so. Forty-eight a minute in 2402 and fifty-seven today, and the foundation never laid a finger on it. I've known since Friday that it wasn't ours. I've known it in my head." He looked down at his left hand in his lap. "I didn't know it here until twenty-two minutes past six. The foundation found counting ash in 2391, Mr. Renn, the way a boy finds a coin in the road. We've been claiming the road ever since."

He wrote again.

"*It was never ours. We found it. A. Halden, the Proprietor, at the Saltmere harbour pump house, on the outfall walk, at twenty-five minutes to seven on Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, 2681.*"

He held out the pen.

"Witness it," he said. "Under your own name. Not the foundation's clerk. Not Halden License's blade. Yours."

Kellan took the pen. He wrote under the old man's line, in his own small careful hand, *Witnessed, K. Renn,* and the time by his mother's watch, which was four seconds behind the Proprietor's, because it lost four seconds a day and he knew which four.

"It doesn't settle anything," said the Proprietor, taking the board back and looking at the two lines. "I'd like you to know I know that. It doesn't say I was wrong. They didn't go white this morning, the lamps. They didn't go out. I don't know what they did." He folded the fault paper once, very carefully, with one hand and his knee. "I've believed one thing for seventy-seven years, Mr. Renn. I believe it still. I'm only no longer the man who owns it."

He gave the folded sheet to Kellan.

"Give that to the Clerk," he said. "In your own hand. Then go and find your four. I've kept you from them all morning, and they'll want to know what you counted."

# Chapter 46 — Kiva: What Was Written

At a quarter to seven, on the floor of the Graving, with the flood standing brown against the Master's ward and the Barrel's held water lying grey against the other side of it, Kiva had laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the wet stone, because it was the hour.

She had done it with her right hand. She had picked up the left by the wrist and set it down on the stone, palm down, with the two dead fingers flat, the way you set a cup on a table for somebody who cannot lift it. Then she had waited.

Nothing. Not *something's pushing.* Not *keeps time.* Not the stillness of the slack, which was a thing in itself, and which the fingers had always told her about. Nothing at all. The hand lay on the stone and the stone lay under it and the one did not say anything about the other.

She had written it in her book with her right hand, for the first time since Sunday, with the pencil held badly in fingers that had only just finished peeling. *Wednesday the 23rd. A quarter to seven. The floor of the Graving, forty-five paces in. No answer.*

It was the first entry in the column that was not *yes.* She had looked at it for a while. Then she had shut the book.

---

Aske took the hand at twenty past seven, on the sill of the shut gate, after he had finished with Bannock.

He held it as he always held it, palm down and then palm up, and pressed the backs of the fingers and the heel and the inside of the wrist. Then he did a thing he had not done before. He took a pin out of his lapel and pricked the ball of her thumb with it, very lightly, and watched her face.

"Nothing," said Kiva.

"I know. I watched." He put the pin back. A small bright bead of blood stood up on her thumb and she did not feel it. He pricked the inside of her wrist, an inch above where the hand ended. "There?"

"Yes. That's me."

"That's you." He wrote. "To the wrist. Clean. Not cold; gone." He looked at the hand a moment longer, lying in his own. "The rest of the arm will ache to the shoulder for a few days, I'd think, the way it ached after the sixth. Then it'll stop. The hand stays like this. Three weeks, if I guessed right on Monday. Then it comes back as it was, with your two fingers as they are, and nothing more."

"And the card."

"I'll write the card tomorrow morning, at the window, when I've seen the hand after a night, the way I always have." He capped his pen. "You know what I'll write. I said it twice. I'll not say it a third time on a sill at twenty past seven with a dead hand in my lap." He put her hand back on her knee, carefully, the way he had once put it back on a blanket. "That's the bill. It's paid. It's what I said it would be. I'd like that written somewhere too: that I said it right."

"I'll write it."

"Good." He stood up, slowly, with his bag. "Now there's a girl at the pump house with a hand starved white to the wrist on a cell, your aunt says, and she sent for me half an hour ago, and I've been here." He looked across the shut gate at the basin, where the flood had covered the mud and the six outfalls poured into it on six counts, each its own. "A starved hand's an easy thing, if there's plain. Ward-trace, plain, a dram and a little more. The Order's four jars are at the end of the Mole with a woman who's just been carried there on a cart and won't be fit to unlock her own door till noon." He looked down at her. "Where's your tin?"

The tin was in Jude's satchel, under the book, where it had been since the second month.

Seven stood round it in the keeper's room at the harbour pump house at twenty-five past seven: Perrin with his hand pressed to his side, Jude with his knuckles white across the backs where the cask had wrapped them, Anwen with her cap gone and her hair down, Pru with her caps torn on two fingers from the catchers' plank, and Kiva with her left hand hanging. Peverell had given them the room. On the keeper's table, where the three training cells had stood on Thursday night, there was nothing now but the tin, small and round and sealed with Anwen's wax, with the label on its lid in Perrin's slow careful capitals.

*W. PLAIN. 2.0. FOR A HAND. NOT FOR A BANK, A FLOOR, A CROSSING OR A SALE.*

Loveday Hosken sat on the keeper's stool beside it with her right hand in her lap. It was white from the fingertips to the wrist, the fingers half curled and not closing. Mara stood behind her with one hand on the girl's shoulder. Aske stood at the table with his bag open.

"It's a hand," said Perrin.

Nobody answered him for a moment.

"I'm saying it," said Perrin, "because the Harbour Committee's chairman asked for this tin in the Salt Hall on Friday night for the bank, and I said no in front of the whole town, and I'd said it was for a hand." He looked at Loveday's white hand in her lap. "It's a hand. It went white on case nineteen at the bank, keeping it on because she was told to. That's not for the bank. That's a hand." He breathed carefully. "I'd like that known before it's opened. Not after."

"It's known," said Anwen.

Jude opened the book on the table and found the page and took the pencil from its string. He wrote, and turned the book round so that they could all read it.

*Wednesday the 23rd. The keeper's room, the harbour pump house, at twenty-five past seven. Sealed medical, W, plain, opened for a hand: L. Hosken, fitter, the right, starved to the wrist on case nineteen. By the healer, Aske. Used: as the healer says.*

"Sign it," said Jude. "Cade and Rowan. It's bound in both books."

Perrin signed. He gave Kiva the pencil. She signed it right-handed, worse than she had ever signed anything, and gave the pencil to Jude.

Anwen broke her own wax with her thumbnail. Aske took the lid off.

The ash inside was grey and fine and lay in the tin like flour. Aske did not comb it. He had combed it at the gate on the first day of the Measure, and every day since it had sat sealed in Jude's satchel under the book. He measured it out with a horn spoon onto a square of linen, a little at a time, watching the girl's face, and folded the linen round her white hand and held it there between his own two hands.

It took ten minutes. Kiva watched the whole of it. She had seen it done once before, on a kitchen table in Coper Street in the autumn, to her aunt's reading hand, and she had been too frightened then to watch properly. She watched now. She watched the white go out of the girl's fingertips first, slowly, from the ends in, the way frost goes off a window when the room warms, and the colour come back after it, pink and then red. She saw Loveday Hosken bite her lip when the feeling came back, because coming back hurts, and saw Mara's hand tighten on her shoulder.

"There," said Aske at last.

He unwrapped the linen. The hand lay in it, red and whole and closing. Loveday closed it, and opened it, and closed it again, and looked at it.

"I can feel the stool," she said.

"You'll feel everything for a day, too much," said Aske. "Then it'll settle." He tipped what was left on the linen back into the tin with great care, and looked at the tin, and then at the scale he had taken out of his bag. "A dram and two-tenths. The same as your aunt's hand took in the autumn, Miss Rowan. Starved hands are all much of a size." He put the lid back on. "Eight-tenths left."

"Eight-tenths," said Jude, and wrote it. *Used, 1.2. Left, sealed, 0.8. Bound as before.*

Anwen took a stick of wax out of her pocket and a box of matches, and sealed the tin again, on the table, with her thumb pressed into the warm wax where the old seal had been.

"It's eight-tenths," said Perrin. "Like it was. Before the Order." He looked at it. "It was eight-tenths in the autumn and Rowan said it'd stay eight-tenths till somebody killed something quiet."

"Nobody's killed anything quiet," said Pru. "Nobody's going to. There isn't anything quiet."

"Then it'll stay eight-tenths," said Perrin, "till somebody's hand goes white again. That's all right. That's what it's for."

Loveday Hosken stood up off the stool. She was a head shorter than Pru, with ink on her apron and her red hand held against her chest the way she had held a jar against it along the Long Quay at half past five. She looked at the five of them round the table, and at the tin with Anwen's wax going hard on it.

"I don't know any of you," she said. "I'd like to say thank you, and I don't know whose it was."

"It was the four's," said Kiva. "And the Order's. The Master gave them a dram and two-tenths in the second month to make it up to two, for a hand." She looked at the girl's fingers, closing and opening. "It's gone back to a hand. That's whose it was."

They bagged the purse from half past seven until eleven, and Kiva could not help with any of it.

The Graving was shut. The gate stood seated on its sill with the flood brown against its outer side, and the Company's men had closed every sluice in it and in the dock's walls by a quarter to eight. Inside, from the gate to Noa's chalk forty-five paces up and a little beyond, the dock floor lay under a shallow grey pool, a hand deep at the mouth and less further in, perfectly still. The Barrel's ash lay on the stone under it in long soft drifts where the held water had carried it, grey on grey.

"It's a purse," said Coombe, at the top of the dock-mouth steps, looking down at it. "Shut at both ends. Nothing gets out. It was always a purse; it's only that nobody'd ever drawn a thing this size into one before." She took her rope off her shoulder. "Catchers. Sheets."

The catchers went down into it with their own sheets.

She had never watched a bagging she could not kneel at. She had knelt at every one since the spring, at a hem or a corner or a far edge, with her hands dark or lit, saying two words or none. She stood on the granite now with her right hand in her pocket and her left hanging, and watched Pru on her knees in a hand of grey water with her capped fingers on the edge of a sheet, folding it in from the far side over a drift of the Barrel's ash, and Treen at the other end of it saying *now,* and Pru saying *yes,* and the two of them lifting together.

Kellan Renn, standing at the back of the small crowd at the gate with his mother's watch in his pocket and a folded sheet of fault paper that he had given to the Clerk at a quarter past eight, did not say anything.

The warrant pays no share on red. Write that, please. *No share paid. No claim. Held.*"

Jude went to Ropery Row at half past nine, while the catchers were still in the pool, and Kiva went with him because there was nothing in the Graving she could do with one hand.

The water was gone. It had come up through the gratings in the low town's gutters at twenty-two minutes past six, when the six pumps stood, and spread along Ropery Row and the lanes off it, brown and quiet, to a depth of four inches in the street and further in the cellars, and at twenty-nine minutes past, when the sixth pump started, it had begun to go down. By half past nine it was gone from the street. There was a line on every doorstep, a dark tide mark on the stone four inches up, and the cobbles were wet and shining, and the gratings in the gutters gurgled and rattled.

They did not rattle four and four. Kiva noticed it on the first grating she stepped over. She stopped and stood on it. Through the sole of her boot it went three and a quick, and then two, and then something long, like six bands at a fair, as Hart had said. It belonged to nobody.

People were out in the street with brooms. A man was sweeping water out of his front room into the gutter in long strokes, with his trousers rolled to the knee. Two children were sitting on a step with their feet in a bucket, for no reason Kiva could see, laughing. A woman at an upstairs window was hanging a rag rug over the sill to dry. Nobody looked frightened. They looked like people who had been wet before and would be wet again, and had moved the coal up a stair in the dark, and were now moving it down.

Jude went along the row looking at the doors. Kiva did not ask him which he was looking for. She knew.

It was the seventh house, a narrow one with a green door. The door was open. Inside, in a stone-floored kitchen two steps down from the street, a woman of about sixty was sweeping water toward the back door with a stiff broom, in long strokes. She was small and square, with grey hair pinned up and her sleeves rolled. There was a copper in the corner on a brick stand, and a mangle, and lines of washing across the ceiling on pulleys, out of the wet.

Jude stood in the street at the top of the two steps.

"Mrs. Penhale," he said.

The woman stopped sweeping. She looked up at him, a tall young man in a Meridian coat with his left arm held a little away from his body and his right hand white across the knuckles.

"I was at Mallow Creek," said Jude. "On the Wednesday. I helped carry him up the bank." He did not move from the step. "I'd want it said I'm sorry. I'd not have come except that it's this morning, and your kitchen was wet, and I wanted to see if it was dry."

Mrs. Penhale looked at him for a long time. Then she leaned her broom against the copper.

"It's dry enough," she said. "Four inches. I've had a foot in a gale." She looked at the water still lying in the low corner by the back door. "Davy used to sweep it out for me. He'd come down off the Longshore wet to the waist and sweep my kitchen before he'd change his own boots." She looked back up at Jude. "You'd best come down and finish it, if you've come all this way to look."

Jude went down the two steps and took the broom.

Kiva stood at the top of the steps in the street and watched him sweep a dead man's mother's kitchen floor with one good hand and one bad, in long strokes toward the back door, carefully, getting into the corners. Mrs. Penhale stood by the mangle with her arms folded and watched him too. Neither of them said anything for a long while.

"They say it's out," said Mrs. Penhale at last. "The bank."

"It's out," said Jude. "Each cell on its own count. Not two alike. The pumps on their own six each."

"And the water came in for seven minutes and went out again, and that's the price of it." She looked at the tide mark on her doorstep. "Davy'd have liked to see that. He was always saying the town kept one count in everything like soldiers, and soldiers fall over all together when one trips." She was quiet. "He'd have been on the mud this morning, if he'd been here. On the wall. He'd have counted it in fathoms."

Jude finished the corner. He leaned the broom against the copper, where she had leaned it.

"Ma'am," he said. "The Company's offered me his place. Not his. A place. On Mrs. Yeo's closing crew. From the first of next month."

"I know. Mrs. Yeo told me on Saturday. She came down to tell me before she carded you." Mrs. Penhale looked at him with her small hard grey eyes. "She asked if I minded. I said I didn't know you. Do you mean to take it?"

"I said I'd answer after Wednesday." Jude scratched his jaw with his right hand. "It's after Wednesday."

From behind Kiva, in the street, somebody said, "It is."

Yeo was standing on the cobbles in her green coat with gold at the collar, with her notebook in her hand and her boots wet to the ankle, as though she had been walking the whole of the low town looking at doorsteps, which she had. She looked down the two steps at Jude in the wet kitchen, and at the broom, and at Mrs. Penhale.

"I came to see the line on her step," said Yeo. "Four inches. It's on every step from here to the Tanyard." She looked at Jude. "Well, Talley."

Jude looked up at her from the kitchen floor for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "With one thing on the card."

"Say it."

"I asked you in the second month what ash was for," said Jude, "on the Ropewalk steps. You said keeping the sea out. On Tuesday I asked you what the Barrel's ash was for, when it was cut, and you said nothing; it'd be red, and go in the Board's press, and stay there." He did not look away. "I'll carry it up a bank for six months. Every bag. I'd want it on my card that I'm told where every bag goes after, and what it's for. Even if it's for nothing. I'll not carry ash up a bank and not know."

Yeo looked at him. Her flat brisk face did not change, and Kiva, who had watched it decide a thing at Mallow Creek in three seconds that was in no book, watched it now take longer than that.

"No Company hand's asked that in my time," said Yeo. "Nor in my mother's." She opened her notebook and wrote in it, standing in the street, fast, in her brisk upright hand. "*Talley, J. Closing crew, under the officer. From the 1st of the fourth month. Six months, and then we look again. To be told where every bag he carries goes after, and what it's for, by the officer, in writing, whatever the answer is.*" She shut the notebook. "I'll have it on a proper card by tonight. You'll sign it at the yard."

"I'll sign it," said Jude.

Mrs. Penhale had picked up her broom again.

"He'll do," she said to Yeo, as though Jude were not there. "He swept the corners." She looked at Jude. "Davy never did the corners."

Jude came up the two steps into the street. His face was doing something Kiva had not seen it do before, which was nothing at all, very carefully.

"I'd want it said," he said to her, when they were twenty paces down Ropery Row and the green door was behind them, "that I've seen the sea come in over somebody, and go out of somebody's kitchen, both. In a week." He stopped on a grating and stood on it, and it went three and a quick under his boots, and two. "I think that's what it's for. The ash. I think it's for that. I'm going to find out."

The Board's black iron box with three locks was carried out of its tent onto the boards of the timber pond at noon, and set on a trestle in the open, because Mara had asked that it be opened in daylight where anybody who wanted to could stand and hear.

A good many wanted to. The boards were crowded to the edge of the black water and along the brick wall and up the Graving's landward lip, where people stood at the rail looking down at the shut purse eighty feet below and then back at the box. The Mercury's young man was at the front with all three of his pencils. Kiva stood near the trestle with her left hand in her coat, beside Hart, who had come from the landing with his book of slacks in his jacket and his bad hand out of it.

Mara had named four people to stand at the box. Hart. Peverell, in her apron, who had not slept, again. Kiva. And Loveday Hosken, who stood very straight at the end of the row with her red hand held against her chest, because Mara had said that the fitter with case nineteen had been the last to say *mine,* and somebody ought to stand at the box who had been the last to say anything. The Board had named Yeo and Rusk and the Chair.

The Clerk unlocked the three locks herself.

She took out the paper and held it up so that the boards could see the wax, the Board's seal and the hour, and broke it, and read it aloud in her even voice, every word of it, as it had been written on the 11th at the keeper's desk with Peverell and Yeo watching and Rusk watching it written.

When she had read it she did not lower it.

"The Board has the morning's record," she said. "From the Works' reader on the landing, by her watch. From Mr. Hart's hand and his level. From the Board's reader at the lane mast, by his clock. From the officer's slate." She looked at Hart. "The Board will read each line against what happened, and say right or wrong, and the man who wrote it will say whether he agrees. Mr. Hart."

"Yes, ma'am."

"*The slack in the basin will begin at nine minutes past six by the pump house clock, and not before seven past nor after twelve past.*"

"It began at eight past," said Hart. "By my hand, at the grating. Miss Rowan's hand had it first, in the dock, a breath before; the Board's reader wrote it at eight past by his mast clock, set to the pump house clock at five." He did not look at his book. "Inside the limits. A minute off the figure. I've never been early, and I was early. I don't know why."

"Right in the limits," said the Clerk, "wrong in the minute." The Board's clerk wrote it. "*It will last not less than eighteen minutes nor more than twenty-two.*"

Hart was quiet for a moment.

"I can't give you an honest length," he said. "From the first quiet at eight past to the ache at thirty-one is twenty-three minutes. That's over the limit by one. But in the middle of it, from sixteen past to twenty-two past, my hand ached. It ached because the pumps were going on Mr. Halden's count, and the thing that had been holding the harbour's count was shut in the dock behind the Master's ward, and there was nothing in the basin holding anything but the pumps." He looked at the Clerk. "That's our doing. We put it there. When he let go, it went quiet again, and stayed quiet till thirty-one, though six pumps were running by then, every one on its own count." He stopped. "I don't know why six counts at once didn't ache it. I'd guess. I'll not give the Board a guess."

"Then it's wrong," said the Clerk. "Twenty-three, and not a clean figure. Write that, please, as he said it." She went on. "*When it begins, the ring at the outfall will rise from the mud. It will stand at four feet eleven inches on the basin's staff, and not less than four feet ten.*"

"Four foot eleven," said Hart. "By my level, at nine past. Right."

"*It will lie down when the slack ends.*"

There was a short silence on the boards.

"Not tested," said Mara. It was the first thing she had said. "We took it off the water at ten past and cut it in the Graving at sixteen past. It never got the chance to lie down. We made sure it couldn't." She did not look at anybody. "I'd want that line written as *not tested, by design,* and not as right."

"*Not tested, by design,*" said the Clerk. "*While it stands, the water inside it will keep the bank's count, four and four, at fifty-seven to the minute or faster.*"

"Fifty-seven," said Hart. "Miss Dray counted it from the wall, by her watch, for every pass. She's given me her figures. Fifty-seven, every minute she counted. Right."

"*Nothing else in the basin will keep any count, by the hand on the grating, though the pumps run.*"

Hart looked at his bad hand.

"Wrong," he said. "Twice. By our own hands. From nine past, the wards and the Embers and the still doors on the mud kept Miss Dray's count, on purpose, so the thing would come to them. We found on Tuesday that it had to, or it'd go to the pumps. And from sixteen past to twenty-two past the pumps kept Mr. Halden's, because we asked him to hold them." He did not look up. "We wrote that line on the 11th before we knew either. It's wrong. It's wrong because we made it wrong. I'd want both written."

"Both written," said the Clerk. The clerk wrote.

# Chapter 47 — Kiva: Its Own Count

Aske wrote the fourteen at seven o'clock on Thursday morning, at the north window, with the hand lying in his own like a thing he had been lent.

He had pricked it again first, the thumb and the heel and the place on the inside of the wrist, and watched her face each time. He had pricked an inch above the wrist and she had said *there,* and he had nodded. Then he had laid the hand back on her knee and taken out the card and his pen, and written, standing, against the window frame, slowly, so that she could read every word over his arm.

*Thursday the 24th of the third month, 2681. The left hand, as Wednesday: gone to the wrist, not cold. Feeling begins an inch above. The arm aches to the shoulder; less than last night. Three weeks, I think, from the 23rd. Then as before.*

*From this day: one crossing in fourteen days, at the most. For the year, at the least. The hand seen the next morning.*

*The three doors are not on this card. They were never on it.*

*I said the price on Monday and on Wednesday, before. I said it right.*

He capped his pen and turned the card round, though she had read it.

Hart was on the landing at nine, kneeling at the grating with his bad hand on the bars, though there was no slack at nine and no ring and nothing in the basin but the flood going out under the sea gate.

Kiva went down the ladder. She did it one-handed, with her right hand on the rungs and her left arm held against her coat, and he heard her coming and did not look round.

"It doesn't ache," he said, when she was on the landing. "That's what I'm doing. Six pumps on six counts, ten feet from my hand, and it doesn't ache. "Has it?"

"No." He took it off the grating and sat back on his heels. "It never tells me why. He looked at her left hand, hanging at the end of her arm against her coat. "Aske's card says three weeks."

"Three weeks."

"Lay it on," said Hart. "Next to mine. I'd like to see what it does."

She knelt. She picked up her left hand by the wrist with her right and laid it on the grating's wet bars, palm down, with the two dead fingers flat, beside his. Then she took her right hand away and left it there.

Nothing. The iron was under it, she supposed. She could see it was. She could not feel it at all.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing," said Hart. He looked at the two hands on the bars side by side, his ruined one and her gone one, for a long time. "When you came, you asked me what your two fingers could tell you. I said that was the wrong question. Ask them the same question at the same hour every day, yes or no, and write it down, and let the book do the telling."

"I've done it every day since the 13th."

"I know. You wrote *yes* every morning. Then yesterday you wrote *no answer.*" He did not look at her. "Your aunt told me. She tells me what's in your book, sometimes, when she's tired, and then she's angry with herself for it." He was quiet. "You'll be wanting to stop. For three weeks. Because there's no answer, and it seems foolish to lay a dead hand on a rail at a quarter to seven and write down that it's dead."

Kiva did not say anything.

"Don't stop," said Hart. "That's all I came down here at six to work out, and I've worked it out." He looked at his own hand on the iron. "I told you I asked mine the wrong question for ten years. I didn't tell you what it said, the first of those years, when I'd only just come to this coast and didn't know what to ask at all. It said nothing. Not ache, not quiet. I'd lay it on the grating every morning because the grating was here, and it was a lump of meat on the end of my arm, and I'd write *nothing* in a book, and I never knew why I bothered." He turned the hand over. "Then one morning it ached, at the pumps, and nowhere else. And I'd a year of *nothing* written down at the same hour to set it against. That's how I knew it was new. Kiva came to the van at noon, as she had been told, with her left hand in her coat and her right in her pocket, and found Rhea on the tailboard with a sheet of paper on her knee and Pike beside her with an empty cup.

"Sit down," said Rhea.

Kiva sat on the cobbles with her back against the van's wheel, because there was nowhere else, and Rhea looked down at her from the tailboard, as she had looked down at her from a hundred tailboards since the first month, slate blue, very straight.

"The card ends today," said Rhea. "It said we'd look again. I've looked." She did not look at the sheet on her knee. "I'll ask you first, because I'm the officer. Graywater's van goes north tomorrow. Pike's hands are coming back. Aske says by the summer they'll close two-thirds, which is more than anybody thought in the first month. He'll want a second at his other hem through the winter, the way he's had one this winter." She paused. "I'd not write you a seat. I've said so from the start, and I'll not unsay it. I'm asking if you'd want me to ask Graywater for you to be his second again next winter. When your hand's back."

Kiva looked at Pike on the tailboard. He was looking at the cobbles.

"No," she said.

Rhea did not move.

"Because I'd stay," said Kiva. She heard her own voice and kept it level, because it was a thing she had decided, and decided things are said levelly. "If I came back to the van next winter, I'd be good at it, and you'd be good to me, and Pike would nearly smile at me once a night. And I'd come back the winter after. And I'd be Graywater's second for five years, and I'd never call anything." She looked at Pike. "He doesn't need a second next winter. He needs somebody at his far edge. Nairn's watched me do it for six nights. Nairn can do it."

"Nairn can do it," said Pike, to the cobbles. "I've told him so."

"That's no, then," said Rhea. Her face did not change at all. "Good." She was quiet. "I'd have said no too, if you'd asked me to write it. I'd want you to know I'd have said it." She put her hand flat on the sheet on her knee. "Ask me something. It's either of us. You've a no of your own to take, if you want it."

Kiva thought about it, honestly, because she had been asked.

"Come on the commission," she said. "Coombe's. As an officer. You've been in more cellars than anybody on that list. I'd want you there."

"No," said Rhea.

She said it at once, as Kiva had known she would, and as Kiva had needed to hear her say it.

"Because I'd be your officer again," said Rhea. "On somebody else's commission, under somebody else's hand, and in a month I'd be standing behind you on a dock floor with my slate thinking *mine.*" She looked down at Kiva steadily. "I thought it on the Saturday of the Lane, on the fourth tier, when you shook your head at your captain. In my own head. It's not a thing an officer should think about a second. It's the start of keeping somebody." She took her hand off the sheet. "Graywater's mine. You're not. That's the no."

Pike, beside her on the tailboard, had shut his eyes.

Rhea picked up the sheet off her knee and held it down to Kiva, over the edge of the tailboard, the way she had once held a slate-blue card across a kitchen table.

"I've written you in," she said. "Not to Graywater. To Coombe. The Board's travelling commission, under her hand, from the 15th of next month to the last of the ninth. It goes to every municipal bank on the Board's roll in two years and puts it on its own count, and takes out the ones that have stepped as plain ash can be found, and writes down in seven columns what was got wrong first." She did not look at what she had written. "No seat. A line. Your name on it under another officer's hand, and not mine anywhere."

Kiva took the sheet with her right hand. She read it on the cobbles with her back against the van's wheel. She read it twice, as Anwen read a card before she signed it.

She read *Caller of the pass in three.* She read *one in fourteen days,* and *the three doors not to be asked,* and *under D. Coombe's hand.* And at the foot, in a different ink, as though it had been written a little after the rest, *Then we look again, and either of us may say no.*

"You've put *either of us* on it," she said.

"It's Coombe and you, this time. Not me." Rhea's face did not change. "I'd want it on every card anybody ever writes you. It's the only advice I've got left, and it's not advice, it's a line." She drank the last of her tea, which was cold. "You'll take it, or you won't. If you take it, you take it on whatever you say now, and Coombe writes it under. She's said so. She's coming along the quay; I can see her rope."

Kiva looked up. Coombe was coming along the cobbles of the Company yard in her grey Board coat with her rope over her shoulder, unhurried, the way she came to everything.

Kiva got up off the cobbles. She did it one-handed, and nobody helped her, and she did not need them to.

"I'll take it," she said, when Coombe stopped in front of the van. "On two conditions. Mine."

Coombe took the rope off her shoulder and began, very slowly, to tie a knot in it.

"Say them," she said. "Once. I'll write them."

"Every bank I call a pass at," said Kiva, "I teach the call to two people there who aren't me, before we leave. Out loud, on that bank, with whatever's to hand, a sack or a tun or a rope's end. And I leave it in seven columns in that house's book, in their hand as well as mine. And the next bank we go to, if somebody who isn't me can call it, they call it, and I keep the count, or the far edge, or nothing." She held the sheet in her right hand. "My sheet said the caller had to have been inside both gaps. It was wrong. Ivey called it in the Company yard on Thursday with one door and a girl of sixteen counting. I'll not be the only one who can. If the commission needs me to be the only one who can, then it's not a method, it's me, and I said no to that on a quay last Wednesday to a man in a chair."

Coombe's hands stopped on the knot.

"That's one," she said.

"The dream's not the commission's." Kiva did not look away from her. "Nobody on it asks me about it. Not the officers, not the readers, not the Board. If I've a thing to put on a table, I'll put it there myself, under my own name, the same night, the way I did in the Salt Hall. If I haven't, nobody asks." She was quiet. "I've a thing I've no word for. It's mine. It stays mine on the commission, the same as it's mine in a loft."

Coombe looked at her for a long time with her pale eyes.

Then she finished the knot, and took the sheet out of Kiva's hand, and laid it on the van's tailboard beside Rhea's knee, and took a pencil out of her coat, and wrote under the last line, standing, in her square unhurried hand.

*On her two conditions, said first. One: she teaches the call at every bank to two who are not her, and leaves it in seven columns in that house's book, and the next bank is called by somebody else where somebody else can. Two: the dream is hers and not the commission's; she will put a thing on a table herself if there's a thing; nobody asks. Accepted. D. Coombe, Hunt Marshal.*

"There," said Coombe. "That's a better first line than mine was." She handed the sheet back. "Your aunt signs it beside you. You're seventeen."

"She'll sign it."

"I know she will. She'll not ask you anything about it either. I've met her." Coombe put her pencil away and her rope back over her shoulder. "The 15th. At the Board's office at Meridian. Bring your book. Bring that hand, if it's back, and if it's not, bring it anyway, and we'll put you at a far edge till it is." She turned to go, and stopped, and looked back at Rhea on the tailboard. "Sorn. You wrote her well."

"I wrote what she is," said Rhea. "I don't write good."

"No," said Coombe. "That's why I asked you."

She went away along the yard with her rope. Pike, on the tailboard, opened his eyes and looked at Kiva standing on the cobbles with the sheet in her right hand.

"You've two noes and two conditions and a line," he said. "That's a good day's work for a girl with one hand." His face did the thing it did, and then, as it had in the cellar, it went the whole way. "Go and get your aunt's name on it before you lose it."

Mara signed it at four, at the table in the Works' lodging, with her reading hand.

She read it first. She read every line of it, Rhea's and Coombe's, the way she read a fault report across a counter, dry and exact, with her lips shut. She read the two conditions twice. Then she took the pen from the inkstand and signed beside the space where Kiva would sign, *M. Rowan, Grade Two, guardian,* in her own small exact print, and blotted it, and turned the sheet round.

She did not ask anything. Kiva had known she would not.

"The 15th," said Mara. "At the Board's office."

"Yes."

"The Works' transit goes on Friday. Rusk's been asked by the Board to write the Saltmere sheet, and he's asked me to write it with him, *How the Saltmere bank was taken out of step, with what was got wrong first,* and he says the first thing got wrong was a number I let a keeper say in a Salt Hall." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "You'll come home on the transit with us. On the Board's fare. Coombe's paid it; she says commission hands travel on the Board." She looked at Kiva. "Three weeks in the shop, with a hand that's no use and your aunt at the counter. You'll hate it."

"I'll like it."

"You'll like it for a week and hate it for two." Mara put the pen back in its stand. "Then you'll go. And I'll have the shop, and the Works, and Orla being rude to me on Tuesdays, and a sheet on the dresser that's longer than the Ferren one." She looked at the signed paper on the table. "I took it on my name, on Wednesday. You took yours. Neither of us owned the other's. I'd like that to go on being so."

"It will."

"Good." Mara stood up. "Then sign it. I can't hold the paper still for you all evening; I've a sheet to write."

Kiva signed it right-handed, under her aunt's name, *K. Rowan,* worse than anything she had ever signed, and blotted it on her sleeve.

Anwen went north at seven on Thursday morning, the 1st of the fourth month, on Graywater's van.

The van stood at the head of the Long Quay with its horses in and its lamps lit against the grey, slate blue, with Mace on the box and Nairn beside him and Ruddock and Tibb and Stiles behind, and Pike on the tailboard where he always sat, and Rhea standing at the horses' heads with her slate on her belt. Anwen's bag was already up. Anwen was not. She was standing on the quay with her cap on and her hair pinned under it and her hands in her sleeves, and Kiva and Pru and Jude and Perrin stood round her, because that was how they stood.

"I've a thing for you," said Kiva.

She had written it on Wednesday night, right-handed, at the table in the loft, three times, until it was legible. One sheet, in seven columns. She held it out.

*The pass in three. Second edition. What was got wrong first: the first line of the limits. "A caller who has been inside both gaps." Wrong. A caller who can hear the count, and somebody who keeps it who isn't carrying.*

Anwen took it and read it, all of it, as she read a card before she signed it. It took her a long time.

"For Mace's line," said Kiva. "If you want it."

"I want it." Anwen folded it once and put it in her sleeve. "I want it said I'll teach it. On the van. To the line, and to whoever Rhea puts at the far edge." She took her hand out of her sleeve again, and there was something in it, small and dark: a stick of sealing wax, red, half used, with the end of it melted and shaped by her thumb. "And this is Seven's. I've had it since the autumn. Every seal on the book and the tin. It's not mine." She held it out to Pru.

Pru took it. She went scarlet.

"I'll seal things," she said.

"Seal them properly," said Anwen. "Thumb in the middle. Not the edge." She looked round at the four of them. At Perrin with his hand pressed to his side, at Jude with his white knuckles and his card from the Company in his coat pocket, at Pru with the book under one arm and the wax in her capped hand, at Kiva with her left hand in her coat. She did not go red. Anwen never went red.

"I'm not going to say anything about the year," she said. "You all know it. I'd only say it wrong."

"Say one thing," said Perrin.

Anwen looked at him.

"I was the best student wall in Meridian," she said, "and I stood in the way of things for three years, and I thought that was what I was for." She looked at Kiva. "On Wednesday at ten past six I put a wall under a thing that wanted me, on a count that wasn't mine, and held it, and let it go into the air on a word. That's not standing in the way. I don't know what it is. I'm going to find out on a van." She was quiet a moment. "And you're going to find out what you are on a commission. In two places. Not one of us for the other." She took Kiva's right hand in hers, briefly, and held it. "I want it said that I'd not have had it any other way. And I'd want you to write and tell me what you get wrong first."

"I'll write it."

"Every bank," said Anwen. "I'll read them on the van."

Then she let go, and turned, and went up onto the van's tailboard beside Pike, and sat, with her hands back in her sleeves and her face toward the quay. Rhea went up onto the box. Mace shook the reins. The van went away along the Long Quay at a walk, between the white lamps on their fluted posts that the lamp-men were putting out one after another in the grey morning, and turned at the end of the quay toward the hill road and the north, and was gone.

Perrin let out a breath.

"I'd like it known," he said, "that I've never once in my life seen her cry, and I'd not have wanted to just then, and she didn't, and nor did I."

"Nobody did," said Jude.

"Pru did," said Perrin.

"I did not," said Pru, scarlet, with her face wet.

At a quarter to seven on Thursday evening Kiva laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron rail at the end of the Mole, with her right hand holding it there by the wrist, because it was the hour.

No answer.

She wrote it in her book, right-handed, leaning on the rail. *Thursday the 1st of the fourth month. A quarter to seven. The rail at the end of the Mole. No answer.* It was the ninth day of *no answer.* She did not mind it. Hart had told her what it was for. In three weeks there would be a morning when it said something else, and she would know it was new by setting it against these.

Jude was beside her. He had come out along the Mole with her after supper because he wanted to see the sea, he said, and he had not seen it from the end of the Mole at dusk yet, and he was going to be living beside it for six months and thought he ought to start. He leaned on the rail with his good hand and looked out past the sea gate's arch at the grey water going dark.

Behind them, along the Long Quay, the lamp-men were lighting the lamps, one after another, white and steady on their fluted posts. In the low town the yellow lamps came on in the windows of Ropery Row. At the head of the basin, the pump house's six outfalls poured into the evening flood on six counts, every one its own, and none of it came up through the stone of the Mole into Kiva's boots. She had stopped noticing that it did not. She noticed now that she had stopped noticing, and was surprised by it.

Noa came along the Mole at five to seven with a sheaf of yellow forms in his hand and his pencil behind his ear.

He did not hurry. He never did. He came along the top of the Mole between the bollards in his grey coat with the forms held against his chest, past the Company's swept watch hut at the end where the Master of the Order of the Even Hand had lain on Wednesday night with her hands folded, and gone home on Friday's boat without coming into the town again. He stopped beside Kiva at the rail.

"The coast came in last Thursday and Friday," he said. "Every lamp office on the Board's roll from here to Westerley, and the ships in the roads between: the same nine seconds, every lamp to a point. That's in my column already." He touched the yellow forms. "These are new. The Board's wire office has had them since four. From over the water, through the cable office at Westerley. The Clerk's read them and said I could." He did not give them to her. He held them. "I'll read you three lines. I've written them in my column. I've drawn nothing under them."

"Read them," said Kiva.

He read them in his thin clear reader's voice, as he read returns, without looking up.

The first was from a harbour master in a port over the water whose name Kiva had seen once, on the Board's big map in the Salt Hall, and could not have said aloud. *HARBOUR LAMPS AND SHIPS' LAMPS IN THE ROADS ALL TO A POINT OF LIGHT AND BACK. NINE SECONDS. RECKONED TO SALTMERE TIME TWENTY-TWO MINUTES PAST SIX MORNING 23RD THIRD MONTH. ENTERED. NOT FAULT.*

The second was from another port, further south, in another tongue, put into the Board's words by the cable office at Westerley with a note at the foot to say so. It said the same thing. It gave the same minute.

The third was from the port over the water that a ship's engineer had come from, off a hull, in the autumn. He had said, on a green-edged sheet from the Saltmere Company's hall that Noa had read aloud at a table in Meridian before Kiva ever came south, at the foot, *as a thing is written that the writer will not vouch for,* that his own port's bank had fallen into step at the same hour as Saltmere's, reckoned back. It said: *POINT OF LIGHT AS ABOVE, SAME MINUTE. AND IN ANSWER TO THE BOARD'S QUESTION: OUR HARBOUR BANK FELL INTO STEP 17TH NINTH MONTH LAST AT AN HOUR WHICH RECKONED TO SALTMERE TIME IS TWENTY PAST TWO AFTERNOON. AS OUR ENGINEER REPORTED. VOUCHED.*

Noa lowered the forms.

"Vouched," he said. "I've had that line in my column since the autumn with *not entered as fact* beside it. I've struck the note out this afternoon. Neatly, so it can still be read." He put his pencil behind his ear again. "That's three ports over the water in the same nine seconds. And a bank over the water that fell in in the same minute as ours. I've written them. I'll send them to every pump house and lamp office on the Board's roll, as they are, with the rest." He looked at the sea. "I'll not draw anything between. I drew one wrong once."

Jude had not taken his eyes off the water.

"Rowan," he said.

She looked where he was looking.

There was a hull standing in from the south.

It was a long way off, out beyond the sea gate's arch and the end of the Mole, on the grey sea going dark, a low black shape with its masts against the last of the light, standing in toward Saltmere on the evening flood as the coast boat had stood in a month ago. It was too far off to see what flag it flew, or whether it flew one. It had a light on its bow.

Kiva had seen every kind of lamp there was in Saltmere in a month. The white ones on the Long Quay on their fluted posts, and the yellow ones in the low town's windows, and Peverell's oil lamp on the landing, and the three rows of white globes along the Graving's tiers, and the lamps on the Company's punts and the coast boat's bow and the Board's tent and Noa's desk. She knew them as she knew the order of her doors.

The light on the hull's bow was not like any of them.

It was not white. It was not yellow. It was not the colour of anything she could have pointed to on the quay behind her, and it did not flicker, and it did not burn the way a lamp burns. It stood on the hull's bow out on the dark water, small and steady, coming in, as though somebody aboard had looked for a long time for a lamp that was like nothing and had found one, and had lit it, and had pointed the ship at the place where a light had answered.

Noa had seen it too. He stood at the rail with the yellow forms against his chest and did not say anything. Jude did not say anything. Behind them along the Long Quay the lamp-men went on lighting the white lamps one after another, and none of them looked out to sea.

Kiva stood at the rail with her right hand on the iron and her left hanging, and watched it come.

She did not know who was on that hull. She would not know tonight. She thought, watching the light on its bow come steadily in over the dark water toward the sea gate, that somebody on it had dreamed, a long way off, with a fever, of roads and lights and nothing over them and something paying attention. She thought that they had been at a window, perhaps, on the morning of the 23rd, at twenty-two minutes past six by Saltmere's clock, and had seen every lamp they could see go to a point and come back. And that they had got up from the window and found a ship, and come to see who had answered.

She did not think anything else about it. She did not let herself.

She put her right hand inside her shirt and found the disk.

It was cold. It had been cold at the far end of a polished table, and on a bench in a dock with a Glory master at the gate, and on a dock floor with two Glory fields touching across a grey ward and every lamp on the coast gone to a point. It was cold now, with a light on a hull's bow coming in over the water that was like no lamp in Saltmere. It lay against her chest on its cord, a round flat cold thing, and did not tell her anything, and she had not expected it to.

She took her hand out of her shirt and opened her book on the rail.

There was a column in it that nobody had ruled but her, the fourth, on the left, where she wrote within the week after any crossing what the disk had done. She had written in it on Wednesday night, for the two crossings, one line: *Cold. Both. As before.* She had thought that was all she would write in it until the next crossing, fourteen days off at the least.

She wrote in it now, right-handed, badly, leaning on the iron rail at the end of the Mole in the last of the light, with Jude on one side of her and Noa on the other and the hull standing in.

*Thursday the 1st of the fourth month, 2681. Evening. The end of the Mole. A hull standing in from the south, with a light on its bow like no lamp in Saltmere. Wires from three ports over the water: the point, the same minute. The engineer's line vouched.*

*The disk is cold.*

She looked at it. Then she shut the book, and put it in her coat, and stood at the rail and watched the light come in.
