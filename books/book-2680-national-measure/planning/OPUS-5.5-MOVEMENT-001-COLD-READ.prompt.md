# Chapter 1 — Kiva: A Count Already on the Water

The parcel had been tied by somebody with ten fingers, and Kiva carried it down to the river as one carries a thing that cannot be done up again.

It was eleven sheets, squared and taped, with the ring of the brass weight still printed in the top one. She had it under her right arm. That was a rule she had made in the last week, without writing it down: nothing that mattered went in the left hand alone.

The coast boat lay against the Hythe stair with her deck lamps lit and a plank out. It was ten minutes to seven on Monday the 17th, and the mist stood on the river like wool on a comb.

"Papers," said the mate. He had a manifest on a board and a stub of red wax behind his ear. "Who to?"

"Officer Yeo, of the Saltmere Company. And the keeper of the harbour pump house. It's written on the tape."

"From?"

"M. Rowan, Grade Two. Reader to the Municipal Works."

He wrote that. He wrote slowly, with his tongue in his cheek, like a man who was paid by the entry.

"And what is it? I've to put something. *Papers* won't do for the Company; they'll want to know whether it's a bill."

"It's a method. How a pump bank was taken out of step, with what was got wrong first."

"What was got wrong first?"

"A good deal," said Kiva. "It's the widest column."

The mate looked at her for the first time. He seemed to be deciding whether that had been cheek.

"It goes under the boat's wax," he said. "Sender presses. I witness. Then it's the boat's until Saltmere, and if it's opened, it's my name."

He lit the stub at the deck lamp and let it run onto the knot. He held out the seal by its wooden handle.

Kiva did not take it.

She had known, coming down the Hythe stair, that there would be something. There was always something. It had been a tin lid on Tuesday, and a tram ticket on Thursday that she had torn in half without feeling it go. She had a list of them in her book, in her own square hand, and she had read it over at breakfast as another girl might have read a timetable.

"I can't press it," she said. "I've two fingers on this hand that don't report, and it's the hand I'd steady the parcel with. And I can't tell when wax has gone off. I'd have to look, and in this light I can't. You'll have to press it. I'll witness."

"It's the boat's rule that the sender presses."

"Then I'd like it written that I asked you to."

The wax was going dull at the edges. He looked at it, and at her left hand, which lay on the tape with its first two fingers a little straighter than the rest.

He pressed the seal himself. He did it with a small grunt, and held it down for a slow five.

"*Pressed by the mate, at the sender's asking; her hand,*" he said aloud, writing. "Sign under. With whichever one signs."

She signed with her right. *K. Rowan, for M. Rowan.*

"That's a Circuit band on your arm," said the mate. "Third Division. I've a nephew in the Fourth. What do they have you down as?"

"Catcher."

"With that?"

"Yes."

"What does a catcher do with a hand like that?"

It was a fair thing to ask. It was the fairest question that she had been asked in a week, and it had come from a stranger with wax behind his ear.

"Says so, mostly," said Kiva.

He laughed, once. He put the parcel under his own arm, quite gently, and went up the plank with it.

She stood on the stair a moment longer. Healer Aske had counted the weeks for her, so that she need not, and she had gone on counting them anyway. There had been eleven and four days. There were ten and four days now. *Not a step,* he had said. *Not a ward for a tap.* The ash office had stopped her Monday thimble on the strength of his card; a candidate who was stood down drew nothing, and that was only sense.

She had three doors, and every one of them was shut, and a good many people had gone to some trouble to see that they stayed so.

The boat's bell rang. She did not wait to watch the plank come in. Her aunt was a mile off with one hand on a card and a drum to take out of step, and the books were under Kiva's coat.

---

The nineteenth drum house stood where the Brickfields road came down off the levee, between a chandler's yard and a row of carters' stables.

It was like all the others. It was a brick hut behind an iron rail, with a slate roof and a green door and a number on the lintel, and inside it one drum taller than a man. Kiva had walked past it a hundred times. Through the open door she could see the needle on its face, under glass.

The needle nodded. It went up, and came down, with a slow dreadful ease. It had been doing so for a month, alone, with the bank that had taught it the habit long since cured.

"There you are," said Mara. "Did it go?"

"Ten to seven. He pressed the seal. I asked him to."

"Good." Her aunt did not ask more. She was in her work apron, with the license in its case on a cord, and she held her left hand a little away from her side, as she had held it for eleven days. "I'm allowed a drum. I've it in writing. I am not allowed a bank until the first of next month, and Aske has said so twice, in case I'm deaf as well. You'll stand on the pavement."

"I know."

"You'll hold the book to the lamp when I ask. You're not Bench-passed, and that's a municipal cell, and I'll not have a line in Rusk's ledger that says my niece was over a sill. If you feel anything through a wall, say so. In the same words as before."

"*I can't weigh it.*"

"Those. I've come to depend on them."

There were four of them besides. The warden of nineteen was a stout man in a carter's apron, with his handbell hung at his belt like a tool. There was a Works fitter whom Kiva knew, since he had once dug up the mark that she landed on, and with him his boy, Ned, who was fourteen and had silt in his hair on the last occasion that she had seen him. Ned had a can on a hand truck. He looked at Kiva's left arm with frank interest and was nudged.

"Seven drums," said Mara. "One a day. I mean to do them as I did four-and-twenty on a wall, since I know of no other way, and since it worked. So I'll say it aloud, the whole of it, and anybody who hears a thing left out is to stop me." She held up her right hand, the one that told her nothing, and counted on it. "The streets are warned, and on their boxes for an hour. The drum's quenched. The dead can comes out. A fresh one goes home, with a pinch in it of ash that has never heard a beat. It's open for a slow sixty, and in that sixty it's tuned to a count of its own, off a card. And nothing near it keeps time."

"What's near it?" said the fitter.

"Nothing. That's the beauty of a drum. It's alone on an open link. At the pump house I'd a neighbor on either side of every cell, leaning. Here I've a brick wall and a chandler." She looked at the nodding needle with dislike. "It ought to be the easiest thing I've done this year."

The warden rang his bell down three streets and came back.

"They're on boxes. Mrs. Dunnet says an hour's an hour, and she'll be timing it."

"So shall I. Your winter stock, please."

It was a stone jar, the size of a loaf, with a brown card round its neck. He brought it out of a cupboard inside the drum house door. *Handful, plain. Tinned the 9th of the first month, 2671. For the wardens of the Levee end, against a hard winter.*

Mara did not take it.

"Where's that been kept?"

"Where it's always kept. In the cupboard."

"Beside the drum. For a month." She was silent a moment. "I can't say that it matters. I've no instrument for what a jar has listened to. I'll not open it in here, at any rate. Kiva. The pavement."

The warden handed it over the rail. It was heavy, and it had a tin lid.

Kiva looked at the lid. It was the cold-room kind, with a lip.

"Ned," she said. "The lid's yours. I can't find the lip."

"The what?"

"The edge of it. Under the rim. You've to get a nail under, and I can't feel where the rim stops." She held the jar out, in both hands, with the right doing the holding. "I'd have it on the pavement in pieces. It's on a list."

Ned took the lid off as though he had been asked to disarm it. Mara came to the sill with a bone spoon, and took a pinch, and went in.

It was done very quickly after that. Kiva saw it through the door, past the warden's shoulder. The red handle came over. The needle lay down against its pin, and for the first time in a month there was a drum on that road that kept no time at all.

The fitter drew the dead can and slid the fresh one home.

"Open," he said. "One. Two."

Mara had her left palm flat on the iron. She had a card in her right hand, and she slid it under the handle without looking at it.

"Three," said the fitter. "Four."

He counted like a man driving piles. At thirty Mara's lips began to move. At forty the needle rose off its pin, and trembled, and stood.

"There," said the warden.

"Not yet."

At fifty it was still standing. Kiva found that she had the rail in her right hand, hard.

At fifty-one it trembled.

At fifty-eight it went up, slowly, and came down.

---

Nobody spoke for some time. The needle nodded behind its glass, exactly as before.

"It's gone back in," said Mara.

"With what?" The fitter had sat back on his heels. "You said yourself, missus. There's nothing. The bank's out. I stood in that pump house on Thursday and heard three pumps arguing."

"I know what I said." Her voice was quite level. "Then it's the salt, and I was right to dislike the cupboard. That jar's been four weeks at the elbow of a thing that keeps time. I'd no reason to think that it mattered and I used it all the same, since it was nearest."

"It's plain," said the warden, rather hurt. "It's on the card."

"It was plain in 2671. I can't tell you what it is today." She turned her head. "Ned. Have you that bicycle?"

"Against the stable."

"The warden of twenty keeps her jar in her own kitchen. I asked her on Friday. Tell her that Mrs. Rowan wants it, and that she's to give you the jar and not a pinch in a twist of paper. Don't open it. Don't so much as shake it."

He was gone before she had finished.

Kiva waited on the pavement.

It was one of the things that she was learning about being dark. There was a great deal of standing. A catcher with her doors open was always a half count ahead of something, and a catcher with her doors shut had the trunk book against her chest and a view of a warden's back.

She leaned against the rail. Her left hand lay along the top of it, on the cold wet iron.

*Something's pushing.*

It was the first two fingers that said so. It was the only thing that they ever said, and she had stopped listening to them a fortnight ago, as one stops listening to a clock. She listened now. It was very slight, and she did not trust it.

She took the hand off the rail and knelt down. She put her right palm on the flagstone in front of the sill, and beside it the heel of her left.

There was the cold, and the grit. And under those there was a push.

It was not the one that she knew. She had had that one under her palm for ten days in the autumn, slow and even and many, four in and four out, and she would have known it in her sleep. This was slower. It came, and then there was a long nothing, and it came again.

"Mara."

"I'm thinking."

"There's something here. Through the flag. It's keeping time." She kept her hand where it was. "It isn't the old one. It's slower. I can't weigh it."

Her aunt came to the sill. She looked down at her niece on the pavement, and then at the needle, and then at the road.

"Count it for me."

"I can't."

"You can count to ten."

"I can't count this." It was not a comfortable thing to say on a public pavement, in front of a warden and a fitter. She said it anyway, in a carrying voice, as she had said it once in a yard. "I've one hand that hears and one that only knows there's a pavement. I took the middle of them in the Halden yard and nipped a first-year's fingers. The count of a thing that keeps time is somebody else's. I've it on a list."

"Then whose is it?"

That stopped her.

It was not the question that she had expected. She had said her piece, and it was a true piece, and she had rather thought that somebody would then take the thing away from her. Mara only stood on the sill and waited.

Kiva looked at what there was.

There was a warden, who could see a needle. There was a fitter, with a watch in his waistcoat. There was a boy coming down the road on a bicycle with a stone jar clasped to his chest, riding with one hand. And six paces out, between the tram rails, there was a grating of the old thick iron with a cord tied off to its bars. Her aunt had hung a cork in every hole on that road, and chalked a mark beside each.

"Fill it again first," said Kiva slowly. "With the kitchen jar. If it holds, I've felt a tram."

It did not hold. It went in at sixty-one.

"So it isn't my jar," said the warden of nineteen, with some satisfaction.

"No," said Mara. "It isn't the salt, and I've spent two cans and the best part of a morning learning it." She looked very tired. "I've three streets on their boxes and an hour that was up at ten. Kiva. You were going to say something, and you made me fill a can first. I approve of that. Say it now."

---

"I don't want anybody to count," said Kiva.

She had got up off her knees. She found that it was easier to say standing.

"I want two people to say *now.* That's all. The warden's at the door, where he can see the needle. I'd like him to say it each time the needle's at its top. And Ned's to lie down at that grating with a lamp, and say it each time the cork comes up to the chalk."

"That's not a reading," said the fitter.

"No. I'd not call it one."

"What does it tell you?"

"I don't know yet. It tells me whether they're the same thing." She looked at her aunt. "You'll hear it. Anybody would. It doesn't want a hand."

Mara said nothing for a moment. Then she nodded at the warden, once, as she nodded at an operator who had asked for a handle.

Ned lay down in the road. A carter going by with a load of hay stopped his horse to look, and was told by the fitter that it was Works business, and stayed where he was.

"Now," said the warden. His voice was rather too loud, like a man asked to sing in church.

There was a long pause. The needle came down, and stood, and began to rise.

"Now," said Ned, from the road.

"Now," said the warden.

They had said it together.

It might have been chance. Kiva held the book against her chest and did not breathe. The needle sank. Down in the brick throat of the grating, a cork on a cord sank with it.

"Now," said the warden.

"Now," said the boy.

It was like hearing two people say grace. They said it ten times, a warden at a green door and a boy on his stomach between the tram rails, and each time the two words fell on one another as a hand falls on a table. By the sixth the carter was saying it with them, under his breath.

Mara had shut her eyes.

Her lips moved. She was counting in between, and Kiva watched her do it and felt nothing but relief. That was a count, and it was being taken by somebody with a whole hand to check it against.

"Four. Five. *Now.*" Her aunt opened her eyes. "It's a five. It's a slow, lame five, with most of the push at the front of it."

"Is that a pump?" said Kiva.

"That's my third pump. I started it myself, on cells seventeen to four-and-twenty, at six-and-twenty minutes by an old man's watch, with water going over the sill." She went in. She took the card from under the red handle, and turned it to the door, and held it there for them to see.

It had one figure on it, in her own exact print.

*5.*

"I'd seven cards over from the pump house," said Mara. "They were in my apron. I took the top one."

Nobody said anything. The carter clicked to his horse and went on, since he could see that it was finished.

"*Not two alike in this room,*" said her aunt. She said it to the card. "I wrote that. I wrote it on a sheet that's gone down the river this morning under a boat's wax. I meant the room. I never thought of the water."

"It's kept its count, though," said the fitter. He sounded like a man who wished to be fair. "You told it five and it's doing five."

"It's doing the pump's five. There's a difference, and I can show it you." She put her left palm on the drum. "Give me the key. I'll tune it up to six. If the five's its own, it'll take a six and thank me."

She tuned it. Kiva saw the needle check, and hang, and for perhaps ten counts it stood and trembled on a time of its own. The warden opened his mouth.

Then it leaned. One could watch it happen. It was drawn back, a little at a time, as a cart is drawn into a rut, and by the fiftieth count it was nodding again.

"Now," said Ned faintly, from the road. Nobody had told him to stop.

"There," said Mara. "It was never in step with the bank. Not today. I took it out of one step, quite correctly, and I handed it to another with a card to say so. There's a pump two hundred paces off that drives this water, on a five. And I gave the nearest drum to it the same number."

"Will it hurt?" said the warden. "As it is?"

"It fed your streets for a month on a four. It'll feed them a night on a five. Ring them off their boxes, and tell Mrs. Dunnet that I owe her an hour and ten minutes." Her aunt began to unpin her apron, and then seemed to recollect that the day was not over, and pinned it again. "I'll want a third can tomorrow. And a count that's on nothing."

---

They did the sum on the pavement, with the plan spread on the lid of the hand truck.

It was Mara who did it. Kiva held the corner down with the heel of her left hand, which was a thing that the hand could still be asked.

"What's on this water?" said her aunt. "Say it. I'll write."

"Three pumps. A three, a seven and a five."

"And what did I mean to put on it?"

Kiva read it off the back of the trunk book, where the seven had been pencilled on Sunday night. *19: five. 20: six. 21: eight. 25: nine. 26: ten. 27: twelve. 28: fourteen.*

"Five, which is a pump. Six, which meets the three at every second stroke. Nine, which meets it at every third. Ten, which meets the five. Twelve. Fourteen, which is twice the seven." Mara's pencil went down the column like a grader's. "I've chosen seven numbers and five of them shake hands with a pump. I couldn't have done worse with a blindfold."

"Does a six matter? It isn't a three."

"I don't know that it matters. I don't know that it doesn't. I know what a five did, an hour ago, to a can with my own salt in it." She ruled the seven out, with one line, so that they could still be read. "I shall keep off the pumps and off everything that they divide, until somebody shows me that I needn't. And I'll write beside it that I don't know. Give me numbers that divide by nothing."

"Eleven," said Kiva. "Thirteen. Seventeen. Nineteen."

"Three-and-twenty. Nine-and-twenty. One-and-thirty." Mara wrote them. "They're slow."

"A drum's a slow thing. You said so."

"I said that it was patient. I was being sentimental about it." She capped the pencil. "Eleven, then, for this one, tomorrow at nine. And there's a twelfth sheet to write tonight, which is a week behind the other eleven and can't catch them. *Not two alike on one water, and the pumps are on the water.* They'll have had seven days at Saltmere of believing that I knew my business."

"You did know it. It worked on the hill."

"It worked in a room. I thought that I'd written down a method, and I'd written down a room." Her aunt looked down the road, toward the levee, where the pump house stood out of sight behind the stables. "Thirty-six cells on a harbour. I wonder what's on *their* water."

Kiva did not know. She had never seen a harbour. She had seen a map of the coast on a watch room wall, and the sea on it had been a flat pale blue, with nothing written on it at all.

Drum nineteen came out on Tuesday, on a count of eleven.

It took a quarter of an hour. The streets went onto their boxes and came off them. The third can went home with a pinch from the kitchen jar, and the fitter counted his sixty, and at forty the needle rose and stood.

It was standing at sixty. It was standing at six hundred, when the warden had rung his bell and come back, and Ned had been allowed up off the road. It trembled a little, as a needle does, on a time of its own.

"That's a drum minding its own business," said Mara. She took her palm off the iron and looked at it. It was pink. "And that's a hand which has read one, on a card, and has no complaint. I shall tell Aske so, and he'll say that he never doubted it."

"Well, that's one," said the fitter. "Six to do."

"Six to do. Twenty tomorrow, on thirteen."

She had the trunk book open on the rail. She wrote in it standing, in the small exact print, and then she read it over aloud, since there was nobody in that family or that trade who could enter a thing without a witness.

"*Drum nineteen. Fell in with the third pump on a five, twice. My count; my fault. The salt was suspected first and was not to blame. Found from the pavement, by two voices and no meter. K. Rowan, clerk. Out, Tuesday the 18th, on eleven.*"

"Clerk," said Kiva.

"You're not Bench-passed. I can't write *reader.* I can't write *catcher;* you caught nothing." Something moved at the corner of her aunt's mouth. "I could write *niece.* Rusk would enjoy that."

"Write clerk."

"I have."

Ned had come to the rail. He stood on one leg and rubbed the other shin with his boot.

"How did you know to do it with two?" he said. "I'd have counted it. Anybody would."

"I didn't know. I'd nobody that I could ask to count it for me who had a hand on it. I'd two people with eyes."

"It was only saying *now.*"

"Yes."

"I could have thought of that."

"You could," said Kiva. "You'd have counted it first, and been right, and nobody would have had to lie in the road."

He considered that. He seemed to find it reasonable, and rather a pity.

She went home on the tram with the books. It came up the Brickfields road at the pace of a tired horse, and she had the seat by the door, and she watched the drum houses go by. Twenty nodded. Twenty-one nodded, at the gasworks corner. They would not nod for long. Her aunt had six numbers that divided by nothing and a hand that had been given back to her.

It was Tuesday. That meant that Kellan was on a blue brick floor at the refinery, with his own cuff, cutting the top rung of a string that had been in a cage since the thaw. Pike had Pru, and a waxed cord, and a number that he made her say aloud. Jude Talley had seven drams and eight-tenths by name, in one autumn, and a draft board would be wanting to know how.

Everybody was getting on. She was glad of it, and that was true, and she had looked under it to see what else was there.

What was there was a mate on a boat, with wax behind his ear. *What does a catcher do with a hand like that?*

She had the answer for the hand. She had made it herself, in a hall yard, in front of four people and a sandbag, and she had been saying it ever since until it came out as easily as her name. It was the other half that she had not got. A catcher with a bad hand named what she could not do, and somebody else did it. A catcher with a bad hand and no doors named what she could not do, and then stood on a pavement.

She had asked two people to say a word. She could not find more in the day than that, and she had gone over it twice.

There was a large person sitting on the laundry step when the tram let her down at Coper Street, and he had a cardboard box on his knees.

"I can't get it on," said Perrin Cade.

He said it with great dignity. He was strapped from the armpits to the hips, under his coat, and he sat as a man sits who has been packed for a journey.

"What is it?"

"It's my Hall's second team. It came this morning, with my name sewn in the neck." He lifted the lid. It was a jersey, grey, with a band of dull red across the chest, and it was folded as only a shop folds. "I've been promised the second team since the ninth month. I've three hundred and eighty kilos cut in a stone. And I can't get my arms above my ears, so I've yet to see myself in it."

"I can't help you. A sleeve wants holding open. That's two hands."

"I know. I've been waiting for Pryce. I thought that I'd wait in company." He moved along the step. "What did you do today?"

Kiva sat down beside him, with the books on her knee.

"I stood on a pavement. Yesterday I knelt on one."

"I sat in the front row of the Long Terrace and watched a second-year lift three-forty. He did it rather well. I clapped." Perrin looked at the jersey. "I'm to lift nothing until the strapping's off. That's the twentieth of next month, and then it's a pail, and then two pails. I'm to stand no floor before the new year. I asked whether I might call *off* from a chair, and the healer said that he supposed nobody could stop me."

"That's ten weeks."

"And four days. I counted."

"So did I."

"Aske counted mine as well?"

"He counted mine. I only check him."

They sat for a while. It was getting dark, and the lamplighter was working his way along Coper Street on his board, a hand's height off the paving.

"Then that's two of us who are no use until the first of the first," said Perrin. "I'd like it known that I intend to be extremely bad at it. I've never had to sit still. I've a notion that I shall mind, and say so, and that people will get tired of me."

"People aren't tired of you."

"It's only been twelve days."

The laundry door opened behind them. Anwen came out in her brown coat, with her hair pinned for a ward line, and stopped with one foot on the step.

"No," she said.

"You don't know what I was going to ask."

"It's in a box and you're holding it like a baby. I've a wall at six." She looked at the two of them, and at the jersey, and breathed out through her nose. "Stand up. Arms forward. Not up. Kiva, the hem's yours; you've a right hand. I'll have the neck."

It took three of them, and it was not quick. Kiva hauled, and Anwen steered, and Perrin said that he was being very brave, in a voice that came out of a great deal of grey wool. Then his head came through, red, with the hair on end.

He stood on the laundry step in it, under the lamp.

"Well?"

"It's a jersey," said Anwen. "You're in it."

"I want it said that it fits."

"It fits." She did up his coat over it, since he could not reach the top button, and she did Kiva's cuff as she passed, without remark. "Mace says that a relief who can't walk a wall forty paces by midwinter isn't a relief, she's a guest. I can do twelve. So I'm going." Her level eyes came round. "What's behind the pavement?"

"A five. My aunt gave a drum the same count as a pump, and it fell in with the pump. It's out now. On eleven."

"Who found the five?"

"A warden and a fitter's boy. I asked them to say *now.*"

Anwen looked at her for a moment longer than the answer wanted.

"I'd like that told properly," she said. "On Saturday. The Low Lane, at two. Jude's called a floor."

"Jude has?"

"He's the only one of us with nothing strapped, stopped or spoken for. He says that somebody had better." She went down the step. "Bring your knees. You're a corner."

She wrote it up that night at the kitchen table, on the left, where the things went that she had seen.

It took a page. She put down the jar, and the lid, and the two cans. She put down the push through the flag, and that it was the first two fingers which had spoken first, on a rail, and that she had not believed them. She ruled a line under that and wrote, *They were right. I don't know that they'd be right twice.*

Then she came to the part with the two voices, and she stopped.

She had meant to write what had happened. She found that she was writing what to do. *One at the needle. One at the water. Neither counts. Each says "now" at the top. If the two words fall together ten times, the drum is keeping the water's time and not its own. Then somebody with a whole hand counts between.*

It did not read like her book. Her book was full of things that had been felt in the dark by one person, and paid for, and set down afterward with *I can't weigh it* at the end. This could have been pinned to a drum house door. A warden could have done it, or a carter.

She looked at it for some time. It was a good deal less than she was used to being.

"You've stopped," said Mara.

Her aunt was across the table with the twelfth sheet. It had seven columns, like the others, and the fourth was already the widest.

"I've written it so that Ned could do it."

"Ned did do it."

"I mean without me there."

"So do I." Mara blotted her line. "That's what the sheets are. I stood between four-and-twenty cells with one hand and didn't know for six minutes whether I'd keep it. I've spent a fortnight writing that down in such a way that a harbour keeper I've never met needn't do the same. It isn't a comedown. It's the trade." She looked up. "Your sponsor reads that book on Sunday."

"She reads it every Sunday."

"I know that she does. I was only thinking that she'd find it a change."

Kiva turned the page back, and read the entry through from the top.

She had not torn a page out of that book in a spring and a summer and an autumn. She did not mean to begin. But she took up the pencil again, and at the foot of the right-hand side, where the reasons went, she wrote one line more. She wrote it small.

*I don't yet know what I'm for, dark. Today I was for this.*

Then she shut the cover, and laid the book where it lay every night, by the brass weight, for her aunt to read.

# Chapter 2 — Kiva: A Corner Any Warden Could Be

The Charter Board's notice went up in the Measure Hall basement on Thursday, and by four o'clock nobody could get near the ash office for the crowd in front of it.

It was a printed sheet, with a seal that Kiva had not seen before. The Circuit's own notices were in a clerk's hand and smelled of paste. This had come two hundred miles in a tin tube.

*The Charter Board. The National Measure, at Saltmere, 2681.*

*Six circuits. The first company of each: four fours. Twenty-four fours in all.*

*The gate opens on Tuesday the 1st of the third month. Seeding through the fortnight.*

*Four boards. Monday the 14th: the Bench. Tuesday the 15th: the Carry. Wednesday the 16th: the Hunt. Thursday the 17th to Saturday the 19th: the Lane.*

*Each board is marked by place: twenty-four to the first four, and one to the last. The four marks are added.*

*The first eight fours stand on the national license roll for the year.*

*Conditions of entry, and the warrant, to each circuit in the first month.*

"Ninety-six," said Kellan.

He had got to the front, as he always did, by being narrow. He had his own pencil out.

"Twenty-four a board, and four boards. Ninety-six is perfect. Nobody will have it; you'd have to win a bench and a hunt and a lane with the same four people." He was writing on his cuff. "We were fourth of sixteen at the Regional. Turn that up, and it's sixth of twenty-four. If every circuit were Meridian."

"Are they?" said Perrin.

"Saltmere's had its load plate beaten four times since the thaw. That was on Bexley's sheet. I'd say that they aren't worse."

"Then we're not sixth."

"I said *if.* I'd expect nothing else of a sum than that somebody should take the cheerful half of it."

Perrin was standing very upright, in his coat, with the grey jersey showing at the neck. He had worn it every day since Tuesday. Nobody had mentioned it.

"What's the roll?" said Pru. She was at Kiva's elbow, on her toes. "The license roll. Is that the same as a step?"

"It isn't," said Noa.

He had not pushed. He had simply been there already, at the end of the board, with a roll of tape under his arm, as a post is there.

"A step's the Circuit's. It says what bell you may answer. A first step answers a Handful. A second answers a Flask, behind a chartered crew. Seven has a second step in its own right, since the fourteenth." He said it to the notice and not to them. "A license is the Charter Board's. It doesn't say what you may answer. It says what you may be sent for, or go and look for. The national roll's the top of it. Eight fours, licensed for a year to stand a Barrel response under a company officer."

"What's a Barrel?" said Jude.

"I've never seen one. Nor has anybody in this basement. There's a column for it in the yield book, between a Flask and a Well, and it's had four entries in my lifetime." Noa went a little pink. "I looked."

"So the roll's a list of people who get sent for," said Anwen.

"Yes."

"Like the Line's."

"Like the Line's, for the whole country. And with a wage."

Kiva was reading the four boards again.

She did it as she read a floor, a corner at a time. *The Bench.* That was banks of cells with a fault planted, to be found and quenched and tuned again. It was on her certificate, in the Chair's hand: *not at the Bench.* Seven would stand that board with three, and Pru.

*The Carry.* That was Perrin's. It always had been.

*The Hunt.* She did not know what that was.

"It's in the small print," said Noa. He had seen where she was looking. "Under the seal."

It was. *The Hunt: a licensed hunt on tidal ground, under company officers. Marked on clean yield after shares, less every remark. A four holding a Flask license in its own right hunts on its own entry. A four without one hunts attached. Students close nothing.*

"A hunt," said Jude slowly. "On purpose. They take us out on the sand and we find a thing and kill it, and somebody weighs what's left." He scratched his jaw. "I've been found by a husk. I never needed a license for that."

"Clean yield," said Kellan. "That's the whole of the board in two words. Yield's the size of the thing. A Handful's four-and-twenty drams by the book, a Flask is the best part of five hundred. Grade's how well you tinned it. And clean is what the comb says. Under trace on the seventh reed, it's clean and it counts. Over, and it's red, and it's evidence, and you've been out in the wet all day for a tag."

"It's all had the hair since the thaw," said Pru.

"A hair's trace. Trace is clean. I've read the rule three times, since somebody will try it on." He looked at her, not unkindly. "You wrote a red tag in the autumn on the best part of sixty drams off my cut. On that board that's sixty off our mark, and it's your word that takes it off. You'd better like the sound of your own voice."

Pru went scarlet, and said nothing, and did not look sorry.

"Is there sea at all four?" said Jude. "Or only the one?"

"It's a harbour city. I should think that there's sea at breakfast."

"Then I'll stand. If I'm asked by name."

"You were asked on Saturday," said Anwen. "It's in the book."

"I like being asked. I've found that it doesn't wear off."

Somebody was being wheeled through the crowd. It parted for her, as a crowd will for a chair.

Selka Dray had her leg out in front of her still, but the board on it was shorter. It stopped at the knee. She had a rug, and Wilf Marten at the handles, and she read the notice from top to bottom without moving her head.

"Forty degrees," she said, to nobody in particular. "He wanted thirty. I argued." Her sunk grey eyes came round, and found Kiva, and rested there. "The Bench is first. You're barred it."

"I know."

"So Seven's a four of three on the first board, and the Chair's reader takes a mast to the other three for your sake. I asked for that. It went under seal." Her jaw moved. "I'd not have you think that I'd forgotten. Push, Marten."

They went. Perrin watched the chair go up the passage.

"I'd like it known," he said, "that I find her restful. One always knows where one is."

Kiva was still looking at the sheet.

She had counted it up, and she did not much care for the sum. On the first board she was nothing. On the second she was whatever a carry team wanted fetched. On the third and fourth she was a catcher with one door, declared, and a mast on her; and that was only if a healer signed a card in ten weeks' time, for a hand that he had already said he could not promise.

There was a line at the foot which she had read over twice. *Entries under each team's own hand.*

Kellan had read it too. He had stopped writing on his cuff. He was looking at another part of the board altogether, where the Regional result was still pinned, with a name at the top of it in black and gold.

He saw her see. He put his pencil away.

Jude Talley called his floor for two on Saturday, in the Low Lane, and he began it by saying what it was for.

"I'm the only one here who's allowed to be useful," he said. "So I've called it. I'm not the catcher and I'm not anybody's captain. If I say a thing that's wrong, Finch is to tell me, since it's her cloth."

"Me?" said Pru.

"You. Rowan's a corner. She said so in a hall yard."

It was the first frost. The Low Lane was a long brick floor under a slate roof, built against the core house wall, and it was never warm; but in the summer the hum came through the bricks like a kettle on the hob, and one forgot. Now Kiva could see her breath. The mast at the far end was sixty years old and drew one line for whoever happened to be lit in front of it.

Gerald hung from the hoist. He was a hundredweight of sand in a sack, with a face that Perrin had drawn on him in the eighth month, and he had been a rung for them all autumn.

"It's a rung to a bag, under a porch," said Jude. "That's what Stopgate was. A heap comes down, open, within reach of a lit box, and the wall stands between the two while the cloth's drawn. Pryce has the porch. There's the box."

It was a lamp-cell on a stool, lit, eight paces off. Anwen stood between it and the cloth with her hands low.

"Finch catches. Renn and I have the north corners. Rowan's south-east, which is a right-hand corner; I asked her which. Cade's the clock."

"I mind being a clock," said Perrin, from the bench by the door. He had a watch on his knee, borrowed from his father, the size of a turnip. "I'd like that understood before we begin. I've been a carry and a litter and a brake. I've never been furniture."

"You're calling sixty. Aloud."

"I can hear that I am."

Pru threw the cloth. It lay flat at the third try. Jude let Gerald go.

"*Dark! One! Corners!*"

Kiva hauled.

She had her corner in her right hand and the slack of the hem along her good side, where she could feel it come. It came. She brought it in a hand, and another, low, and the neck drew up in the middle of the cloth like the mouth of a sack. Behind her a wall of silver stood between the heap and the lamp and did not flicker.

"Thirty," said Perrin. "Thirty-one."

Pru had the cord at her elbow. One turn. Two.

"Sealed," she said. "Fifty-four."

"That's a bag," said Jude. "Again."

They did it again. Kiva felt the same things in the same order, and thought, rather drearily, that she could have been a pulley. The neck closed. The cord went round, once, and twice, and on the third turn something happened that she did not see, and Pru said a word that she had certainly not learned at home.

"Sixty," said Perrin. "Sixty-one. I'm told that I'm to go on."

"It slipped," said Pru. "The third. It's the wax. It's gone like wood."

"Warm it," said Kellan.

She put the cord under her arm, inside her tunic, and they waited two minutes by the turnip. Gerald went up on the hoist. Gerald came down.

It slipped again.

It slipped a third time. On the fourth Pru got it round and it held, and she sealed at fifty-nine with her face the color of brick, and on the fifth it went from under her fingers like a fish.

"That's three lost in five," she said. Her voice had gone up. "I do it right thirty in thirty-four. Pike counted. I've done it at a planked grating, in the dark, with a warden for a corner. I don't *lose* them."

"It's cold," said Anwen, without turning round. "My wall's stiff too. Everything's slower."

"I've no business being slower. It's the only thing I'm for."

Nobody answered that. Jude had sat down on the edge of the cloth, with his long forearms on his knees, and he was looking at Kiva.

"I'll ask a thing," he said. "I'm on the line by name, and I've a head's share, and I'm told that a warrant to the coast is two drams a head at the least. So I'll ask it. I'd sooner ask than count it up at night."

"Ask," said Kiva.

"Finch has the cloth. She's the count on it, and the cord, and a red tag with her own name. You're a corner. You're a good one; you're low, and you don't snatch." He scratched his jaw. "I held a corner at Stopgate with a warden on the one beside me, who'd never seen a hem in her life, and she did as she was bid and it was a bag. So a corner's a thing that any warden could be. What are we taking you to Saltmere for?"

The Low Lane was very quiet. Anwen's wall was still up. Kiva saw it waver, once, at the top.

"I want it said," said Anwen, to the lamp, "that nobody else on this floor was going to ask that. And that we ought to have."

"I'm not saying leave her," said Jude. "I'd want that written. I'm saying I don't know what I'd tell a clerk."

Kiva had an answer.

It was about three doors, and a hill, and an arm that had gone white to above the elbow so that a gate could be shut. It was all true. It was a list of things that she had done once, at a price, and was forbidden to do again; and it would have sounded, on that cold brick, exactly like a girl reading out her own notices.

She put it where she had put the others.

"I don't know either," she said. "Not today. In ten weeks I'm a catcher with one door, if Aske signs. Today I'm a corner. I'd sooner you'd asked than taken me from habit." She found that her mouth was dry. "If I've no better answer by the time there's an entry to sign, you'll say so again. At the table. I'll not have it settled round me."

"That's Renn's line," said Jude.

"I know. I've been keeping it."

Kellan looked up, sharply. Then he looked at his boots.

"Again," said Pru. She had got up. She had the cord in her fist and her chin stuck out. "I don't care about any of that. I've a turn to get right."

Kiva watched her hands.

She had not been asked to. She was a corner, and a corner's business was her own two yards of hem. But the neck of the cloth came up not a pace from her right knee, and Pru's hands came down into it, small and red and quick, and there was nothing else on that floor that Kiva was any use for.

She watched as she had once watched a post in the dark. She did not look for what was wrong. She only looked at what there was.

*One.* The cord came off the elbow and across the neck, and Pru's left thumb came down on it, flat, and pinned it.

*Two.* The right hand took the long end over and under. The thumb stayed where it was.

*Three.*

It was very quick. The long end wanted to go back under the first turn, between the cord and the canvas. On a warm day it went through like a needle. Today the wax had made a rod of it, and it butted, and would not go.

And Pru lifted her thumb to let it by.

It was not for a count. It was for less than a blink. The thumb came up, the stiff end went under, and the thumb came down again; and in the time that it was up the first turn eased by the width of a thread.

That was all. One would not have seen it from a pace farther off.

"Sealed," said Pru. "Fifty-six." She let go, and the knot held, and she sat back on her heels and breathed.

"Do it again," said Kiva. "Not for a bag. Just the cord, on your knee. Slowly."

"Why?"

"I think that I saw something. I'd like to see whether it was there."

"You can't even do the third turn."

"No," said Kiva. "I can't do any of them. I can only see it."

It was not said to wound, and Pru had not meant hers to, quite. They looked at one another over a hundredweight of sand.

"Slowly," said Kellan. He had come round from his corner. "I'd like to see as well. I've never once looked at what a catcher does with her hands. I've only ever waited for the word."

Pru did it slowly, on her knee, with her tongue between her teeth.

"There," said Kiva. "Your thumb."

"I didn't move my thumb."

"You lifted it. On the third. When the end won't go under, you lift the thumb to let it through, and the first turn slackens. A hair. If the wax is soft, it doesn't matter; the turn's stuck to itself. If it's hard, there's nothing holding it." She stopped. "I think. It's what I saw. I can't feel a cord, so I don't know that it's why."

"I don't lift it."

"Do it once more."

Pru did it once more, and watched her own left hand as though it belonged to somebody whom she did not trust.

The thumb came up.

"*Oh,*" said Pru.

She did not say anything else for some time. She turned the cord over. She tried it with the thumb held down, and the stiff end jammed, and she swore again, quite quietly. She tried it with the cord flat. Then she did a thing with her little finger, hooking it across the first turn from the far side, and the thumb came up, and the end went under, and nothing slackened at all.

"It's that," she said. "It wants a second pin. I've been letting go of it for half a breath every time I ever tied one, and it only shows when it's cold." She looked up, and she was not grateful. She was something more useful than that. "How long have you been watching my hands?"

"Since the third bag."

"I'm not your cord."

"I know that you aren't."

"I mean that I don't like it. Being looked at. I'd forgotten that anybody could see." She went scarlet to the ears. "That came out wrong. I'm glad you did. I only don't like it."

"Pike's going to ask me to do it to him," said Kiva. "In the new year. It's on a card."

"Then he'll not like it either," said Pru, with some satisfaction, and threw her cloth.

They had nine bags of the next ten. Perrin called every sixty of them, and at the end he shut the turnip with a snap and said that he would like it noted that he had been a great deal better than a clock, since a clock could not have minded.

Jude wound the hoist down. He was looking at Kiva again.

"Well," he said. "That's a thing that a corner did."

"It's a thing."

"It isn't what I asked."

"No," said Kiva. "I know that it isn't."

She had thought about it while she rolled the hem. A second pin on a cord was Pru's. She had not found it; she could not have found it in a year, with a hand that did not know one thread from two. All that she had done was to say what was there. Anybody with eyes could have said it. It was only that nobody else had been kneeling in the right place with nothing better to do.

It was not an answer for a clerk. But she found that she did not mind having done it, and she had rather expected to.

"*Saturday the 22nd. The Low Lane. A rung to a bag under a porch; fifteen thrown, eleven sealed. Lit: Pryce, a porch, 0.16. Renn, two cuts on the post, 0.07. Talley, three, 0.09. Rowan, nothing; dark, on her healer's card. Cade, nothing; a clock.*" He did not smile. "*Spent: 0.32. Held: 25.21. Sealed, medical, W: 0.8.*"

"That adds," said Anwen.

"It always adds. That's the least that can be said for it."

He signed. He held the pen a moment afterward, as he had held a pencil on Thursday at the board, and then he passed it on.

Orla Dane read the book on Sunday evening, under the lamp at the first shed, with her stick hooked on the crate beside her.

She read it every Sunday. It was a condition, written in a Chair's level hand under a certificate, and she had thought it a nuisance in the spring. She had since come to wait for it rather as some people wait for the post.

The crate stood a pace north of where it had stood for one-and-twenty years. She had not got used to that. She kept finding the bottom wall a yard farther off than her shoulder expected.

It had been a quiet week in the book. There were no doors in it. There was a boat, and a seal that a stranger had pressed, and a drum on the Brickfields road.

She read the drum twice.

It was not like the girl's other pages. Orla had been reading that square pencil hand since the fourth month, and she knew what it did. It set down what had been felt, on the left, and what was thought about it, on the right, and it left *I can't weigh it* lying about like a tool that was always wanted. This did that for half a page. Then it changed.

*One at the needle. One at the water. Neither counts.*

That was an instruction. It was written to somebody who was not in the yard.

She turned over, and found Saturday. *P. Finch's third turn: the left thumb lifts when the cord is stiff, and the first turn eases. I saw it. I can't do it. She found the mend herself; a second pin, with the little finger. Mine: what was there. Hers: why, and what to do.*

And at the foot of Tuesday, small, on the right: *I don't yet know what I'm for, dark. Today I was for this.*

Orla shut the book on her finger.

There was a card in her coat. It had been there for nine days. It was a cheap one, such as a fitter used for his accounts, and it had four lines on it in a round hand, and she could have said them over with her eyes shut.

*Tell the one who writes everything down that I should like to read it.*

She had told nobody. She had not even told herself that she was not telling; she had put it in the inside pocket, on the Friday that it came, and on the Sunday she had sat on this crate and watched the child lay a hand on a proof sheet with two dead fingers, and had thought, *not tonight.* That had been a decent thought. She had been pleased with it.

It had gone on being *not tonight* for nine nights.

She made herself look at the reasons, since the dark was a good place for it and she had had some practice.

The first was that the girl was hurt. She had an arm that had gone to candle and come back, and a healer's card, and eleven weeks of nothing. One did not hand a thing like that card to somebody in a sling. That was true. It was also the reason that Orla would have given aloud, to a Chair or to an aunt, and she had learned in twenty years to distrust whichever reason she would have chosen to say aloud.

The second was plainer. She wanted him to have the book.

She wanted it more than she had wanted anything since she came off the Line. She wanted a man of forty on the south coast to sit down with those pages and see what a seventeen-year-old with his kind of trouble had done when it went wrong. She had written it down. She had written down *I stacked* and *I can't weigh it* and *the count is somebody else's,* in pencil, with the cost beside it, and had not torn out a leaf.

He had never been let write anything down. Orla had had it all worked out for him beforehand. She had built him a step at a time, with every step explained, and she had been proud of how little there was left for him to find.

So she wanted it, and it would feel like paying something. And that was the best reason in the world to keep her hands off it. A sponsor who used one candidate to settle her account with another had learned nothing whatever.

She had got that far by the Wednesday. She had been rather comfortable there.

It was the third reason that she had not looked at, and she looked at it now.

The card was not hers.

He had not written, *I should like to read it, if you think it wise.* He had not asked for her judgment of what a girl could bear. He had sent four lines, and the third of them was a message, and it was addressed to somebody else. *Tell the one.* It was the only thing that Jessamy Hart had asked of her in twenty years, and she had sat on it for nine days while she decided whether a person of seventeen ought to be let have her own post.

*I never asked him anything,* she had said to Rhea Sorn, in a watch room, not three weeks ago, *since I knew the answers.*

She had said it as a thing that she used to do.

Orla took the card out. She did not read it. She only held it, in the lamplight, and looked down the lane at a black post with a bar hanging on the wall behind it.

She had sat under that for one-and-twenty years without knowing what it was for. It had been a handle. It had been waiting the whole time for somebody to say aloud who had it.

"Well," she said, to the post. "It isn't me."

She would tell her tomorrow. She would do it here, since this was where she told people things, and she would put the card into the girl's own hand before she said a word about it. Then she would say what she wanted out of it, first and flatly, so that it could be allowed for. And then she would shut her mouth, which she had always found the hard part.

He might write to the child after that, and not to her. She turned that over. It was very likely. It was, when she came to weigh it, no more than his right.

She found that she could bear it. She had not expected that, either.

Her knee had stiffened. She got up by the crate, in two stages, and unhooked the lamp.

It took a fortnight of evenings, and for the first three Kiva did not write a word.

She read. She took the book from where it lay by the brass weight, and she began at the spring, and she tried to go through it as a man might who had never stood in the Old Yard in his life.

It was a strange, uncomfortable thing to do. She had always read that book as one reads a diary, hurrying, knowing how each day came out. Now she made herself stop at every line and ask what a pump-fitter on the south coast would be able to do with it.

Mostly he could do nothing.

*Stance clean. Ground took me. Orla says wider.*

*Crossed. Landed. Cold to the wrist after. 0.31.*

*Stacked again. Soft. I knew as I did it.*

It was all true, and it was all written by somebody who had been there to somebody who had been there. There was not a page in the first forty that told a stranger where to put his feet.

She ruled a sheet of the Works paper on the fourth evening. It was good stiff stuff, and the seven columns were printed faint blue, with nothing at their heads. She wrote the heads herself, as they stood in a manual three flights down under the Library, and on a sheet in a tin case at the Home Line hall. *What. How. Went wrong. Cost. Second try. Why.* The first column had no name. It was only for what the thing was called and where it lay.

Then she began to move the book across.

*A crossing, Ember to still. At Meridian, in the yard under the south wall, and since on sanctioned masts.*

*What: one door is shut before the next is opened. Between them there is a gap with nothing open at all.*

*How: test the ground first. Open the still door where you mean to land, by its corners, and shut it again; if there is a room underneath, do not land there. Then say aloud what you are doing, to a count, so that anybody near can hear which door and when. Bound low. Shut the first door at the top of the bound. Wait until the last of it has run home along the ribs. Then open the second, wide, before the foot comes down.*

She read that over. It was longer than anything in the green pages. It had cost her a summer, and it went into nine lines.

*Went wrong: opened the second door before the first was shut. Three times. That is a stack. It is soft, and it costs a third of the price, and it lands. It is also two doors open together, and open to anything that is near. I am forbidden it, and I was right to be.*

*Cost: three-tenths of a dram, about, of which twenty-six hundredths is the gap. And the left hand cold to the wrist for an hour after.*

*Second try: counted it aloud, and had it said back.*

She stopped at the last column.

*Why.* She knew why. She had known since a night on a hill. But she had never once written it, since nobody who read her book had needed to be told.

*Why: because a door that is open is open to whatever is by. A gap is dear, and it is shut.*

It looked very bare. She left it.

There were five entries by the end of the first week. There was the crossing. There was the ground test, by itself, with its own *went wrong:* a cross in the dirt that she had landed on all summer, with eight feet of drain under it. There was the list of things that her two fingers could not do, set out as a method, since that was what it had become: *say it before, by name, to whoever has the other end.* There was a drum on the Brickfields road, and two people saying *now.*

And there was the long thing.

She had not known whether to send it. He was twin-kindled, Ember and hand. His doors had fought on a live cell in front of four thousand people. It seemed a hard thing to post to such a man that somebody had since done it with three.

But he had asked what she had written down, and it was in the book. She had written it three times.

*Three doors in sequence. Hand, then Ember, then still. Not certified. Not classified. Not to be asked of anybody; the Chair has written so.*

*What: catch, carry, plant. Two gaps.*

*How:*

She sat with the pencil over that square for the best part of an hour.

She could write the order. She could write that the ward went under the thing on its slack, bent like the bowl of a spoon, for one tap. She could write that it was folded from the far edge to a penny before the shove arrived.

She could not write the fold. It was not hers. She had learned it off a first-year on a pail, and did it with two fingers stuck out like a fork; and when she tried to put down what the hands did, she found that she was describing Pru's.

She could not write the second gap either.

The first was only dear. One paid for it. The second came, each time, out of somewhere that had already been emptied, and something paid all the same, and it went out through her left arm. She had felt it pass the wrist. That was not a method. It was a thing that had happened to her three times and that she did not understand.

And at the end of it there was a line of Orla's. *Arrive before you're there.* It was the truest instruction that she had ever been given. It only worked when it was said aloud, by a woman on a crate, to somebody already in the air.

She looked at the sheet.

She had always thought that she wrote everything down. A pump-fitter thought so, two hundred miles off, on the strength of one line in a letter. And here were three places where she had written that a thing happened, for a spring and a summer and an autumn, and had never once said how.

It struck her, and not pleasantly, that those were the three places where the hand was.

She did not fill them. That would have been the tidying kind of lie. She ruled each square off with a line across its corner, as a clerk voids a cheque, and under the first she wrote what was so.

*I cannot say this so that another person could do it. I have done it. I have not got it in words yet.*

Then *Went wrong,* which was easy, and long.

Then *Cost.* She had the cost by heart. It was in a foreman's ledger, and on a healer's card, and it had been said aloud both times before she put a hand up. *The first two fingers of the left hand, for good, at the second. They bend and grip and report pressure and nothing else. At the third, the arm, without feeling to above the elbow, for four days; it came back to where it was. Her healer does not know that it would a fourth time. Nor do I.*

She wrote *her,* and saw that she had, and left that as well.

She came to the page of the sixth on a Thursday, with the lamp turned up and her aunt across the table reconciling the trunk.

*Pump house, about one. M.R.'s reading hand starved to the wrist. Eased by Aske with 1.2 of our sealed W. A.R. held the comb: plain. Lumb's book for 2604, one leaf, to be kept; see M.R.*

It was not that she had forgotten. One did not forget it. But she had not let herself look straight at it since the night in the pump house, with three pumps going and a calf book open under a desk lamp.

She had been thirteen the first time. It had come again in the spring, on Classification Day, with a fever after it. There had been no roof. That was the part that she could never get said properly, even to Mara. It was not that the roof was gone and one saw the dark. There was nothing over her at all, and the nothing was not empty. It was enormous, and it was paying attention.

A woman had written that down, lifetimes ago, in a house on a hill. A pump keeper had written it in 2604, in brown ink, with his needles standing up. *Not dark. Nothing.*

And on the seventeenth of the ninth month, at twenty past two, a harbour bank had fallen into step two hundred miles off, and a man had known what it was with a hand that reported very little.

*Somebody at Saltmere had a temperature as well.* Lumb had said that, rubbing his nose.

She could ask him.

It would go into four lines. *Did you have a fever that night, or any night? She found that she wanted the answer so much that her hand had gone to the pencil of its own accord.

She made herself take it away again and think like somebody who had been brought up over an instrument shop.

What would she do with the card when it came?

It would come in a round hand, by the coast boat, to Coper Street. It would say yes or it would say no. And either way it would be an answer to a question, and the question would have had the roof in it.

There was a page under a seal in the Library's press. Mara had written it and had the hour put on it, and she had done that for one reason: so that nobody could ever say that a girl of seventeen had read an old Master's fever and borrowed it. It was worth something only for as long as it had been got clean.

A needle told one nothing if it had been told beforehand what to read. She had heard that across a shop counter since she was five.

If she wrote to a stranger, *did you dream that there was no roof,* and he wrote back, *I did,* then that was not a second reading. That was her own, come home by boat.

So it was not to be asked. It was not even to be hinted. If Jessamy Hart had a thing to say about a fever, he would have to say it without her having put the words in his mouth; and if he never said it, then she would never know, and that was the price.

It was a good deal to give up for the sake of a seal that might never be broken. She sat and felt how much.

Mrs. Pryce could not send her home, since Kiva had been in and out of that laundry from the age of nine, and had been fed there.

It was Saturday the sixth. The streets off the twenty-ninth drum had come off their boxes that morning, a month to the day, and all Coper Street was washing. The ironing room was full of steam and the smell of starch. There was a deal table in the middle of it, eight feet long, scrubbed white, and Mrs. Pryce stood at the end of that table with her sleeves rolled and folded double sheets by herself.

It took two to fold a double. Everybody knew that. Kiva and Anwen had done it in the drying yard since they were small, a girl at each end, walking in.

Mrs. Pryce did one in the time that it took to say so.

"Watch if you like," she said. "You'll not learn anything. It's only folding."

Kiva sat on the mangle bench with a laundry ticket on her knee, face down, and a pencil.

She had been told not to look for mistakes. There were none to look for; the woman had been at that table for thirty years. So she did what she had done with a thumb. She looked at one thing at a time, as though she were going to be asked for an inventory.

The sheet came off the pile damp, in a rope. Mrs. Pryce shook it down the length of the table, away from her. Then she did not walk round to the far end.

She leaned. She put her hip against the table's edge, hard, and the near hem was caught between the two. That was her second pair of hands.

*Hip pins the near hem. Both hands free.*

She reached for the far hem and did not pick it up in the middle. She took it by its two corners, with her arms wide, and brought the corners to her, and the whole far edge came over the top of the sheet in one piece and was laid on the near edge, corner to corner. She never let go of it. There was not a moment, from the far side of the table to her own chest, when the last of that hem was out of her hands.

*Folds from the far edge. Always has the last of it.*

Kiva looked at what she had written.

She knew that fold. She had learned it off a first-year, on a pail, and it was not done with linen. Pru put up a ward the size of a tea tray and brought the far edge of it home to her palms in just that way; and for the same reason, very likely, though neither she nor a laundress had ever said what the reason was.

She wrote, small, in the corner of the ticket: *Same as P.F.'s ward.* Then she went on looking.

The third fold was the odd one. Mrs. Pryce brought the doubled sheet up off the table and snapped it, once, like a carter's whip, so that it cracked. Then she laid it down with the thick side away from her.

*Snaps it at the third. Why? Thick edge to the far side. Why?*

There was a girl at the copper, wringing. Her name was Dilys. She was thirteen, and new since the tenth month, and she had been tried at the table on Monday and had put a double on the floor.

"Dilys," said Kiva. "Would you do one? I'll say it to you. I can't do it; I've a hand that won't. I only want to know whether I've seen it right."

Dilys looked at Mrs. Pryce's back, which was bent over the starch tub, and came.

"Shake it down the table. Now don't go round. Put your hip against the edge, on the hem. Harder. That's holding it for you."

"It's wet."

"I know. Now the far corners. One in each hand, arms out. Bring them to you over the top, and don't let go of either until they're on the near ones."

The far edge came over. It came crooked, and then it came straight.

"Up. Snap it. Once."

It cracked like a flag. Dilys laughed out loud.

"Thick side away from you. Lay it."

It lay on the scrubbed deal, square, a little damp, a double sheet folded by one person of thirteen who had put its fellow on the floor five days before.

"What's that?" said Mrs. Pryce.

She had turned round. She had flour on one forearm and starch on the other, and she looked at the sheet as she might have looked at a strange dog in her scullery.

"I did it," said Dilys. "She told me how you do it."

"I don't *do* it any way. I fold it."

"You put your hip on the hem," said Kiva. "So that you needn't walk round."

"I never do."

"You do, Mam," said Anwen.

"I want it said. You've done it since before I could see over the table. I thought that every mother had a wet patch on her apron just there." She put the basket down. "I've folded with you a thousand times and I never knew that it was for anything."

Mrs. Pryce folded another. She did it slowly this time, and watched her own hip arrive at the edge of the table, and went red, exactly as her daughter did.

"Well, I'll go to sea," she said. "It's for the weight. A wet double's as heavy as a child of three, and I'd one of those on my arm for the best part of ten years. If I'd gone round the table I'd have had to put her down." She looked at Kiva with a certain respect and no warmth at all. "And you sat there and wrote that on a ticket."

"I didn't write why. I didn't know why. I wrote that you did it."

"And the snap?"

"I've got *why* against that as well. And the thick side."

"The snap's for the air. A damp sheet folds a bubble in, and it sours in the press. The thick side goes away since that's the side the next one's laid against." She said it crossly, and all in one breath, like a woman turning out a drawer. "Nobody ever asked me. I couldn't have told you that I knew."

Kiva wrote it in. It went on the back of the ticket, in seven small columns that she had ruled without thinking, and she noticed when she had done that there were two hands in it. *What* and *how* were hers. She had seen those from a mangle bench. *Why* had had to come from the person at the table, and it had come out sideways, and cross.

She had not thought of it so before. She had supposed that a second saw the whole of a thing. A second saw the half that the doer had stopped being able to see; and the other half was still the doer's, and had to be asked for.

"Do another," said Mrs. Pryce to Dilys. "Since you're so clever."

Dilys shook one down. She was in a hurry now, and she had left her peg bag at the far end of the table, on the sheet.

She pinned the hem with her hip. She took the far corners and brought them over. And the peg bag came with them. It rode home on the fold, quite quietly, and was laid in the middle of the sheet with the far edge on top of it, and vanished.

"There," said Mrs. Pryce. "That's what it's *not* for. You never fold with a thing lying on the far side. It all comes to you. I've found a flat-iron in a bolster case." She fished the bag out. "And you're standing on it."

Dilys looked down. The sheet had slipped off the table's end, and she had a boot on the trailing hem. She hauled at the corners and nothing came.

"A sheet with a foot on its far hem doesn't fold for anybody. Get off."

Kiva wrote both of those down too, under *Went wrong,* since they were there.

She did not think about them again for eleven weeks.

*K. Rowan, catcher.*

"It doesn't mention me."

"No."

"Good. I looked for it. I'd have thought the less of both of us." She gave it back. "Three empty. What's in them?"

"The hand, mostly."

"Mm." Her sponsor looked down the stair at the boat. "I never got that into words either. I thought that it was because I taught badly. It may be that I did; but I begin to think that there's a part of it that doesn't go."

"It has to go. If it doesn't, then nobody can do it but me."

"And is that so dreadful? There's many a girl would like it."

"I've done it three times," said Kiva. "It cost me two fingers and an arm for four days. If it only lives in my hand, then every time it's wanted somebody has to turn round and not look at me. I'd sooner that it were a thing with handles."

Orla did not answer. She looked, for a moment, like somebody who had been handed a card of her own.

"Papers," he said. "Same method?"

"A different one. It's mine."

"Who to?"

"J. Hart. Pump-fitter. At the harbour pump house, by favour of the keeper."

He wrote it, with his tongue in his cheek. He pressed the seal and held it down.

"*Her hand,*" he said. "Sign."

She signed with her right, and the boat's bell rang, and six sheets with three holes in them went up the plank under a stranger's arm.

# Chapter 4 — Kiva: A Needle with No Scale

They took the strapping off Perrin Cade on Saturday the twentieth, and by two that afternoon he was carrying Kiva across the School's bottom yard on a litter.

It was the only use that anybody had been able to find for her.

November had gone by in small pieces. The last of the seven drums had come out on the twenty-fifth of the month before, on a count of one-and-thirty, and there was no needle on the Brickfields trunk now that nodded. Mara had been let read a bank on the first. She had gone down to the pump house on the second with her left hand in her apron pocket, and laid it on four-and-twenty drums in turn, and come home and eaten two suppers. Kellan was at the refinery on Tuesdays and Thursdays and said very little about it. Anwen could walk a wall thirty paces. She had also been to the ash office, with the book under her arm, to ask the price of plain ward-trace, and had been told by the clerk that he could not quote a price for a thing that he had not seen since the thaw.

And Kiva had a ticket with a laundress on it, and five weeks and six days to go.

"You're the casualty," said Jude. "It's the Carry board. A rescue against a clock, and the board doesn't say what's in the way, so we'll have a wall in it. Rowan's the right weight if we put a sandbag on her shins."

"I'm ballast."

"You're ballast that can tell us what we did. Gerald never has."

So she lay on her back on a canvas litter, in her coat, with twenty kilos of sand across her ankles, and looked at a grey sky with a rook in it.

The road was the one that Hollis Vane had walked in the dark. It ran from the sheds along the Old Yard, past a black post with a bar hung up behind it, to the low wall. For today the gap in that wall was shut with a hurdle. The litter had to go over the top, chest high, and on across the cobbles to the black gate.

"You're being rescued," said Perrin, somewhere above her head. "It's on a count. Don't help."

He had the front poles. Jude had the foot. They lifted, and she rose a yard into the air as smoothly as a tray.

It was a curious way to see four people whom she knew. She had Perrin's chin, upside down, and the underside of his jaw working as he counted. She had Jude's forearms at the far end of her, corded, steady as a pair of shafts. She could hear Anwen and Kellan on the other side of the wall, where they stood to receive.

"*Post, left,*" said Perrin. "*Keep right.*"

"Post left, keep right," said Jude.

They said it for her now. She had made them, once, on a Saturday night, and it had gone round a School.

The wall came. She saw the top course of it slide in over her forehead.

"*Up!*" The litter rose, and tipped, and the front poles went onto the coping with a knock that she felt in her teeth. "Renn. Pryce. *Yours!*"

"*Mine!*" said two voices.

And between the one word and the other, she fell.

It was not far. It was the width of a hand. The head of the litter dropped under her, and her stomach stayed where it had been, and for something less than half a count there was a girl in the air over a brick wall with nobody holding the front of her. Then four hands bit on the poles, and she stopped.

"Down," said Kellan. "Got her. Walk."

They walked. She went feet first off the wall and across the cobbles at a trot, and was set down under the School's device with a bump.

"A minute and nine," said Pru. She had the turnip watch. "From the sheds."

"I'd want it under a minute," said Jude. "What did we do, ballast?"

Kiva sat up. Her heart was going rather quickly, and she waited until it was not.

"You dropped me."

"We never," said Perrin.

"At the wall. Between *yours* and *mine.* There's a place in the middle where neither of you has me."

"There isn't. I called it. Renn called it back. It's a lane call; we've done it since the fifth month, and it's the one thing that this four has always had right."

"I was in it," said Kiva. "I'd know. There's nobody else on this yard who could."

That silenced him. He stood with his big hands hanging, flushed from the lift, breathing with care.

"Do it again," said Anwen. "Slowly. I'd like to see my own hands."

They did it again, at a walk, and this time Kiva did not look at the sky. She turned her head on the canvas and watched the coping come.

The front poles went onto the wall. Perrin said *yours.* And he let go.

That was the whole of it. He let go on his own word, as a man does who has said a thing and means it. Kellan and Anwen had their hands out, an inch under the poles, and they closed them on *mine;* and *mine* came after. It could not come before. They were answering.

"There," said Kiva. "You give, and then they take. It's only the length of a word. But I'm what's given, and for that long I'm not anybody's."

"It's half a count," said Kellan. He had his head on one side. "Less."

"It felt longer from where I was."

"What would you have, then?" said Perrin. "I can't hold on after I've said it's theirs."

"Why not?"

He opened his mouth, and shut it.

"Say it the other way about," said Kiva slowly. She was working it out with her back on the cobbles. "They say *mine* first. They take it. Then you say *yours,* and let go of a thing that's already held. I'd sooner be caught and then let go than let go and then caught."

"That's four on the poles at once," said Jude. "On top of a wall."

"For half a count."

"It's a half count of four people's hands in two people's room." He scratched his jaw. "I don't say no. I say it'll cost."

It cost. They tried it, with *mine* called first, and it was like being handed across a counter. She did not fall. The litter went over the coping as though it had grown there. But Kellan's knuckles and Perrin's met on the same pole, and for a moment the whole weight of her and the sand hung on the wall with four people sorting out their fingers.

Perrin made a noise through his nose.

"Ribs?" said Anwen.

"They're only telling me. I'd like it known that they tell me a great deal more with somebody else's hand on my pole." He set his end down, very gently, and stood and pressed his side. "That's the first lift since the sixth of last month. It was a girl and a bag of sand, and I'm blowing like a tram."

"A minute and fourteen," said Pru.

"So it's slower," said Jude.

"By five. And nobody dropped her." Kellan had taken out his pencil. "I can't put a number on not being dropped. A clock can't either. That's a nuisance, on a board that's marked by the clock."

They looked at one another. It was not settled, and nobody pretended that it was.

Kiva lay down on the canvas again, since she was wanted.

She had found a thing out, and she turned it over while they lifted her. A bearer could not feel the pass. Perrin had been certain, and honest, and wrong, since from the poles a clean handover felt like a clean handover. The only person on that yard who could feel the place where the load belonged to nobody was the load.

It was not much of a trade. One could hardly put *lies down, complains* on an entry.

"That's two," said Jude, over her feet.

"Two what?"

"Two things you've done from where there was nothing to do. A thumb and a drop." He had the foot poles up. "I'm counting. It still isn't what I asked."

"I know," said Kiva. "I'm counting as well."

The packet came up from the coast boat on Friday the third, at noon, by a Works boy on a bicycle, and Mara Rowan signed for it across her own shop counter with a hand that could feel the pen.

She noticed that. She noticed it every time. It was eight weeks since the pump house, and a month since Aske had let her near a bank, and she still could not pick up a pencil without a small private surprise that it had a shape.

The wax was green. That was the Saltmere Company's. She cut the tape with the counter knife and laid what was inside in a row, squared, as she would have laid out a customer's meter in pieces.

There were four things. There was a letter, on a half sheet, in a brisk upright hand. There was a report, of two leaves. There was a list. And there was a card, folded once, with a name on the outside that was not hers.

She read the letter first.

*Mrs. Rowan. We tried it on the 20th and 21st of last month, as written, all twelve sheets. It did not answer.*

*I enclose the keeper's account. She has set it out in your own seven columns; she says that she would not insult a method by reporting on it in any other shape.*

*The short of it is that our water does not lie still. Yours is a drain. Ours is a harbour, and the sea comes in under the sea gate twice a day whether a pump turns or not. We stopped the pumps eleven minutes, which is as long as the low town will bear on a making tide. Six cells were filled of thirty-six, each on a count of its own and none of them a pump's, with a reader between. All six went back in.*

*Our fitter wrote down the minute at which each one went. They are not alike. He says that you will want them.*

*We used six jars of plain off the Company's reference shelf. The keeper of that shelf will not give me six more, and I cannot tell her that she is wrong.*

*I asked in the autumn whether your Works would lend its reader. I ask again, in writing, for the spring. Yeo, officer.*

Mara read it standing. Then she pulled the high stool to her with one foot, and sat.

She had known for seven weeks that it would not answer. She had known since a boy lay down in the road at drum nineteen and said *now.* A method that had wanted a twelfth sheet inside a day was a method that had been written about one room; and a harbour was not a room.

She had not expected to mind how they told her.

The keeper's report was in seven columns. Somebody had ruled them by hand, with a pen, on the pump house's own fault paper. *What. How. Went wrong. Cost. Second try. Why.* They were filled in a small crabbed script, with nothing left out that she could see, and the fourth was the widest.

*Went wrong: the water. It was not still with every pump stopped, and it is not ever still. The fresh cans leaned to it from about the fortieth count. The readers felt them go and could not hold them.*

*Cost: six jars. Eleven minutes of the low town's patience, twice. Two readers with their hands white to the knuckle; they were eased, and are well.*

*Why: I do not know. I have put that I do not know, as your twelfth sheet does.*

They had read it properly.

That was what undid her. It was not the failure. She had been failing in front of people since she was licensed, and had learned to do it with her chin up. It was that a keeper whom she had never seen, in a pump house two hundred miles off, had taken twelve sheets in a stranger's hand and done every line of them, in order, with the *I don't know* left in; and had then sat down and answered in the same shape, so that the two accounts could be laid side by side.

Nobody had ever done that with a thing of hers. Rusk had signed them. The Chair had sealed them. This woman had *used* them.

She found that she had her left hand flat on the counter. She was reading the grain of the wood with it, as she had done every day for two months when she thought that nobody was by.

"Well," she said aloud, to an empty shop. "That's what it's for."

She took up the list.

It was on a fitter's card, of the cheap sort. The hand was round, and careful, and rather childish. There were six cells on it, under two dates.

*The 20th. Filled at two minutes past nine in the morning.*

*Cell 4. In at 9.03.*

*Cell 11. In at 9.04.*

*Cell 12. In at 9.03.*

*The 21st. Filled at twenty to four in the afternoon.*

*Cell 20. In at 3.52.*

*Cell 26. In at 4.21.*

*Cell 27. In at 3.59.*

Mara read down it once, quickly. Then she read it again, with her finger.

A minute. Two minutes. A minute. Those were the three of the first day. They had gone as a can goes on a stool with its feet in a trough, inside the slow sixty or just beyond.

Twelve minutes. Forty-one minutes. Nineteen.

She sat quite still.

It was the same bank. They were the same pumps, stopped for the same eleven minutes, with the same salt and the same readers. Nothing was different between the two days but the hour of the clock. And on the second day a fresh cell had stood on its own count for forty-one minutes, by a fitter's watch, in water that was *not ever still.*

"It isn't the cells," said Mara. "It's the hour."

She did not know what that meant. She was a river woman. She knew that the sea came and went, as she knew that the moon did, and with about as much use for either. But she knew a reading when one was laid in front of her. Something in that harbour was louder at nine in the morning than at four in the afternoon, and a man with a watch had caught it at both.

*He says that you will want them.*

"He's quite right," said Mara. "I want a great many more."

She drew a sheet of Works paper to her and wrote, without sitting any straighter. It was a short letter. She asked for the harbour's tide table, for the whole of the year. She asked for the pump house's fault column for the two days, and for the week on either side. She asked whether the fitter who kept a watch would do so again, for every cell that was ever filled, and whether he would write down what the sea was doing at the time, in his own words, if nobody had told him the proper ones.

She did not answer the last paragraph of the officer's letter.

She read it again instead. *I ask again, in writing, for the spring.*

It was thirty-six cells. She had done four-and-twenty, with a reader's hand and a room full of operators, and at the eighteenth she had ceased to feel the iron. She had one hand that told her anything. The right had been silent since a cell on Linden Row. If the left went as the right had gone, she would be a woman with a Grade Two license who could not tell a live drum from a doorpost, and there would be a shop on Coper Street with nobody in it who could read.

She was not brave about it. She had never said that she was.

And the water at Saltmere did not lie still, and she had no method for water that did not. She had twelve sheets and a list of six minutes. To go south on that would be to stand in a strange pump house, on a harbour, and be wrong in front of everybody at a much greater expense.

*Not yet,* she thought. It was a reason, and she looked at it with some suspicion, since it was so convenient. But it was also true. One did not send for a reader before there was a thing to read.

She folded the officer's letter and put it in the drawer with the license.

That left the card.

It was folded once, and it had *For K. Rowan, by favour* on the outside. She did not open it. She had been tempted to a good many things in all her years of being that child's guardian, and reading her post had never been among them.

But she laid it beside the list, on the counter, and looked at the two together.

It was the same hand. The round *a* was the same, and the way that the figure four was left open at the top.

"So you *had* had your thumbs on my tape," said Mara.

She put the card under the brass weight, where it would be seen.

Kiva read it at six, standing in the shop in her coat, with the tram's cold still on her.

*I read it. Some of it twice.*

*You ruled off three places. I know them. I ruled them off for twenty years and called it having nothing to say.*

*What do the two fingers tell you through iron? Try it properly, and write down how it comes out, whichever way.*

*J.H.*

"Well?" said Mara.

"He's read it."

"I gathered that he could read. He writes a list like a man who's been made to stand by what he puts down."

"He doesn't ask what I left out." Kiva turned the card over. There was nothing on the back, and she had known that there would not be. "I said that there was a line missing, on purpose. He's let it be. He doesn't so much as say that he saw."

"Then he knows what a shut door is. I told you that I'd think the more of him."

"There's nothing for Orla."

"No," said her aunt. "I looked at the outside. It's for you by name."

Kiva read the third line again. *Try it properly.*

He had not said *what do they tell you.* She could have answered that from the tram. They told her that something was pushing. He had said *through iron,* and *properly,* and *whichever way;* and those were the words of a man who had been asked a careless question once and had not cared for the answer that he gave.

"He was the fitter," she said. "At their pump house. With the watch."

"He was. Look."

Mara laid the list in front of her, and Kiva read six minutes in a round hand.

"One held forty-one."

"On the second day, at an hour when the first day's would have been in before I'd capped my pencil. I've written for their tide table. I don't know what I shall do with it; I've never read one in my life." Her aunt was looking at the list as she looked at a drum that had begun to tremble. "They did every sheet. They did the twelfth. They wrote me back in seven columns with *I do not know* in the last. And it failed, and I'm more pleased than I've been since the bank came out, and I can't altogether account for it."

"It went," said Kiva. "The method. It got there, and somebody could do it."

"It got there and it didn't work."

"It didn't work in their water. That's a thing that they know now, and they'd not have known it if the sheets had only said *I stood between the cells.*" She heard herself, and stopped. It had come out with a good deal of heat. "I wrote three squares that only say that. He's told me so."

"He's told you that he did the same for twenty years."

"That isn't comfort."

"I don't believe that it was meant for comfort. I think that it was meant for a start." Mara took the list back. "Is a tide a count?"

"What?"

"You're the one who kneels on pavements. It comes in and it goes out. Twice a day. If that's a thing keeping time, it's the slowest that I ever heard of."

"I don't know. I can't weigh it."

"Nor can I. I shall put it in the margin with a query." She looked at her niece's left hand, which was holding the card by its edge, with the first two fingers stuck out. "And what do they tell you through iron?"

"I've never tried it properly."

"Then I've a surface plate, and a vice, and a free Saturday," said Mara. "I'd like to see an honest test of anything, for once. I've been a month reconciling other people's figures."

She set it up on the shop bench the next afternoon, with the blind down, and she went about it as she would have gone about proving a meter for the Works.

The surface plate was a slab of grey iron a foot square and two fingers thick. She stood it on edge in the big vice and screwed it up tight. On Kiva's side of it there was a stool. On the other side there was Mara. Between the two, across the top of the plate, hung a work apron on a string, so that nobody on the stool could see so much as an elbow.

"Three sorts," said her aunt. "Nothing at all. A steady lean, with my thumb, as one leans on a door. And a counted one: on, and off, to a count that I shall take from the shop clock and not tell you. I've a tin with twenty tags in it, of three colors. I draw one. I do what it says. You tell me which, and then, if it's counted, what count."

"The two fingers only."

"The two. If you put your palm on that plate, it's a different instrument and a better one, and I've no interest in it today."

Anwen had come to keep the tally. She had brought the laundry's order book for the purpose, turned to a clean page, and had ruled it before she sat down.

Pru had come to hold a wrist.

That had been Kiva's notion, in the night. She had lain awake with the card, thinking about a rail on the Brickfields road. Her fingers had spoken there, and they had been right; but she had been leaning on that rail with half her weight, and her heart had been going. She had a pulse in those two fingers as she had in the other eight. She could not feel a pin with them. She had never thought to ask whether she could feel her own blood.

"I want my pulse counted," she had said. "By somebody else. The whole way through. I'd not know it from a push."

"That's the count of a thing that keeps time," Pru had said. "It's on your list."

"Yes. It's mine, and I still can't have it."

So Pru sat on a box at her right side, with two fingers on Kiva's good wrist and her eyes on the shop clock, and moved her lips.

"Begin," said Mara, behind the apron.

Kiva laid the first two fingers of her left hand against the iron.

It was cold. She knew that it was cold; her thumb, a half inch off, told her so. The two fingers told her that there was a plate.

She waited.

*Something's pushing.*

It was not much. It came, and it stayed. It did not go away again.

"Steady," she said.

"Steady it was," said Anwen. She made a mark.

"Again."

There was nothing. There was a plate. Kiva waited through a slow twenty, and it went on being a plate.

"Nothing."

"Nothing it was."

The third came and went. It came, and there was a gap, and it came again. She could not have said where the gap began. That was the queer part. With any other finger she would have felt the pressure rise, as one feels a step coming on a stair. These two knew only that it was there, and then, some time after it had stopped, that it was not.

"Counted."

"Counted. What count?"

"I don't know. Six?"

"It was a four," said Mara's voice. "Go on. Don't mend your guesses. I want them as they come."

They did twenty. It took the best part of an hour, and by the tenth Kiva's shoulder ached from holding two fingers to a slab of iron with exactly the weight of a hand.

By the twelfth she had begun to be afraid of being wrong, and she pressed harder.

*Something's pushing. And not. And pushing.*

"Counted."

"That was a blank," said Anwen. "There wasn't anybody touching it."

"Ninety-four," said Pru.

They all looked at her. She went pink, and kept her fingers where they were.

"Her pulse. It was seventy-two when we began. It's been going up since the ninth, and it went to ninety-four just then. I thought I ought to say."

Kiva took her hand off the plate.

The two fingers lay in her lap. If she pressed them against her own knee, hard, she could feel it. It was very small. *Pushing. And not.* It was her heart, come all the way down her arm to tell her that something in a grey iron slab was keeping time.

"That's it," she said. "That's what they do when I lean. They read me."

"Then don't lean," said Mara. "Go on. Eight more. I'm not stopping a proof at twelve since the instrument's been found to have a fault. That's when one learns something."

They did the eight. She kept the touch as light as a moth, and twice she asked Pru for the number before she answered.

Anwen added it up at the end, in ink, and read it out as she would have read a team's book.

"*Twenty tries. Counted, seven. She called six of them counted. Steady, seven. She called four; two she called counted, one she called nothing. Blank, six. She called three; three she called counted.*" She looked up. "*Of five wrong calls of counted, four were at a pulse over ninety, by Finch. What count: asked seven times. Right, none. She said six for a four, and four for a nine, and five times she said that she didn't know.*"

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"I'd like it said plainly," said Anwen, "since somebody will want it to be more than it is. That isn't a hand coming back."

"No," said Kiva. "Aske had the pin on them on Monday. It's as before."

"It isn't a sense, either. Not one that I'd stand a wall on."

"No."

"Then what is it?" said Pru.

Mara had come round the vice. She unhooked the apron and put it on, since it was hers, and looked at the plate, and the stool, and the page.

"It's a needle with no scale on it," she said. "It tells you that a thing is keeping time, six times in seven, through two fingers' thickness of iron. It can't tell you what time. And if the person holding it is frightened, it reads the person." She tied the strings behind her. "I've sold worse. I've sold a good deal worse, to people who were glad of it. I never yet sold one as better than it was."

"Is it any use?"

"It's a reason to look. That's all that you ever claimed for a flagstone, and you were right about the flagstone." Her aunt's voice was quite dry. "I'd not act on it. I'd not have anybody else act on it. If those two fingers tell you that something's keeping time, then that's the moment to ask somebody with a whole hand. And have your wrist held while you do it."

Kiva looked at the tally.

She had hoped for more. She found that out by the size of the disappointment. A man had written *I knew it with the hand, the bad one,* and for six weeks some part of her had been turning that over like a coin, wondering what the other side of a ruined hand might be. This was the other side. It was six in seven, through iron, with a fault.

But it was hers, and it had been tried properly, and it was written down by somebody who would not round it up.

"There's one thing more in it," she said. "It can't count. A whole hand feels the push begin. These don't. I always thought that was only a lack."

"It is a lack."

"It's a lack that doesn't get in the way. On a flagstone I've the cold and the grit and the tram, all at once, in the good hand. I've to listen through them. These two have nothing to listen through." She turned them over. "It's like a room with the window shut. Mrs. Renn said that about plain ash. It isn't that it hears more. It's that there's nothing else in there."

"That's what you saw," said Mara. "I'll not have it in the *why* column. It goes on the right, with your initials."

"I know where it goes."

She wrote the card that evening. It took four tries to make it fit.

*Through iron, with a light touch, they can say that a thing keeps time. Six in seven, by a proper test.*

*They cannot say what time. I guessed twice and was wrong both.*

*If I lean, or I am frightened, they say it about my own pulse. Somebody else must hold my wrist.*

*What does yours tell you? K.R.*

It was a question. She weighed that, with the pencil in her hand. But it had no roof in it. It was about a hand, and he had asked first.

She told Orla on Sunday, in the yard, before the book was opened.

"He's written."

Her sponsor did not move. She had the lamp on its hook and the stick between her boots.

"To you."

"To me. Four lines."

"Is there—" Orla stopped. She had her mouth a little open, and she shut it. "No. There wouldn't be."

"No."

"Well." It was said very flatly. The crooked face looked down the lane, at a post, for as long as it took a junior at the sheds to drop a quench box and be sworn at. "I said that I could bear it. I find that I can. It's only that I'd hoped to be surprised."

"He asked about my fingers. He set me a thing to try, and to write down however it came out. I did it yesterday, on my aunt's bench. It's in the book."

Orla read it. She read the tally in Anwen's ink, pasted in, and the lines on the right. She was a long time over the card.

"*Whichever way,*" she said at last. "He's set you an exercise."

"Yes."

"With the *how* left to you, and no answer at the back." Something was happening to her mouth that was not quite pain. "That's teaching. I never knew that he could. I never left him anything to do it with."

She shut the book.

"Three weeks and five days," said Orla. "And then that healer of yours lets you light a door, if he's a mind to. Have you thought which?"

"All three. One at a time. He'll want to see each."

"And then?"

"And then I'm read," said Kiva. "On the Circuit's rigs. The Chair's sent for me on the fourth."

She had not been measured since the spring. She had a ledger line that said *three point one,* and a board that could not tell what she was, and a gallery that had laughed.

She found that she was not afraid of the number. She was only very curious to see it.

# Chapter 5 — Kiva: The Frame Again

Healer Aske saw the hand on the first morning of the year, at seven, by lamplight, with the frost still white on the Wren House yard.

He had the towel. He had the pin, and the penny, and the cup, and the scrap of waxed cord. He went through them in the same order as he had on a Monday in the tenth month, and Kiva gave him the same answers, and neither of them remarked on it.

"As before," he said. "Eight parts that report and two that tell you something's pushing. I've looked for the hair every week since the tenth of the tenth, and I've not found one in either direction." He capped his pen. "Eleven weeks and four days. I said that I'd counted them so that you needn't. I'm told that you counted them regardless."

"I checked."

"So did Cade. He was at my colleague's door at six, in a jersey." He took down his coat. "You'll come into the yard. I'll not have a door opened in a room with a bed in it. Your sponsor's below; I heard the stick."

Orla was on the flags, in the cold, with her collar up. She had brought the crate. Kiva did not ask how.

"One at a time," said Aske. "Declared, each, to me. I'll want the hand between. You've no ration until Monday, so it comes off my own card; seven hundredths, entered as medical, and I'll not be thanked for it. Which first?"

"The still."

"Why?"

"It's the one that I'd fall over without."

"That's a reason. Go on."

She stood on the frosted flags with her feet a little apart.

She had thought about this for eighty-one days, and she found that she had thought about it wrongly. She had imagined a door that had been shut so long that it stuck. It was not like that. It was more like a leg that has been in a splint. One knew perfectly how it worked, and one did not at all believe that it would bear weight.

"Still door. Rowan. A stance, for a count of five."

She opened it.

She set it by its corners, wide, as she had learned to over a cistern. It went down through the frost, and the flag, and the packed rubble under the flag, and it found the ground.

The ground took her.

That was all. There was no light with a still door, and no sound. But the whole of the Wren House yard came up under her two heels and held, like a wharf under a mooring, and for five counts she was the heaviest thing in it. There was no room underneath. She had looked for one. It had become a habit.

She shut it.

"Hand," said Aske.

She gave it to him. He turned it to the lamp, and pressed the palm and the wrist.

"Warm. Good. Next."

"The hand door. A ward, for one tap."

It came up low, at her right hip, where she had kept it since the autumn. It was silver, and no bigger than a tea tray. The first two fingers of her left hand stood clear of it, stiff, like the prongs of a fork, and the other eight held it as one holds a thing that is not heavy and might be dropped.

She had forgotten how cool it was. The hand door had always been the near one. It was close, and it liked whatever was by, and it lay in her palms now with a small bright eagerness, like a dog let out.

She folded it from the far edge. Far edge, near edge, a penny. Shut.

The Ember door had woken.

She felt it come, hot, under her breastbone. It had heard the cupboard open. And lower down the still door stirred as well, heavy and slow, since it had been used not a minute ago and saw no reason to have been put away.

They did not open. She had her back against both.

That was the summer's work, and the autumn's, and she had not known until that moment whether eleven weeks would have undone it. Three doors in one person wanted to be three open doors. She had learned, at a quarter of a dram a time, to have one and to mean it.

"Hand," said Aske.

It was cool to the knuckle, where the ward had lain. He held it a long minute.

"Sulking. A little. Less than I'd have wagered." He let go. "The last."

"The Ember door. A step. One bound."

The struts ran down the backs of her calves and along the soles of her feet. They were a dull orange, the color that her Ember's light had always been. She went six paces across the frosted yard in one long low bound, and came down light, with the three heat-lines on her chest drawn tight as wires and her bad knee saying something sharp.

She shut it at the landing. She stood on her own feet, on the flags, with every door closed.

Her breath smoked. She had expected to be glad. She had not expected to want, quite badly, to laugh.

"Well?" said Orla, from the crate.

"They're all there."

"I could see that they're there. I've eyes. I meant, what's it like?"

"It's like being let put my hands in my own pockets."

Her sponsor made a noise that might have been approval. Aske had the hand again. He did not hurry.

"As before," he said. "I've said that to you oftener than I've said good morning." He took out a card and wrote on it against the yard wall, and read it over aloud, since there was nobody in Meridian who seemed able to do otherwise. "*K. Rowan. Single doors, declared, one at a time, from this morning. A crossing: one in seven days at the most, and the hand seen by me the morning after. The first not before Thursday; I want three clear mornings. The longer thing: not on my card.*"

"One in seven at the most," said Kiva.

"At the most. It's a ceiling. I've known candidates take a ceiling for a floor." He gave her the card. "That hand came back to where it was after four days in a sling. I don't know why. I've a case, and it's one. I'd be obliged if you didn't hurry to give me a second."

"I'm read on Monday. On the Circuit's rigs."

"So I've been told. They'll want a door at a time, and that's on the card." He looked at her over his spectacles. "If the Chair's frame asks you for more than one, you'll say that your healer was disagreeable about it. I've found that it saves argument."

The Measure Hall was nearly empty on Monday, and that was the first thing that was not like the spring.

There had been four hundred in the gallery on Classification Day. There were perhaps thirty now, on the front benches, in their coats. It was a re-reading by appointment. The Circuit's clerk had the ledger open, and the long projected table on the east wall showed one name only, with the columns under it blank.

*Rowan, K. Draw. Hold. Rank. First door.*

"I'll say what this is," said Ysra Crest. She stood by the clerk's desk with her hands folded. "It is a reading. It is not a classification, and nothing that these instruments say this morning alters a word on the candidate's certificate. She has not been measured since the spring of last year. I've a National entry to countersign by the end of the month, and I'll not send a name to Saltmere with figures on it that are ten months stale."

"One door at a time," said Aske, from the wall. "It's on her card."

"I've read her card. So has Mr. Bexley."

Noa was at the mast. It was the hall's own, and sanctioned, and he had a white flag and a red one stuck in his belt like a signalman. He did not look round.

Her four had come. Anwen was on the front bench with the team book, and Perrin beside her in the grey jersey, which he was at last entitled to. Kellan sat at the end with his pencil. Pru was on her feet, since she could not see sitting.

There was somebody else, three benches back. She was standing. She had a stick in each hand, and her left leg was straight, and she was quite upright.

"The draw bench," said the clerk.

Kiva knew it. It was a lamp-cell under glass, on an oak stand, with a needle on the wall behind it. One held one's palms an inch above the cell and lit it, and kept it lit for sixty seconds, and the needle said what had been burned to do so.

"Which door?" said Ysra.

"Ember."

"Declared. Begin."

She held the pattern. The lamp lit a dull orange, and flickered, and she steadied it.

Then her hand door woke.

It did exactly what it had done in the spring. It lifted inside her, cool and close, reaching for the cell through her palm, since the cell was right there and the hand door liked cells. In the spring the Ember door had pushed back, and the lamp had stuttered, and the needle had swung, and somebody in the gallery had laughed.

She did not let it reach.

It was not a fight. She had thought once that it would always be one. It was more as one says *no* to a child at a shop window, for the fortieth time, without heat. The door went. The lamp did not so much as waver.

At sixty the clerk called time.

*Draw: 1.6.*

It came up on the east wall in figures a foot high.

Nobody laughed. Kellan had his pencil going before the lamp was out.

"It was three and a tenth," he said, to the bench at large. "It's a hair over half. One's standard. One point six is six-tenths over what the book says a minute of light ought to cost. A good single-door second-year pulls nine-tenths. I pull eight-two." He looked up. "So it's fallen a long way, and it's still what a clerk would call poor."

"I can read it," said Kiva.

"I know that you can. I was telling Finch."

"I can read it too," said Pru. "I'm only short."

"The hold benches," said the clerk.

There were three, in a row, each with its own load and its own needle. One opened a door against the bench and kept it open while the bench drew on it, harder and harder, until the door was taken out of one's keeping. The needle stopped where that happened.

She did the Edge first. The bar came down along her right fist, long and narrow and dark, and she lit it, and the bench leaned.

*Edge: 3.1.*

"That's a tenth," said Kellan. "In ten months. I'd not want it on a wall."

The ward was next. She put it up low, at her hip, a tray of silver with two fingers standing out of it, and the bench came against it like a hand on a door.

It was the same bench. But she had put up a ward for one tap and folded it away a great many times since the fifth month. She had done it under the arm of a thing in a pit, and under a cord on a hill. A ward that was folded and unfolded a hundred times before breakfast learned to stay where it was put.

The needle went past where it had stopped in the spring. It went on. She felt the bench take it from her at last, quite gently, like a tin lifted off a shelf.

*Ward: 4.5.*

"*Oh,*" said Anwen.

It was the only word that she said. She was hand-door and always had been, and she had held a ward on that same bench in the spring for longer than anybody in the Third Division. She knew what one and nine-tenths was, in ten months.

*Stance: 6.5.*

"Three-tenths up," said Kellan. "The class median's six and a tenth. That's the one honest figure she has, and she had it already."

"The frame," said Ysra.

The gallery had gone quiet.

It stood in the middle of the floor, as it had in the spring: four oak posts and a lintel, with brass along the inner faces, and above it the board that had flickered. Kiva had stepped into it once with three doors that she could not keep apart. It had woken all three at the same instant, and they had lit one another, and the Chair had said *shut it down.*

"One door," said Ysra. "Which?"

"Ember. Declared. The other two shut."

"The frame will ask for them. That is what it is for; it finds what a candidate has and calls it up, so that the board may see the whole. I can't instruct it to be civil." The level voice did not alter. "You will keep them shut if you can. If you cannot, you will say *off,* and the clerk will throw the switch, and I shall write what happened."

"Yes, ma'am."

She stepped in.

The air thickened. It was like walking into a room where a great many people have been talking and have just stopped. She opened the Ember door, and the bar came down her fist, and the frame took hold of it and looked.

Then it went looking for the rest.

She felt it. It was not rough. It was only thorough. It went down through her like a hand through a drawer and found the hand door, cool, behind its latch, and called to it. It found the still door underneath. The three hot wires on her chest drew tight.

*No,* she said to the first. *No,* to the second.

They pressed. It was sixty seconds, and it was a long sixty. The hand door wanted the brass. The still door wanted the floor, which was oak on stone and would have taken a stance beautifully. She had her back against both, and the Ember lit in front of her, and she did not look up.

"Time," said the clerk.

She looked up.

The board over the lintel was steady. It did not flicker. It had one word on it.

*Rank: Flame.*

"Step out, please, Miss Rowan."

She stepped out. Her legs were not quite her own. She put her right hand flat on her chest, over her tunic, and pressed until the burning went down to an ache.

Ysra had the ledger.

"Draw, one point six. Hold: Edge, three point one. Ward, four point five. Stance, six point five. Rank, with one door declared and two held shut: Flame. First door: none recorded." She read it as she read everything, without leaning on a word. "The clerk will ask me what *held shut* is doing in a rank line. I'll save him the trouble. It's there since it's so."

"It's a rank with a clause in it," said Kellan.

"It is. I've no other kind to give her." The Chair closed the ledger. "Miss Rowan. This is a reading of a summer and an autumn. You've not lit a door since the fifth of the tenth month. Whatever you have done in the three months since, no instrument of mine can see it. I don't say that there was nothing. I say that I've no rig for it."

"No, ma'am."

"Your conditions for the year I shall post with the entries. You'll have them before you stand on any floor of mine."

She went out by the side door. The clerk began to cover the lamp.

Somebody was coming along the front benches on two sticks. She came slowly, and she did not let anybody help.

"One point six," said Selka Dray.

"Yes."

"It's half what it was. It's still poor. I've a blade in my four who pulls seven-tenths and thinks nothing of it." Her sunk eyes were on the dark board. "I'd want that said to you by somebody who isn't pleased. Everybody on that bench is pleased."

"It's said."

"And *Flame.* With two held shut." Her jaw worked. "That's the first reading of yours that I've believed. One door, declared, and the frame leaning on the others for a minute, and you kept them shut. I watched your face. It wasn't easy."

"No."

"Good. I'd have distrusted easy." She shifted a stick. "I'm walking by the third month. I said so. Don't suppose that I've come round."

"I'd not."

Dray went up the aisle. It took her some time. Perrin watched her to the door.

"*Flame,*" he said. "I'm a Flame. I've been one since I was fifteen. I'd like it known that I've never before felt that there was anything to it."

---

She made the crossing on Thursday, on the same floor, with the frame's board lit and the mast at her back.

It was the one that she was certified for. It was on her sponsor's line, in so many words: *Ember to still, low, to a mark, declared to a count, on a sanctioned mast read by the Chair's reader.* She had done it six times in the summer for Orla, and once under a foreman's slate, and once, half, in a lane.

She had not done it since the ninth month. It cost the week's go. It came off her own thimble, and it left her two-tenths until Monday.

"Step first," said Orla.

"I know."

She walked to the chalk mark, eight paces out, and opened the still door on it, and shut it.

"Clean."

"Then say it."

"*Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on three.*"

Noa had his white flag up. The clerk had his pencil. The Chair stood where she had stood on Monday, and Aske by the wall.

"*One.*"

The struts ran down her calves.

"*Two.*"

She went. It was low, and long, and at the top of it she shut the Ember and did not listen for it. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs.

"*Three.*"

Nobody.

She was nobody, in the air over an oak floor, with nothing open and nothing to stand on. She had forgotten that it was like that. One never did remember it properly. It was a quarter of a dram going out of her like a breath, and her left hand went cold to the wrist as it went.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the stance. Wide. By its corners.

Her right foot came down on the chalk, and the floor took her.

"Planted," said Kiva.

"One cliff," said Noa. His voice cracked on it. "Twenty-six. Nothing open across it; I've the two lines, and there's a gap between them that you could drive a tram through. Thirty in all. That's a crossing."

"Mr. Bexley is satisfied that it is not a stack," said Ysra.

"It isn't anything like a stack, ma'am."

"Then look at the board, and tell me what it says, since I'd sooner the clerk heard it from a reader."

They all looked.

It had said *Flame* when she left the ground. She had seen it over her shoulder. It did not say so now. Where the word had been there was a short row of dashes, steady, as a clerk rules a line for a figure that has not come in.

"It went at the cliff," said Noa. "The instant the first door shut. It hasn't flickered. It's just gone to dashes and stopped."

"So it has." The Chair did not sound surprised. "Enter it as it stands. *Rank, with two doors used in sequence: none shown.* I said in the spring that I'd no column. I said it again in the autumn. I find that the board agrees with me, which is more than can be said for most of my colleagues."

"It ranked me on Monday," said Kiva.

"It ranked a candidate with one door. It will do so again whenever you care to be one." Ysra looked at her quite steadily. "I've a page, Miss Rowan, headed *Witnessed.* It has two lines on it. I don't propose to add a third for a thing that you're certified to do."

Aske had her hand already.

"Cold to the wrist. As it was in the summer." He turned it over. "Tomorrow at seven. If it's as before, then the card stands."

It was as before. He said so at seven on Friday, with the pin.

She climbed the stair that creaked that same afternoon, since it was within the week, and wrote in the fourth column with Hollis Vane to witness. *Cold. Thursday the 7th, on the Measure Hall floor. After one crossing, Ember to still, declared and read. Cold before, during and after. K.R.*

"You've a habit," said Vane.

"You said that in the autumn."

"I'll say it in the spring. It's a comfort to a watch officer, a thing that doesn't change."

The boy came to Coper Street at nine on Saturday night, with a slate-blue card. It was three words long. *Yard. Now. Finch.*

It was a bell on the Kiln loop. Kiva heard it from the van, over the whine of the rings, two and two, a mile off in the frost.

She sat on the side bench with her knees against a roll of canvas. Pru was beside her, with a hank of cord inside her tunic. Across the van, by the shuttered lamp, sat four wards in slate blue whom she knew by their boots: Tibb, who hummed, and Stiles, and Greta Stroud, and a fourth. Doran Mace had the bench end, with his board. Nairn was at the back, long-legged, with his blade arm across his knees. There were two men whom she did not know, in canvas aprons, with lead weights hung at their belts.

"Purvis," said the nearer. "That's Hobday. We're his corners."

"I'm his second."

"We heard. We've a shilling on how long you last with your mouth shut."

"Which of you has me lasting?"

"Neither," said Hobday.

Pike sat by the tailboard with a cloth on each shoulder. He had not spoken. He had his right hand inside his coat, against his chest, as a man carries a watch.

The van stopped. Rhea Sorn got down first, and stood with her hands in her pockets, and read.

It was the old ring kiln. Kiva had passed it on the tram a hundred times: a long low oval of blackened brick, with arched wickets all round it at the height of a man, and a chimney that had not smoked in her lifetime. Behind it the ground fell away to the clay pond, which was frozen. There was a row of kiln-hands' houses on the far side of the road, and every window in it was dark.

Something was standing in the third wicket.

It was a string, and it stood on end, as a string ought. It had three knots, each as big as a barrel, one above another, and it had frost on it from the crown to the knee. Its blunt top was turning, slowly, a quarter of a circle.

"Gate," said Rhea.

Four wards walked out and brought a wall up across the road, twelve paces from the kiln, silver from curb to curb. The string's head came round. It found the light. It walked out of the wicket on four thick legs, without hurry, and leaned on the gate like a drunkard on a lamp post.

"That's a string on a gate," said Rhea. "I've had two hundred. I'll read the slate, since I've a second who made it a condition, and since I've found that I rather like hearing it." She tilted it to the van's lamp. "*Before. A string of three, on end, at the old ring kiln. Its tear's at the clay pond, forty paces north. Nothing lit inside sixty paces but my gate; the row's dark, on the warden's word. Asked of anybody beyond their card: nothing. The second kneels at the catcher's left hem and holds her tongue until the bag is sealed.*" She lowered it. "What's there? Mace."

"A join that I like. Stiles is snug."

"Nairn."

"Three rungs. The top's skinned on the south face; I'd come at it from the north."

"Pike."

"Cobbles. They fall to the gutter, a hand in three paces." He had not looked at the cobbles that Kiva had seen. "I'll lay west of it."

Rhea did not ask Kiva. She did not look at her, either. It was on the slate.

"Then it's a top rung, on the catcher's word. Go."

Pike walked out onto the road, and Kiva went after him, and knelt where he pointed.

"My left hem," he said. "That puts the slack on your right. I was told." He did not say by whom. "Hold it down. That's all. If it comes proud under your hand, you bring it in. You don't say so."

She held it down.

It was the oddest floor that she had ever knelt on. She was a yard from the best catcher in Meridian, at a live rung, and she had nothing to do but keep two feet of canvas on the ground and look.

So she looked. She did it as she had looked at a thumb, and a hip.

He had not thrown the cloth yet. He stood over the place, and he scuffed the cobbles with his right heel. He did it twice, a pace apart. Then he threw, and the cloth lay flat at the first cast, and she saw that its long seam ran straight up the fall of the road.

He knelt. He put his left knee on the hem, a hand from hers, and it stayed there. That was why he had both hands free.

His right came out of his coat with the cord in it. It had been against his chest the whole way from the hall.

He was not looking at the string. She could see his face from below, grey in the gate's light, and his eyes were level. They were on something at the height of a boot.

"*Not yet,*" said Pike.

She could not see what he saw. She had her back half to the thing. She could hear it: a low dry creak, like a gate in a wind.

"*Not yet. Now.*"

There was a light. It was yellow, and level, and it lasted most of a second. It lit the cobbles and the hem and the two fingers of her left hand, lying on the canvas with nothing to say.

Sixty drams came down in the middle of the cloth like sand out of a sack.

"*Dark! One.*"

The wall went out over the heap, all but a cart's width at Stroud's end, which the book said should be left lit. What remained of the string turned its head and walked to that, and leaned there. The road went black, and then grey.

He did not call the corners.

She had been waiting for it. *Dark, one, corners;* she had called it herself forty times. Pike knelt there with his knee on the hem and his hands open and looked at a heap of ash, and said nothing at all.

She counted. She could not help it. She had got to eight when he spoke.

"Corners."

Purvis and Hobday hauled. The neck came up between them. It was beautiful to watch from underneath; there was no snatch in it anywhere. The hem ran in under her right hand, an inch at a time, exactly as fast as she could give it.

The cord went round. One. Two. On the third she saw his hands change over. The left took the long end under, and the right only held.

"Sealed," said Pike. "Forty-four." He had his palm on the neck. "Ward. Full. It's this winter's skin, and no louder than it ought to be."

They did the second rung, and the third.

On the second she counted eleven before he called the corners. And at about the twelfth count of the haul, the hem on the far side of the cloth lifted. It was Hobday's corner. It came up proud, a hand high, in a long loose fold.

She saw it. She had her mouth open.

She shut it. Her teeth clicked.

Pike's left knee moved. It slid an inch along the hem, toward the fold, as a man shifts his weight on a bench.

"Hobday," he said. "In a hand."

The fold lay down.

He had not turned his head. He had felt it through the canvas, with his knee, a yard and a half off, at the same moment that she had seen it with her eyes.

"Sealed. Forty-six."

"Finch," he said, at the third. "Your cord."

Pru came up out of the dark with her chin stuck out. She made three turns over a neck that a chartered catcher was holding shut for her, and Kiva watched the little finger come across, and nothing slackened.

"Sealed," said Pru. It was nearly a squeak. "Fifty-one."

"That's slow. It's also a bag." He took his knee off the hem. "You'll be at forty-five by the spring, or I'll know why."

There were two people in long coats at the clay pond, with a handcart, bent over the ice. Kiva heard one of them call *closed,* not loudly, as a man says that he has shut a gate.

Rhea read the slate in the road.

"*Closed, ten minutes past eleven. Lives: none. Works: a kiln wicket, frosted. Tinned: three bags, clean, at trace. A hundred and sixty-two drams, of a hundred and eighty by the book. Remarks: none.*" She hung it on her belt. "That's a night. Stroud, you were proud on the left for a count and mended it; I'm saying that I saw."

"I heard myself," said Stroud.

"I know. That's why I'm only saying."

---

They rode back with the bags in the rack and the lamp shuttered.

Pike sat by the tailboard. He had his right hand in his coat again. He waited until they were past the gasworks.

"Well," he said. "What did I do that I didn't say?"

Kiva had it ready. She had been putting it in order since the kiln.

"You scuffed the road with your heel. Twice, before you threw. And then you laid the seam up the fall. You told the Torch that the cobbles fell a hand in three paces, and you'd not looked at them. You'd felt it through your boot."

"That's a catcher's trick. I was shown it."

"You didn't say it. You said *I'll lay west.*"

Purvis laughed. Pike did not.

"Go on."

"The cord was inside your coat. From the hall. It's warm when it comes out."

"Wax," said Pike. "It's January. Any fool keeps his cord warm."

"Pru had hers under her arm. She kept it at her elbow until I saw her lose three in the cold."

"*I* keep it in my tunic now," said Pru. "I'd like that entered."

"What else?"

"You don't call the corners at *one.*" She watched his face. "You call *dark, one.* Then you wait. I counted eight the first time. Eleven the second, and nine the third. You look at the heap. Then you call them."

"I call the corners when it's down."

"It was down at one."

"I don't wait."

"Eleven," said Hobday, from the dark. "I've had my hands on that hem for nine years, cursing you. I never counted it."

Pike was silent. The van went over the tram points with a double knock.

"I watch it lie," he said at last, slowly, like a man reading small print. "I suppose that I do."

"What for?"

"I don't know that I've ever said." He took his hand out of his coat and looked at it. He shut it, and opened it, twice. "Write that one with the others. I'll read them by the stove. And the fourth."

"I've only said three."

"You've a fourth. You've had it behind your teeth since the first bag." He nearly smiled. "You shut your mouth on Hobday's corner so hard that I heard it. What would you have said?"

"*Hobday's proud.*"

"And I had it."

"Yes. Before I'd have got the words out."

"So there'd have been two voices at one hem, and the second telling the first a thing that he knew." Pike put the hand away. "That's why your mouth's shut. It isn't manners. A corner can only listen to one."

Kiva thought about that as far as the levee.

She had learned a good deal on her knees. She had also learned the size of what she had not seen. He had found a proud corner with his kneecap. He had read the fall of a road through a boot sole. There were twenty-six years in that square grey man, and she had got three things out of a whole night, and the fourth she was not sure of.

Rhea Sorn had turned round on the front bench.

"It's winter by the almanac," she said. "So you may have the piece that I've been keeping. That was a quiet night. A string came to a gate and was taken down a rung at a time by people who've done it two hundred times, and nobody was hurt, and you'll write it up beautifully."

"Yes."

"Don't mistake it for the trade."

She turned back. The rings whined. Pru had fallen asleep against the canvas, with her hand still shut on her cord.

# Chapter 6 — Kiva: Seven Columns

She wrote him up on Sunday, at the kitchen table, on the Works paper that was ruled in seven.

It took the whole afternoon. She had nine things by the end of it, and each of them had a line to itself: what it was called, and what he did, and how. The last column she left empty all the way down. That was his.

The eighth entry took longer than the other eight together.

*The third turn of the cord.* She had seen it three times, on three bags. *What: he changes hands. The right has the first turn and the second. On the third the right only holds, and the left takes the long end under.*

It was there. She had watched it as she had watched a laundress's hip. But a hip was a trick, and this did not look like one. It looked like a man sparing something.

He had said that he did not want what was wrong. He had also said *which hand I use, and when,* in so many words, by a stove.

"You've had that pencil in the air for ten minutes," said Mara.

"I don't know whether it's what he does or what's the matter with him."

"Is it what you saw?"

"Yes."

"Then it's what he does. What's the matter with him is his to put in the last column." Her aunt did not look up from the tide table, which had come by Friday's boat and was spread across her half of the oilcloth like a railway guide. "I've had Rusk's thumbs described to me by three different fitters. He's never thanked one of them. He's also never dropped a can."

Kiva wrote it in.

---

Pike read them on Tuesday, by the stove in the mending room, with his spectacles on.

She had not known that he owned spectacles. He took them from a tin in his apron, and hooked them on one ear at a time, and held the sheet a long way off.

"You brought me a laundress first," he said.

"You said somebody who'd been good at a thing for longer than was decent."

"*Hip pins the near hem. Folds from the far edge; always has the last of it.*" He read it without expression. "That's a ward-fold."

"It's Finch's. I wrote that in the corner."

"So you did." He laid the ticket on his knee and took up the sheets. "Now me."

He read as he sewed. He set his eye to a line and pulled it through, and did not go on to the next until it was fast.

"*The heel. Twice, a pace apart, before the cloth.*" He took her pencil out of her hand, without asking, and wrote in the empty column at the right, in a square slow print. "Why. Since ash runs to the low side while it's open. Lay the seam up the fall and it runs to the neck. Lay it across and you've a heap in your corner-hand's lap." He went down a line. "*The cord in the coat.* Wax. *Kneels with the gate at his back.* That's the book; I'll not write the book out. *Left knee on the hem.*"

"I put *so that he has both hands.*"

"Under *how.* It's well enough. There's more." He wrote. "A knee hears a hem. Not so well as a hand. But a hand's wanted, and a knee's doing nothing."

"That's how you had the proud corner."

"That's how I had it. I'll come to what you wrote about that." He turned the sheet. "*His eyes are level. He does not look at the thing. He looks at something at the height of a boot.*"

"I couldn't see what."

"Her heel. Sorn's. The left." It was the nearest that she had seen him come to being pleased. "A blade's feet settle a count before her arm goes. Hers come down flat when she's ready and not before. I've called *now* off that heel for eleven years. She doesn't know it."

"Shall I write that she doesn't know?"

"Write that the catcher watches the blade's feet. Leave whose." He wrote it himself.

Then he came to the wait, and stopped.

"*After* dark, one, *he does not call the corners. He waits between eight and eleven counts. He looks at the heap.*" He read it twice. "I said in the van that I didn't."

"You said that you watched it lie."

"So I do. I've been thinking since Saturday what for." He had the pencil over the last column. He did not put it down. "It's open for a slow sixty. Everybody knows that. In the first ten it's at its loosest, and it leans. It leans to whatever's nearest that keeps a pattern. That's why the wall goes out over it, all but the one end; I'd not have it take a ward's shape. And if it leans to the end that's left lit, and settles, then there's nothing else on that ground, and I bring my corners in."

"And if it leans somewhere else?"

"Then there's a thing on my floor that I wasn't told of. A box somebody's left lit in a cellar. A blade not gone dark." He looked at the stove. "I'd move the cloth. I'd have my corners haul from the other side, and get the neck between the heap and whatever it was."

"Have you?"

"Not above four times. It's leaned to the lit end, or it's lain flat, for six-and-twenty years." His square face did not change. "Since the thaw it leans a hair every time. To nothing that I can find. I'd not thought about that until you made me say what I was looking at."

He wrote in the column. It was the longest that he had written. *Why: to see what the heap is listening to, before I put my people's hands near it.*

"That's a good one," he said, grudgingly. "I'd have gone to my grave not telling a corner that."

He went on. He came to the eighth.

Kiva watched him read it. She saw him get to the end of the line, and go back to the beginning, and she saw the grey come up over his collar.

"That's not a habit."

"It's what was there. On all three bags."

"I tie right-handed. I've tied right-handed since I was sixteen."

"You tie two turns right-handed. On the third the right holds and the left goes under."

"Since when?"

"I don't know since when. I've only seen three."

He said nothing. He took his right hand out from where it lay on the canvas, and held it to the stove, and shut it. It shut as a stiff gate shuts. The fingers went, and the thumb came after, late, by the width of a breath.

"The autumn before last," said Pike. "It's the cold. It closes behind the rest. I can't trust it to pin on a third turn, so the left does it, and I'd have told you on oath that nobody could see."

"I didn't write that it was wrong."

"No. You wrote *he changes hands on the third.* In a column headed *how.*" His voice was quite flat. "A corner-hand reads that. He thinks, that's how Pike does it, that's the way, and he spends a year learning to tie cack-handed for the sake of an old man's thumb."

"Is it the way?"

"It's my thumb."

"Then that's the last column," said Kiva. "I left it empty. It's yours."

He looked at her over the spectacles. It was not a friendly look. He had asked for a thing, and been given it, and it had turned out to have an edge.

He wrote. He bore down on the pencil.

*Why: his right thumb is slow. This is not method. Do not copy it.*

"There," he said. "That's a chartered catcher's hand, in writing, saying what's the matter with it. I've not told Sorn. I suppose that she'll read it."

"Nobody reads it unless you say."

"Don't be soft. If it's not to be read, it's not worth the paper." He turned to the ninth, roughly. "*Went wrong. Second bag. Hobday's corner proud at about the twelfth count. He had it with his knee. I saw it at the same count and would have spoken.*"

"That's what went wrong."

"Nothing went wrong. It was mended inside a count."

"It was proud for a count."

"And that's a man nine years on my hem, with his name in a column." He struck through one word and wrote another over it. *A corner.* "It's not about him. It's not about me, come to that. It's about you. *Would have spoken.*"

"I put it since it was so."

"Then leave it. I'd sooner have a second who writes down that she nearly opened her mouth than one who pretends that she never wanted to." He squared the sheets on his knee. He took the spectacles off, an ear at a time. "Nine. And two that I'd not have known I did, and one that I'd have paid you not to see."

"Is that all of them?"

"No."

She waited.

"There's one that you've missed. It's the one that matters most to anybody kneeling where you kneel." He nearly smiled, and thought better of it. "I'll not tell you. If I tell you, you'll look for it, and then you'll see it whether it's there or not. You'll find it or you won't."

He gave her the sheets back. He kept the laundry ticket.

"I'm having this," said Pike. "I've a notion to read it to Finch."

The Charter Board's conditions went up on Friday, on the same board, under the same seal.

Kellan read them aloud. He had not been asked to. He began in his ordinary voice, as one reads a tram notice.

"*Entries by Saturday the 30th of the first month, at the office of each circuit, under each four's own hand and its captain's.*

"*A captain shall be a classified candidate of the four, in a declared role. The captain is the one voice that a referee or a marshal hears. The captain declares every role and every change, and calls a withdrawal.*

"*A four is four and one reserve. A candidate stands for one four only, declared at entry. There are no changes at the gate.*"

He stopped there. It was not for long. But Kiva was standing next to him, and she saw his ears go white.

"*The warrant,*" he said. "*Three drams a head, the reserve included, for passage, bed and the gate comb. Paid with the entry.*"

"Three," said Anwen.

"I said two. In the tenth month, at your table. I said that it wouldn't be less." He was writing on his cuff, quickly, as though it were a relief. "Five heads is fifteen. We hold five-and-twenty and twenty-one hundredths, if nobody lights anything tomorrow."

"We're lighting tomorrow," said Perrin. "Old Four's coming to the yard."

"Then we hold less. Go on, there's more." He found his place. "*The Hunt Marshal, D. Coombe, Blaze, will come to each circuit in the second month to prepare its first company for the Hunt. Her floors are at each four's own charge, at a dram a head.*"

"A Blaze," said Pru. "I've never seen a Blaze."

"There isn't one on the Circuit's roll," said Noa. He was at the end of the board again. "Sorn's a Torch. So's Miss Dane. It's the rank above. Coombe ran mixed fours on the coast for thirty years, and the Board's had her write its hunt rules." He went pink. "I asked the clerk."

"A dram a head," said Kellan. "Four heads on a floor. If she gives us one floor, that's four. I don't know how many she gives." He added it. "So it's fifteen and four. That's nineteen, before anybody's so much as bought a cord. Out of five-and-twenty."

"*A captain,*" said Jude.

They had all been not saying it.

"A classified candidate," said Kiva. "I'm not classified. I've a rank with a clause in it and a board that goes to dashes."

"I'm not on the book," said Jude. "I'm a head's share and a name."

"I'm a reserve," said Pru. "I'm a first-year."

That left three. Anwen had the team book under her arm, and she did not open it. Perrin was looking at the ceiling. Kellan was looking at the line above. *A candidate stands for one four only.*

"We've a fortnight," said Anwen. "Fifteen days. I want it said that I'd sooner this were settled at a table than in a basement, with half of Stonehand reading over our shoulders."

"I've to think," said Kellan.

"For how long?"

"I don't know. It's thinking."

"It looks a good deal like sulking from the bench," said Jude, quite gently.

Kellan did not answer him. He put his pencil away, and he went up the stairs two at a time.

Kiva watched him go.

She had done the sum that he had done, and she had done another, which was not on the board. If Kellan Renn stood for Halden License, then Seven was a catcher, a Hold, a carry and a Kill by name. If he stood for Seven, then a Works yard with a string in a cage would have a peg with nothing on it, and the best feet in the School would have turned their back on it twice.

She did not know which she wanted. She found that she was rather ashamed of how much she wanted to be asked.

Old Four came to the Old Yard on Saturday at two, in their coats, with a dummy of their own on poles.

"It's a yard," said Orla. She was on her crate, a pace north of the south row, with a sand-glass on her knee. "It's not sanctioned and it's not read. There's no mast on anybody. So I've no need of the Chair's paper, and I'll have my own instead. Rowan. Say it."

"One door. The still. Declared for all three carries."

"And if you open another?"

"Then I've lost the carry and I've told you that I've no business on a floor."

"That'll do." Orla turned the glass over in her fingers. "Circuit rules. Three for the litter over your own line. One for a down. If the glass runs out, a point to whichever half it's lying in. Seven's line is the sheds. Old Four's is the gap. Three carries."

The litter lay in the middle of the lane, twenty paces from either end, between the two rows of black posts. It was Gerald. Somebody had given him a hat.

Kiva stood on the scraped line by the sheds with her four.

It was her four, for that afternoon. Perrin had the middle, out of his jersey for the first time in three months, with his sleeves rolled. Anwen was on his left with her hands low. Kellan was on the right. He had come down the lane at five minutes to two, in his own coat, and laced his cuff, and said nothing about anything.

Jude and Pru had the shed step. There were juniors on it as well, a dozen of them, in grey smocks, packed like sparrows on a gutter.

"Is she going to use all three?" said a small sandy voice.

"She's declared one," said Pru.

"What's declared?"

"It's when you say what you'll do, aloud, and then you've got to."

"That's hard."

"Yes," said Pru. "It's meant to be."

"*Go,*" said Orla, and turned the glass.

Kiva did what she always did.

She went up the lane at a walk, on the north side, and four paces short of the litter she stopped. She opened the still door on the packed dirt, by its corners, and shut it. *Clean.* Then she went on to the litter's flank, and Perrin had the front poles already, and she came back to her tested ground as he lifted, and planted.

The yard took her.

Old Four did not come at a run. They never had. Lowen Brask walked to the head of the litter, square and quiet, and set his feet in front of Perrin, and opened a door of his own.

The lane boomed. It was like two drays meeting in a gateway.

He did not strike. He leaned. A still door did not push the man in front of it; it pushed through him, as a hand pushes through a row of books, and what moved was whatever stood at the far end. That was Kiva. She felt it come in at her shoulder, where it touched Perrin's back.

It stopped there. She was planted. It went down through her into the ground, as a corner had once, and Perrin did not move by the width of a boot.

"*Mine!*" said Anwen. A wall of silver came up on the left, cart-wide, and Edda's came against it from the other side with a noise like a shut drawer.

Kellan had gone.

She did not see him go. He was there, at her right hand, and then there were two long low bounds along the north row, with the struts running down his calves, and a line of blue-white behind a post.

"*Now?*" he said.

He said it to her. He was three paces off, behind Old Four's blade, with his edge lit, and he looked over his shoulder at his catcher.

"Now."

He touched Gower between the shoulders. It was as light as a tap on a door.

"Down," said Orla. "Gower. Kneel."

After that it was only walking. Kellan took the foot poles. Brask stood where he was, a post with nothing to lean on, and they carried Gerald home past him with his hat on.

"Three and one," said Orla. "Seven, four. Old Four, nothing. Set."

"He asked her," said one of the juniors.

"He always asks her," said Jude. "He learned it. It took him a spring."

---

Brask had his notebook out.

Kiva saw that from the line. He did not write in it. He only looked at something that was there already, and shut it, and said three words to Wace.

"*Go.*"

She walked up the north side. She stopped four paces short, and tested, and shut. *Clean.* She went on to the litter.

When she turned round, Wace was standing on it.

He had walked there. He had simply come up the lane at his own pace, as she left the mark, and put his two boots where hers had been and opened his door. He was a big placid boy, and he stood on her tested ground like a cow in a gateway.

"That's mine," said Kiva.

"It was," said Wace. "I've been a post before."

Brask and Gower had the litter. They had taken it while Perrin was looking at Wace. They were walking it east.

She needed ground.

She had one door, and it was no use in the air. She looked for the nearest place to stand that was not under somebody, and it was south of the litter, two paces off, hard by the south row.

She opened the door on it.

There was a room underneath.

She had known. She had known as she did it; she had written it in a proof sheet, in pencil, with her initials. The main came along under the wall to the leaning post and went on west beneath the south row, eight feet down, bricked. Nothing in it kept time any longer. But it was hollow, and her stance found the top of it and rang, and for half a count she had her weight on nothing.

She shut it. She did not plant.

"*Rowan!*" That was Perrin. He had got a hand to a pole. Brask leaned on him, and this time there was nobody behind.

He went back three paces on his heels, like a wardrobe on castors.

"Down," said Orla. "Cade. On your back counts."

It was Gower who had done it, with a touch as he went by. Kellan had Wace a moment after, since Wace was planted and could not have dodged a tram. It did not matter. The litter was over the line at the gap, and Old Four's ward had not so much as lit.

"Three and one," said Orla. "And one to Seven for Wace. Seven, five. Old Four, four."

Kiva stood in the lane with nothing open.

She had been read. It was as plain as that. She had a habit, and it was a good one; she tested her ground before she stood on it, every time, and she had never landed on a room since the fifth of the tenth month. And a boy with a notebook had watched her do it since the Pit, and had seen that she did it in the open, in front of everybody, a full count before she used it.

It was a signpost. She had been putting one up at every floor that she stood on.

And he had known about the drain. He had felt that post in the dark, on a night with a litter. He had left her the south side on purpose.

"*Go.*"

She tested farther back, nearer her own line. Edda walked onto it. She tested a third place, and Wace was there before she had shut the door.

"Stop it," said Perrin.

He had come up beside her. He was breathing hard, and he had dirt down his back.

"You test, and then we all look at it. *He* looks at it." He jerked his head at Brask. "I'm a still door. I can stand on a bit of ground as well as Wace can, and I'm on your side. Test it under me."

"What?"

"I stand first. You open your door under my heels. If it's clean, you say so, and I don't shift until you've said *mine.* Then I say *yours,* and I go to the poles." His big red face was quite serious. "It's the wall. It's what you made us do with you on the litter. Caught, and then let go."

She stared at him.

It was hers. She had lain on her back on the cobbles and said it, in the eleventh month, with a bag of sand across her shins. She had not once thought of it as a thing that could be done to a piece of ground.

"North of the litter," she said. "Two paces. Go."

He went. He planted, and the lane boomed. Wace, coming up the north side, found a carry of Stonehand's second team on the place that he had meant to stand, and stopped, and looked at Brask.

Kiva opened her door under Perrin's boots.

*Clean.*

"Mine."

"Yours," said Perrin, and stepped off forward, and she was planted before his heel had left the dirt.

Nobody had seen her test. There had been nothing to see.

Brask leaned. It came down the litter and into her and stopped.

But it had taken time. There had been three false marks and a conversation, and Old Four had dragged Gerald a full pace into their own half before she was set. Anwen had come off her wall to cover Perrin's back while he stood. Gower touched her as she turned.

"Down," said Orla. "Pryce."

The last grains ran out of the glass with nobody moving. The litter lay a yard east of the middle.

"One for the half, and one for the down. Old Four, six. Seven, five." She set the glass on the crate. "That's the afternoon. If you've ash left and want a fourth for nothing, I'll turn it again. It doesn't go on any sheet."

"Turn it," said Kellan.

They ran the fourth on the last of his ash and Anwen's.

Perrin was standing north of the litter before the sand had begun to fall. Kiva tested under him, and said *mine,* and he said *yours.* Brask leaned on a thing that did not move, and went on leaning. Kellan asked her *now?* from behind a post, and she said it, and he touched Gower, and came back for the foot poles at a walk.

It was the best carry that they had made since the summer. It was not on any sheet.

Brask came down the lane afterward with his notebook in his hand.

"You always test first," he said. "I've had it written since the Pit. It's the best habit in the Third Division. I'd not have you drop it."

"You had Wace stand on it."

"I'd have stood on it myself, only I was wanted at the pole." He was not apologizing. He was a boy who read forms for a living, and he had read one. "And I left you the south. I felt that post once, with a litter. I knew what was under it."

"That's fair."

"I thought so. I'd expect a Saltmere four to see it by the second carry, and they'll not have the drain." He looked at Perrin, who was sitting on Gerald. "That was good. Holding it for her. Whose was that?"

"Mine," said Perrin. "I'd like it known. I thought of it with dirt down my neck."

"It costs you your carry for three counts."

"I know what it costs. I counted them." He got up, with care. "We've one who can stand on a thing, and I'm wanted at the front. It wants a fifth, and a lane's four."

Nobody had an answer to that. Kiva wrote it down as it stood.

*Went wrong: tested in the open. They stood on it. Second try: held first, by Cade; tested under him; "mine" before "yours." It works. It costs the carry three counts, and there is nobody to spare.*

They signed on the lid of the quench box. Kellan read it, and it was short.

"*Saturday the 16th. The Old Yard. Three carries against Old Four, and a fourth for nothing. Lost, six to five. Lit: Pryce, 0.21. Cade, 0.12. Renn, 0.24. Rowan, eleven stances, off her own thimble. Spent: 0.57. Held: 24.64. Sealed, medical, W: 0.8.*"

He signed first. Then he sat with the pen in his hand, and looked at the three names that would go under his.

"That adds," he said.

It was all that he said. He gave Anwen the pen.

Kellan Renn counted the rivets on Thursday.

He had not meant to. He had not done it since the ninth month. But he came in under the refinery gate at four, with his own cuff in his pocket, and found that he was at two hundred and six before he had told himself to stop.

That was a bad sign. He counted things when he had already decided and did not care for the answer.

The license yard was as it always was. There was the blue brick, and the lattice house at the north end, and the paid wards standing easy along the wall. There was the hum coming up through his boot soles, slow and even, the tread of a great many people going by on the far side of a wall. His feet wanted to fall in with it.

He counted threes against it, as he had since the autumn, and they steadied.

"Top rung," said Birch. "Yarrow has the cloth. You'll ask him."

"Yes, sir."

He went up the back of the string in two bounds, dark, on his struts. It was the same string. It had been in that cage since the thaw, and he had taken its top rung twenty-six times. He knew how it stood as he knew his own stair.

"*Now?*"

"Not yet. It's swaying. Now."

He lit. It was four-tenths, to a hair. The edge went through level, and he was dark before his feet found the brick, and sixty drams came down on Yarrow's cloth with the sound of sand.

"*Dark. One. Corners.*"

Pettit and Hesketh hauled. It was very clean. It was cleaner than anything that he had seen on a Circuit floor; these three had had a contained string to themselves two days in the week since the thaw, and it showed in every hand.

"Sealed," said Yarrow. "Forty-one."

A grader in a black coat held a comb over the neck. Kellan could not hear the reed from where he stood. He could see the tag that the man wrote, and it was red.

It was always red on this floor. He had stopped noticing that.

He noticed it now, since he had spent a fortnight with a printed sheet in his head. *Marked on clean yield.* Clean was under trace on the seventh reed. This was a yard that kept step, with a bank under the brick that had been salted to a beat before he was born, and every rung that had ever fallen on it had been open for a slow sixty with that under it.

He had the figures. He kept everything. Nine rungs since the new year, and nine red tags.

It did not matter on a license yard. Halden did not tin what fell here; it was a practice string, and the ash went back into the Works' own stock, where the Step was wanted. It would matter a great deal on a tidal flat, with a marshal's comb at the gate.

He put that in his cuff, in two figures, and did not say it. He did not yet know whom he would be saying it to.

"Renn," said Birch.

The foreman had come to the rail. He had a board under his arm, with a printed sheet clipped to it, and the seal at the head of the sheet was one that Kellan had been reading for three months.

"That's our entry. It goes to the Chair's office on Saturday week." Birch turned it round. "Yarrow, catcher. Hesketh, Hold. Pettit, carry, and captain. I've a line that says *blade,* and I've had nothing to put on it since the fifth month, when ours went to the Line. You cut for these three at the Regional. You've cut for them twice a week since."

"Nothing owed. You said."

"Nothing's owed. I'm not calling it in. I'm asking, which is a thing that I do about twice a year and dislike." He scratched his neck. "The Works pays the warrant. It's three a head. It sends its four down by station transit on the twenty-eighth of next month; I'm told that it's a day and a half, with a bed."

"Seven goes by the road. And a ferry, and the river, and the coast boat."

"I dare say that it does."

Kellan looked at the line.

It was a very ordinary line. It had a ruled space after it, the width of a name.

He made himself do it properly, as a sum, with the working shown.

Halden License had been first at the Regional. It had been first with him on the peg. It would go to Saltmere as one of the three or four fours in the country that might win outright, and whoever cut for it would be read by every draft board on the coast, on a blade that he had never drawn so true as on this brick. He was seventeen. He was a Fire. He had a Draw of eight-two and feet that a foreman looked at before he looked at a face.

On the other side there was a kitchen table.

There was a team that had been sixth by yield, in brackets, and was fourth by no better title than that it had stood on a hill in the dark. There was a catcher with one door declared and a rank with a clause in it. There was a Kill who came down heavy and had seven drams and eight-tenths by name. Seven would not be worse at the cut without him. He had seen Talley on a tenth knot. It would be worse at one thing only. It would have a blade who had not spent a spring learning to say *now?* over his shoulder.

And he had declared for them. He had done it at the Regional, under rule nineteen, in front of eleven hundred people, with this yard's jacket on his back. He had taken the jacket off.

*I'd do it again tomorrow.* He had written that to Birch.

He found that it was still true. He would turn his back on this floor again, for that four, on any day when it was the four or the floor.

It was not that kind of day. Nobody was asking him to wear anything. There was a board with ninety-six marks on it, and a roll that took eight, and he wanted to know what he was. He wanted to stand in the best four in the country and find out whether he was the weak corner of it. He would never learn that at a kitchen table, where he was the one whom the others looked at when a thing wanted cutting.

That was the whole of it. It had no shine on it whatever. He turned it over, looking for something better underneath, a reason that he could have said to Pryce without his ears going white.

There was not one.

*I want it said,* she would say. And he would have to say it.

"You'll have my name," said Kellan.

"I'd thought that I might."

"Not today. On Tuesday. I've to say it at a table first, on Monday, to four people and a first-year. I'll not have them read it off a board."

Birch looked at his feet. He looked at them for some time.

"They've not got worse," he said. "I'd have said that it wanted more nerve to stop where you were."

"It would. I've looked. I haven't it."

"No." The foreman took the board back under his arm. "Well. There's not many would have told me so."

Kellan went and unlaced his cuff.

He did not count the rivets on the way out. He had what he had come for, and it was exactly as heavy as he had expected, and there was a tram to Coper Street on Monday that he would have to be on.

# Chapter 7 — Kiva: The Far Edge

The card came at ten on Friday night, and it was the same three words.

There had been a week of frost. The river smoked with it. The van went down through the low streets of the Hythe at a walk, with the rings whining and Ruddock leaning on the tiller, and from every door that they passed came the flat iron *clack* of a red handle being pulled.

"Boxes dark," said a warden's voice, somewhere ahead. "Odd numbers. Evens, wait for your bell."

Kiva sat where she had sat a fortnight before, with her knees against the canvas. Pru was beside her. The cord was inside Pru's tunic, where it lived now.

"You lasted a night," said Purvis, across the lamp. "Hobday and I had a shilling each that you'd not. We've had to give them both to Ruddock."

"I nearly didn't."

"We heard. He told the whole hall about your teeth."

Pike was by the tailboard with a cloth on each shoulder and his right hand inside his coat. He had read nine entries by a stove, and written in the last column of each, and he had not mentioned them since.

"It's the coal wharf," said Rhea Sorn, from the front bench. "Below the Hythe stair. The warden says that it's standing still, which I don't care for. A thing that's come up by water generally wants to be somewhere."

The van stopped.

It was a place that Kiva knew by daylight. She had carried eleven sheets down the stair beside it, in the tenth month, with a parcel under her arm. It was a cobbled apron, forty paces across and thirty deep, that ran down from a pair of iron gates to the river's edge. There were mooring rings along the edge. On the west side the coal stood in sacks, row on row, under tarpaulins white with rime, and a hand-crane leaned over them like a heron. On the east a flight of stone steps went down into the water.

There was a blue line standing on those steps. It was thin, and it was the height of a door.

And ten paces up from it, in the middle of the wharf, stood a string.

It was on end, as a string ought to be. It had four knots, each as big as a barrel, and frost on every face of it from the crown to the knee. Its blunt top was turning.

"Gate," said Rhea.

Mace's four walked out and brought the wall up across the head of the wharf, in front of the iron gates, silver from wall to wall. The string's head came round. It found the light, and it walked up the fall of the cobbles on its four thick legs, without hurry.

Something came with it.

Kiva saw it as the thing passed under the gate's light. There was a white patch at its feet. It lay flat on the cobbles, three paces long and two across, as thin as breath on a window, and it trailed down the wharf behind the string as a train trails behind a bride. When the string walked, it slid. When the string leaned its head on the wall, it lay still.

"What's that at its heels?" said Rhea.

"Hem," said Nairn. He had got down beside her, with his blade arm across his chest. "Lying."

"A hem that's down is ground," said Mace. "That's the book. It's a page and a half."

"I know what the book says. I've seen a hem lie down. It lies down when the thing that hung it is dead." Rhea had her hands in her pockets. "I've not seen one lying under a thing that's on its feet."

Pike walked out onto the wharf.

He went alone, at his square pace, to the foot of the white patch, and he put his right heel on it. He scuffed it, twice, a pace apart, as he scuffed a road. Then he stood on it with his whole weight.

Nothing happened. The string leaned on the wall and took no notice.

"It's hard," he said, coming back. "It's cold, and it doesn't take a boot. I'd call it ground."

"I'll read it, then." Rhea tilted the slate to the van's lamp. "*Before. A string of four, on end, at the coal wharf below the Hythe. Its tear's at the river stair. A hem lying at its foot, three paces by two, which the book calls ground. Nothing lit inside sixty paces but my gate; the Hythe's dark, on the warden's word. Asked of anybody beyond their card: nothing. The second kneels at the catcher's left hem and holds her tongue until the bag is sealed.*" She lowered it. "What's there? Mace."

"A join that I like. Tibb's got ice under his left boot and knows it."

"Nairn."

"Four rungs. It's skinned to the knee all round; I've not seen one so well wrapped. I'd take the top from the east."

"Pike."

"The wharf falls south. A hand in two paces; it's steep for a cloth. If a rung runs here, it runs to the river." He was looking at the cobbles and not at the string. "I'll lay south of it, seam up the fall. That puts my cloth on the hem. There's nowhere else that the rung will come down."

"On the hem."

"I've had my heel on it."

Rhea looked at the white patch for a long moment. The pale eyes went over it as they went over a street.

"I don't like it," she said. "I've no reason. I've only a dislike, and I've stood a good many floors on a dislike and been wrong." She hung the slate on her belt. "Top rung, on the catcher's word. Go."

She did not ask Kiva. She had not, on the ninth. It was on the slate.

---

Kiva knelt where she was pointed, on the east side of the cloth, a pace and a half down from its corner.

It lay very well. Pike had thrown it once, and it had gone down across the fall of the wharf like a sheet onto a bed, with its long seam running straight uphill to the string's heels. It was six paces square. Somewhere under the upper half of it there was a patch of white, and she could not see so much as the edge.

He knelt at the head of the cloth, a yard from the thing's legs, with the wall at his back. His left knee came down on the hem beside her. His right hand came out of his coat with the cord in it.

"Hold it down," said Pike. "That's all."

Purvis and Hobday had the bottom corners, six paces off, toward the river. Pru stood behind, off the canvas, with her own cord round her wrist.

Kiva held it down.

She had her right hand on the hem, where the slack would come. Her left lay beside it, flat, on the canvas, with nothing to do. The cobbles were under the canvas, and the cold came up through both.

*Something's pushing.*

It was the first two fingers.

She did not move. She had been taught, on a shop bench, what to do when they spoke. She made the touch light. She took the weight of her arm off them until they lay on the canvas like two straws.

*Pushing. And not. And pushing.*

It was very slow. It was slower than a pump.

*Six in seven,* said Anwen's ink, in a laundry order book. *Of five wrong calls, four were at a pulse over ninety.*

Her heart was going like a tram bell. She knew that it was. She was kneeling a yard from the best catcher in Meridian, at a live string, on a cloth that had been laid over a thing which an officer disliked; and there was nobody holding her wrist, and nobody whom she could ask.

She lifted the two fingers off and put her right palm where they had been.

Cold. Grit. The weave of the canvas. There was a great deal in a good hand, and she listened through all of it, and could not find a push.

So it was one or the other. It was a needle with no scale, saying that something under a hundredweight of waxed cloth was keeping time. Or it was a frightened girl reading her own blood. She had the method for telling which. It wanted a second person and a word spoken aloud, and she had her tongue held by a card with four signatures on it.

*It's a reason to look.* Mara had allowed it that much.

She looked. There was nothing to see. There was a square of grey canvas on a dark wharf, and a man's knee, and the light of a ward wall lying along the seam.

"*Not yet,*" said Pike.

His eyes were level. They were on a boot heel that she could not see.

"*Not yet. Now.*"

The light was yellow, and level, and it lasted most of a second.

Sixty drams came down in the upper middle of the cloth with the sound of sand, and lay.

"*Dark! One.*"

The wall went out over them, all but a cart's width at the eastern end. The wharf went black, and then grey. Kiva heard the string's legs shift on the cobbles.

It did not walk to the lit end.

She noticed that. She had seen three rungs come down at a kiln, and each time the thing had turned its head and gone to what was left of the gate, as a moth goes. This one stayed where it stood. It had its four feet at the head of Pike's cloth, and it kept them there.

He was watching the heap.

She knew what for now. He had written it himself, in the seventh column, in pencil. *To see what the heap is listening to, before I put my people's hands near it.* So she watched it with him, and counted, since she could not help counting.

*Three. Four.*

It ought to have leaned. A heap leaned toward the lit end, a little, like a head turning, and then it settled.

*Six. Seven.*

It did not lean east. It did not lean anywhere that she could have pointed to. It sank. The top of it went down, softly, as a cake goes down when the oven door is slammed, and the whole heap sat lower and broader on the cloth than it had fallen.

It was leaning straight down.

*Nine. Ten.* That was his eleven, nearly. He had never gone past eleven.

He did not call the corners. He knelt there with his hands open and his square face perfectly still, and she understood that he was looking at a thing which he had not seen before in six-and-twenty years.

*Twelve.*

Something moved at the bottom of the cloth.

It was not a corner. A corner came up proud in a loose fold, a hand high, where one man had hauled before his neighbor. This was a line. It ran straight across the canvas from one side to the other, below the heap, as true as a rule, and it was rising. The cloth humped along it like a carpet with a draught beneath.

It rose from both its ends at once.

She had seen that. She had sat on a mangle bench with a ticket on her knee and written it down. *Takes it by its two corners, arms wide. Folds from the far edge. Always has the last of it.*

And under it, in a cross woman's own words: *You never fold with a thing lying on the far side. It all comes to you.*

It was not a hem. It was a sheet, and it was being folded, and there were sixty drams of open ash lying in the middle of it.

She had her mouth open.

*A corner can only listen to one.* He had said that in a van. He had found a proud corner with his kneecap before she could get two words out.

She shut her teeth. She looked at his knee.

It was on the top hem, at the head of the cloth. The line was three paces below it, under the middle of the canvas, with a heap between. His eyes were on the heap. Down at the bottom corners, Purvis and Hobday had felt their cloth come slack toward them of its own accord, and each had taken in a careful hand of it, pleased.

Nobody had it.

That was a count. She had spent a whole count finding out that nobody had it.

"Pike," said Kiva. "Far edge."

It was not loud. On that wharf, in the dark, after *one,* it carried like a dropped plate.

His head came up.

He saw it. She watched him see it. The line was a pace nearer than it had been, and a hand higher, and the bottom half of his cloth was coming up the wharf toward him like the lid of a box.

"*Leave go!*" It was the first time that she had heard him shout. "*Purvis! Hobday! Off the cloth!*"

They let go. They were nine years on his hem, and they went back off the canvas as one man.

He had time.

She knew that afterward as she knew her own name. He was on one knee at the top of the cloth, with nothing in his hands but a cord. He could have stood up and stepped back a yard, and been a man on a wharf with a lost rung.

The heap began to slide.

It came toward him. The far edge was lifting it, and the whole of that broad low heap ran up the canvas ahead of the fold, as flour runs on a tilted board, toward the top hem and the four thick legs that stood behind it.

"*Neck!*" said Pike. "*To me! Finch, the cord!*"

He reached.

He went forward off his knee with both arms out, and he took the top hem in his two hands and bunched it, to make a mouth of it, between the heap and the string's feet. It was what he had said that he would do. *Get the neck between the heap and whatever it was.* He did it faster than she had ever seen a man move on his knees.

And Pru, behind him, had a foot on the canvas.

She had started at her name. The cord was in her fist. A catcher called the cord and the cord came; that was a month in a van, on his knee, thirty times in thirty-four.

She stopped.

"It's a fold," said Pru. Her voice went up into a squeak and she did not care. "Pike, it's a *fold,* it's from the far edge. Come out. *Come out of it!*"

The far edge came home.

It came over the heap, and over the canvas, and it was laid on the near edge corner to corner, exactly as a laundress lays one. There was no gap in it anywhere. There was a white fold a pace high, with a cloth inside it, and sixty drams of ash inside the cloth, and at the top of it a man's two arms going in to the wrist.

Pike did not cry out. He made a short hard sound, like somebody lifting.

Then the fold drew.

It went back toward the string's legs, all of a piece, as a hand goes back with something in it. It took the canvas a yard along the cobbles. It took him with it, on his knees.

Kiva had him by the collar.

She had not thought about it. It was a thing that could be done with one good hand and no door. She got her right fist into the back of his coat and both her knees into the cobbles and hauled, as she had hauled on a grey jersey over a boy's head, with her whole weight going backward.

For a count nothing gave. She had a square heavy man by the scruff, and something on the far side of him had his hands.

It let go.

It let go of everything that was not ash. The purse sheet slid out of it, limp, and lay in a heap. Pike came out. He came back against her so hard that they both went down on the cobbles, and she had his shoulders across her knees.

She saw the fold open. It unrolled down the wharf, quite gently, and lay flat again, three paces by two.

There was nothing lying on it.

Above it the third knot from the top had grown. It stood out from the others like a cask among kegs.

"It's kept it," said Purvis, in the dark. He sounded as though he had been struck. "It's eaten its own rung."

Pike was looking at his hands.

He held them up in front of his face, and she saw them over his shoulder, against the last cart's width of the gate. They were white. They were white, and hard, with rime on the knuckles, to a hand's breadth above each wrist. The cord was still in the right one. He could not open the fingers to drop it.

"That's not hem," he said. His voice was quite steady, and very far back in his throat. "Sorn. That's skin. It's put its skin down for a cloth."

---

Rhea Sorn had heard two words.

She had been standing a pace from the thing's flank, in the black, with her blade gone dark on her arm and her left heel flat on the cobbles. She had cut a rung. She had gone dark on a catcher's word, as she had gone dark some thousands of times, and she had been waiting for the string to turn its head and walk to Stroud.

It had not walked. That was the first thing.

Then a girl's voice had come up from the hem.

*Pike. Far edge.*

She knew the voice. She had written a card for it, with the ends put on deliberately. *She sets nothing. She calls nothing.* She had stood on the cinders of a pit and told that girl that she would kneel at a hem and see every single thing that she would do differently, and was not to open her mouth until the bag was sealed.

It was not sealed. There were sixty drams lying open on a cloth.

What went through her then was cold, and quick, and she did not like to look at it afterward. *I knew she'd not keep it.* A van could not have two people calling at one hem. She had seen a corner-hand lose three fingers on the Ferren flats to a second voice, in her first year as an officer, and she had written the rule that night.

Then Pike shouted, and she had never heard him shout.

It was over in perhaps six counts. She did not see it. She was on the wrong side of four knots as big as barrels. She heard a first-year scream *come out,* and she heard a sound like a drawer shut on a tablecloth, and something heavy went over on the cobbles.

"Wall," said Rhea. "All of it. Up."

It came up silver from end to end, and the wharf had edges again.

*What's there?*

A string of four, with three knots where four had been and the middle one fat. A patch of white at its heels, lying as flat as it had lain when a catcher put his boot on it. A purse sheet, in a heap. Two corner-hands on their feet with nothing in their fists. And her catcher on his back across a girl's knees, holding up his hands.

She went round. She did not run. She had not run on a floor in twenty-three years, and it had never yet been the night to begin.

Ruddock was there already with the towel. He had one of Pike's wrists in it.

"Taken," he said, without looking up. "Both. To a hand above the wrist. It's Stroud over again, and worse; she let go."

"I'd a rung in them," said Pike.

"You've a cord in one still. I can't get it out. I'll not try here."

*What's behind me?*

She turned round, as she always did. There was a pair of iron gates, and beyond them the Hythe: three hundred people standing in dark kitchens over their cellars, with every box pulled. There was a tear on the river stair that would go on giving for as long as it stood. And there was a thing on her wharf that took what was cut off it and put it back.

That was the sum. Every rung that she cut, it would fold. It would stand there and grow.

The book had a page for a catcher down. *The officer calls off. The wall is held. A second catcher is sent for.* Imrie's van was at the tram sheds, forty minutes away, with a catcher on it who would kneel at the head of a cloth on a white patch and do exactly what Pike had done.

She was still angry. She found that it had nowhere to go.

"Say it again," she said to Pike. "What you said."

"It's skin. Not hem. A hem hangs off a cord and it's only cold." He was speaking through his teeth, carefully, like a man walking on ice. "This is ward. It's the same stuff that it wears. It's taken it off and laid it down behind it, and when a rung's on it, it folds it home. From the far edge. Like a catcher with a ward."

"From the far edge."

"That's what she said. I had my eyes on the heap. It leaned straight down, and I'd never seen that, and I was a fool about it for five counts. I never looked past it."

Rhea looked at the girl.

She was sitting on the cobbles with a chartered catcher's head against her knee. She had her right hand shut in his collar still. She was as white as he was, and her mouth was closed so tightly that it had no color in it at all.

She had broken a card. She was keeping it now, to the letter, with a man's weight across her legs.

Rhea had been an officer for eleven years. She had named the price before she cut exactly once, on a hill, for this same child, and had thought herself very much improved by it. She saw that she had named the wrong price tonight. She had read out *holds her tongue* as a thing that cost nothing.

"Rowan," she said. "I wrote that you'd say nothing until the bag was sealed. There's no bag. I'm lifting it. That's on my slate and not on your card." She did not soften her voice. "You saw it before he did. Say the rest."

The girl swallowed.

"It isn't a method. It's a laundry."

"I didn't ask what it was."

"A sheet's folded from the far edge. You take the two far corners and bring them to you, and you never let go, and anything that's lying on it comes home inside." It came out fast and flat, like a tally read aloud. "There are two things that I wrote down. If there's a thing on the far side, it all comes to you. And a sheet with a foot on its far hem doesn't fold for anybody."

"A foot."

"A girl stood on one. By accident. Mrs. Pryce couldn't shift it."

Rhea looked at the white patch. Pike had stood on it, with his whole weight, at the near end. It had not minded. He had not stood on the far edge. Nobody had; it was three paces down the fall of the wharf, toward the river.

"Stroud," she said, without turning. "If I put a ward on that, what happens?"

"It goes up me to the elbows," said Greta Stroud, from the wall.

"Iron?"

"A fitter tried iron on a cord at the Halden yard. It frosted to the handle and was taken."

"Then I want something dead. Something heavy, with nothing in it that a ward can get hold of, and a great deal of it." She had her eyes on the west side of the wharf, where the tarpaulins were. "What's coal?"

Nobody answered. Nobody had ever asked.

"It's a hundredweight to the sack," said a man's voice at the iron gates. It was the wharfinger, in a nightshirt and an overcoat, with a warden's bell in his fist. "It's dug out of the ground. I've four hundred of them, and they're counted."

"I'll want a dozen, and I'll sign for them. Nairn. Purvis. Hobday. There's a barrow under that crane. You'll bring them round by the river side. You'll not set a boot on that white. You'll sling them onto its far edge, from below, in a row, as close as bricks."

"And if it takes the sack?" said Nairn.

"Then we've learned what coal is, and I've lost a hundredweight of somebody's fuel."

They went. She heard the barrow's wheel on the cobbles.

It took four minutes. She stood behind the thing's legs with her blade arm down and watched three men swing sacks. The first landed on the far edge of the white with a thump that she felt in her boots.

Frost ran over it. It went white from the bottom to the neck, like a loaf dusted with flour, and creaked.

It stayed where it was.

"That's one," said Hobday. He sounded surprised to be alive.

They laid eleven. They laid them shoulder to shoulder across two paces of cobble, and each went white and creaked and stayed. When it was done there was a low wall of rimed sacks along the river side of the patch, and the string stood at the other end of it with its head on Mace's gate.

"Finch," said Rhea.

The first-year was at Pike's side, on her knees. She had her hands in her sleeves.

"You've a sheet on that van with your own name on the pole. You'll throw it where he threw his. Over the white, sacks and all." She saw the round face go rigid. "It's not on your card. Your card says the cord. So you may say no, and I'll hold that thing on a gate until Imrie comes, and think none the worse."

"Who calls *now?*"

"I do," said Pike.

He had got himself up onto his knees. Ruddock had both his hands in the towel, bound, like a man in a muff.

"I've a voice. It was never in my hands. I'll kneel where I kneel and she'll tie where I tell her." He turned his head, slowly, on his thick neck. "And I want my second where she was."

"She'll be where she was," said Rhea. "Rowan. The left hem. You've one thing, and I'm giving it to you by name, aloud, so that there's no doubt whose it is. The far edge. If a sack shifts, or that white so much as lifts a corner, you say so. Two words. Nothing else on this wharf is yours to say."

"The far edge," said the girl. "Mine."

"Then say it back to your catcher. She's the one who'll have her hands in it."

"Pru. The far edge is mine. I'll say if it moves."

"Yours," said Finch. Her voice shook, and held.

Rhea walked back round the string. She set her left heel down flat, where a square grey man could see it, and for the first time in eleven years she knew that he was looking.

---

The second cloth was stiffer than the first, and it smelled of wax.

Pru threw it twice. The second time it went down over the white patch and the eleven sacks together, and lay lumpy along its lower edge like a tablecloth over the bread.

Kiva knelt on the east hem. She put her right hand on it. She put her left beside it, and then she turned her head and looked down the fall of the wharf at a row of humps under canvas, and she did not look at anything else.

It was the strangest post that she had ever been given. She could not see the string. She could not see Pru's hands, or Pike's face, or the heel that he was watching. She had her back to all of it. She had eleven sacks of coal.

"*Not yet,*" said Pike. His voice was thick. "*Not yet. Now.*"

The yellow light went over her shoulder and lit the humps.

Sand fell. It was a great deal of sand; it was the fat knot, and it came down like a load off a cart.

"*Dark! One!*" That was Pru.

The canvas stirred.

She felt it under her right hand before she saw it. Something beneath the cloth had tried to rise. It lifted along the row of sacks, the width of a finger, and the sacks creaked, all eleven, like a gate in a wind.

They held.

"Far edge," said Kiva. "Holding."

"Then I'll wait my ten," said Pru, behind her, between her teeth. "Since he does. *Four. Five.* It's sinking. It's going straight down."

"That's what it did," said Pike. "Let it. It can listen all it likes. Call them."

"*Corners!*"

Purvis and Hobday hauled. Kiva felt the hem come in under her right hand. She gave it, an inch at a time, and kept her eyes on the sacks.

The third from the west had begun to lean.

It was only a hand. The frost had split it along one seam, and coal was running out of the split in a slow black trickle, and as it emptied it sat lower.

"Far edge. West end. A sack's going."

"*Nairn!*" said Rhea, somewhere above.

She heard boots, and the barrow. A fresh sack came over the river side and landed against the split one with a grunt from whoever had slung it. It went white. It stayed.

"Holding."

"Thirty-one," said Pru. "Thirty-two. It's too big. I can't get the neck up; it's as heavy as Gerald."

"Then don't lift it," said Pike. "You're not lifting. Purvis, give her your knee under it. Finch. First turn. Where's your thumb?"

"On it."

"Second."

"Round."

"Third. And I want to see that little finger come across before the end goes under, or I'll know why."

There was a pause. Kiva watched a row of sacks.

"*Sealed,*" said Pru. It was not a squeak. "Fifty-eight."

"That's two counts inside it. I've had worse off my own hands." He drew a breath that she could hear. "Put your palm on it."

"It's loud. It's as loud as the one at Stopgate. Red."

"Then write it so. Your name. Under her slate."

They took the last two knots one after the other.

It was not clean. On the third rung two sacks split together, and the white came up between them like a blister, a hand high, before Hobday got a third across the gap; and Kiva said *far edge* four times in thirty counts, in a voice that she did not know for her own. Pru sealed at sixty-one. That was a count outside. Pike said so, and said that it would be weighed as it was.

Nairn cut the fourth. It came down small, and it lay, and it leaned toward the lit end of the gate like a head turning.

"There," said Pike. "That's a heap. I'd forgotten what one looked like."

"*Sealed. Forty-nine.*"

Nothing moved under the canvas after that.

They drew the cloth off. The white patch lay on the cobbles where it had lain all night, with a row of rimed sacks along its foot. Kiva watched it. It went as breath goes off a window. It thinned from the edges, and was grey, and was a film of fine ash on wet stone, no thicker than dust on a shelf.

Purvis swept it into a tin with a hand brush. He held the comb over it himself, since there was nobody else to.

The third reed sang. It was clear, and thin, and it filled the wharf.

The seventh sang under it.

"Ward," said Purvis. "Full. And stepped to the top of the reed." He put the lid on. "There's a dram and a half of ward-trace in that, and I'd not put it on a baby's graze."

Two people in long coats were bent over the river stair with a handcart. After a time one of them said *closed,* as a man says that he has shut a gate.

"It kept its own purse," said Ruddock.

He was binding Pike's arms to the elbow, in the gate's light, on the tailboard of the van.

"That's what I'd call it. I've been at this tiller fourteen years. I've seen strings on end and one laid on its side. I never saw one come with a sheet." He tied off. "A purse string. Since it holds them."

Nobody laughed. But Hobday said it over, under his breath, and after that it was what the thing was called.

---

They carried him up the iron stair at the hall between Nairn and Ruddock. He walked, but he could not take the rail.

Kiva waited in the yard with Pru. It was half past two. The hooded lamp burned over the tub where Rhea Sorn had once sat with her elbows on her knees.

"I'd have had my hands in it," said Pru.

She had not spoken since the wharf.

"He called the cord. I'd my foot on the cloth. I was a pace behind him, and I'd have gone in under his arms with it, as I've done forty times." She held her hands up to the lamp and looked at them, front and back. "I'd have ten fingers like his."

"You stopped."

She turned it over.

"*Remarks: two. The book calls a lying hem ground. That remark is the book's.*" Her voice did not change. "*And the second spoke before a bag was sealed. Two words. They were true. They were a count late, and the count was mine; I had told her to hold her tongue. That remark is the officer's.*"

She hung it on her belt.

"The card says that a second calls nothing," said Rhea. "You called nothing. You said what was there. I've been an hour on that stair deciding whether there's a difference, and I've not finished." She put her hands in her pockets. "Go home. Both of you. Come on Sunday, when he's had a night."

"Is the card—"

"The card's got four names on the back of it. I don't tear a thing that a team's signed. I said that I'd not finished. Go home."

It was past three when Rhea went up to the mending room.

The stove was banked. He was in the chair beside it, with his arms laid along a pillow on his knees, bound white to the elbow. Greta Stroud sat on a box at his side with a cup, and she held it to his mouth as a thing that she had been waiting some months to be asked for.

"A spoon held," said Pike. "She's reminded me twice."

"I've not said a word," said Stroud.

"You've a way of holding a cup."

Rhea stood by the stove. There was a sheaf of ruled paper on the bench under the window, with a laundry ticket on top. She had read it on Wednesday, when he left it on his knee and went to see about a hem. He had meant her to.

"Did you hear her?" she said.

"I heard her."

"Before you saw it, or after?"

"Before. I've told you. I was looking at the heap." He moved his head against the chair back. "It's a thing that I do. I didn't know that I did it until a fortnight ago. I watch what a heap's listening to, and that one was listening straight down, into my own cloth, and I'd nothing in six-and-twenty years to set beside it. I'd have gone on looking until it had me by the knees."

"Did it help? Her speaking."

"It gave me a count. I looked up, and there it was coming, and I'd time to get my corners off. They've their hands." He was silent a moment. "I'd time to come out."

"And you reached."

"I reached." It was said quite flatly. "There were sixty drams of mine running to a thing on a wharf. A catcher doesn't leave a rung. I've never put my hands inside a cloth in my life; I work a neck from the outside, with a knee and a cord, and I'd have told a corner so if I'd known that I knew it. And the one night that the book ran out, I went in to the wrists." His mouth moved. "Don't put that on her."

"I've put it on me. It's on the slate."

"I read the slate. Stroud held it." He looked at the stove. "It was the right rule on the ninth. I'd say so to anybody. She'd have told me that Hobday was proud, and I had him already, and there'd have been two of us calling one corner."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight the far edge was three paces off, under my own canvas, and it was nobody's. My knee doesn't reach three paces. My corners had their cloth coming in sweet and thought well of themselves. There wasn't a soul on my floor looking at that edge but a girl with nothing else to do." He nearly smiled. It went wrong halfway. "That's what a second's for, it seems. I asked for somebody to tell me what I do. I never thought to ask for somebody to look where I don't."

Rhea turned that over.

*What's behind me?* It was her own oldest question. She had asked it on every floor for twenty-three years. She turned round, each time, and looked at what her back was to.

Nobody had looked behind the cloth.

She had a column for *before.* She had ruled it in this room, with a sponsor sitting on that bench saying that she had not climbed two flights for a yes. She read it aloud each night now, to everybody, since a girl of seventeen had made it a condition. And tonight she had read out a line in it that had cost a man his hands for six weeks, and she had read it as one reads the date.

She took the slate off her belt and turned it to the stove.

She was tempted. She looked at that, since Pike was watching her and would have seen it anyway. She could tear the card. It had *either of us* on it. She could say that a coal wharf was no place for a child with eight fingers, and send her back to a yard, and call it care.

She had burned a card once that said *mine.* This would be the same card, only kinder.

"I'll not change what's signed," she said. "She's a second. She sets nothing and calls nothing. That stands."

"And the slate?"

Rhea took the chalk. She wrote at the foot of the narrow column on the left, under the word that she had put there in the autumn, where it would be read out before every cut.

*The far edge: the second's. To say. Two words, once.*

"That's a standing line," she said. "It's not on her card. It's on my floor. Whoever kneels at your left hem has it, and says so before the cloth's thrown, and the catcher says it back."

"I've no left hem. Not for six weeks."

"You've four nights on a card. Two next month and two the month after. *Second to Pike.* I'll not have a signed card made a liar of by a pair of hands." She hung the slate up on its nail. "Imrie lends me Varley for the cloth. You'll ride. You'll kneel where you kneel, in linen, and you'll have the say. She's your second and nobody else's."

"A voice at a hem."

"You said yourself that it was never in your hands. I heard you tell a first-year so, in front of a string."

Pike looked at his arms.

"She's written the thumb," he said. "In a column. With my own word under it."

"I read it. It was on your knee for an hour."

"You never said."

"You've told me for two winters that it was nothing. I've been watching you tie left-handed since the autumn before last." Rhea put her hands in her pockets. "I thought that if you wanted it known, you'd find somebody to write it down."

Stroud laughed, once, into the cup.

Rhea went down the stair. It was nearly four. There was a slate on a nail behind her with a new line on it, and she had not the least idea whether it was a wise one. She only knew what it had cost to find out that it was missing.

# Chapter 8 — Kiva: Under a Captain

Ruddock had cut the cord out of his fist on Saturday, a thread at a time, with a pair of nail scissors.

It lay on the bench under the window on Sunday afternoon, in eleven short pieces, beside nine ruled sheets and a laundry ticket. Pike had asked for it to be kept.

"I waxed that in the tenth month," he said. "I'd like to know that it finished as a thing with ends."

He was in the chair by the stove. His arms lay along a pillow, bound white to the elbow, and he held them a little away from himself, as one holds a pair of boots that belong to somebody else. Greta Stroud had gone to her dinner. Pru sat on the box in her place, with the cup, and was not allowed to say that she minded.

"You've your paper," said Pike.

"Yes."

"Then you'll write. I can't, and I've a thing or two that I'd sooner were in my words than in Sorn's." He moved his head against the chair. "You found the one that you'd missed."

Kiva had found it on the wharf, at about the fourteenth count. She had not known that she had until she lay awake afterward.

"Your hands never go inside the cloth."

"Never."

"Not on any of the three at the kiln. You work the neck with your knee and the cord. You touch the outside of it, to hear whether it's loud. You don't put a finger past the hem."

"Not in six-and-twenty years. I couldn't have told you so on Tuesday week. I'd have said that I caught as anybody catches." He looked at the stove. "A cloth's got open ash in it. Whatever's in there with the ash takes what the ash takes. My first master had three fingers on his left hand, and he never told me how he came to be short. He only never let me reach."

"Shall I write that?"

"Write that the catcher's hands stay outside. Under *why,* put that anything inside a cloth with open ash belongs to whatever the ash is listening to." He waited while she did it. "Now the next column. *Went wrong.*"

She had the pencil on the line. She did not want to write it.

"He reached," said Pike. "Three words. I'll spell them if you like."

"I was a count late."

"So you've said. So Sorn's said, on a slate, and taken it for hers." He was not gentle about it. He had never been gentle, and he did not begin now. "I'll tell you what a count's worth. I looked up at the fourteenth and it was a pace nearer. If you'd spoken at the thirteenth, I'd have looked up and it would have been a pace farther off. And I'd have had my corners clear, and thought, there's my rung running to a string, and gone in after it just the same."

"You don't know that."

"I know what I did with the time that I had. I'd no more of an answer at thirteen than at fourteen. The book had run out." He drew a breath. "You spent a count keeping my rule. I'd have spent it. Don't make a wound of it. I've two already, and I'll not be put to the trouble of minding a third."

Pru made a small sound into the cup.

Kiva wrote it. *Went wrong: he reached.*

It was the plainest line on the sheet. It looked like a meter reading.

"And *why,*" said Pike. "That's mine, and I've not got it. I've been at it since four yesterday morning. *A catcher doesn't leave a rung.* That's what I'd have told a corner." He shook his head. "It's not a why. It's what I did. Put that I don't know. Put that the book stopped, and that I went on without it as though it hadn't."

She put that.

"Now a fresh sheet."

She ruled one. It was the last of the Works paper that her aunt had given her in the autumn.

"What's it called?"

"Ruddock's named it. A purse string."

"Then that's the first column. *A purse string. At Meridian, on the coal wharf below the Hythe.*" He shut his eyes. "*What.* A string on end that lays its skin on the ground behind it, for a cloth. It is not hem. It lies under a rung, and it folds the rung home from the far edge. Have you that?"

"Yes."

"*How.* That's how it's taken, and it's not been taken properly yet; we did it once, badly, with coal. Dead weight along the far edge before a cloth goes near it. Not a ward. Not iron. As much as will lie shoulder to shoulder. Then the cloth, over all. Then somebody at the left hem who has the far edge and nothing else, and says so before the cut, and has it said back."

"That's on the Torch's slate."

"I know where it is. I was looking at it for an hour last night; she's hung it where I can't help." He opened his eyes. "*Cost.* A catcher's hands, for six weeks, if Ruddock can count. Thirteen sacks. And a rung and a quarter that was sealed outside its sixty and will weigh short."

"*Second try.*"

"The weight. And I'll have it written whose that was. I'll not have it go about the Hythe that Graywater thought of coal."

"It was the Torch who thought of coal."

"It was a laundress who thought of a foot on a hem. Sorn only looked round a wharf for the heaviest foot that she could find." He nearly smiled. "Put her name."

So it went into the sixth column of a chartered company's craft, in a catcher's second's square hand. *Mrs. Pryce, laundress, Coper Street: a sheet with a foot on its far hem does not fold for anybody.*

"She'll want it struck out," said Kiva. "She says that it's only folding."

"Then she and I shall get on."

There was one square left, at the right.

"I can't fill that," said Pike. "Why it lays a skin down. Why a thing with nobody in it should do what Finch does with a ward. I've no more notion than that stove has."

"Nor have I."

"Then leave it white. I've seen a deal of old lane sheets in my time, and it's the ones with every square filled that I never believed."

She left it white.

She squared the ten sheets on her knee. They were not hers. They were a man's whole trade, as far as she had been able to see it from a hem; and the last of them had been made up on a wharf, three nights ago, by a Torch and a laundress and a boy with a barrow. It was the first thing that she had ever written down from beginning to end that had nothing whatever to do with her own hands.

She found that she did not miss them in it.

"February," said Pike. "Two nights. Imrie's lending Varley for the cloth; he's a decent pair of hands and he's never been made to say why. I ride. "Once."

"Once. I read the slate." He shifted his arms on the pillow, and his face went grey, and came back. "Finch. That cup's been empty for ten minutes. You're holding it at me like a collection plate."

"I wasn't going to say."

"No. That's why I'm telling you."

"You thought of it. I didn't. It was my own notion, off a litter, and I'd not seen that it would go."

He looked at her helplessly.

"And you'll have to say every role and every change," said Pru. She had gone pink. "Aloud. To a marshal. Rowan's one door, before each board. You'd be the one who says it for her. I'd sooner it was somebody who'd mind getting it wrong."

That finished him. Kiva saw it go home.

"I'd like it known," said Perrin, with great bitterness, "that I didn't want it. I'd like it written on the entry."

"One can't write that on an entry."

"Then in the book."

Anwen wrote it. She read it out as she did so, since there was nobody at that table who could have borne her not to.

"*Captain, for the National entry: Cade, P., carry. Chosen by the four, the reserve and the Kill. He minds. Entered at his own request.*"

"That's handsome," said Perrin. "I've a place in my Hall's second team, and a figure in a stone, and now I've a hat." He put his head in his hands. "I got everything that I wanted in the autumn. I'd no notion that it would be so much work."

Jude had come away from the dresser.

He did not sit. There was no chair. He stood at the end of the table with his long forearms folded, and he looked at Kiva.

"I asked a thing in the tenth month," he said. "On a cold floor. You said that if you'd no better answer by the time there was an entry to sign, I was to ask again. At the table."

"I did."

"There's an entry. What are we taking you for?"

She had known that he would. She had kept the place for him.

It was a strange thing to have ready, since it was so small. In the tenth month she had put away an answer about three doors, on a hill, at a price. She still had it. It was still true. It was also not what she had been for, on any day since.

"I'm a catcher with one door," said Kiva. "Declared. That's what goes on the sheet, and it's a rank with a clause in it, and I'll be read from a mast. I can plant a corner. I can put a ward under a thing for a tap. I can't do both, and I'll say which before every board."

"That's what you are. I asked what for."

"I look where the others aren't looking. And I say what it is, and whose." She found that she could say it without her mouth going dry. "A thumb. A place over a wall where nobody had the litter. A mark that wanted holding before it was tested. The far edge of a cloth. I don't mend any of them. I can't. I've a hand that doesn't know a thread from two. I only say it so that somebody else can."

"It's not much."

"No. There's a slate at Graywater with a line on it. That's all it comes to."

Jude scratched his jaw.

"It's four things from where there was nothing to do," he said. "I've been counting. And there's a first-year with ten fingers." He nodded, once, as a man nods who has been paid. "I'll tell a clerk that. I'd not have known what to tell him in the tenth month."

"I want it said," said Anwen, "that that's an answer."

She did not write it. She only shut the book.

"You've all been a great while," said Mara, from the dresser, without turning round. "There's bread. Somebody will have to cut it. I've a reading to finish, and my niece chases a loaf."

---

The ash office weighed fifteen drams out of Team Seven's press on Friday, at four, and it took the clerk a quarter of an hour.

He did it in five lots, a head at a time, into five sealed tins with the Charter Board's label. He held the Circuit's comb over each. The seventh reed stirred, and touched its neighbor, and came away.

"Trace," he said, five times. "Clean. You'd be astonished what I've had across this counter for a warrant."

"Nobody's astonished any longer," said Perrin. "There's a man at the Works who's given it up."

He was standing very upright, in the grey jersey. He had been a captain since Monday. He had asked the clerk what he was to do, and had been told that he was to sign, and had seemed to feel that there ought to have been more.

The entry lay on the counter. It was a printed sheet with a seal at the head, and somebody had written it out fair.

*T7, of the Meridian Collegiate Circuit. Captain: Cade, P., carry. Catcher: Rowan, K. Hold: Pryce, A. Kill: Talley, J. Reserve: Finch, P., catcher.*

They signed under it. Kiva signed second, with her right hand, and Pru put a finger on the corner of the sheet for her without being asked.

"*Friday the 29th,*" said Kellan.

He had the team book. He had asked for it at the foot of the stairs. *I'm on it for calls,* he had said. *I can still add.*

"*Warrant, the National Measure, at Saltmere. Five heads at 3.0: Rowan, Pryce, Cade, Talley, and Finch, reserve. Paid with the entry: 15.0. Held: 9.64. Sealed, medical, W: 0.8.*" He did not look up. "*Spoken for and not yet spent: the Hunt Marshal's floor, four heads, 4.0. Hand to Ember, six goes, if the catcher's sponsor has her way, 1.8. Uncommitted: 3.84.*"

"That adds," said Anwen.

"It always adds." He signed it, under four names and a new fifth. He blotted his own. "It'll be somebody else's to read from tomorrow. I'd give it to Talley. He counts everything and trusts none of it."

"I'd sooner have had the something over," said Jude. But he took the book.

There was a second sheet pinned to the board outside, beside theirs. It was in black and gold at the head.

*Halden License. Captain: Pettit, carry. Catcher: Yarrow. Hold: Hesketh. Blade: Renn, K.*

Kiva read it once. It was a very ordinary line. It had a name in it that she had been used to seeing somewhere else.

"The Chair's countersigned yours," said Noa.

He had come down the passage with a paste pot. He put up a third sheet, under the two, in a clerk's fair copy of a level upright hand.

*Entered by the Chair. Rowan, K., catcher, T7. Her conditions for 2681, as for 2680, re-entered line by line.*

*One door declared to the referee or the marshal before every board, and any change declared; at Saltmere, by her captain's voice. Her book to her sponsor weekly. She is not at the Bench. She is read from a sanctioned mast by a reader of this office. One crossing is certified, Ember to still; a stack so read is an incident. The longer thing is not to be asked of her.*

*One incident at a sanctioned board ends her season. I have had that line in front of me since the autumn. I have left it in. Y. Crest.*

"She thought about it," said Perrin.

"She says so. She doesn't say which way."

"*By her captain's voice.*" He had read it twice. "So if I say *Ember* and you've declared the still, that's an incident. That's your whole year. On my tongue."

"Yes."

"I'd like it known that I've stopped enjoying the hat."

Somebody was coming along the passage on two sticks. She came slowly. She had climbed the basement stairs by herself, since Kiva could hear Wilf Marten three steps behind her, not helping, by instruction.

Selka Dray read the three sheets from the top.

"Cade," she said.

"Yes."

"He'll call *off* early."

"He'll call it when it's wanted," said Kiva. "He kept the word for me on a hill. I'd not have anybody else."

"I didn't say that it was a fault. I said early." The grey eyes went to the second sheet, and stayed there a moment. "And Renn's gone to the Works. I'd have wagered on that in the ninth month."

"He told us himself. At a table."

"Did he. That's more than I'd have looked for." Her jaw moved. "Twenty-Eight's is Marten. They'd not have a captain who couldn't walk to the referee. I came up those stairs to argue it, and I shall, and I shall lose."

She went on toward the Chair's stair. It took her some time.

"Tomorrow's the thirtieth," said Kellan. He had his pencil out again; he could not help it. "The gate's the first of the third month, so we're on the road before the second month's out. That's thirty days at the outside. And the Bench is the fourteenth. Forty-four."

"Forty-four days," said Pru.

"To the first board. On three drams and eighty-four hundredths, with a captain who minds and a catcher on one door."

"And a Kill who comes down heavy," said Jude.

"And a sheet," said Anwen. "I've asked my mother. She said that she'd never heard such nonsense, and then she said that we might have it on Saturday if it came back clean."

Kellan put the pencil away. He stood for a moment in front of the board, with one name on each of two sheets.

"I'd expect nothing else," he said. He did not say of whom.

He went up the stairs. He did not take them two at a time.

---

Perrin Cade called his first floor for three on Saturday, in the Old Yard, and he opened it by apologizing.

"I've asked nobody," he said. "I'm told that's what the hat's for. If it's a stupid floor, it's mine, and I'd like that understood before anybody's cold."

It was the stupidest floor that Kiva had ever seen laid.

There was a double sheet spread on the packed dirt, between the two rows of black posts. It was grey with washing, and it had a seam down its middle where it had been turned. Mrs. Pryce had sent it up on the tram in a basket. She had also sent Dilys.

"I'm the far edge," said Dilys. She was thirteen, and she had never been inside a school of the Circuit in her life, and she looked at the posts as one looks at the masts of a ship. "Me and her. We're to take the corners and bring them home."

"As fast as you can walk," said Anwen, at the other corner. "Faster. You're a thing with nobody in it."

Gerald hung from a pole across two trestles, in his hat.

"It's a rung to a bag," said Perrin. "On a skin. Finch throws on it. Talley lets go the rung. And those two fold it, from down there, as soon as they like and without telling anybody. If there's a hand inside the cloth when the far edge comes home, that hand's lost, and I say *off,* and we've all been killed by a laundry."

"And me?" said Kiva.

"You've the left hem. You've one thing. I'm told that there's a line on a slate."

There were four sandbags by the south row. Jude had carried them down from the Low Lane, two under each arm. They were to be coal.

She knelt on the east side of Pru's cloth, a pace and a half down from the corner.

It was a clear cold afternoon. The bar hung on its two hooks on the bottom wall. Orla was on her crate, a pace north of where she had sat for one-and-twenty years, with her stick between her boots, and she had said nothing whatever since they laid the sheet. She had only looked at it as though it were the first new thing in that yard since a lid was dug up.

There was somebody on the shed step.

He had come by the side gate, with Greta Stroud to open it. He sat with his arms laid across his knees, bound white to the elbow, and his square grey face was turned to the cloth.

"I'm not here," said Pike. "I've no card for this yard. I was passing."

"You were passing a school on a hill," said Orla. "On a Saturday. In a tram that doesn't come within half a mile."

"I'd a notion to see a sheet folded."

"Then you may have my crate. It's got a back to it, and you've nothing to hold on by."

He looked at her. He got up, with care, and crossed the lane, and she moved her stick to make room. They sat side by side on a crate that was a pace out of its place: a sponsor who had once had a boy hurt, and a catcher with his hands in linen.

"Pru," said Kiva. "The far edge is mine. I'll say if it moves."

"Yours."

"*Go,*" said Perrin.

Jude let go the rung. A hundredweight of sand came down in the upper middle of the cloth with a sound like a dropped mattress.

"*Dark! One!*"

Kiva did not look at it.

She had her right hand on the hem. She had turned her head, and she was looking down the lane at the bottom of a grey sheet, where a laundry girl and a ward-holder stood with a corner each. They were grinning at one another. Dilys had her tongue out.

It was absurd. A carter would have laughed. There was a sandbag in a hat, and a bedsheet, and a boy in a jersey with a turnip watch in his fist.

It was also the only place in Meridian, that afternoon, where five people were kneeling on a cloth with somebody set to watch the edge of it.

"*Four,*" said Pru. "*Five.* I'm waiting my ten. He does."

"Who told you that I waited ten?" said a thick voice from the crate.

"It's in a column. She read it to me."

The far corners lifted. They came up together, an arm's width apart, as true as a rule.

"Far edge," said Kiva.

"*Off the cloth!*" That was Perrin. He did not say *off;* he had been told that the word was for a withdrawal, and he was keeping it clean. "*Hands out! Talley, the weight!*"

Jude slung a sandbag. It landed on the bottom hem with a thump, and Dilys, hauling, found that she had a sheet with a foot on it and sat down hard in the dirt.

"It doesn't fold," she said, very much pleased. "I told you. I did that in the ironing room and got shouted at."

"Again," said Perrin. "The weight was late. It was three counts after she said. I want it before she's finished saying."

"I can't sling before I'm told."

"Then be standing where you can."

They did it again. They did it eleven times before the light went, and on four of them somebody would have lost a hand.

Kiva said two words each time. She did not say anything else. It was all that she had, on that floor, and for once it did not feel like less than the others. Pru had a cord and a second pin. Anwen had the corner of a sheet, and was learning what a wall looked like from the far side. Jude had the weight. Perrin had a watch, and a word that he was saving, and a look of acute suffering.

Between the seventh and the eighth she sat back on her heels to rest her knee.

There was a sheet of paper in her coat. It was not Works paper; that was gone. It was the back of a laundry order, ruled in seven with a pencil, and she had headed the first column on the tram.

*A purse string. Drill for a four, with a sheet. At Meridian, in the yard under the south wall.*

She had put *what,* and *how.* She had put *went wrong:* the weight three counts late, since the Kill waited to be told. She looked at the last column.

"Mr. Pike."

"I'm not here."

"Why does the weight go on before the cloth? On the wharf we threw the cloth over the sacks. Perrin's had Jude sling after."

There was a pause. On the crate, a square grey man shut his eyes.

"Since it folds when there's something on it worth folding," he said slowly. "And not before. It lay there under my heel and never stirred. If your weight's down before your rung is, it's got a foot on its hem before it's a mind to move. If you wait until it's lifting, you're racing it." He opened his eyes. "I don't know that. I've seen the thing once. Put that it's what I'd try."

She wrote it in his words. *What P. would try. Not known.*

It was a little thing to be doing on the last day of a month, with dusk coming and a frost behind it. She was putting down somebody else's craft in a column, for four people and a reserve to use, off a method that nobody had yet got right. It had a laundress's name in it. It had a square left white.

Perrin had chalked a figure on the shed door. He had done it before anybody came, and had said nothing about it. It was a foot high.

*44.*

"Tomorrow that's forty-three," said Pru.

"I know what it is tomorrow. I'm the one with the chalk."

Beyond the bottom wall the long hill went down, straight as a rule, with a lamp at every twentieth pace, to the Brickfields road and the river. There was a boat on that river every Monday. There was a road that went round the Sag, and doubled at a ferry.

She had thirty days, at the outside, before she was on it. She had one door at a time, and a card that said one crossing in seven days at the most, and two fingers that were a reason to look. She had a team that was three drams and eighty-four hundredths from having nothing, and a captain who had not wanted the place.

"Rowan," said Perrin. "Your knee's had its minute. I'd like it known that I'm being firm."

She put the paper in her coat. The pocket had a button. She left it for whoever came by.

"Pru. The far edge is mine."

"Yours."

Jude let go the rung.
