## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that

- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,

## Where we enter

## Pressure and what this movement is for

**Three boards in three days, and the third is alive.** The Measure should feel like a
national event, with crowds and press and the Board's flat calls, and also like a place where
people the reader knows are put under load: Perrin on his own board, the Bench without its
catcher, the Hunt on tidal ground where nothing is clean.

- **The Bench, without Kiva.** She watches. What a catcher does when she cannot do the thing

  holds her place on the floor; Kiva's columns and her voice matter from the tiers. Let the
  Bench be a real board with real banks and a planted fault, not a montage, and let it
  connect to the harbour's problem without solving it.
- **The Carry, which is Perrin's.** A full scene with a clock. He has the captaincy and
  something with only his name on it. **Seven wins this board outright**, on his back, and he
  finds that being envied is no better than envying. Do not make it a walkover: the Ropewalk,

## Craft lessons from Movements Two and Three (measured, simulated, same-family)

- Movement Two came out bimodal (short beats plus 60–90-word chains); Movement Three
  over-corrected (forty-word sentences 0.4% against the formula's ~3.3%; median 7). Write
  both: clear 8–25 word narration sentences, short beats for contrast, and in each chapter a
  few well-built 35–55 word sentences with one main clause and clear subordination, readable
  aloud, in descriptive and reflective passages. No *and … and* chains over about 45 words.
- Scenes were too short and too frequent (sections averaging 625 words, 14.5 breaks per 10k
  against ~950 and ~8.7). Do not cut a continuous fight, dinner or board with a section break;
  break where place, time or viewpoint changes. Developed scenes near 900–1,000 words.
- A shared idiom spread to almost every mouth ("I'd want it said", "I'd like it known",
  "said nothing", blushing). Keep each person's voice distinct: Jude diffident ("I'd want it
  said"), Anwen "I want it said", Perrin alone "I'd like it known", Pru scarlet, Aske's price
  speeches, Orla rough and brief, Hart plain and gauge-like, Mara dry, Kellan sums, Coombe
  drills, not speeches. Ration "said nothing", negation runs and causal *since* (use *because*).
- Each figure, rule and tally arrives once. Check every sum, weekday, count of nights, minute
  and score against the calendar before it is spoken; the readers love the exact counting and
  catch the slips. Board totals must add up (a lane that sums to 5–4 is not 6–4).

# Chapter 24 — Kiva: Twelfth of Twenty-Four

"No," said Aske.

It was half past seven on Saturday morning. Kiva was sitting on the edge of her bed by the north window in a dry shift, with the soaked blanket gone down to Quiller's yard to be hung, and her lane boots in her hand. She had put the left one on. The right one was in her hand.

"Not the morning after a hundred and twelve," said Aske. He did not raise his voice. He never did. "Not on a floor eighty feet down a dry dock, against a four that's been waiting a fortnight for you. You'll sit on the tiers in a blanket and watch, and I'll sit beside you, and if you so much as stand up to see better I'll put you back down."

"It's a seeding lane. Seven needs a catcher."

"Seven has a catcher." Aske looked across the bed. "Finch."

Pru was sitting on her own bed with her cord in her lap. She had not slept either. She looked up when Aske said her name, and went scarlet, and did not look away.

"I'm the reserve," she said. "And the second catcher. It's on the entry. *Reserve: Finch, P., catcher.* The Board doesn't care which one of us stands, if the captain declares it." She looked at Perrin. "If the captain will."

Perrin was at the long table with the slate. He had been there since six, drawing pens on it and rubbing them out. He looked at Kiva, and at Aske, and at Pru.

"Rowan," he said. "Is it yours to say?"

Kiva sat with the right boot in her hand.

It was not hers to say. She knew that before he asked it. It was the captain's, and the healer's, and the healer had said it. She had sat in a fever all night with her teeth together, and her hands still shook a little when she held them out. She would not have let Pru stand on a floor with hands that shook. She knew it was not hers to say. She wanted to say it anyway, and she did not.

"No," she said. "It's yours."

"Then Finch catches," said Perrin. "Ward, declared. I'll say it at the gate." He put the slate down. "Finch. You've never stood a lane for us."

"I've stood them in the Old Yard against juniors every Tuesday since the first month. I've watched Rowan stand every one we've had." Pru wound the cord round her fist. "I'm not her. I don't call it as she does. I'll call it as I do." She looked at Kiva, quite straight, for once without going red at all. "I'd like you to watch. And tell me after what I didn't say aloud."

The Graving was full.

Kiva had stood at its landward lip on the first day and looked down eighty feet to a floor with men chalking lines on it. Now she sat on its tiers, two-thirds of the way down, wrapped in a grey blanket with Aske on one side and Orla on the other. Every tier above her and below her and across the dock was full. They were Saltmere green, mostly, and the brown and buff of the Tarn, and Meridian's colours in a knot near the middle. The iron and glass roof went over all of it, so that the light, which came down through the glass grey and even as it comes into a church, fell alike on green and buff and on the white chalk of the pen eighty feet below.

At the seaward end, three hundred paces off, the floating gate held the harbour out. Kiva could hear the sea on the other side of it. It was not loud. It was a long low breathing, coming and going against the timber. Under that, faint, through the stone of the tier she sat on, there was the town's count.

The pen was chalked on the dock floor, twenty paces wide, as at home. The Tarn's first four stood behind their hurdle in brown and buff. Kiva knew their captain. He was the broad slow boy with a face like a gatepost who had put his hand on a plaited girl's shoulder at the gate, eleven days ago, over a stone jar that sang thin. The girl was on the reserve bench now, at the dock's side, with her plaits pinned up.

"Trewin," said Orla. "Their captain. Still door. He's very good. Slow, but he never moves when he doesn't mean to." She did not look at Kiva. "They sequence, the Tarn. As we do. They've got nobody who lays. They'll be a plain fight."

Seven came out onto the floor. Kiva watched them from above, very small. Perrin first, with the slate under his arm, and Anwen and Jude, and Pru at the end with her cord at her belt.

Perrin stood at the marshal's box and declared. His voice came up the dock's stone walls clear and flat, so that the whole Graving heard it.

"*Team Seven. Cade, stance. Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Finch, ward, catcher. Rowan stands down, on her healer's card.*"

A sound went round the tiers. It was not the warm sound. It was a murmur of people asking each other a question.

Kiva sat in her blanket and let them ask it.

She had never watched Seven from above.

She had never watched Seven at all. She had always been in it, on the floor, at the hurdle or behind the litter, with her eyes on the ground and the heels and the far edge. Now she sat eighty feet up and fifty paces off. She saw the whole pen at once, chalk squares and posts and hurdles and eight people, like a game laid out on a table.

It looked quite different.

From the floor she had always seen what was close: a heel, a pole, a square of clean dirt. From here she saw shapes. The Tarn came over their hurdle at the bell in a square, two forward and two back, and the square moved down the pen toward the litter without any of its corners changing place. Seven came over in a line, Perrin forward, Jude and Anwen wide, Pru behind. The line bent round the litter like a hand closing.

The first carry went to the Tarn. Trewin planted at the litter's head before Perrin could get there. Their wall stood on the north side, their blade went south, and their carry walked the Commodore round the end of Anwen's wall as Halden had walked Gerald round the end of hers in the second month. It went over their hurdle two to two. Three. And their blade had touched Jude on the way, because Jude had gone for the pass and not for the blade.

"Four," said Orla, under her breath. "Talley went for the pass."

"He was told to watch the blade," said Kiva.

"Then somebody didn't tell him again." Orla's crooked face did not change. "Finch didn't. Watch her."

Kiva watched her.

Pru stood behind the hurdle with her ward shut and her cord at her belt. She was not watching the heels. That was the first thing Kiva saw. Kiva had always watched heels, because Pike watched heels, and Varley had learned to watch heels off her sheet. Pru was watching hands. She was watching Trewin's hands on the litter's poles, and the Tarn carry's hands. When the second carry began, and the Tarn's square came down the pen again, Pru said something, quite loud, that Kiva could hear even from the tiers.

"*Hands off! Left!*"

And the Tarn's carry took his left hand off the pole to reach for a pass. Anwen was there. She had been told *left,* and she had gone left, and her wall came up between the carry and the hurdle before he had finished reaching.

"*Down,*" said the marshal, far below. It was Jude, touching the carry on the shoulder as he turned.

"She watches hands," said Kiva.

"She's a hand door," said Aske, on her other side. "She folds with her hands. She'd watch hands." He did not take his eyes off the floor. "You're a still door. You watch where people put their feet."

Kiva had not thought of that. Aske meant Pru's one door, the ward, the tray she lifted and folded with, and not a hand like the cold one in Kiva's own blanket. She sat with it.

The second carry went to Seven. They took the litter wide along the north side, as they had learned at the Ropewalk. They made the Tarn's square stretch to cover it, and the square was slow to stretch, because it had never been made to. Perrin tapped once and let his shoulders go, and Anwen took the poles. Pru called *now* to Jude, off the Tarn blade's hand coming down, and Jude was between him and the pass. The Commodore went over Seven's hurdle two to two.

"*Three to Seven,*" said the marshal. "And a down. Four to four."

The tiers made a sound. The Meridian knot in the middle stood up and shouted. Kiva did not stand up. Aske's hand was on her blanket at the knee, quite lightly, and she did not stand.

The third carry was the glass.

The Tarn had read the north side. Kiva saw Trewin read it from eighty feet up, as she had seen Carrow read the north wall, and she saw him say something to his carry in a low voice behind their hurdle. When the bell went, Trewin did not go to the litter. He went to the north side of the pen and planted there, between the posts and the litter, in a clean square of dock floor, and he did not move.

He was a post. He was not laid. He was one still door, planted, and he was the slowest thing in the pen, and nobody could go round him on the north side.

Seven went south.

They had to. The Tarn's wall was on the south side, and their blade, and it was a plain fight in the middle of the pen with the glass running. Pru called hands. Jude touched nobody, because nobody would come near enough. Anwen walked her wall from the far end and it came round, and the Tarn's wall did not come round, and for a long moment it looked as though the litter would go over Seven's hurdle again.

Then the glass ran.

The Commodore was lying on the middle line. Kiva could see it from the tiers, a canvas dummy in a tarred hat lying across the white chalk. The marshal came down off his box and looked. He walked round it twice.

"*The Tarn's half,*" he said. "By the head. A point. Five to the Tarn. Four to Seven."

Kiva let out a long breath.

Below, on the floor, Perrin stood with his hands on his knees. Pru stood behind the hurdle with her cord still at her belt and her face scarlet, and Kiva saw her look up at the tiers, at the place where Kiva sat. She was not looking for comfort. She was asking a question.

Kiva did not wave. She put her right hand up, flat, and held it there for a moment, the way a referee holds a hand up for a call that was right. Then she put it down again.

Pru looked at it. Then she turned round and went to shake hands with the Tarn's catcher.

"I know I did." Pru was winding her cord. She had wound it four times since the floor. "I didn't know I would till I was standing there. I'd meant to watch heels, like you."

"Don't."

Pru looked up.

"You called *hands off, left* in the second carry," said Kiva. "Before their carry had moved. You saw it in his fingers. Pru stood with the cord in her fist and her face going slowly from scarlet to something else.

She went. Kiva watched her go along the dock's edge, small and quick, with her sleeves rolled up, and thought that she had never seen anybody walk so exactly like a catcher.

He said it on Sunday morning, at the long table in the loft, at eight. Kiva had slept from seven on Saturday evening until six on Sunday morning and woken hungry. The rig was the one from the Old Yard. "A week."

Pru folded at ten past ten.

She put her ward under the cord on its slack, low and flat, and tapped it once with her right hand, and folded it from the far edge to a penny. Kiva watched her hands. They were steady.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva, short, with the swallow.

Kiva went down to the pump house at a quarter to seven on Sunday evening, by herself, and stood on the outfall walk at the rail.

It was not a slack. It was the middle of a neap flood, and the brown water was coming in under the sea gate and filling the basin quietly, without hurry. The six outfalls poured into it on the count. There was nothing at the place ten paces out from the grating. There was only brown water, a little rough on the surface where the outfalls stirred it, and under it the mud. Under the mud there was a ring that would stand up again at dawn on the 23rd, at nine minutes past six, by the pump house clock, if two people and a sealed paper were right.

She took out her book. She did not open it to the page with the crossings, or the fever, or the lanes. She turned to the back, to a clean page, and she ruled it with her pencil into four columns, as she had seen them ruled in a small thick book with a cloth cover.

*Date. Hour. What I laid them on. Yes or no.*

She did not write *what.* She wrote *yes or no.*

Then she laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron rail. The hand was cold to the wrist still; the fingers were cold. It did not matter. They did not feel cold in any case. She laid them on the rail at a light touch and did not lean, and she waited.

*It keeps time.*

She wrote it. *Sunday the 13th of the third month. A quarter to seven in the evening. The rail of the outfall walk, Saltmere. Yes.*

That was all: one line, which told her only what she had known since the first night, with two fingers on a bollard, that a thing kept time in a town where everything kept time.

But tomorrow at a quarter to seven she would lay them on something again, wherever she was, and the day after, and the day after that. She would write *yes* or *no,* and never *what.* And after a year, if a man with a ruined hand and a book of slacks was right, the book would know something that the fingers never would.

What it would know, she could not say. That was the point of it.

She shut the book. Behind her, on the gallery, a door opened. She heard a step she knew, slow and uneven, and did not turn round.

"Same hour every day," said Hart, from the gallery. "Never on purpose."

"Never on purpose."

"Then you've started." He did not come down. "I'll not ask what you wrote. It's your book." She heard him turn his watch on his wrist. "Your sponsor came. Sunday last. She'll have told you."

"Today."

"Good." He was quiet for a moment. "It went badly."

"She said."

"It went badly on both sides," said Hart. "I've said so now. To somebody." The door shut behind him. She heard his step going away along the gallery, slow and uneven, toward the pumps.

"I know you know. I'm saying it." He looked out of the north window, at the harbour, at the Long Quay's lamps, which were still lit in the grey morning and did not flicker. "It's the fourteenth. The Bench is at nine. You're not at the Bench. It's on your card."

"It's on my card."

"Then you'll sit on the tiers with Aske, and watch us do it, and Halden, and Old Four, and Twenty-Eight. And tomorrow there's the Carry." He shifted the rope on his shoulder, and shifted it back. "That's mine. The Carry. I'd like it known that I've not slept."

"Nobody's slept," said Anwen, from the next bed, putting on her boots.

"I'd like it known all the same."

Jude was standing at the window with his good arm on the frame, looking at the sea. Pru was winding her cord. Kiva got up off the bed and put on her boots, both of them, and stood. She did not sway.

Out beyond the Mole, the sea was grey and breathing, every count at once. In the basin at the head of the harbour, under a hand of brown water and a yard of mud, there was a ring that had been seen by more people than one. In a black iron box with three locks at the back of the Board's tent there was a paper with a seal on it, and the hour, waiting for the 23rd. On a landing over a grating there was a man with a book of slacks who had said *it went badly on both sides,* and meant it. And down the Long Quay, in a tall grey house with every window lit, there was a man with his hand on a glass case who had asked her for the one thing that she would not sell, and been told no, and laughed.

Under all of it, through the floor and the chandlery and the granite of the quay, the town's count went on. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster.

The disk was cold. She had looked.

She went down the stair after the others, into the grey morning, to watch a Bench that she was not let stand at. On the first step she laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the banister rail, lightly, without meaning to, as one touches a thing for luck.

*It keeps time.*

It was not a quarter to seven. It was not on purpose. She did not write it down.

## 1. Book promise

Kiva Rowan ended her first collegiate season seated, unclassified, with eight fingers that
report and a company's card that promises no seat. Book Two takes her two hundred miles to the
coast. She spends a winter with her hands dark, kneeling at a professional's hem with her
mouth shut. She trains for Riftspawn that no longer die by the book. She stands the National
Measure at Saltmere among twenty-four fours and schools that solved her problem a different
way. And she finds that the thing Meridian did once, by hand, to one bank on one hill, has to
be done on water that never lies still.

The title carries three meanings, and the book pays all three: the competition; the nation
counting what it has (keepers' books, banks, fevers, plain ash); and **the beat itself**, the
count of four that more and more of the country's ash is keeping.

## 2. The argument

Book One asked why the post is crooked and answered: keep the reason with the method. Book
Two asks what happens when a method has to **travel.** Mara's eleven sheets reach the coast and
fail there, because a harbour is not a storm drain. Kiva's three-door sequence cannot travel
at all; it lives in one damaged hand. What can travel is the part she has been made to learn
all winter: say what the piece is, who has it, and when it changes hands.

Her earned capability in this book is not a fourth three-door. It is the **handed crossing**
(working term): catch, carry, and plant done by three people with three unlike doors, with
one caller who has been inside both gaps and can say when the load passes. It costs almost no
ash. It costs trust, rehearsal, and a count that is not hers.

## 3. Complete ending

**The Measure is read in full.** No board is struck. The Ropewalk School's first four wins the
National Measure. Halden License is second. **Team Seven finishes seventh of twenty-four and
makes the national license roll, which takes eight, by one mark.** Seven wins one board
outright, the Carry, on Perrin Cade's back. Selka Dray beats Kiva in a lane, on a declared
count, with a needle on her, exactly as promised; Seven wins the lane on the other three.

Kiva loses that exchange by choice. One crossing in seven days is all her card allows, and by
then she knows what the week's crossing is for. She fights Dray on one door and says why
beforehand, to Dray's face.

**The dream becomes a public fact.** Before the Charter Board, Kiva has Mara's sealed page
opened: timed and sealed before she ever read Annick, it proves that her account was her own.
Beside it lie Annick's entry, the Ferren keeper's line of 2604, the Saltmere harbour keeper's
line for the same night, and the first returns of a national reading of remarks columns. Two
living Glory masters then say under their own names that they have dreamed it: Clemency
Thrale, Master of the Order of the Even Hand, and Ambrose Halden, the Proprietor, who was
alive on the night in 2604. A Board clerk borrows Annick's phrase, and the papers print **the
Unroofed Sky.** Nobody can say what it means. The two masters read it in opposite ways.

**The Slack.** At the dawn low-water slack of Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, a mixed
cohort from the Measure's roll and three professional crews take Saltmere's harbour bank of
thirty-six cells out of step. The water has to be still, and harbour water is still for about
twenty minutes. The thing standing in the basin has to come off the water first. It is the
first Barrel-class expression on the page, and it is keeping the harbour's time.

- **Students** stand the wall, carry, cut on a foreman's slate, and bag. Kiva calls three
  handed crossings in a row and tests every mark first.
- **When a link fails,** she supplies it herself: one certified crossing, on a sanctioned mast,
  read by the office's reader. It is the first decisive thing she has ever done that was
  entirely inside her certificate. **When a second link fails,** she makes a second crossing in
  the same hour, against her healer's card, with the price said first.

- **The Proprietor** holds the harbour's pumps on his own pattern so that the low town does

  He lets go: of the bank, and in writing, of his foundation's claim on "all ash exhibiting the

The bank comes out. The harbour's pumps stand, and the low town takes water. A professional
has died on this coast earlier in the book, and nobody dies at the Slack. The Charter Board
orders every municipal bank in the country put on its own count and kept there: a slower,
dearer, deliberately uneven grid. It is a real national answer and it costs light, money, and
an industrial house its oldest property.

**What it does not settle** is whether the Proprietor was right. He has believed for
seventy-seven years that stepped lamps are the ones that go white instead of out.

**Kiva's cost.** The hand is gone to the wrist for three weeks and returns as before. What
does not return is the rate: Aske's card now reads **one crossing in fourteen days.** Three
doors remain three clean. The fourth was asked for, by a Glory master, and she said no.

**The last of the third month.** Rhea Sorn and Kiva look again. Either may say no, and each
says no to something. Rhea names no seat; she writes Kiva's name on the Charter Board's
travelling commission, under another officer's hand, with *either of us* on it again. Kiva
takes it on two conditions of her own. Team Seven's four go four ways by their own choices.

**The last image.** Wires vouch what the ship's engineer said: ports over the water logged the
same point of light in the same minute. A hull stands in from the south with a light on its
bow that is like no lamp in Saltmere. Somebody else has dreamed, and has come to see who
answered. The disk is cold. Kiva writes that down.

## 6. Six movements

### Movement One — The Second (chapters 1–8)

**Entry pressure.** Stood down from every door for eleven weeks, Kiva has to be worth
something with her hands dark. A method that worked once is being carried to seven drums and
then to the coast. A company's card says she may watch.

**Engine.** The handoff list becomes a craft. Orla tells her about Hart, and Kiva decides what
of her book a stranger may read. The new year brings the first re-reading of her figures
since Classification Day. Two nights as Pike's second teach what a professional catcher does
that he never says aloud, and where twenty-six years of it stop being enough.

**Action and turn.** Drum work under Mara. A professional call where the book answer fails
against a mixed expression and a professional is hurt. Kiva's two dead fingers are tested by
dark-hand work at a right-hand corner. The warrant for Saltmere is posted, and it is dearer
than Kellan's sum. Saltmere answers Mara's sheets: tried, and failed, on water that moves.

**Exit.** Kiva has started writing down Pike's method in seven columns. Kellan has chosen his
four. Seven's entry is signed under a captain nobody expected. After a fifteen-dram warrant,
the book has 10.53 drams left; about 5.8 of that is already spoken for by the planned
hand-to-Ember crossings and Coombe's floors, before any call reserve. The only plain
ward-trace is the sealed 0.8 medical reserve. The Measure has four boards, one of them a live
hunt.

### Movement Two — Something Quiet (chapters 9–16)

**Entry pressure.** The second month to make a four that can stand a licensed hunt against
things its kill drills were not written for: thirty days until the road south and forty-four
days from Movement One's last day to the first board, on a book whose available ash is
already spoken for.

**Engine.** Several connected chapters of training for stronger and more varied spawn under
Hunt Marshal Dinah Coombe, a Blaze who has run mixed fours on the coast: packs that keep a
count among themselves; a hem that folds like a ward; a form that takes a stacked door as an
invitation. Kiva spends her weekly go certifying a second crossing, hand to Ember, and the
hand sulks longer each time. The handed crossing is assembled from parts the reader already
owns: the plank, Pru's fold, the Line's old word, and Pike's unwritten habits.

**Action and turn.** A learning fight against Halden License in a prepared lane, where Kellan
stands opposite. Seven's first licensed hunt in its own right, in the hills round the Sag,
looking for something quiet enough to refill a medical reserve. They kill well. Nothing comes
up plain. The only plain ash on the road south is in the house of the Even Hand, whose door
has been on the latch since the summer.

**Exit.** Seven has its Flask license and a method with three weeks of rehearsal in it. Kiva
has stood near a Glory master for the first time, and the dream has come back, with a fever,
on the ferry.

### Movement Three — Water That Keeps Time (chapters 17–24)

**Entry pressure.** Saltmere is proud of its harbour bank. Thirty-six cells in step have not
dropped a stroke in six months, and the Works' reader from Meridian is a guest telling her
hosts that their best machine is a hazard.

**Engine.** Other schools solve the same problem in ways that look incompatible. The Ropewalk
School has a column for two doors; it teaches them **laid**, open together and agreeing, which
Meridian calls a stack and forbids. Dray's sealed mast rule makes the host's best candidates an
incident, and Meridian is disliked for it. Jessamy Hart turns out to be the man who can tell
the Works when the harbour is still. The harbour keeper's book has an entry for the 11th of
the third month, 2604. Something stands in the basin at each spring tide, and stands a little
taller each time. Hart and Mara seal a prediction.

**Action and turn.** The gate fortnight's seeding rounds. A coast night on Graywater's van,
called south under a Charter Board warrant. The Proprietor asks to see the plate and is
answered by a watch's vote. He then asks Kiva for the three-door, on a Halden mast, for a
price. She says no.

**Exit.** Seven is seeded in the middle of twenty-four. The prediction is under seal. Orla and
Hart have spoken once, badly.

### Movement Four — The Longshore (chapters 25–32)

**Entry pressure.** Three boards in three days, and the third is alive.

**Engine.** The Bench, without Kiva. The Carry, which is Perrin's. The Hunt, on the tidal
flats, scored on clean yield after shares, with every team's ash combed for the Step.

**Action and turn.** The Hunt is the book's midpoint set piece. Coast forms work the tide. A
laid door is entered, as Coombe's drill said it could be: Maud Lyle of the Ropewalk loses a
door, perhaps for good. A professional catcher of the Saltmere Company is killed making the
recovery that the book prescribes. The handed crossing is used for the first time against
something live, by Seven and one borrowed still-door from another circuit, and holds.

**Exit.** After three boards Seven stands ninth, a place off the roll. The Ropewalk's captain,
Juniper Carrow, has shut one of her own doors and is learning to declare. The Measure goes on
under a death.

### Movement Five — A Declared Count (chapters 33–40)

**Entry pressure.** The sealed prediction falls due on the 23rd, four days after the final.
Kiva has one crossing a week. The lane against Dray and the Slack both want it.

**Engine.** The Lane, over three days. The Charter Board's hearing on the three sheets. Draft
boards. Kellan's numbers, which run against his own house. Kiva's choice to put the dream on a
table.

**Action and turn.** Developed lane fights against rivals who have adapted: Carrow on one
door; Dray on her feet. Kiva loses the exchange she wanted, on purpose, and Seven wins the
lane. The sealed page is opened. Two Glory masters testify. The Measure is read.

**Exit.** Seven is seventh, on the roll by one mark, warranted for the Slack. Offers are on the
table for Anwen, Kellan, Jude, and Perrin. Everybody now knows that Kiva dreams.

### Movement Six — The Slack (chapters 41–48)

**Entry pressure.** A scheduled operation with a twenty-minute window, planned by people who
disagree, against a thing that has grown at every spring tide since the autumn.

**Engine.** Rehearsal, in developed scenes, with one bad assumption found in time and one that
is not. The Graving, a dry dock turned arena, becomes the purse that catches the ash when its
gate is shut.

**Action and turn.** The Slack, as section 3 gives it: three handed crossings, two failed
links, two crossings of her own, a master who has to let go, a point of light in every lamp.

**Exit.** The complete ending of section 3.

## 1. Rules that do not move (`ESTABLISHED`)

- One source, three traditions: Ember, rift-granted magic (the hand door: Ward, Sustain), and
  internal cultivation (the still door). The soul fills. Ash is ownerless, finite fuel.
- The rank ladder: Kindled, Ember, Flame, Fire, Torch, Blaze, Glory. Rank and breadth are one
  axis; skill and control are another. Hold, Draw, Call, yield, grade, resonance, license,
  and standing are separate measures, and none pays for another.
- **Draw** is ash spent on a piece of work over the book expectation for it: 1.0 is standard,
  lower is better.
- A kill does not raise rank. Ash funds the next attempt. Learning is what makes it survivable.
- Ash is recorded by **yield** (Handful, Flask, Barrel, Well), **grade**, and **resonance.**
  The comb's third reed sings for ward-trace (W). The seventh (I) is cut to the Step.
- Glory stops ordinary aging, is rare, and cannot be granted, bought, or stolen. It widens
  what one person can hold: throughput, duration, precision. It never makes ash free.
- Riftspawn expressions echo worked powers with nobody home to direct them. Spawn anatomy
  as trained: breath; posts or load paths; knots; a cord; a hem; the tear; closure.
- Students close no tear. Licensed crews do. Radiation work and licenses not held are not
  taken over in a crisis.
- Classification is physical: rigs, masts, pens, tapes, projected tables, human rulings. No
  private overlay.
- **Doors, for Kiva.** A competition is all doors open and fighting. A stack is two open
  together and agreeing: soft, cheap, and open to anything near; forbidden to her. A sequence
  shuts each door before the next opens, and each gap costs about a quarter of a dram with
  nothing open. A crossing is one gap. Three doors are two gaps.
- **The disk** does not teach, speak, map, choose, open a door, or answer. Two bearers have
  written *warm, once,* after a first crossing, and *cold* ever after.
- **The dream** is a fever, not a prophecy that can be consulted. It cannot be summoned. Each
  recurrence has a bodily cost. Triggers on record: fever, ash overload, filter instability,
  close contact with Glory.
- **The Step** (Halden's foundation patent, 2391): a row of cells salted late with one pinch
  of ash already made to keep a beat thereafter beats as one and can be driven by one
  operator. Used in Halden's yards, refineries, lift yards, and big tram sheds. Pump houses
  and tenement boxes were never meant to be stepped. The claim covers "all ash exhibiting the
  Step, however produced." The caution: "Not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under
  load." Before the patent the Wren House called it *counting ash.*
- Shown, not explained, in Book One: stepped pumps make water rise and fall on a count of four
  and four; a cell joined to a stepped bank by nothing but that water goes into step; a tear
  wants "the thing that keeps time" and not the bright thing; a string with an arm in the
  water is itself a pump; cut cords spill open ash that can cook a bank.

## 3. Saltmere (`PLANNED`)

- A harbour city. The Saltmere Company's green is `ESTABLISHED`, as is its harbour bank of
  thirty-six cells, in step since twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month, 2680.
- **The Ropewalk School** leads the host circuit. It teaches two-door candidates to open both
  doors together and twist them to agree: **a lay,** *laid* doors. It is what Meridian calls a
  stack. It is cheap, quick, and soft, it wins lanes, and its circuit has a column for it. It

  the gate shut and the sluices closed, the dock is a basin of still water whatever the tide
  does outside.

  still only at slack, for about twenty minutes. The Ferren method needs still water. Mara's
  sheets were tried at Saltmere in the winter and failed for that reason.
- **Many in Saltmere like the stepped bank.** It has not dropped a stroke. One operator can
  drive it. That is a fair position held by sensible people.

## 4. Spawn in a national season (`PLANNED`)

All new behavior obeys trained anatomy and combines or redirects it. None is a bigger Flask.
Working descriptions follow; the crews on the page give the names, as Book One's did.

- **Packs.** Husks that keep a count among themselves and move on it. Cutting one out of time
  breaks the pack for a few counts. Cutting in time does nothing.
- **A folding hem.** A hem that folds from its far edge, as a catcher's ward does, and so
  comes off a purse sheet.
- **An entering form.** A cord that goes to an open stack as a string goes to water that
  keeps time. A sequenced door gives it nothing to enter. A laid door is an open door.
- **Coast forms.** Things that stand in tidal creeks and work the flood and the ebb, where no
  sheet can be laid and ash goes into the water.
- **The first Barrel.** The expression in the harbour basin, grown at each spring tide. Its
  shape is the author's. It is not the Pit's square and not Stopgate's string abreast.
- **Yield** is higher on average and nearly all of it reads I above trace. Rich ash that
  cannot go on a floor, into a pump house, or into a medical tin.

## 5. Ash in a national season (`PLANNED`)

- **Plain ash is disappearing.** Natural yield carried the Step in trace by the spring of 2680
  (`ESTABLISHED`: "it's all had the hair since the thaw"). By the winter no fresh kill reads
  plain, in Meridian or in the hills.
- **Plain ash is what un-stepping needs:** a jar a cell, of ash that "has never heard a beat."
  Old stock is finite: reference shelves, wardens' winter stores, sealed medical reserves, and
  the cellars of an Order that never stepped anything.
- So the price of ash turns over. The richest red bags are close to worthless for safe work,
  and eight-tenths of a dram of plain ward-trace is worth an argument.
- A healer can ease a **starved** hand with plain ward-trace and cannot ease one that is
  **turned out** (`ESTABLISHED`). Stepped ward-trace is no use for it.

## 6. The handed crossing (`PLANNED`; the working term may be replaced on the page)

- **What it is.** Catch, carry, and plant, each done by a different person through one door,
  with a caller. A hand door lifts and folds. An Ember door takes the folded load across. A
  still door plants it. Between people there is no quarter-dram gap, because nobody shuts and
  opens.
- **What it costs instead.** For an instant at each pass the load belongs to nobody. A pass
  called half a count wrong drops it or nips someone (`ESTABLISHED`: Pru's fingers). Each
  holder must take a count that is not theirs. It needs rehearsal with those exact people.
- **Why it was not already done.** Schools teach one door and declared roles; a pass between
  unlike doors belongs to nobody's curriculum. The Home Line had a word for it once. A
  single-door fighter cannot feel the far side of a gap. Somebody who has been through both
  can call it.
- **Limits.** It is slower to set than a three-door sequence. It fails if any one of three
  people is hurt, late, or unwilling. It cannot be done by strangers. It does not close a
  tear, kill anything, or mend a bank.
- **Kiva's part** is the call, the ground test, and the written columns. Under her certificate
  she may also stand as one link by making one declared crossing on a sanctioned mast.

1. **The Step is a mark.** It is what rising pressure from beyond the filter leaves in ash.

3. **People and spawn are answering one pressure.** Noa's columns get a fourth, for ash, and
   it rises with the other three.
4. **It is quickening.** Old Halden bench books give the Step a slower count than today's
   four and four. (The figures are the author's, after reading the patent scene in Book One.)
5. **It is not uniform.** The notch that follows Kiva's cliff by eleven seconds at Meridian
   follows it sooner on the coast. Nobody can say what that distance is a distance to.
6. **The dream is shared.** Annick (Glory, 2431; her entry undated); the Ferren keeper and the
   Saltmere keeper on one night in 2604; Kiva in 2676, proved independent by a sealed and timed

7. **A T-0 can be local and can be wide.** When two Glory fields from unlike traditions touch

8. **The readings disagree.** Halden: step the country, so that its lamps go white and not

## 5. Saltmere, as on the page

## 6. The Hoop and the prediction

- **The Hoop** (low-town children's name; Peverell's "the ring at the outfall"): a grey ring, frost on its lip,

  low-water slack** and lying down at its end. Inside it, harbour water **held level with its lip**, above the
  basin, **trembling four and four at the bank's count**. While it stands, **Hart's hand on the grating is
  quiet** (nothing in the basin keeps time) though the pumps run. It lies down and the held water spills in a

## OWNER VOICE — APPROVED

# Monroe Jackson — Owner Voice

Status: owner-confirmed seed. Revise only from accepted owner edits. This layer

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

## Learning discipline

- One edit is an example, not a universal law.
- Promote a broad instinct only when several accepted edits support the same
  reader-facing preference, or when the owner states the preference directly.
- Do not learn voice from continuity fixes, factual corrections, typographical
  repairs, rejected suggestions, reverted edits, or untouched AI prose.
- Do not carry names, setting facts, or plot events from an example into a new
  movement. Learn the difference between WAS and NOW, not the example's subject.
