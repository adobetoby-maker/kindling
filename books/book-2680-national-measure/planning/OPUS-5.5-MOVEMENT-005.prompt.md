## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,
  consistent with an action/interiority-forward book rather than a dialogue-driven one.
- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —
  present throughout, not just in set-piece fight chapters.
- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult
  crossover register, short-to-medium sentence construction, low jargon-density outside the
  progression-vocabulary spikes.
- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a
  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

# Chapter 32 — Kiva: The Measure Goes On

Aske greased the knuckles of her right hand in the healers' tent at two, with the flap tied back and the Longshore going under the flood outside.

He did it with two fingers and a pot of yellow grease that smelled of sheep, slowly, as he did everything, and then he wound linen round the hand from the wrist to the first joints and tied it off. He did not look up while he did it. He had done Pru's first, all ten fingertips, each in its own small cap of linen, so that she sat on the next stretcher with her hands held up in front of her like a surgeon waiting to be gloved.

"A burn of cold," he said. "Across the knuckles and the backs of the first two fingers. It'll blister tonight and peel by Sunday." He tied the knot. "It's your right hand. It's not the left. Understand that. It's the one that works, and it'll go on working, and in a week you'll not know it happened."

"And the Ember?"

"The Ember's not in your hand. It's under your breastbone, where it's always been, and it's done nothing wrong today." He looked at her at last. "It carried more than I'd have said it could. I watched you come across that channel from the causeway with a glass, the way you'd watch a horse go at a wall you'd told it not to jump. It held. It's tired. So are you."

"Thursday," said Kiva.

"Thursday's the soonest. I said so this morning, and nothing that happened on that mud has moved it." He put the grease pot in his bag. "The left hand's warm. The right's burned. Neither of them's the hand I'm counting days for." He paused. "I'll say the price on Thursday, if anybody asks for one. I'll say it twice."

On the stretcher at the far end of the tent, Bridie Hebden lay under two blankets with her legs bare to the knee on a folded sheet. They were white to the knee, not grey, and the Company healer was laying wet linen on them, cool, very gently, and changing it. Bridie was not talking about fathoms any more. She was looking at the canvas roof of the tent with her plaits down over the stretcher's edge, and she was not crying. Trewin sat on an upturned crate beside her with his boots off and his feet in a basin of water, white to the ankle, and his hat on his knee.

He had been sitting there for half an hour without saying anything. Now he looked up, across the tent, at Kiva.

"You've plain," he said.

The tent went quiet.

"Two drams," said Trewin. He said it slowly and politely, as he said everything. "Sealed. Ward. You declared it at the gate on the first day, in front of the whole queue. Everybody saw it go silent on the seventh. It was the only one." He looked at Bridie's legs. "Plain ward-trace is for the cold. That's what my grandmother said. A healer can ease the cold with it, if it's plain."

Kiva did not answer at once. She looked at Aske.

Aske had not moved. He sat on the end of her stretcher with his bag on his knees and looked at Trewin, and then at the Company healer, and then at Bridie's white legs on the folded sheet.

"It's for a hand," said Kiva. "That's what's written on it. In two books, under two names. *For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.*" She made herself go on. "It's Seven's word. It's my captain's name and mine. If it were wanted, I'd have to go to my four and to the Order, and say it was wanted, and ask."

"I know," said Trewin. "I'm not asking you to break your word. I'm asking whether it's wanted."

"It's not," said Aske.

They both looked at him.

"Plain ward-trace eases a starved hand," said Aske. "A hand that's had the ash drawn out of it through a door, so that the door's hungry and the flesh round it's cold from inside. I've done it. Her aunt's hand was starved once, in the autumn, and plain eased it inside the hour." He nodded at Bridie. "That's not starved. That's burned. It's a burn of cold from outside, off a skin, the same as frost off a gate. It wants grease and wet linen and a fortnight. Plain ash on that would do nothing that grease won't do better, and you'd have spent the last plain ward-trace on the road south for a pot of grease." He looked at Trewin. "I'm sorry. I'd say so if it were otherwise. I'd say so to her four and to the girl's own face."

Trewin looked at him for a long time. Then he nodded, once.

"Thank you," he said. "I'd sooner know."

"You'd sooner know," said Aske. "So would I. So would she." He got up. "Miss Hebden. The linen's to be changed every quarter of an hour till dark, and you're not to stand on them till Friday, and you're to eat something. I'll come at six."

Bridie looked at the tent roof.

"He stopped at three," she said, to nobody.

The Board combed the Hunt's bags back at the gate on the timber pond, as the Clerk had said it would, from four until after six.

Every four came in off the Longshore with its bags, and every bag went on the long table under the mast, and a clerk in grey held a comb over it, and another clerk wrote down what the comb said. The queue went back along the boards and round the end of the Graving's wall. Nobody talked much in it. The word had gone along the causeway and along the Mole and into the town long before any of them came in, and the queue had heard it, and it stood with its bags at its feet and waited.

Seven came to the table at a quarter to six.

There were three things to comb. The two bags off Mallow Creek, tied at their necks with the purses' own drawstrings, grey canvas, wet, with the mud of the east bank still on them. And the tin from the punt, where Ivey had netted the shed rung off Anwen's wall in the morning.

The clerk held the comb over the tin first.

Kiva heard the third reed sing, high and thin, the way it sang for ward-trace. Then she heard the seventh. It was not a hair but a voice, and it sang over the third and went on singing after the clerk had taken the comb away.

"Red," said the clerk. "I above trace. Nine drams and a tenth." He did not look up. "Wet on the ebb?"

"Wet on the ebb," said Anwen.

"They all are. I've not had a wet one under all day." He tied a red tag on the tin's handle and wrote. "Next."

The first bag. The rung off the near post, the short one, cut at fourteen minutes past twelve and shut in the slack. Kiva had hauled it. *One fathom. Two fathom. Three.*

The clerk held the comb over it. The third reed. Then the seventh, a voice, over the third.

"Red. Thirty-eight drams." He tagged it. "Next."

The second bag. The far post's, the tall one, cut at twenty-four minutes past and hauled by Pru. The clerk held the comb over it.

The third reed sang.

The seventh did not sing; it hummed. It was a hair, a thread of sound, so faint that Kiva would not have heard it if she had not been listening for it with her whole body. It was the sound that she had heard from every can in Meridian since the thaw, and every tin on the road south, and every bag from Cowl Edge. It was the hair.

"Trace," said the clerk. He looked up for the first time. "Under trace on the seventh. Clean." He looked at the bag, and at the comb, and at them. "Twelve drams and six-tenths. Clean."

"Clean," said Jude.

One in four that's dry, perhaps, combs clean. Nobody's had a wet one under." He tied a white tag on the second bag. "Twelve and six, clean, Team Seven. The Board's half, the Company's quarter, your quarter. Three drams and fifteen hundredths, to your book." He looked down the queue. "Next."

"*Wednesday the 16th,*" he said aloud. "*The Hunt, Mallow Creek. Pryce, a wall on water, seventeen. Talley, two cuts, ten. Finch, a fold, twelve. Rowan, an Ember, her own thimble. Spent, thirty-nine.*" He wrote. "*Combed: fifty-nine drams and seven-tenths; forty-seven and a tenth red, and tagged. Twelve and six clean. The four's quarter, three and fifteen.*" He did the sum in the margin, twice, as he always did. "*Held, six and twenty-six.*"

"Six and twenty-six," said Anwen.

"The most we've had since the hills." Jude did not look up. "I'd give it all back."

Nobody said anything to that.

At the next table along the boards, a man in Halden's black and gold was sitting with a ledger.

Kiva had seen him from the queue and had not thought anything of it. Halden's technical office had a clerk at every board; Mr. Adlam had said so. He sat with his ledger open and wrote, and every time a bag on the comb table got a red tag, he leaned over and read the tag and wrote a line.

Kellan was standing beside him.

Kellan was not in the queue. Halden had been combed at five. He stood by the clerk's table in his black and gold with his hands in his pockets, reading the ledger upside down over the clerk's shoulder, and his face had the look it had when a sum would not come out.

He saw Kiva and came over.

"Skerry Pill," he said.

"A door." Kellan looked at the boards. "Her ward. Gone, not shut. She held a boom against a shed rung on Carrow's word, and the lodger came out of the pill's mouth like an eel, and Treen called run, and she didn't. I cut it on the mud. I was too late." He said it flatly, the way he did sums. "Bray wrote a remark against me. A dram off Halden on the mark. And our second line was half set when I left it, and at the slack a part of our second rung went past the purse. Bray put that one down to the pill, not to me. I'd have put it to me." He shrugged. "We were combed at five. Eight and two, after shares and the remark. Pettit says he'd have gone himself."

"I'm sorry," said Kiva. "About Lyle."

"So am I." Kellan looked at her bandaged hand, and at Pru's capped fingers, and away. "I heard about Mallow. Everybody on the causeway heard. A Company catcher, and a girl of the Tarn under him." He was quiet. "And a pass in three. Nobody holding it twice."

"It held."

"I know it held. Coombe said so, on the causeway, to the Clerk, at two. In front of everybody." He took his hands out of his pockets. "I wanted to tell you something else. That's not why I came over."

He nodded back at Halden's clerk with his ledger.

"He's writing every red tag," said Kellan. "Every bag in the Hunt that combs over trace on the seventh. He writes the four, the beat, the weight, and the tag number, and then a line under each. I read the line." He did not lower his voice. "*Claimed, under the patent of 2391. All ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.*"

Kiva looked at the clerk. He was a young man, neat, with ink on his second finger, writing carefully.

"It's not a crime," said Kellan. "It's the patent. The house has claimed it since 2391, and the Board's never said it can't. He's doing his work. He'd be doing it badly if he didn't." He did the sum aloud, as he always did. "But I've been standing here an hour. I've watched every bag. One in four that's dry combs clean, the comb clerk says. Nothing that's been wet combs clean. So call it nine-tenths of what twenty-four fours killed on the Longshore today combs red." He looked at her. "The house has a claim on nine-tenths of the Hunt. On paper. Tonight."

"What does it do with it?"

"Nothing, tonight. It's red; it's evidence; it's tagged. Nobody can use it." Kellan put his hands back in his pockets. "I don't know what it does with it next year. I don't know what it does with it if next year it's ten-tenths." He was quiet. "I'm not saying anything's wrong. I'm saying I did the sum, and it came out, and I didn't like the number. I'd expect nothing else of a sum. I thought somebody should know it besides me."

He went back to the clerk's table and stood there, reading upside down.

Anwen Pryce climbed Tar Lane at seven with tar on her boots and her hands in her sleeves, and did not know what she was going to say.

The Ropewalk was lit. Its windows went away along the ridge in a long line of yellow squares, as they had the evening she had first come up, and the doors at the near end stood open. Anwen stopped at the door.

Carrow was walking up and down the near end of the shed, slowly, ten paces and back, in her shirtsleeves, with her edge lit on her right fist. Only her edge. Anwen could feel it. There was no post going into the ground under her, no stance; her feet came up off the boards at every step like anybody's feet. She walked ten paces, and turned, and walked back, and at every step her edge wavered on her fist the way a candle wavers when somebody opens a door.

"It's loud," said Lyle, from the bench, without looking round. "That's what she's finding. I told her." She had seen Anwen in the doorway. "Come in, Pryce. She'll not stop for you."

"She called boom." Lyle turned the towel over in her hands. "She says she's called boom a hundred times and never once thought about what she was calling. She says she doesn't know whether it was wrong. She says that's worse."

Carrow turned at the end of her ten paces. Her edge wavered and steadied. She did not look at the bench.

Anwen looked at Lyle's hands on the towel.

"Can I see?" she said. "I'm not asking to be kind. I'd want to know. If it were me."

Lyle looked at her. Then she put the towel down on the bench and stood up.

Nothing came up.

She held her hand there, and Anwen watched it. It was a Hold's hand, broad, steady, held at the hip with the palm up, the way Anwen held her own a hundred times a day. There was nothing on it. "There," said Lyle. "That's all."

She shut her stance. She sat down again and picked up the towel.

"It's quiet," she said. "I always said laid was the quietest thing you'd ever feel. Two hands on a rope. You stop having to think about either." She folded the towel. "It isn't. This is. There's one hand on the rope now, and I can't stop thinking about the other one."

Anwen sat with her own hands in her sleeves.

She had thought, all the way up Tar Lane, that she would not say *I told you.* She had decided it on the causeway, and again at the bottom of the lane, and again at the door. It had been easy to decide. It was harder to sit on a bench beside the girl she had told, twice, and not have it in her mouth.

It was not in her mouth. She found that it was not there at all.

"I want it said that I'm sorry," said Anwen. "That's all I came to say. I'm sorry."

"Because it was coming down the pill," said Anwen. "And you were the boom."

Lyle looked at her then.

"Carrow said I was the best wall on the coast," she said. "I said I wasn't. I said I was the best laid wall on the coast, and if you took my feet out of the floor I'd be a wall like anybody's." She almost smiled. It did not get as far as her mouth. "They took the wall. I've got my feet. I'm a floor now, like anybody's. Less, maybe."

"You leaned well," said Lyle. "In the first lane, against us. You leaned from the floor; your heels were right. I said so." She put the towel round her neck. "Will you show me? How you lean from the floor. When you've nothing on your hands. I've a floor left. I'd like to be good with it."

"Yes," said Anwen. "Tomorrow. Before the Lane."

"Tomorrow." Lyle nodded. "Thank you, Pryce."

At the end of her ten paces Carrow stopped, and turned, and shut her edge. She stood on the bare boards in the lamplight with her hands at her sides and nothing open at all, and she looked at the bench for the first time.

"Gapper," she said to Anwen. Not unkindly. As a fact about the weather. "How long did it take you? To stop hearing it. One door."

"I've never stopped," said Anwen. "That's how you can hear it. That's how you know it's there."

Mara Rowan stood on the bank gallery of the harbour pump house at half past ten, with Peverell beside her and Hart behind, and took her reading hand out of her pocket.

The thirty-six cells stood along the two galleries, eighteen a side, in their tall glass cases. Their lights turned. They turned together, every one of them, at the same pace, four and four, fifty-seven to the minute, and the six iron pumps below went up and down on the same stroke, and the whole hall hummed with it like a held note. Mara had stood in this hall every day for a fortnight. She had not once put her hand on a case.

She held it out now. She held it an inch from the glass of the first cell on the north gallery, where the light inside turned slow and yellow-white, and she did not lay it on.

Peverell watched her. She had her spectacles on their cord and her apron on, and her small crabbed hands folded on the rail. Hart stood a pace behind with his watch on the inside of his wrist and his bad hand in his jacket.

"A Company man," said Peverell. "On the Longshore. Davy Penhale." She said it quite flatly. "I knew his mother. She takes in washing in Ropery Row, below the high-water line. Her kitchen's dry because of these." She did not look at the cells. "His mother's kitchen."

"I know," said Mara.

She held her hand an inch from the glass. She could feel the count in it, through the air, faint. Four and four. She could feel what her hand would tell her if she laid it on. She had felt it on twenty-four cells on a hill and seven drums on another, and a trunk of drums under a city, and she knew what it would cost and how long it would take to come back.

"I'll not," she said.

Hart did not say anything, but Peverell turned her head.

"I've been deciding for a fortnight," said Mara. "Whether to touch one. Rusk has asked me twice. You've asked me with your face every morning. Jessamy said on Monday night that somebody would have to, before the 23rd, or we'd be standing on the landing watching a ring we'd written down." She did not take her hand away from the glass. "He's right. Somebody will have to. Somebody will have to touch all thirty-six of them at once, on one word, at nine minutes past six, with the water still. If anybody's going to do it at all."

"Then touch one," said Peverell. "Now. And tell me what it says."

"If I touch one," said Mara, "then I'm the one who reads them. Then on the 23rd there are thirty-six cells and one hand, and the hand's mine, and it's been starved once already, and if it goes on the third cell, or the tenth, there's nobody." She looked at her hand, an inch from the glass. "That's what this bank is. One engine. Thirty-six cells and nobody can do anything alone. I'll not make the thing that takes it out of step another one engine, with my hand for the count."

"Then who reads it?"

"Thirty-six hands," said Mara. "Not mine. Your fitters. Six of the Works'. Rusk's men, if he'll send them, and he will, because I'll ask him tonight. The Company's closing crews, if Yeo will lend them, and she'll lend them now." She took her hand away from the glass at last and put it, not in her pocket, but on the gallery rail, flat. "I'll teach them. Every one, a cell each, by the 22nd. Jessamy will teach them when the water's still; his hand knows it and mine doesn't. You'll teach them your cells. Peverell looked at her for a long time.

"I'll not be the keeper who stopped the pumps," she said.

"You'll be the keeper whose fitters read them," said Mara. "That's a different line in your book."

Peverell said nothing. Then she took a pencil out of her apron pocket and a fold of the pump house's fault paper, and she began, slowly, in her small crabbed hand, to write names.

Hart had not moved. Mara turned her head and looked at him.

"Well," she said. "You've been quiet."

He turned his watch on his wrist, once.

"Thirty-six," he said. "On one word. With the water still." He looked at the cells. "That's the right number. I'd not have said it. I'd have said one good hand." He was quiet a moment. "I've one hand that reads and one that doesn't, and the one that reads is the one that's ruined. I'll teach your thirty-six when the water's still. I'll teach them what quiet feels like." He looked at her. "You've decided."

"I've decided."

"Then I'll write it down," said Hart, and took his book of slacks out of his jacket with his left hand.

The loft was dark except for the candle on the sill by the north window. Aske had gone to his own bed. Perrin was asleep on his back with his hand on his ribs, breathing short. "She will."

"I know she will." He was quiet a long time. "I asked everybody since the autumn what it's for. The ash. I asked a Marshal and a foreman and my own four. I asked Yeo on the Ropewalk steps. I asked the sea last night, from the quay." He looked at the dark water. "Today I carried a man up a bank on a board who'd counted fathoms to a girl under a skin to keep her still. That's what it's for. I think. Not the ash. The bank. Carrying it up the bank." He stopped. "I don't know. I'd want it said I don't know. I'll stay anyway. When it's done. If she asks."

Kiva did not answer. There was nothing to say that would not have been smaller than what he had said, so she stood beside him at the window.

She took her book out with her left hand and sat on the edge of her own bed by the candle. She opened it to the back, where the clean pages were, past the stub where she had torn one out on Monday for Pru. She ruled a fresh sheet into seven columns, badly, with the pencil in her left hand, because her right was in linen. At the head of the first she wrote the word that went there.

*Before.*

Under it, in the first line, she wrote what Coombe had told her to write, and what Ivey had told her to put there, and what she would have put there herself without being told.

*D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company. Reserve to the officer. Went to the near edge by the book. Counted fathoms under it to keep a girl still. Stopped at three.*

She looked at it for a long time and did not cross anything out.

Then, though there had been no crossing and the fourth column was owed nothing, she did a thing she had no need to do. She took the disk out of her coat on its cord and laid it on the page, on the line she had just written, and looked at it in the candlelight.

It was cold.

She had known it would be. It had been cold through every crossing since the first, and through three doors and a fever and a Glory master's table, and it was cold now. She did not write that down; there was no column for it tonight. She put the disk back inside her coat.

Down the room, Aske turned over in his bed and said, without waking, or perhaps not, "Thursday. Soonest."

Kiva blew out the candle.

Outside the window, under the white lamps and the yellow ones and the dark, the town went on keeping its count. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster. On the Longshore the flood was at the top of its neap, quiet, over the flats and the creeks and Mallow Creek, over the place where a stow had stood and a man had knelt. In a shop on Quay Street a printer was setting a page, a letter at a time, with a dead man's name at the top of it. In a pump house a keeper was writing thirty-six names on fault paper. In a long tarred shed on the ridge, a girl with one door was walking up and down by lamplight, ten paces and back, learning to hear it.

The Measure went on. In the morning there would be the Lane.

She lay down in the dark with her bandaged hand on her chest and her warm hand at her side, and the first two fingers of it, without her meaning them to, came to rest on the iron rail of the bed.

*It keeps time.*

It was not a quarter to seven. It was not on purpose. She did not write it down.

## 1. Book promise

Kiva Rowan ended her first collegiate season seated, unclassified, with eight fingers that
report and a company's card that promises no seat. Book Two takes her two hundred miles to the
coast. She spends a winter with her hands dark, kneeling at a professional's hem with her
mouth shut. She trains for Riftspawn that no longer die by the book. She stands the National
Measure at Saltmere among twenty-four fours and schools that solved her problem a different
way. And she finds that the thing Meridian did once, by hand, to one bank on one hill, has to
be done on water that never lies still.

The title carries three meanings, and the book pays all three: the competition; the nation
counting what it has (keepers' books, banks, fevers, plain ash); and **the beat itself**, the
count of four that more and more of the country's ash is keeping.

## 2. The argument

Book One asked why the post is crooked and answered: keep the reason with the method. Book
Two asks what happens when a method has to **travel.** Mara's eleven sheets reach the coast and
fail there, because a harbour is not a storm drain. Kiva's three-door sequence cannot travel
at all; it lives in one damaged hand. What can travel is the part she has been made to learn
all winter: say what the piece is, who has it, and when it changes hands.

Her earned capability in this book is not a fourth three-door. It is the **handed crossing**
(working term): catch, carry, and plant done by three people with three unlike doors, with
one caller who has been inside both gaps and can say when the load passes. It costs almost no
ash. It costs trust, rehearsal, and a count that is not hers.

## 3. Complete ending

**The Measure is read in full.** No board is struck. The Ropewalk School's first four wins the
National Measure. Halden License is second. **Team Seven finishes seventh of twenty-four and
makes the national license roll, which takes eight, by one mark.** Seven wins one board
outright, the Carry, on Perrin Cade's back. Selka Dray beats Kiva in a lane, on a declared
count, with a needle on her, exactly as promised; Seven wins the lane on the other three.

Kiva loses that exchange by choice. One crossing in seven days is all her card allows, and by
then she knows what the week's crossing is for. She fights Dray on one door and says why
beforehand, to Dray's face.

**The dream becomes a public fact.** Before the Charter Board, Kiva has Mara's sealed page
opened: timed and sealed before she ever read Annick, it proves that her account was her own.
Beside it lie Annick's entry, the Ferren keeper's line of 2604, the Saltmere harbour keeper's
line for the same night, and the first returns of a national reading of remarks columns. Two
living Glory masters then say under their own names that they have dreamed it: Clemency
Thrale, Master of the Order of the Even Hand, and Ambrose Halden, the Proprietor, who was
alive on the night in 2604. A Board clerk borrows Annick's phrase, and the papers print **the
Unroofed Sky.** Nobody can say what it means. The two masters read it in opposite ways.

**The Slack.** At the dawn low-water slack of Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, a mixed
cohort from the Measure's roll and three professional crews take Saltmere's harbour bank of
thirty-six cells out of step. The water has to be still, and harbour water is still for about
twenty minutes. The thing standing in the basin has to come off the water first. It is the
first Barrel-class expression on the page, and it is keeping the harbour's time.

- **Students** stand the wall, carry, cut on a foreman's slate, and bag. Kiva calls three
  handed crossings in a row and tests every mark first.
- **When a link fails,** she supplies it herself: one certified crossing, on a sanctioned mast,
  read by the office's reader. It is the first decisive thing she has ever done that was
  entirely inside her certificate. **When a second link fails,** she makes a second crossing in
  the same hour, against her healer's card, with the price said first.
- **Professionals** close the tear. Fitters and keepers, under Mara Rowan, Jessamy Hart, and
  the harbour keeper, fill thirty-six cells on thirty-six counts.
- **The Proprietor** holds the harbour's pumps on his own pattern so that the low town does
  not flood. The operation cannot finish while he holds it, because his pattern *is* the Step.
  He lets go: of the bank, and in writing, of his foundation's claim on "all ash exhibiting the
  Step, however produced." **Master Thrale** holds a ward across the dock mouth and cannot move
  while she does.
- For some seconds two Glory fields from unlike traditions touch. **Every lamp on the coast
  shows a point of light.** Three people who have dreamed are seen to count together.

The bank comes out. The harbour's pumps stand, and the low town takes water. A professional
has died on this coast earlier in the book, and nobody dies at the Slack. The Charter Board
orders every municipal bank in the country put on its own count and kept there: a slower,
dearer, deliberately uneven grid. It is a real national answer and it costs light, money, and
an industrial house its oldest property.

**What it does not settle** is whether the Proprietor was right. He has believed for
seventy-seven years that stepped lamps are the ones that go white instead of out.

**Kiva's cost.** The hand is gone to the wrist for three weeks and returns as before. What
does not return is the rate: Aske's card now reads **one crossing in fourteen days.** Three
doors remain three clean. The fourth was asked for, by a Glory master, and she said no.

**The last of the third month.** Rhea Sorn and Kiva look again. Either may say no, and each
says no to something. Rhea names no seat; she writes Kiva's name on the Charter Board's
travelling commission, under another officer's hand, with *either of us* on it again. Kiva
takes it on two conditions of her own. Team Seven's four go four ways by their own choices.

**The last image.** Wires vouch what the ship's engineer said: ports over the water logged the
same point of light in the same minute. A hull stands in from the south with a light on its
bow that is like no lamp in Saltmere. Somebody else has dreamed, and has come to see who
answered. The disk is cold. Kiva writes that down.

## 6. Six movements

### Movement One — The Second (chapters 1–8)

**Entry pressure.** Stood down from every door for eleven weeks, Kiva has to be worth
something with her hands dark. A method that worked once is being carried to seven drums and
then to the coast. A company's card says she may watch.

**Engine.** The handoff list becomes a craft. Orla tells her about Hart, and Kiva decides what
of her book a stranger may read. The new year brings the first re-reading of her figures
since Classification Day. Two nights as Pike's second teach what a professional catcher does
that he never says aloud, and where twenty-six years of it stop being enough.

**Action and turn.** Drum work under Mara. A professional call where the book answer fails
against a mixed expression and a professional is hurt. Kiva's two dead fingers are tested by
dark-hand work at a right-hand corner. The warrant for Saltmere is posted, and it is dearer
than Kellan's sum. Saltmere answers Mara's sheets: tried, and failed, on water that moves.

**Exit.** Kiva has started writing down Pike's method in seven columns. Kellan has chosen his
four. Seven's entry is signed under a captain nobody expected. After a fifteen-dram warrant,
the book has 10.53 drams left; about 5.8 of that is already spoken for by the planned
hand-to-Ember crossings and Coombe's floors, before any call reserve. The only plain
ward-trace is the sealed 0.8 medical reserve. The Measure has four boards, one of them a live
hunt.

### Movement Two — Something Quiet (chapters 9–16)

**Entry pressure.** The second month to make a four that can stand a licensed hunt against
things its kill drills were not written for: thirty days until the road south and forty-four
days from Movement One's last day to the first board, on a book whose available ash is
already spoken for.

**Engine.** Several connected chapters of training for stronger and more varied spawn under
Hunt Marshal Dinah Coombe, a Blaze who has run mixed fours on the coast: packs that keep a
count among themselves; a hem that folds like a ward; a form that takes a stacked door as an
invitation. Kiva spends her weekly go certifying a second crossing, hand to Ember, and the
hand sulks longer each time. The handed crossing is assembled from parts the reader already
owns: the plank, Pru's fold, the Line's old word, and Pike's unwritten habits.

**Action and turn.** A learning fight against Halden License in a prepared lane, where Kellan
stands opposite. Seven's first licensed hunt in its own right, in the hills round the Sag,
looking for something quiet enough to refill a medical reserve. They kill well. Nothing comes
up plain. The only plain ash on the road south is in the house of the Even Hand, whose door
has been on the latch since the summer.

**Exit.** Seven has its Flask license and a method with three weeks of rehearsal in it. Kiva
has stood near a Glory master for the first time, and the dream has come back, with a fever,
on the ferry.

### Movement Three — Water That Keeps Time (chapters 17–24)

**Entry pressure.** Saltmere is proud of its harbour bank. Thirty-six cells in step have not
dropped a stroke in six months, and the Works' reader from Meridian is a guest telling her
hosts that their best machine is a hazard.

**Engine.** Other schools solve the same problem in ways that look incompatible. The Ropewalk
School has a column for two doors; it teaches them **laid**, open together and agreeing, which
Meridian calls a stack and forbids. Dray's sealed mast rule makes the host's best candidates an
incident, and Meridian is disliked for it. Jessamy Hart turns out to be the man who can tell
the Works when the harbour is still. The harbour keeper's book has an entry for the 11th of
the third month, 2604. Something stands in the basin at each spring tide, and stands a little
taller each time. Hart and Mara seal a prediction.

**Action and turn.** The gate fortnight's seeding rounds. A coast night on Graywater's van,
called south under a Charter Board warrant. The Proprietor asks to see the plate and is
answered by a watch's vote. He then asks Kiva for the three-door, on a Halden mast, for a
price. She says no.

**Exit.** Seven is seeded in the middle of twenty-four. The prediction is under seal. Orla and
Hart have spoken once, badly.

### Movement Four — The Longshore (chapters 25–32)

**Entry pressure.** Three boards in three days, and the third is alive.

**Engine.** The Bench, without Kiva. The Carry, which is Perrin's. The Hunt, on the tidal
flats, scored on clean yield after shares, with every team's ash combed for the Step.

**Action and turn.** The Hunt is the book's midpoint set piece. Coast forms work the tide. A
laid door is entered, as Coombe's drill said it could be: Maud Lyle of the Ropewalk loses a
door, perhaps for good. A professional catcher of the Saltmere Company is killed making the
recovery that the book prescribes. The handed crossing is used for the first time against
something live, by Seven and one borrowed still-door from another circuit, and holds.

**Exit.** After three boards Seven stands ninth, a place off the roll. The Ropewalk's captain,
Juniper Carrow, has shut one of her own doors and is learning to declare. The Measure goes on
under a death.

### Movement Five — A Declared Count (chapters 33–40)

**Entry pressure.** The sealed prediction falls due on the 23rd, four days after the final.
Kiva has one crossing a week. The lane against Dray and the Slack both want it.

**Engine.** The Lane, over three days. The Charter Board's hearing on the three sheets. Draft
boards. Kellan's numbers, which run against his own house. Kiva's choice to put the dream on a
table.

**Action and turn.** Developed lane fights against rivals who have adapted: Carrow on one
door; Dray on her feet. Kiva loses the exchange she wanted, on purpose, and Seven wins the
lane. The sealed page is opened. Two Glory masters testify. The Measure is read.

**Exit.** Seven is seventh, on the roll by one mark, warranted for the Slack. Offers are on the
table for Anwen, Kellan, Jude, and Perrin. Everybody now knows that Kiva dreams.

### Movement Six — The Slack (chapters 41–48)

**Entry pressure.** A scheduled operation with a twenty-minute window, planned by people who
disagree, against a thing that has grown at every spring tide since the autumn.

**Engine.** Rehearsal, in developed scenes, with one bad assumption found in time and one that
is not. The Graving, a dry dock turned arena, becomes the purse that catches the ash when its
gate is shut.

**Action and turn.** The Slack, as section 3 gives it: three handed crossings, two failed
links, two crossings of her own, a master who has to let go, a point of light in every lamp.

**Exit.** The complete ending of section 3.

## 1. Rules that do not move (`ESTABLISHED`)

- One source, three traditions: Ember, rift-granted magic (the hand door: Ward, Sustain), and
  internal cultivation (the still door). The soul fills. Ash is ownerless, finite fuel.
- The rank ladder: Kindled, Ember, Flame, Fire, Torch, Blaze, Glory. Rank and breadth are one
  axis; skill and control are another. Hold, Draw, Call, yield, grade, resonance, license,
  and standing are separate measures, and none pays for another.
- **Draw** is ash spent on a piece of work over the book expectation for it: 1.0 is standard,
  lower is better.
- A kill does not raise rank. Ash funds the next attempt. Learning is what makes it survivable.
- Ash is recorded by **yield** (Handful, Flask, Barrel, Well), **grade**, and **resonance.**
  The comb's third reed sings for ward-trace (W). The seventh (I) is cut to the Step.
- Glory stops ordinary aging, is rare, and cannot be granted, bought, or stolen. It widens
  what one person can hold: throughput, duration, precision. It never makes ash free.
- Riftspawn expressions echo worked powers with nobody home to direct them. Spawn anatomy
  as trained: breath; posts or load paths; knots; a cord; a hem; the tear; closure.
- Students close no tear. Licensed crews do. Radiation work and licenses not held are not
  taken over in a crisis.
- Classification is physical: rigs, masts, pens, tapes, projected tables, human rulings. No
  private overlay.
- **Doors, for Kiva.** A competition is all doors open and fighting. A stack is two open
  together and agreeing: soft, cheap, and open to anything near; forbidden to her. A sequence
  shuts each door before the next opens, and each gap costs about a quarter of a dram with
  nothing open. A crossing is one gap. Three doors are two gaps.
- **The disk** does not teach, speak, map, choose, open a door, or answer. Two bearers have
  written *warm, once,* after a first crossing, and *cold* ever after.
- **The dream** is a fever, not a prophecy that can be consulted. It cannot be summoned. Each
  recurrence has a bodily cost. Triggers on record: fever, ash overload, filter instability,
  close contact with Glory.
- **The Step** (Halden's foundation patent, 2391): a row of cells salted late with one pinch
  of ash already made to keep a beat thereafter beats as one and can be driven by one
  operator. Used in Halden's yards, refineries, lift yards, and big tram sheds. Pump houses
  and tenement boxes were never meant to be stepped. The claim covers "all ash exhibiting the
  Step, however produced." The caution: "Not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under
  load." Before the patent the Wren House called it *counting ash.*
- Shown, not explained, in Book One: stepped pumps make water rise and fall on a count of four
  and four; a cell joined to a stepped bank by nothing but that water goes into step; a tear
  wants "the thing that keeps time" and not the bright thing; a string with an arm in the
  water is itself a pump; cut cords spill open ash that can cook a bank.

## 3. Saltmere (`PLANNED`)

## 4. Spawn in a national season (`PLANNED`)

All new behavior obeys trained anatomy and combines or redirects it. None is a bigger Flask.
Working descriptions follow; the crews on the page give the names, as Book One's did.

- **Packs.** Husks that keep a count among themselves and move on it. Cutting one out of time
  breaks the pack for a few counts. Cutting in time does nothing.
- **A folding hem.** A hem that folds from its far edge, as a catcher's ward does, and so
  comes off a purse sheet.
- **An entering form.** A cord that goes to an open stack as a string goes to water that
  keeps time. A sequenced door gives it nothing to enter. A laid door is an open door.

- **The first Barrel.** The expression in the harbour basin, grown at each spring tide. Its

  cannot go on a floor, into a pump house, or into a medical tin.

## 5. Ash in a national season (`PLANNED`)

- **Plain ash is disappearing.** Natural yield carried the Step in trace by the spring of 2680
  (`ESTABLISHED`: "it's all had the hair since the thaw"). By the winter no fresh kill reads
  plain, in Meridian or in the hills.
- **Plain ash is what un-stepping needs:** a jar a cell, of ash that "has never heard a beat."
  Old stock is finite: reference shelves, wardens' winter stores, sealed medical reserves, and
  the cellars of an Order that never stepped anything.
- So the price of ash turns over. The richest red bags are close to worthless for safe work,
  and eight-tenths of a dram of plain ward-trace is worth an argument.
- A healer can ease a **starved** hand with plain ward-trace and cannot ease one that is
  **turned out** (`ESTABLISHED`). Stepped ward-trace is no use for it.

## 6. The handed crossing (`PLANNED`; the working term may be replaced on the page)

- **What it is.** Catch, carry, and plant, each done by a different person through one door,
  with a caller. A hand door lifts and folds. An Ember door takes the folded load across. A
  still door plants it. Between people there is no quarter-dram gap, because nobody shuts and
  opens.
- **What it costs instead.** For an instant at each pass the load belongs to nobody. A pass
  called half a count wrong drops it or nips someone (`ESTABLISHED`: Pru's fingers). Each
  holder must take a count that is not theirs. It needs rehearsal with those exact people.
- **Why it was not already done.** Schools teach one door and declared roles; a pass between
  unlike doors belongs to nobody's curriculum. The Home Line had a word for it once. A
  single-door fighter cannot feel the far side of a gap. Somebody who has been through both
  can call it.
- **Limits.** It is slower to set than a three-door sequence. It fails if any one of three
  people is hurt, late, or unwilling. It cannot be done by strangers. It does not close a
  tear, kill anything, or mend a bank.
- **Kiva's part** is the call, the ground test, and the written columns. Under her certificate
  she may also stand as one link by making one declared crossing on a sanctioned mast.

3. **People and spawn are answering one pressure.** Noa's columns get a fourth, for ash, and

4. **It is quickening.** Old Halden bench books give the Step a slower count than today's

5. **It is not uniform.** The notch that follows Kiva's cliff by eleven seconds at Meridian
   follows it sooner on the coast. Nobody can say what that distance is a distance to.
6. **The dream is shared.** Annick (Glory, 2431; her entry undated); the Ferren keeper and the

7. **A T-0 can be local and can be wide.** When two Glory fields from unlike traditions touch

8. **The readings disagree.** Halden: step the country, so that its lamps go white and not

## 2. The boards as played

- Six fours a heat, four heats. Banks of six glass cells on iron stands in **one iron water trough (the jacket)**,
  hidden from the floor by a grey wooden skirt; visible from the gallery. The planted cell keeps a count off a

  the Clerk's opening; the draft only had Kiva say it had been read out)*. Halden did not take it.

### The Carry (Tue 15th, the Inner Lock)

  sluices fill it from the wet dock; north ladder of **twenty-two rungs** ten paces from the casualty; tail stair
  fifty paces back. Start on the floor at the tail. Penalties: face 10 s, drop 20 s; nothing lit on the casualty.

## 4. Mallow Creek

- **Terrain:** a creek running south to the sea, thirty paces wide at the top; the **spur**, an old stone cart track
  off the causeway, along the east bank; marsh to the west. Two **stows**, sixty paces apart: the upper (Seven's),
  the lower (the Tarn's) in the bend, where the channel is deeper on the outside, so its far post (*(repair)* on
  the west side of the bend; its sleeve lay downstream on the west mud and swung on that post from downstream to
  inland over the west mud, closed end last) stood in a yard of
  water at low slack and needed the punt. A **tear** in the bed under each stow's mouth.
- **The stow** (coast name, after stow-nets): two grey posts eight paces apart with the mouth of a long grey skin
  hung between them; behind it a sleeve ten or twelve paces long, closed at the far end, streaming with the tide;
  works the ebb (water out through its sides frosted); swings on its posts like a vane to work the flood; at slack
  the posts stand and the sleeve lies down on the mud. Sheds rungs uncut on a surge. **With one post cut, the
  freed side of the mouth falls and the sleeve's far end lifts and folds toward the nearest line. With both cut,
  the stow dies at once and goes to a film of ash.** On a standing post it can swing.
- **Evidence:** the upper stow leaned *before* the 10.50 gust reached it (Kiva, Pru). The lower stow turned at
  **12.44** on its standing post with the slack holding (fingers silent; grass upright; Perrin's watch); the water
  turned at **12.51**. Penhale: "Things like that don't hear."
- **Crew:** Yeo (officer; green notebook); **Penhale** (officer's reserve; four Hunts, never on the mud); **Ivey**
  (catcher to the closing crew); the closing crew of four and their own officer (short grey man, unnamed); a
  boatman with two punts.
- **Seven:** watched the ebb two hours. 10.50: the upper stow's near post shed a yard on a surge; **Anwen laid a
  wall flat on the water** on Yeo's word (first live boom; Kiva: "Down a hand"); netted by Ivey; 9.1 drams in the
  net's tin, red; remark the creek's. 11.30 purses set with Penhale ("one fathom, two fathom, three"; his brother
  went to sea). Penhale asked what *nobody* was and laughed. 12.12 slack. Near post cut 12.14 on Kiva's call off
  Jude's heel; Kiva hauled, shut; **Pru called *far edge* once** as the sleeve's far end lifted; Kiva came off the
  line; the fold lay down where she had been. Far post cut 12.24; Pru hauled, shut; stow dead 12.29; upper tear
  closed by 12.40.
- **The Tarn:** near post 12.35 (red-haired blade from the mud; Bridie called and hauled with Penhale's count).
  The far post needed the punt. The second line caught; **Bridie asked Yeo whether the lying sleeve was ground;
  Yeo answered by the book**; Bridie walked out on it and freed the line. 12.44 the stow turned; the sleeve came
  over Bridie. The corner-hand (thin Tarn boy) had the far edge by the book and was hauling. Kiva shouted *far
  edge* from sixty paces; nobody on that half was listening for it. **Penhale went to the near edge by the book**,
  ward under, lifted, took Bridie's arm; **the sleeve's far end came round over his back.**
- **The pass in three (live, first time):** Pru: "If you cut the post, the skin folds… They're the nearest" (Yeo
  holds the cut). Perrin proposes the pass; Trewin: "I'll plant"; **Yeo: "It's mine. On my word. Do it."** Kiva's
  change from rehearsal: Pru's ward is not put under anything; it is set a pace off as **the nearest thing**, and the
  sleeve's far end folds onto it; Pru finishes the fold to a loaf-sized lump; the whole sleeve comes up off the two
  people after it. *Nobody.* Kiva's Ember (declared, single) carries it **six paces low** across the thread to the
  east mud; it begins to wrap her right fist; *nobody* from the air; **Trewin opens on the word**, plants it; it
  writhes once and lies still. Yeo: "Cut." The far post cut from the punt; the stow dies; the sleeve lies across the
  channel as ash. **It held. Penhale was dead under it already.** Under his grey ward, Bridie alive in an inch of air;
  his hand round her left wrist; he had been counting fathoms to keep her still and "stopped at three."
- Ivey opened his fingers and turned him; "He's gone." Carried up the east bank on the punt's floorboards
  (Ivey, Trewin, Jude, the boatman, Kiva left-handed); **Perrin lifted the head of the boards at the spur's lip
  against Aske's card.** Yeo laid her coat over him and closed his eyes.
- **Yeo's slate (read aloud):** closed at one, on the flood; *Lives: one. D. Penhale… went to the near edge by
  the book and was taken under the far end. The girl lived under his ward.* Hurt as §7. *The skin was taken off
  them, on the officer's word, by a pass in three… Called. Nobody holding it twice.* **Remarks: none, to any
  four. "The remark's the book's. And mine. I said it was ground."**
- Coombe (1.10): kneels with Yeo; to Kiva: "It held… Write it… And his. In the first column. Before."
- Bags: Seven's two dragged up the east bank before the flood (Jude and the boatman); the Tarn's first likewise;
  the Tarn's second (under the far post) unhauled, the creek's. Lower tear under the flood; to be closed near one in the
  morning, at the bottom of the night's ebb, with a guard.

# Monroe Jackson — Owner Voice

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

## Learning discipline

- One edit is an example, not a universal law.
- Promote a broad instinct only when several accepted edits support the same
  reader-facing preference, or when the owner states the preference directly.
- Do not learn voice from continuity fixes, factual corrections, typographical
  repairs, rejected suggestions, reverted edits, or untouched AI prose.
- Do not carry names, setting facts, or plot events from an example into a new
  movement. Learn the difference between WAS and NOW, not the example's subject.
