# Chapter 33 — Kiva: The Week's Crossing

The printer's boy came up Quay Street at five on Thursday morning with a canvas bag on his hip and a stack of pages under his arm, and he was knocking at every door that had a light in it.

Kiva heard him from the loft. She had been awake since four, lying on her back by the north window with her right hand on her chest, because the blisters across her knuckles had come up in the night as Aske had said they would and had woken her with their own small heat. She heard the knocking go along the street toward the Company's office, and stop, and come back. Then she heard Quiller's voice below, cross and thick with sleep, and the boy's voice answering, high and patient.

"Every catcher, the Marshal says. Every one in the town. It's paid for."

She got up and went down the outside stair in her coat with her boots unlaced.

The boy was on the step of the chandlery with Quiller in the doorway above him in his nightshirt. He was perhaps twelve, with ink on both thumbs. When he saw Kiva on the stair he looked at the linen on her right hand, and then at her face, and held out a page without being asked.

"You're the Meridian one," he said. "Mr. Tregear said there'd be one with a bound hand. He said give it to her first if she's up."

She took it in her left hand. It was one sheet, printed on both sides, still faintly damp, and the ink smelled of oil. Across the head of it, above the title, there was a line in a heavier type than the rest.

*After D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company, at Mallow Creek, the 16th of the third month, 2681, who went in by the book.*

Under it, smaller, was the title she had seen in the Marshal's own hand on a wharf at home. *On a Thing That Folds from the Far Edge. The Marshal's page.* Then came the page itself, which was short. Make the thing carry its sheet uphill, if it will. If it will not, put weight on the far edge. The far edge belongs to the second, as a place, and not to the corner-hand. Somebody watches the far end of the thing all through the slack, even while the slack holds. The book is written for a sheet.

That was all. It did not say *a stow* anywhere, or *a sleeve.* It did not need to. Anybody who had stood on the causeway yesterday would read *sheet* and see the other thing.

"Mr. Tregear set it himself," said the boy. "All night. He knew Mr. Penhale's father." He shifted the bag on his hip. "I've two hundred to do before six, miss. The vans and the Company yard and the beats."

"Go on, then," said Kiva. "Thank you."

He went off down the street at a trot. Quiller looked at the page in her hand over the top of his spectacles, and then he took the second copy the boy had left on his step, and went back inside without a word and shut the door.

Kiva stood in the grey street and read it again. She did not cry. She had thought on the cart yesterday that she might, sometime, and had found that it was not that kind of thing. It was a page that had been held back in case it was wrong, and it was out now with a dead man's name at the top, and every catcher on the coast would have it in a pocket by breakfast. She folded it in four and put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where the disk was.

Ivey came up the street at half past five with Kiva's sheets under her arm, tied in their tape.

She had not slept. It was in her face, and in the way she walked, square and steady and a little too carefully, as people walk who have been up all night and mean to go on being up. She stopped at the foot of Quiller's stair and held the bundle out.

"Every one copied," she said. "Twice. One for Yeo and one for me. I've read them to the closing crew's officer already. I'll read them to the crew at six, before the boats." She looked at the Marshal's page showing at the edge of Kiva's coat. "You've had that."

"Five minutes ago."

"So have I. The boy came to the Company yard first." Ivey's square dark hands were empty now, and she put them in her pockets. "Yeo said to tell you two things. She wants your columns for every crew she has, not mine only. And she wants to know who taught you to keep a second's sheet, because she means to have a second on every stow from now to the autumn and she doesn't know where to find one."

"Pike," said Kiva. "Graywater's catcher. He let me watch him."

"Then she'll talk to Pike." Ivey nodded once. "She said, if you'd stand it, she'd like you on the Company yard one evening before you go home, to tell the crews the far edge in your own words. Not the Marshal's. Yours." She half turned to go. "I said you'd say no if it was a Lane night. She said, then not a Lane night."

"Tell her yes," said Kiva. "Not a Lane night."

Ivey went away down Quay Street toward the low town and the Company yard, where the vans were. Kiva stood at the foot of the stair with her own sheets under her arm, which smelled faintly now of the Company's tar and somebody else's lamp, and found that she did not mind it.

---

Aske held her left hand at seven, by the north window, for two minutes by his watch.

The loft was full of people dressing. Halden License was up at the south end, where Pettit was reading something aloud off a slip of paper in a voice too low to carry. Old Four was arguing about who had taken whose boots. Selka Dray sat on the next bed to Seven's with both feet on the floor, lacing a boot, and did not look round. Pru was on her own bed with her hands held up in front of her in their ten little linen caps, waiting her turn. Perrin was sitting very upright on the end of his, in his shirt, with the strapping showing under it, being careful not to breathe.

Aske let go.

"Warm," he said. "Four mornings. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and today." He wrote it on the card against the window frame. "Thursday's come. That's what I said. Soonest."

"I know."

"Then I'll say the price, as I said I would." He did not sit down. He looked at her over his spectacles, and his voice did not change at all. "The fifth went to a hand below the elbow and sulked three nights. The sixth went to the elbow, with an ache in the joint, and sulked four. I think the seventh goes past the elbow. An inch, maybe two. I think it sulks five nights, and I'll not be surprised by six." He put the pen in his pocket. "That's the hand. Here's the other thing, and it isn't the hand at all. It's the calendar. One in seven days. If you go today, the next is Thursday week. If you go Saturday, the next is the Saturday after. There's no crossing between, whatever's on fire."

He did not say *Wednesday.* He did not need to. Everybody within three beds of the window knew what Wednesday was. Dray's hands had stopped on her boot lace.

Kiva sat on the edge of her bed with the hand in her lap.

She had been thinking about it since the cart yesterday, coming back along the causeway with the Longshore going under brown water and Bridie Hebden's legs white under a sheet in the tent behind them. She had thought about it in the printer's street at five, and on the stair with Ivey. She had not said it to anybody, because nobody had asked, and because she had wanted to be sure it was hers before she said it.

On Wednesday at nine minutes past six, if two people and a sealed paper were right, the basin would go still for twenty minutes. Thirty-six hands that were not her aunt's would be on thirty-six glass cases. Somewhere in it, if anybody asked her, she would be calling a thing that three people did, catch and carry and plant, with a count that was not hers. And if one of the three failed, there was exactly one thing in her certificate that could stand in the gap, which was a crossing, on a sanctioned mast, read by the office's reader, one in seven days.

Nobody had asked her for Wednesday. That was the point. If somebody asked on Tuesday, she wanted to be able to say yes without asking anybody to write a new line in a ledger.

"It's not for this week," she said.

Perrin looked up from the end of his bed. He was the captain. He was entitled to ask, and he did.

"Not wanted in the Lane?"

"Not in the Lane. Not today, nor Friday, nor Saturday." She found that she could say it quite plainly. "It's the week's crossing. I'd like to keep it for Wednesday. If nobody wants it on Wednesday, I'll have kept it for nothing, and that's all right. But if I spend it on a lane, I can't have it back."

"You'll fight Saturday on one door," said Perrin. "Whoever we draw."

"I'll fight every day on one door. I've done it before."

He looked at her for a long moment, with his big hands on his knees and his face going the colour it went when he was thinking very hard. Then he nodded.

"Then it's the captain's as well," he said. "Not wanted in the Lane. I'll say so if anybody asks, and they will."

Aske had not moved.

"I've said the price once now," he said, "for a crossing nobody's going to make. I'll say it again on Saturday morning, at this window, in case anybody's changed their mind by then." He looked at her steadily. "I'd sooner say it twice for nothing than once too late. Put that in your book, on the right."

"I will."

Beside her, Dray finished lacing her boot. She pulled the knot tight and stood up and went down the room toward the stair, and she did not say anything, and she did not look at anybody. But Kiva saw her pause at the head of the stair for a moment, with her hand on the post, as a person pauses who has heard a figure that changes a sum.

Aske turned to Perrin.

He did not say anything first. He sat down on the bed beside him, which made Perrin go rigid, and he put two fingers flat on the strapping over the seventh rib on the left side, very lightly, and held them there.

"Breathe in," he said.

Perrin breathed in. It did not get very far.

"Out." Aske moved his fingers an inch. "Again." He listened to something, or seemed to. Then he took his fingers away and looked at the strapping itself, at the place over the rib where the linen had been wound on Tuesday at two. One edge of it had pulled. It had pulled hard enough to fold back on itself, and a crease ran across it like a seam.

"When did you lift?" said Aske.

Perrin went scarlet from the collar up. It was the first time Kiva had ever seen him go the colour that Pru went.

"I didn't," he began, and stopped, because Aske was looking at him over his spectacles with no expression whatever. "At the spur," he said. "Yesterday. The boards. When they carried him up the bank. I took the head of the boards at the lip, for the last two paces. They'd have had to set him down on the stones otherwise." He swallowed. "I knew I shouldn't. I knew it while I was doing it. I'd like it known that I'd do it again."

"I'd have done it myself," said Aske. "I'd have been wrong too." He did not raise his voice. "Here's the price of it, because you didn't ask for it first. A crack across a rib is a crack. A crack through is a break, and a break can go in, and in is your lung. You lifted a dead man's weight on a crack, and the strapping took it, and so did the bone, and I'm glad. It'll not take it twice." He took out his card and wrote, standing, against the window frame, slowly, so that Perrin could read every word over his arm. "*Lifted against the card at the spur, Wednesday the 16th. A fortnight from Wednesday, not from Tuesday. No plant, no lift, nothing heavier than a cup.*" He capped the pen. "That's one day more. I'd have given you a week more if you'd lied to me about it."

"I nearly did."

"I know. You stopped." Aske put the card away. "That's why it's one day."

He looked down the row. Jude was standing by the window with his good arm on the frame, as always, looking at the sea, and his left arm hanging at his side in the sleeve of his shirt.

"Talley," said Aske. "Your arm."

"It's not worse."

"So you've said, on the boat, on the quay, at the Carry, and on the causeway. You've said it to everybody but me." Aske held out his hand. "Roll up the sleeve. Here, in the light, where I can see it. I'll not ask a fourth time, and I'll not stand you in a lane with it on your word."

Jude looked at him. Then he scratched his jaw with his right hand, and came over, and sat down on Kiva's bed, and rolled up his left sleeve to the shoulder.

Kiva had not seen it since the moor. She had seen the sling, and the linen, and Jude holding the arm away from his side as though it belonged to somebody else. She had not seen the arm. From the wrist to a hand's breadth below the elbow the skin was a dull mottled red, shiny in places, with a dry grey edge where the frost had stopped. Along the outside of the forearm there was a darker stripe two fingers wide, where something had pressed. It was not white. It was not black. It looked, Kiva thought, like a burn that somebody had been carrying about in their pocket for a month.

Aske looked at it for a long time without touching it. Then he touched it, very lightly, at the wrist, at the middle, and at the stripe, and watched Jude's face each time.

"That stripe's new," he said.

"The Carry. The sling's loop. Two fingers."

"Two fingers on a frosted forearm with eighty-five kilos of canvas and a carry hanging off it." Aske laid the arm down gently on Jude's knee. "It's mending. You'll keep a mark the length of the stripe, and it'll be stiff in the cold for a year, and you'll never again be able to say it's not worse with a straight face, because I'll know." He took out the card again. "*Talley. Left forearm, frost, mending. The edge on the right fist only. Nothing gripped with the left till Monday.*" He looked up. "You'll fight one-handed. Can you?"

"I've an edge on the right," said Jude. "I've always had. The left was for holding."

"Then hold nothing." Aske stood up. "Finch."

Pru held up her ten caps.

"Ten," said Aske, before he had taken one off. "I know. I did them." He took the first cap off and looked at the fingertip under it, which was blistered and pink and peeling at the edge, and put the cap back on, and did the next. It took him four minutes. Nobody spoke. Dray had come back up the stair for something she had forgotten and stood at the head of it, waiting for him to finish, with her face shut.

"Your door's in your hand," said Aske at last, to Pru. "It's not in your fingertips. You'll catch. You'll not grip a pole with those today, and if you try, you'll tear them, and then you'll not catch on Saturday either." He looked round the four of them, and at Kiva last. "That's your four. A captain who can't plant. A blade with one arm. A catcher who can't grip, and another with a burned hand that writes left-handed." He shut his bag. "I've seen worse go into a lane. Not often."

He went down the stair to his breakfast. Dray stepped aside to let him by, and then went to her bed and took up a glove she had left on the blanket and went down after him.

Perrin sat on the end of his bed and looked at the four of them.

"Well," he said. "I'd hoped to say something about being a team that's been tested. I'd had it ready since five." He breathed carefully. "I think we'll just go and look at the draw."

Anwen Pryce climbed Tar Lane at eight on Thursday with her hands in her sleeves, because she had said *tomorrow* to Maud Lyle on a bench, and tomorrow had come.

The Ropewalk's doors were open. Inside, the long boards ran away under the windows as they had on Wednesday night, bare and black, but they were not empty now. The Ropewalk's first four were at the near end in their shirtsleeves with nobody watching, and Juniper Carrow was walking.

She was not walking alone. Treen walked beside her, a pace back and to the left, with her sleeves rolled and her cord at her belt, and she was talking under her breath. Anwen could not hear the words at first. Then she could.

"*Set.*"

Carrow's right foot came down, and her edge came up on her fist, and stayed. Treen said nothing for three paces. Then she said it again. *Set.* And again Carrow's foot came down hard and square and the edge lit, and Anwen understood that she was not lighting it when she chose. She was lighting it on the word.

Anwen stopped in the doorway.

She had heard that word before. She had heard it from the foot of a ladder in the Inner Lock on Tuesday, in Kiva's voice, flat and clear, every time Perrin's heel came onto a rung. *Set.* Half of Saltmere had been standing on the lock's lip with their mouths shut, and had heard it twenty-two times.

"Pryce," said Lyle, from the bench. "You came."

"I said I would."

"I know you did." Lyle got up. She had the towel round her neck. "She's been at it since six," she said, without looking at Carrow. "Treen calls, and she lights. She says it's the worst thing she's ever done. She says she's never once lit a door on anybody's word but her own, and she feels like a carthorse." Lyle's square face did not change. "She's very good at it already. She's good at everything she hates."

Anwen did not say whose word it was. She thought that Carrow knew perfectly well whose word it was, and that it had cost her something to take it, and that it was not Anwen's business to say so on Carrow's own floor.

"Show me your heels," she said instead.

Lyle opened her stance. Her feet went into the boards, and Anwen felt them go, a post driven into the ridge, sure and heavy. There was nothing at her hip. There was nothing on her hands. She stood planted in the middle of the Ropewalk with her arms at her sides, and she looked, Anwen thought, like a house with the front taken off.

"Lean on me," said Anwen. "Not a wall. You. From the floor."

She put her own shoulder against Lyle's, sideways, as two people stand at a crowded rail. She did not open anything. Lyle leaned. She leaned from the shoulders, as people did who had always had a wall to lean with, and nothing moved, and her stance held her where she was like a nail holds a coat.

"That's from your back," said Anwen. "You're pushing with your back and holding with your feet, and the two don't know each other. That's what a wall did for you. The wall knew both." She stepped away. "Again. Bend your knees. Put your weight down into the floor first, and then let the floor push. Not you. The floor."

Lyle bent her knees. She put her weight down. Anwen saw it go, a long way down, through the boards, into the ridge, and then she saw it come back up, slowly, through Lyle's heels and calves and thighs and the small of her back, until it arrived in her shoulder.

It moved Anwen half a pace.

Anwen stood where she had been put and looked at the boards between her boots.

"That's it," she said. "That's leaning from the floor."

Lyle did not smile. Her face was white, and there was sweat at her hairline, and she was breathing as though she had run the length of the shed. But she looked at the half pace on the boards, and then at Anwen, and something went across her square careful face and stayed there for a moment longer than it usually did.

"Again," she said.

They did it eleven times. On the seventh, Lyle moved her a whole pace. On the ninth, Anwen stopped thinking about it and leaned back, from her own floor, with her own heels right. The two of them stood shoulder to shoulder in the middle of the Ropewalk, neither of them moving, both of them working as hard as they could, and Anwen thought it was the strangest thing she had ever done on a lane floor. There were no doors in it at all, except one still door, and that one was not hers.

At the far end of the near boards, Carrow had stopped walking. She stood with her edge shut and her hands at her sides, watching them, and Treen beside her.

"Pryce," said Carrow. "That's yours, that lean?"

"It's a man called Mace's. On a van's line, at home."

"It's yours now. She's got it off you." Carrow looked at Lyle, and then back at Anwen, with her quick blunt face giving nothing away. "If the lot falls that way tomorrow, she'll lean on your four with it. You know that."

"I know it."

"And you came up the lane anyway."

"I said I would," said Anwen. "She asked me on a bench with her ward gone. I'd not say no to that because of a lot that hasn't been drawn." She took her hands out of her sleeves. "If she leans on us tomorrow, I'll be glad she leans well. That's all."

Carrow considered that.

"In this town," she said, "we'd call that daft."

"In mine we'd call it the same," said Anwen. "I've been called daft before."

Treen laughed. It was a short, startled sound, and she put her hand over her mouth at once, and went red under her dark skin. Carrow did not laugh. But she turned her head and looked at her catcher, and something in her shoulders eased, as though Treen had called a word she had been waiting for.

"*Set,*" said Treen, through her fingers.

Carrow's foot came down. Her edge came up. She walked on down the Ropewalk, ten paces, and turned, and walked back, lighting on the word.

Anwen went down Tar Lane at nine with her shoulder aching and her heels sore. She did not know whether she had done a stupid thing. She thought probably she had. She found that she did not want to have done it any other way, and that she would tell Kiva so, and that she would not tell Perrin, who would only look pleased with her and say something kind.

The draw had gone up on the tent pole at the gate at nine the night before, under the lamp, while the Mercury's boy was selling his late sheet on the Long Quay. Kiva had read it then over the heads of a crowd in green. She read it again now, at a quarter to ten, in daylight, with Perrin at her shoulder and the timber pond lying black and quiet under the boards.

*The Lane. Three lanes for every four, one a day. Thursday's drawn by the standing after three boards: first against twenty-fourth, second against twenty-third, and so down. Friday's and Saturday's drawn by lot at this pole at six the evening before, winners against winners and losers against losers, no four to meet a four twice. A four is placed by lanes won, and then by points for less points against, and marked by place, twenty-four to the first and one to the last.*

And under it, Thursday's twelve lanes, with their hours. Halden License against the twenty-fourth at nine. Old Four at ten. The Ropewalk's first four against the twenty-first at half past ten, in the Graving, before the whole town. And halfway down:

*Two o'clock. Team Seven, Meridian (9), against the Tarn, second four (16).*

"The Tarn," said Perrin.

"Their second four."

"I know which four. I've read it nine times." He had his hand pressed to his side under his coat, where the strapping was. "Their first four's in the healers' tent with their feet in a basin. Their second four's been watching from the tiers all week, and every one of them was on the causeway yesterday afternoon when we came up the bank with Hebden on a stretcher." He was quiet a moment. "They'll not be pleased to see us. They'll not hate us either. I'd sooner be hated. You know where you are."

Beside the draw there was a second board, newer, that Kiva had not seen the night before. It was a plain deal board on two trestles, with a strip of green baize along its top and a row of brass drawing pins. Somebody had chalked across the head of it, in a big careful hand: *DRAFT. Cards may be put up by any chartered company and taken down by the student named. A card is not a seat.*

There were already eleven cards on it.

Kiva did not go close. She saw Jude go close. He went past her without seeming to see her, with the book under his good arm, and stood in front of the board for a long time, reading one card near the middle, in the Company's green. Then he put out his right hand and touched the card's corner, as a man touches the edge of a table in the dark. He did not take it down.

He came back with his face very still and the book under his arm.

"Talley," said Perrin.

"I'll read it again tonight," said Jude. "It's not going anywhere."

He walked on toward the Graving. Perrin looked after him, and then at the board, and then, very carefully, not at the two cards in the bottom corner that had *Cade, P.* written on them in two different hands.

The Graving was half full at two, which was full enough.

It was not the Ropewalk's lane, and the town knew it. The Ropewalk had beaten the twenty-first at half past ten, seven to nothing, in front of every tier in the dock. The green had gone home to its dinner and come back slowly, a few at a time, and it sat now in the lower tiers in the grey light from the glass roof, eating pasties and talking about the morning. The Tarn's brown and buff sat in a knot near the middle. Meridian's colours were above them. Orla was there on the end of a tier, with Aske beside her. Rhea Sorn sat two tiers higher with Pike, whose hands lay on his knees closed halfway.

Down on the floor, eighty feet below the lip, the pen was chalked on the stone, twenty paces wide, with its hurdles and its posts and its seven chalk squares. On its north side, between the pen and the dock wall, the Board had stood a mast. It was not as tall as the gate mast. It came up to a drum at about twice a man's height, with eight pens on eight wires, and two reading desks at its foot. Noa stood at one with his pencil behind his ear. Polglase stood at the other in his steel spectacles, threading tape.

Perrin declared at the marshal's box. He did not go into the pen. He stood behind Seven's hurdle in his coat, with his hands at his sides, and his voice came up the dock's stone walls clear and flat.

"*Team Seven. Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Finch, ward, catcher. Rowan, stance, declared, by her captain's voice. Cade, captain, standing out on his healer's card.*"

There was a murmur round the tiers. *Standing out.* Kiva heard it go round and come back, and she let it go past her. She was looking at the Tarn's second four, coming out of the dock's side door in brown and buff.

They were big. Everybody from the Tarn was big, as if the hills grew them on purpose. Their captain was a thin dark girl with a still door, who walked as though the stone under her were a field she knew. The others came behind her: a heavy blade, a Hold with long arms, a catcher with a fresh bandage on one wrist. They stood behind their hurdle and looked down the pen at Seven, and Kiva saw them look at the linen on her right hand, and Pru's ten caps, and Jude's left arm hanging, and Perrin standing outside the pen with his hand pressed to his side.

Their captain looked longest at Perrin. Then she looked at Kiva, and inclined her head an inch. It was the same nod Trewin gave, done properly.

"She was on the causeway," said Anwen, very low. "Yesterday. She carried one end of Hebden's stretcher to the tent."

"I know," said Kiva.

"*Declared,*" said the marshal. "*Ready.*" He turned his glass.

The first carry showed them what they could not do.

They had made the plan at the long table at eleven, on Perrin's slate, with Perrin drawing it and nobody else allowed to touch the chalk. It was a plain plan. Kiva would plant at the head of the litter with her stance and nobody could shift it while she stood there. Anwen's wall would go up on the south side. Jude would take the north with his edge on his right fist. Pru would stand back of the litter with her ward low and her eyes on hands, and call. When the moment came, Anwen and Jude would lift and walk the Commodore home, and Kiva would shut and run ahead to the hurdle, and she and Pru would take it over from the far side, two to two, top to top.

It went well until the hurdle.

The Tarn came in a square, as the first four had come against Pru in the seeding lane. Their Hold's wall met Anwen's with the sound of a shut drawer, and the two walls leaned, and Anwen's heels were right and the Tarn's were not, and Anwen walked her wall from the far end round in a quarter circle while the other girl was still leaning. Pru called *hands, south* off the Tarn blade's fingers, and Jude was there, and the Tarn blade stopped short of his edge. Kiva stood planted at the litter's head with her stance in the stone and her hands on nothing. Nobody tried to shift her. Nobody wasted the time.

"*Now,*" said Pru.

Anwen and Jude lifted. Anwen had both hands on the head poles, and Jude had his right hand on the foot, with his left arm held against his chest, and the Commodore came up crooked, heavy at the foot, and they walked him. Kiva shut her stance and ran. She vaulted Seven's hurdle and turned on the far side, as Carrow had turned in the Ropewalk, and put up her hands for the head.

Her right hand would not close.

She had known it would not. She had known it at the table at eleven with the chalk in Perrin's fist, and said it, and Perrin had said *wrist, then,* and she had said *wrist.* But a wrist under a pole is not a hand round it, and the head of the Commodore came over the hazel at her and she took it on her left hand and her right wrist and it went down on the right. Pru had the foot. Pru had put her capped fingers round the foot poles, because that was where the foot was. Kiva heard her draw her breath in through her teeth.

The Commodore's foot went down on the hazel.

"*Touched,*" said the marshal. "Dropped. Anybody's."

It was the Tarn's. Their still door was there before Kiva had got her wrist out from under, and their Hold behind her, and they walked the Commodore away from Seven's hurdle as though it had been left out for them. Jude got a half on the Hold's arm as she turned. It was not enough. The Tarn blade touched Anwen on the shoulder as she came round her own wall, quite gently, and Anwen knelt. The glass ran out with the Commodore lying across the middle line on the Tarn's side of it.

"*A down to the Tarn. A point to the Tarn by the half,*" said the marshal. "Two to the Tarn. Nothing to Seven."

The tiers made the warm sound, not loud. Kiva stood on the far side of her own hurdle with her right hand held against her chest. The blisters under the linen had opened. She could feel them wet.

Pru was standing beside her with her hands held up in front of her face. Two of the caps had come off on the poles. The fingertips under them were bleeding a little.

"Don't," said Kiva. "Don't say you're sorry."

"I wasn't going to." Pru's face was white, not scarlet. "I was going to say that I can't grip, and you can't grip, and that's half our hands, and we've two more carries." She looked at the bleeding fingertips with a sort of fury. "Aske said. He said *you'll tear them.* I tore them."

Perrin had come to the hurdle from the outside. He stood with his hands on the hazel, not leaning on it, because leaning would have been a lift of a kind, and looked at them.

"We can't carry it over," he said. "Not two to two. Not with these hands. Not today."

"Then we don't carry it," said Kiva.

They looked at her.

"There's a point at the glass for the half it's lying in." She heard herself say it, and the thing came clear as she said it, as a sum comes clear when you say the numbers aloud. "We can't carry. But I can sit on it." She looked at Perrin. "You said it on the Low Lane, the first year, with a litter pole for a pointer. *They can't carry what they can't lift.* You wanted me on the litter like a hen."

Perrin's face did something complicated.

"I did," he said. "I'd forgotten I said it."

"I'll plant on the poles at the head. Nobody shifts it. Pryce keeps their carry off the foot. Talley takes whoever comes near enough to try. Finch calls." Kiva looked down the pen. "And it's a point. Every carry. And their downs."

"That's not three," said Anwen.

"No. It's one. And whatever Talley cuts." Kiva held her wet right hand against her coat. "And in the third, if it's close, we try the hurdle again. With our wrists and our elbows, and no fingers at all."

Pru was looking at her own hands, at the crooks of her elbows. She lifted her forearms together in front of her, as a girl lifts them to carry a basket of washing on her arms, and looked at the gap between them.

"Elbows," she said. "I can take a pole in my elbows. I've carried coal like that."

"Then that's the third," said Perrin. He took his hands off the hazel. "Go on. Sit on him."

Kiva sat on the Commodore at the start of the second carry and did not move for three minutes and forty seconds.

It was not elegant. Her feet were in the stone. Kiva felt them go, down through the Graving's floor, which was granite and old and very deep, and she felt it hold her as the floor of the Ropewalk had held Lyle. Nobody could shift her. The Tarn's still door came and put both hands on the poles beside her and pulled, and nothing moved, and the girl looked at Kiva from a foot away with an expression of honest surprise.

The blade knelt, a pace from Kiva, with an expression of the deepest disgust.

The third thing the Tarn tried was their catcher. Kiva felt it come up under the canvas. She could not do anything about it with her one door, which was in the floor.

"*Hands,*" said Pru. "*His. North.*"

Jude touched the boy on the arm. It was a half. The boy kept his tray where it was and lifted, and the middle of the Commodore came up off the stone a finger, two fingers, and Jude touched him again on the other arm.

"*Two halves,*" said the marshal. "*Down.* The Tarn."

The boy let go and knelt. The Commodore's middle came down on the stone with a thump, a hand's breadth west of the line. And the glass ran out.

"*The litter's in Seven's half. A point. Two downs.*" The marshal wrote. "Three to Seven. Two to the Tarn."

"I've never seen that done," said the captain. "Not in a lane. Not once."

"It's an old one," said Kiva. "From home. My captain thought of it in our first year, to beat four still doors with."

"It's horrible," said the captain. She said it quite cheerfully. "It's the worst lane I've ever stood. I'll do it to somebody next year." She unfolded her arms. "Hebden's my cousin. Her legs are white, not grey. The healer says she'll stand on Friday."

"I'm glad."

"So am I." The captain looked at Kiva's bleeding hand, and at Pru's. "I'd rather not beat you. I'd rather not lose to you either. It's an awkward thing to feel in the middle of a lane." She turned away. "Third carry. Mind your elbows."

The Tarn girl had to shut to move. Kiva saw her see it. If she stayed planted, Seven would drag the foot home round her; if she came, she would have to shut and stand and lift, and while she lifted she would have nothing open. She chose to come. She shut and came up off the poles with the head in her hands, meaning to run it back east. And Jude was there with his edge on his right fist, and touched her on the back as she turned.

"*Down.* The Tarn."

She knelt. She let go. The head of the Commodore was nobody's.

Anwen had the foot. Jude took the head poles in his right hand only, and the two of them walked the Commodore down the pen to Seven's hurdle, quite fast, crooked, with the Tarn's blade coming and Pru calling *hands* behind them and Anwen's wall coming up and going down again as she needed it. At the hurdle they lifted. Kiva was on the far side. Pru was beside her.

"*Elbows,*" said Pru.

The head of the Commodore came over the hazel. Kiva did not put up her hands. She put up her forearms, crossed at the wrists, as one holds out one's arms for somebody to lay a coat on them. The poles came down into the crooks of her arms and lay there across the linen and the left sleeve, and she held them against her chest as a girl holds a basket of washing. Beside her Pru had done the same with the foot, her capped fingers spread wide and up and out of the way, her forearms crooked.

They walked backward three paces with the Commodore on their arms, clear of the hazel. Nothing touched it.

"*Over,*" said the marshal. "*Three to Seven.*" He leaned over his box and looked at them, at the two catchers standing behind Seven's hurdle with a canvas dummy lying across their arms, and his face did something that a marshal's face is not meant to do. "Six to Seven. Two to the Tarn."

The tiers made a sound that was not the warm sound. It was somebody laughing, high up, and then a great many people laughing, and then the laughing turned into something else. It was clapping, which Kiva had not heard in the Graving all week except for the Ropewalk. It was not loud. It went on a long time.

She and Pru put the Commodore down on the stone together, carefully, as one puts down a sleeping child.

"Elbows," said Pru. She was scarlet now, all the way up. "I said. I've carried coal like that."

"I know. You said."

"I'm saying it again." Pru looked at her own arms, and then at Kiva. "I didn't call that. You didn't call that either. We just both did it." She held up her bleeding fingertips. "That's the first thing I've ever done in a lane that wasn't on somebody's word."

Kiva looked at her. She thought of the yard behind the Long Quay on Sunday, and Pike saying from a tailboard, *you call from the air before you've landed,* and Pru on a seeding floor calling *hands off, left* before the Tarn's carry had moved.

"It wasn't on anybody's," she said. "That's why it worked."

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-34.md

# Chapter 34 — Kiva: Who Has It

The returns were on yellow forms, eleven of them, and Noa had laid them out on the sloping top of his reading desk in a row, weighted at the corners with whatever he had in his pockets.

"The Chair asked for these on the 12th," he said. "It was on her third sheet in the autumn. *A Chair can ask who else has a keeper with a book.* Lumb's notion. She asked the Clerk, and the Clerk asked the Board, and the Board wired every pump house and lamp office on its roll on Saturday morning." He did not look up from the forms. "One question. *Look in your remarks column. Any night on which the whole house did one thing together that was in no column. Wire the date and the line.* The wire office has been bringing them to me since Tuesday, because I'm the one with the columns."

Polglase was at his own desk, a yard off, rolling the day's tape. He did not pretend not to listen.

"Eleven," said Kiva.

"Eleven so far. There are a hundred and forty houses on the Board's roll. Most of them haven't looked yet. Some of them won't." Noa touched the first form with the end of his pencil. "Eight say nothing found. That's a return too. I've written them all down."

He moved the pencil along to the ninth.

*WESTERLEY HARBOUR PUMP HOUSE. KEEPER'S BOOK 2604. 11TH THIRD MONTH. AT THREE THE STEPPED LAMPS ON THE QUAY WHITE. TOWN LAMPS OUT HALF HOUR. KEEPER ILL OF A FEVER, HIS WIFE SAT UP. HE SAID IN THE MORNING THE ROOF WAS OFF. NOT FAULT.*

Kiva read it twice. She was aware of Polglase's tape going round on its spool, and of the timber pond's water moving under the boards, low, on the ebb.

"Westerley's three hundred miles from here," said Noa. "Up the coast. They've a refinery on their quay as well. Not Halden's. A smaller house, under licence." He moved the pencil. "The tenth."

*TARNFOOT WORKS. 2604. 11TH THIRD MONTH. THE FOUR DRUMS STOOD UP TOGETHER A LITTLE AFTER THREE AND LAY DOWN. NO FAULT FOUND.*

"No fever," said Kiva.

"No fever written. That's not the same as none." He did not move the pencil for a moment. Then he moved it to the last form, and left it there.

*HOLLOWAY LAMP OFFICE. 17TH NINTH MONTH 2680. AT TWENTY PAST TWO IN THE AFTERNOON EVERY LAMP ON THE HOLLOWAY RING ONCE. NIGHT LAMP-MAN TOOK ILL THAT EVENING, A FEVER TWO NIGHTS. ASKED BY THE WARDEN WHAT HE SAW IN IT. WOULD NOT HAVE IT WRITTEN. SAID HE WOULD BE LAUGHED AT. A FEVER IS NOT A FAULT.*

Kiva stood at the desk with her bound right hand against her coat and her left hand flat on the wood beside the form.

Twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month. She knew that minute. Everybody who had come south knew it. It was the minute that four husks stood a square in the Pit at home, and Selka Dray went under a corner with her leg broken, and thirty-six cells at the head of this harbour fell into step and had not come out. It was on a bill on every warehouse door on the Long Quay. *IN STEP SINCE TWENTY PAST TWO.*

And two hundred miles off, a man who lit lamps for a living had taken a fever that evening, and had seen something in it, and had not let it be written down.

"He'd be laughed at," she said.

"That's what the warden wired." Noa's voice was quite level. "I've written it as it came. I'll not add to it. I'll not take from it." He took the pencil off the form and put it behind his ear. "There's one more thing. It isn't a return. It's mine."

He unrolled a short length of the gate mast's tape across the forms. It was the night's tape, from midnight to dawn, eight lines running flat along it from the eight pens, because nobody had stood on the mast's crosses since Monday. At one place there was a mark across all eight, top to bottom. It was no taller than a hair is wide.

"Eight minutes to one this morning," said Noa. "By the mast clock. Nobody on the mast." He laid his finger beside it. "I asked the Company's officer at noon whether anything happened on the Longshore in the night. She said her closing crew shut the lower tear at Mallow Creek at the bottom of the ebb, with a guard on the bank. I asked her when. She looked in her book." He did not take his finger away. "Eight minutes to one."

"And yesterday's," said Kiva. "Twelve forty-four."

"Twelve forty-four, when a stow turned. Eight minutes to one, when a tear shut." He rolled the tape again. "That's two. I've looked at every tape since. There's nothing else on them. Two is not a column. I've written them both down, on the same page, and I've not drawn anything between."

"He's begun to look," said Polglase, from his desk, to nobody in particular. "Put that down as well, Mr. Bexley. You've two hairs and an old man who's begun to look."

Noa did not smile. But the corner of his mouth moved.

"I'm putting a fourth column on the sheet for tomorrow night," he said to Kiva. "The Board sits at seven, at the Salt Hall, on the Chair's three. I've people, and rifts and spawn, and Saltmere. I'm adding ash." He touched the ledger on the desk's ledge. "What the comb says. How much of every year's yield sang on the seventh. It goes up with the others. It went up faster from the fifth month, the same as the others." He looked at her. "I'll draw the four on one sheet with one rule along the bottom for the weeks. I'll not draw anything between them. If the Board wants a line, it'll have to draw it itself."

"They'll ask you to."

"They can ask." He began to gather up the forms, one at a time, in their order. "I'd be a fool to draw it. I've drawn one wrong before."

She walked along the boards to the end of the timber pond and back, twice, with the Holloway form in her head.

*Would not have it written. Said he would be laughed at.*

She knew exactly how he had felt. She had felt it on a ferry, with a hand on her wrist and a voice counting, and something in her that had learned at a hem to keep two words behind her teeth until they were owed. She had kept her mouth shut on the ferry and the barge and the coast boat. She had kept it shut at the Proprietor's table with her jaw aching. She had been, she thought, rather proud of how well she kept it shut. It had seemed like a thing she had learned, like a catcher learns not to speak until the bag is sealed.

But a catcher's second keeps her mouth shut so that she can say the thing afterward, to the person who needs it. That was the whole of the rule. Two words, once, and then you said what he had done that he did not say aloud. A second who never said it at all was not a second. She was only somebody standing at a hem.

A lamp-man in Holloway had kept his mouth shut, and nobody would ever know what he had seen, because nobody had been sitting at a kitchen table with a pencil and a clean page. A keeper's wife in Westerley had sat up with her husband, and he had said *the roof was off* in the morning, and somebody had written it in remarks and put *not fault* under it. A keeper at Ferren had written it in brown ink because the needles did it too. A keeper here had written it because his father had told him to write what the needles did. Every one of them had put it in the remarks column, which was where you wrote a thing that was not a fault and not anything else. Not one of them had been believed, because there was no way on earth to tell whether a man with a fever had borrowed it from somebody.

Except that there was one page in the country that had been sealed before anybody could have borrowed anything. It had been written at ten o'clock at night in the girl's own words, on the same night as the dream. It was shut under a reader's wax with the hour written through the join, in a press in a basement two hundred miles away, before the girl had ever opened a grey box and read *the sky unroofed.* Mara had done that so that nobody could ever say Kiva had borrowed. Kiva had thought, last spring, that the seal was there to protect her. She saw now that it had never been for that. A seal is not for keeping a thing safe. It is for being opened in front of people, so that they can believe what is inside.

She stopped at the end of the boards by the Graving's old wall, where she had stood at a quarter to seven with two fingers on a mooring ring.

It was hers. Mara had said so on these same boards a week ago, with a gull on the black water. *You needn't tell anybody. That's the only thing I know for certain about any of this. It's yours.* It was hers to keep, and she had kept it for four years.

It was also, she thought, a thing with no handles. It lived in one head, the way the three doors lived in one hand. It was worth nothing to anybody while it stayed there. It was worth nothing to a lamp-man in Holloway who would not have it written down.

She would put it on the table.

She did not decide it all at once. She found that she had decided it somewhere between the mast and the wall, and that she was only now noticing. It sat in her as the week's crossing had sat that morning. It was not like a door being shut. It was like a coin being put down on a counter, where anybody could see it.

Then she thought about what it would cost, because that was the next thing a person did, and she made herself do it properly.

Everybody in the city would know. Everybody on the tiers would look at her tomorrow in the Graving and see a girl who dreamed, and not a catcher. The Mercury would have it. Halden's counsel would have it, and would want to know what she had seen, and so would the Order, and so would the Proprietor, who had sat across a table from her a week ago and told her about a window in 2604 while she held her teeth together. Dray would have it. Carrow would have it. Her four would have it from somebody else, if she did not give it to them first.

That was the first thing, then. Her four, before anybody.

She went back along the boards toward Quiller's, in the dusk, and she did not hurry. The quay lamps were coming on along the Long Quay, white and steady, one after another, and she counted them as she walked without meaning to, and stopped herself at four.

She asked them to come down to the shop at eight.

Quiller had let her have it before, for Ivey. He let her have it again, without asking why, and went up to his own rooms at the back with a candle and shut his door. The shop was dark and smelled of tar and new rope and the paraffin in the stove. Kiva lit the one lamp on the counter, the one Ivey had read by, and set it between the coils.

They came down the outside stair one at a time. Pru first, with her capped hands held up in front of her and two of the caps fresh, because Aske had redone them at five. Then Jude, with the book under his good arm. Then Anwen, who had been somewhere and had tar on her boots. "I dream," she said.

"Not like anybody dreams," she said. "With a fever. When I was Kindled, at thirteen, in a yard. On Classification Day, in the hall. And on the ferry below the Order's house, last month." She told them.

She told it as she had told it to Mara at a kitchen table at ten o'clock at night, with a wet cloth on her chest. She told them about the height, and being above things, looking down. About the roads, from every side at once, and the lights on them, which were all unlike, and were people. About not knowing who they were, and knowing what they were, because she knew what she was. About looking up, and there being nothing there. Not dark. Nothing. And something in the nothing that was enormous, and close, and paying attention. She told them about the notes, and how she had tried to count them once and lost her number at four, and the lights touching. She told them about the lamps of the whole city answering.

She told them that on the ferry she had been lower than before, and that the lights had been nearer, and that some of them had been going out.

She did not tell them the rest of the ferry. She found that she had not decided, yet, whom that was for. It was not a thing to be said in a shop to four people who had come down a stair at eight o'clock because she asked. It might be a thing for somebody else. She held it back, and knew that she was holding it back, and let them see that she knew.

"There's more about the ferry," she said. "I'll say it where it's asked for. I'm not keeping it from you. I don't yet know who it's for."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Pru had gone scarlet, slowly, from the collar. She did not look away.

"I knew something came," she said. "On the barge. You had your teeth shut so hard I thought you'd break them. And at the Counting House, after, you said *nothing came,* out loud, and I knew that meant it came the other times." She swallowed. "I wanted to know so badly I couldn't sleep. I said I'd not ask. I didn't."

"I know you didn't. That's why you're the first."

"I'm not the first. Your aunt's the first." But Pru's face had changed. "I'm the first in this shop. I'll take that."

Anwen was sitting on the coil of rope with her hands in her sleeves, looking at the lamp.

"What's behind it?" she said.

"I don't know." Kiva met her eyes. "Nobody does. I'm not going to say what it is, because I can't, and I'd be lying if I tried. It's just there. It's been there since I was thirteen, and it's in a Glory master's notebook from two hundred years ago, and in a pump keeper's book at Ferren, and a pump keeper's book here, on the same night in 2604. And this afternoon it came in on a wire from Westerley, and another from Holloway." She told them about the Holloway form. *Would not have it written. Said he would be laughed at.* "That's why I'm telling you. I've been the lamp-man for four years. I'm going to stop."

"Stop how?" said Perrin.

"Mara wrote it down, the night it came, last spring. My words. She sealed it and had a reader at the Library put the hour on it, through the wax, before I'd ever read the notebook. It's in a press in Meridian." Kiva put her left hand flat on the counter. "It's the only page in the country that nobody can say was borrowed. I'm going to ask the Board to open it."

"In front of everybody," said Anwen.

"In front of whoever the Board sits in front of."

Perrin had not moved from the counter. He had his hand pressed to his side under his coat. He looked at her for a long while with his face going the colour it went when he was thinking.

"They'll look at us," he said at last. "All of us. Tomorrow, in the Graving, with Carrow at the other end of the pen. Everybody on the tiers will be looking at you and not at the litter."

"They already look," said Kiva.

"They look at the girl with three doors. That's different. That's a thing you can do." He breathed carefully. "This is a thing you are. They'll look at you as though you'd been sent for. I know you. You'd hate that worse than anything." He paused. "I'm not saying don't. I'm the captain, and it's not a captain's to say. I'm saying I know what it'll cost you, and I'll be on the hurdle with my hands in my pockets when they look."

Kiva found that she could not say anything to that, so she nodded.

Jude had not spoken. He was standing by the door with the book under his arm, scratching his jaw slowly with his right hand, and looking at the counter, at her hand on it.

"I asked everybody what ash was for," he said. "Since the autumn. I asked the sea." He stopped. "I never asked what you were for. I thought you were a catcher with three doors, and that was enough to be going on with." He looked up. "I'd want it said that I'm glad you told us before you told the Board. That's all I've got."

"It's enough," said Kiva.

Anwen got up off the coil of rope. She did not come round the counter. She put her hands flat on it, opposite Kiva's, as she put her hands on a card before she signed it.

"I want it said too," she said. "When they look at you tomorrow, I'll be at your back with my wall up, and if anybody says a word on that floor that isn't about the litter, they'll be saying it to me." She did not go red. Anwen never went red. "And when you tell the Board, I'll be there. Not to say anything. To be there."

"So will I," said Pru. Then she went scarlet all over again. "I'd like to see Halden's face."

Perrin laughed. It hurt him; Kiva saw it hurt him. He laughed anyway, with his hand pressed hard to his side, and then he stopped.

"Who else?" he said. "Before the Board."

"My aunt. Tonight. She's to send for the page, because it comes out to her hand and nobody else's." Kiva counted it on her fingers, the left ones. "Aske, in the morning. Orla. Kellan, before the Lane." She looked at Perrin. "The Board, at the sitting tomorrow night. I'll stand up and ask."

"Not the Clerk first?"

"No. If I ask the Clerk at her tent, she'll write it down and put it in the press and say it'll be heard when it's heard. I want to ask in the room." Kiva took her hand off the counter. "That's where the sheets are. The returns. Noa's columns. That's where it goes. In the first column, with the others. Before."

Nobody argued. Jude opened the book under his arm, and found the page, and took the pencil from its string.

"I'll not write what you told us," he said. "It's not the book's." He wrote one line, and turned the book round so that she could read it.

*Thursday the 17th. At Quiller's, at eight. Rowan told us a thing of her own. We'll stand where she stands.*

Under it, without being asked, the other three signed. Perrin signed last, with his hand pressed to his side and his face scarlet, and then gave her the pencil.

She signed it left-handed. It was very bad. Nobody said so.

Mara Rowan had her left hand, her reading hand, flat on a glass case on the keeper's table at the harbour pump house at ten that night, and it was not one of the thirty-six.

It was the training cell. Peverell had had it carried up from the fitting shop on Wednesday, an old glass case on an iron stand that had been in the Regiment until a crack in its foot put it on the bench in the eighth month. It was not in the bank now. It was not in the water. It sat on the keeper's table under the lamp with its own light turning inside it, slow and yellow, on its own count, salted on Tuesday out of a soot-cut jar that somebody had sold the Company in the winter as plain. Soot-cut plain was no use for a bank. It was good enough to teach with.

Beside it on the table there were two more cells. One was keeping a count Peverell had given it with her own hands that morning, three slow and a quick, like a woman beating a rug. The other was in step. Peverell had salted it from the pump house's own store, which was all the bank's, and it went four and four, fifty-seven to the minute, like everything else in this town.

Round the table, on stools, sat twelve fitters in their aprons, with their hands on their knees.

"Again," said Mara. "Hart's mate. You."

The man she pointed at was a heavy, careful fellow with a burn scar on his wrist. He got up and came to the table and laid his right hand flat on the first case, as she had shown him, with his fingers spread and his palm down on the glass.

"Whose is it?" said Mara.

He shut his eyes. That was allowed. Most of them did it the first week.

"Its own," he said, after a while. "Slow. It doesn't care about anything."

"And that one."

He moved his hand to the second. "Peverell's. The rug." He moved it again, without being told, to the third, and his face changed. "That's the bank," he said. "That's the town."

"How do you know?"

"It's in my teeth." He took his hand away and looked at it. "It's in everything. When I put my hand on the other two I could feel the bank under them, a bit, through the table. When I put it on that one there wasn't anything under it but more of itself."

"That's right," said Mara. "That's exactly right. Write that down, somebody. *There wasn't anything under it but more of itself.*" One of the fitters, a girl of nineteen with ink on her apron, wrote it on a slate. "On Wednesday, at the word, every one of you will have a hand on a cell like that. You'll not be asked to change it. You'll be asked to say whether it's still the town's, when the can goes in. *Town* or *mine.* That's all. Out loud, to the person next to you, and then to me." She took her own hand off the training cell. "Thirty-six people, thirty-six words. I'll not touch a case. I'll stand on the landing with a watch."

She said it, she noticed, as though it were nothing. It had taken her a fortnight.

"And if we're wrong?" said the girl with the slate.

"Then you'll have said *town* when it was *mine,* and the cell will go back in, and you'll have learned something that I couldn't teach you on a table." Mara folded her hands on her apron. "Mr. Hart will have you at the grating tomorrow night at the slack, all twelve. It's the neaps. The quiet walks with the table at the neaps, he tells me, and comes when the sea gate says it will and not a minute before. On Wednesday it won't. On Wednesday it'll come at nine minutes past six whatever the sea's doing, if we're right, and nobody in this room can tell you why." She looked round the twelve faces. "That's not a thing to be frightened of. It's a thing to write down."

Somebody knocked at the keeper's door.

Mara knew the knock before it came again. She had heard it on the shop door in Coper Street for twelve years, at every hour of the day and night, two knocks and a pause and one more. She did not get up. She found that she could not, for a moment, and that her reading hand had gone flat on her apron of its own accord.

"Come in," said Peverell, from the press.

Kiva came in. She had her right hand in fresh linen against her coat and her book under her left arm, and her face was the face she had worn at thirteen on the morning after the yard, when she had come to the kitchen table to say what she had seen. It was white and quite steady.

"I'm sorry," she said to Peverell. "I'd like a word with my aunt."

"Have the room," said Peverell. "Out, the twelve of you. Tomorrow at seven. Bring your slates." She took her spectacles off their cord and put them in her apron, and looked at Mara over the training cell, once, with her small eyes. Then she went out after her fitters and shut the door.

Mara stayed on her stool. She did not ask anything. She had promised herself on a kitchen floor four years ago that she would never ask the girl anything about that, only write it down when it was told, and she had kept the promise through a fever on a ferry that she had heard of by letter, and another on a quay that she had heard of from Aske. She folded her hands.

"I've told my four," said Kiva. "Tonight. At Quiller's. All of it that's on your page, and that it came on the ferry."

"I see."

"I'm going to ask the Board to open the page. Tomorrow night, at the Salt Hall, when they sit on the Chair's sheets." Kiva stood on the other side of the keeper's table with the three cells turning between them. "It comes out to your hand, the Reader said, or a magistrate's. Nobody else's. I'd like you to send for it."

There were a great many things Mara could have said. She knew all of them. She could have said *are you sure.* She could have said *you needn't.* She could have said that the Proprietor would take it to the Board as evidence for stepping the country, and that the Order would take it as evidence for shutting every door but one, and that the Mercury would take it as a headline, and that none of them would ask the girl what she meant by it. She could have said that she had sealed that page so that nobody could ever accuse the girl of borrowing, and not so that a hall full of strangers could read it aloud. She could have said that she was afraid.

Every one of those things would have been true. Every one of them would have been a hand on the choice, and the choice was not hers.

"Who first?" she said instead. "After me."

"My four. Aske in the morning. Orla. Kellan. Then the room." Kiva's voice did not shake. "I'll keep back one thing from the ferry. I don't know yet who it's for."

"That's yours as well." Mara stood up. Her knees had decided, and so she let them. "The wire office at the gate is open all night for the Board. The Reader's at the Library from seven. If I wire her now, she'll have it when she unlocks the door." She took a sheet of the pump house's fault paper from the press, because it was the nearest paper, and sat down again, and wrote.

*L. ARKWRIGHT, READER, COMPACT LIBRARY, MERIDIAN. THE PAGE IN YOUR PRESS UNDER YOUR WAX OF MONDAY 25TH SIXTH MONTH 2680 IS WANTED BEFORE THE CHARTER BOARD AT SALTMERE ON SUNDAY. TO MY HAND. IF YOU WILL BRING IT YOURSELF I WILL PAY THE TRANSIT. IF YOU WILL BRING ANNICK PERSONAL UNDER YOUR OWN CUSTODY THE BOARD WILL ASK FOR IT. M. ROWAN.*

She read it over once. Then she took a second sheet and wrote a second.

*LUMB, KEEPER, FERREN PUMP HOUSE. THE BOOK FOR 2604 IS WANTED BEFORE THE CHARTER BOARD AT SALTMERE ON SUNDAY. GIVE IT TO THE LIBRARY READER IF SHE COMES. IF SHE DOES NOT, BRING IT. M. ROWAN.*

"Sunday?" said Kiva.

"The transit's a day and a half. If she takes the morning one, she's here on Saturday night. The Board won't open a sealed page on a Saturday night in a dock with the Measure being read." Mara folded the two sheets. "You'll ask tomorrow, in the room, and the Board will say Sunday, and that's what it'll say because there's no other day it can say. I'm only writing what's so."

Kiva looked at the two folded sheets on the table.

"You're not going to ask me anything," she said.

"No."

"Not even whether I'm sure."

"You came to the door and knocked twice and once." Mara put the sheets in her apron pocket. "You've been sure since you knocked. I've heard you knock on the shop door like that since you were five. You only ever did it when you'd decided something." She stood up again. "I'll take these to the wire office myself. Now. Go to bed. You've Carrow at eleven."

Kiva did not go. She stood by the keeper's table looking at the three cells turning in their cases, the one on its own count, the rug, and the town.

"The man in Holloway," she said. "The lamp-man. He'd not have it written."

"I read the form. Noa brought it to the Works' lodging at six."

"He'd nobody with a pencil," said Kiva. "That's all. I had you."

Mara found that her reading hand had gone flat on her apron again, and that she could feel the weave of the cloth under it, and every thread. She left it there.

"Go to bed," she said. "I'll wire."

The girl went. Mara heard her boots go down the iron stair to the outfall walk, quick and uneven on the bound side, and then along the walk toward the low town. She stood by the keeper's table with the two folded sheets in her pocket and did not move for some time.

She had not owned it. She had not said one word that put a hand on it. She had written two wires because they were hers to write, and asked one question, and she would carry the wires to the office now, at a quarter to eleven at night, along the Long Quay under the white lamps, and pay for them out of her own purse.

Tomorrow night, at the Salt Hall, she would have to stand up in front of the Board and say what she meant to do on Wednesday, under her own name, with Halden's counsel watching and the Harbour Committee counting the minutes the low town would be wet. She had known that since the afternoon. She had thought it was the hardest thing she would do this week.

She took her hand out of her apron pocket and laid it flat on the training cell, the one on its own count, and held it there. It was slow and yellow and did not care about anything. Under it, through the table and the floor and the old stone of the pump house, faint, there was the town.

"Mine," said Mara Rowan, to nobody, and took her hand away, and went to send the wires.

# Chapter 35 — Kiva: One Door Each

Kellan was at the draft board at eight, in the black and gold, reading his own card with his hands in his pockets.

Kiva saw him from across the boards and went over. She had meant to find him at the loft and had missed him; he had gone down the stair at six, Pru said, before anybody else was dressed, with his boots in his hand so as not to wake the room. Now he stood alone in front of the deal board with the green baize along its top, and there were nineteen cards on it, and he was reading the one with the Halden mark at its head as though it were a sum that would not come out.

He heard her and did not turn.

"You've heard," he said.

"Heard what?"

"Then you haven't." He took one hand out of his pocket and touched the card's corner, as Jude had touched his yesterday, and put the hand back. "It doesn't matter. It's mine. What do you want, at eight in the morning, with your hand in a bandage and that face on?"

"To tell you something before you hear it in a hall tonight," said Kiva. "So that you've had it first, from me."

He turned round then.

She told him on the boards by the draft board, with the timber pond lying black beside them and the gate mast standing over Noa's empty desk. She did not tell him all of it. She told him that she dreamed with a fever, and had since she was thirteen; that it was the thing in Annick's notebook and the Ferren keeper's book and the keeper's book at the pump house here, on the same night in 2604; that her aunt had written it down the same night last spring, in her words, and had it sealed before she read anybody else's; and that she meant to stand up at the Salt Hall at seven and ask the Board to open the page.

Kellan listened with his face quite still. When she had done, he did not say anything for a while. He looked at the gate mast.

"That's not a sum," he said at last.

"No."

"I was trying to make it one. While you talked. I can't." He took his hands out of his pockets. "I'll tell you what I can do with it. The house will want it. He'll want it more than he wanted the three, because the three was a thing you could do and this is a thing you've seen. He's been looking for somebody who saw what he saw for seventy-seven years." Kellan's voice was flat, as it went when a sum was bad. "He'll be glad. That's the part that frightens me. He'll be so glad."

"I know."

"Do you? He sat at that table and told you about a window. I've heard him tell it four times at dinner. Every time he gets to *white* his voice goes quiet." Kellan stopped. "I'm not telling you not to. I'd not. I'm telling you what my house will do with it, because nobody else in that hall will know."

"That's why I told you first," said Kiva.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and glanced back at the card on the baize behind him, with the Halden mark at its head.

"I've a thing of my own tonight," he said. "In that hall. It's not as big as yours. It's big enough." He did not explain. "I'll see you on the floor at eleven. Halden's at nine. I'll be on the tiers for Carrow, with the rest of Meridian, wanting you to lose and wanting you to win." He nearly smiled. "I'd expect nothing else of myself."

He went away toward the Graving's steps with his hands back in his pockets.

Jude was at the board when Kiva turned round, with the book under his good arm, reading his own card again for the fourth time.

He did not mind her reading it over his shoulder. He moved a little, to let her. It was a plain white card in the Company's green, pinned at the top with one brass pin, in Yeo's brisk upright hand.

*The Saltmere Company. Talley, J., edge, Meridian, Team Seven. To the closing crew under Officer Yeo, on the Longshore and the boats, at a Company hand's wage, housed, from the first of the fourth month. Six months, and then we look again. A card is not a seat. Yeo.*

Under it, smaller, in the same hand, there was one more line.

*You asked me on the Ropewalk steps what it was for. Come and find out.*

"She was at the board on Wednesday night," said Jude. "Behind us. She didn't say anything." He looked at the line at the foot. "I knew she would. I said so at the window. I said I'd stay if she asked." He scratched his jaw with his right hand. "She's asked. I'd thought I'd feel something. I feel as though I've been handed a parcel and I don't know what's in it, and it's heavy, and it's mine."

"You don't have to answer today."

"I know. It says six months, and then we look again." Jude almost smiled. "That's Rhea's card. She's copied Rhea's card. She'll have had it off Rowan's columns, through Ivey." He touched the corner of it again. "I'll answer after Wednesday. I want to see the bank come out first. If it comes out." He looked at the sea, past the end of the timber pond and the Graving's gate, grey and breathing. "If I'm to spend six months carrying ash up a bank, I'd like to know first that a bank can be carried down."

Perrin's two cards were in the bottom corner, where they had been yesterday. He was standing three paces off, pretending to read the Lane board.

One card had the Halden mark. *Halden Lift Yards, the Long Quay. The winner of the Carry, Meridian, to the lift yard, carry, at a carry's wage, on the Proprietor's roll, from his healer's leave.* The other was on the Works' grey paper, in a small square hand. *The Municipal Works, Meridian. Cade, P., carry, to the trunk crew at the Brickfields, from the autumn, when his card is up. Rusk.*

"You got face," said Perrin. "Ten seconds."

"I got face. I'm not saying I didn't." Gower bit his pasty. "I'm saying two. Hesketh's been at the board since seven counting other people's. Half the loft's been." He went away, eating.

"I wanted it," he said. Kiva did not think he was saying it to her. "On the boat. Something with only my name on it. I said so to Pryce on the Ropewalk steps." He reached out with his free hand and took the Halden card down off its pin. "That one hasn't got my name on it. It's got *the winner of the Carry.* They've carded a Tuesday."

It was mine, the ladder, but it was one Tuesday. P. Cade.* Then he pinned the card back up, face to the board, so that only the back showed.

"And the other?"

"It says *Cade.*" He did not touch the grey card. "It says *when his card is up.* Rusk read the Chair's ledger and Aske's card both before he wrote it. That's a man who's counted the ribs." He looked at it for a moment longer. "I'll leave it up. I'll answer at home."

"Gower thinks I'm pleased," he said.

"Aren't you?"

"I'm pleased about the grey one." He went on down. "I'd thought being envied would feel like the top of the ladder. It feels like Gower eating a pasty and counting." He was quiet for three steps. "I'd sooner be at the foot of the ladder with you saying *set.*"

The Graving was full at a quarter to eleven, and the whole of it was green.

Kiva had thought it was full on Saturday, against the Tarn. She had been wrong. On Saturday there had been room on the tiers to put a hand down beside you. Now the green came down the steps to the floor's edge and stood there, ten deep on the bottom tier, and the boys who sold pasties had given up selling and were sitting on their baskets. The brown and buff of the Tarn sat in its knot in the middle. Meridian's colours were above it, all four of the first company, and Kellan among them in his black and gold. The iron and glass roof went over all of it, and the grey light came down on the white chalk of the pen eighty feet below as it comes down into a church.

At the seaward end the floating gate held the harbour out, and Kiva could hear the sea breathing against its timber. Under that, through the stone of the dock floor, there was the town's count.

The Ropewalk's first four came out of the side door in their shirtsleeves, and the Graving stood up.

It did not cheer. It stood, all of it, tier after tier, as a church stands. Carrow walked first, with the rope on her sleeve. Lyle walked half a pace behind and to one side, as she always had, but there was nothing at her hip now, and her hands hung at her sides. Tolver came after with his cheerful face. Treen came last, small and dark, with her sleeves rolled and her cord at her belt.

They stood behind their hurdle and looked at the Commodore lying in the middle of the pen. Then Carrow looked down the pen at Seven.

She looked at Perrin outside the pen, behind Seven's hurdle, with his hand on his side. She looked at Jude's left arm, and Pru's ten caps, two of them fresh, and the linen on Kiva's right hand. She looked at them each for exactly as long as it took to see what was there, and then she looked at Kiva and inclined her head, as a fact about the weather.

"*Declarations,*" said the marshal.

"The Ropewalk," said Carrow. Her voice went up the stone walls, low and flat and pleasant, with the coast in it. "*Carrow, edge. Declared. Single.*"

The Graving made a sound. It was very small. It was the sound of four thousand people drawing in their breath and not letting it out.

"*Lyle, stance. Single. Tolver, stance. Treen, ward.*"

At the mast on the north side of the pen, Noa wrote. Polglase wrote. Kiva saw Noa look up at Carrow from his desk, once, with his narrow face quite still, and then down again.

"*Team Seven,*" said Perrin. "*Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Finch, ward, catcher. Rowan, stance, declared, by her captain's voice. Cade, captain, standing out on his healer's card.*"

"*One door each,*" said the marshal, to nobody in particular, and then looked as though he wished he had not. He turned his glass. "*Ready.*"

They had made the plan at the long table at half past nine, and it was Thursday's plan, because Thursday's plan had worked.

Kiva would plant on the litter's head and sit there, as she had sat on it against the Tarn, so that nobody could carry what nobody could lift. Anwen's wall would keep their still doors off the foot. Jude would take whoever came near, with his edge on his right fist. Pru would call hands. And at the glass, the point was Seven's, every carry, and whatever Jude cut, and in the third carry, if it was close, they would try the hurdle with their elbows.

Carrow had been on the tiers on Thursday afternoon. Kiva had seen her, two rows below Rhea, with Treen beside her, watching.

The bell went.

Kiva went for the litter. She got there first. She went down astride the head poles with her forearms along the canvas and opened her stance, and her feet went into the Graving's floor, down through the granite, and held. Anwen's wall came up on the south side of the foot. Jude went north. Pru stood behind the litter's middle with her ward at her hip, low, and her eyes on hands.

Lyle walked down the pen and stopped two paces from Anwen's wall, and planted.

She did not have a wall. She had nothing at her hip and nothing on her hands. She stood with her feet in the stone and her arms at her sides, and then she bent her knees, and put her weight down into the floor, and leaned her shoulder against Anwen's wall.

Kiva saw Anwen feel it. She saw Anwen's heels go into the stone, right, and her wall bow, a finger, two fingers. Lyle was leaning from the floor. She was leaning as Anwen had taught her to lean on a bare board at eight o'clock yesterday morning, with her weight going down and coming back up through her heels and her back and her shoulder, and it was moving Anwen's wall.

"*She's got it,*" said Anwen, between her teeth. "*I gave it her.*"

Carrow had not moved from the Ropewalk's end of the pen.

She stood a pace behind Treen with her edge lit on her right fist, bright as a poker, and her feet on the stone like anybody's, and she was not looking at the litter at all. She was looking at nothing. Treen was looking at Jude.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

Carrow walked. It was not a run. She walked down the south side of the pen, past the chalk squares, toward the litter, with her edge lit and her feet coming up off the stone at every step. Kiva felt it go past her through the floor, loud, as Lyle had said it would be loud. It was one door, walking.

Jude went to meet her. That was the plan. Whoever came near, Jude took them.

"*Hands, north,*" said Pru.

Jude checked. Tolver had come up on the north side, very fast for a big boy, with his long arms out and his still door ready, reaching for the litter's head poles where they stuck out past Kiva's forearms. Jude turned to take him. It was the right thing to do. Pru had called it, and it was right.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

Carrow was a pace from the litter. Her edge came down on the word, flat, quite lightly, on Kiva's shoulder.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "Seven."

Kiva knelt. She had to shut and let go to kneel, and the moment she let go of the poles Tolver had them. He did not lift. He held the head, and Lyle came off Anwen's wall, and the two still doors took the Commodore by the poles together and walked him back down the pen as two men carry a sleeping friend upstairs. Carrow walked beside them with her edge lit. Jude went for Tolver's back and Treen said *hands* and Carrow's edge came round between them, and Jude stopped, because the alternative was to walk into it.

At the Ropewalk's hurdle Carrow shut and vaulted the hazel, and turned, and took the head poles from under on the far side. Treen came over after her and took the foot.

"*Three,*" said the marshal. "*And a down. Four to the Ropewalk.*"

The Graving did not make the warm sound. It let its breath out. It was a long sound, like a wave going back off shingle, and it went round the tiers and came back, and then the green began to talk all at once.

Kiva knelt on the stone in the middle of the pen with her hands on her knees and counted ten, as a downed fighter counts, and did not look at anybody.

She had seen it. She had seen it from her knees, a pace off, with her shoulder still warm where the edge had touched. Carrow had not looked at the litter. She had not looked at Jude. She had walked down the pen with her eyes on nothing and her edge lit, and she had brought it down when Treen spoke and not a hair before. Treen had been watching Jude's hands. When Jude's hands went north, Treen said *set,* and Carrow came down on the nearest thing she could reach, which had been Kiva.

She was not reading the lane. She was being read for.

"She's taking Treen's count," said Kiva, at the hurdle, in the pause.

Perrin was outside the pen with his hands on the hazel, not leaning. Anwen was white and breathing hard, with her forearms shaking in her sleeves. Jude had his edge shut and his right fist opening and closing at his side.

"She's never done that," said Kiva. "Not in the Ropewalk, not at the Hunt. Carrow reads everything herself. That's what she is." She heard her voice go flat, and let it. "Now she doesn't read anything. She walks, and Treen reads, and Treen says *set,* and she brings it down. It's what I did to you on the ladder. *Set.* Off your heel."

"She's had it off you," said Anwen. "On the Inner Lock. Half the town was on the lip."

"She's had it off me," said Kiva. "And it works. She's not got two doors to lay, so she's got two people instead. Treen's eyes and her edge."

"So we take Treen's eyes," said Jude.

"How?" said Perrin.

Kiva had been asking herself that on her knees for a count of ten. She looked at Pru.

Pru had her hands up in front of her face in their caps. She was looking at them, at the linen on the tips of her fingers, two of them fresh, and then at Kiva's right hand in its bandage, and then at Jude's left arm hanging.

"Treen watches hands," said Pru slowly. "Like me. She's a hand door. She watches hands because she is one." She looked up. "That's what you said to me. On the steps. After the Tarn."

"Yes."

"So she called *set* off Talley's hands going north. And she'll call *now* off whoever's got their hands on a pole." Pru turned her capped fingers outward. "We haven't got hands. Not on the poles. Not you and not me. We carried it over on our elbows yesterday."

Kiva looked at her.

"If we carry it on our arms," said Pru, "there's nothing for her to read. No fingers. No hands. Only elbows." She went scarlet, all the way up. "She'll have to read something else, and she's never had to. And by the time she's found it, we're at the hurdle."

Perrin made a sound that was not quite a laugh. He took his hands off the hazel.

"No sitting on it," he said. "Not against a blade who's being read for. We carry. On your arms. Finch and Rowan on the poles, elbows, all the way down the pen, and Pryce and Talley off them." He looked at Anwen. "Lyle's leaning on you with your own lean."

"I know she is."

"Then don't lean back. Walk." Perrin's face was the colour it went when he was thinking very hard. "Walk your wall from the far end, round her, the way you'd walk it round a post. She's planted. She can lean, but she can't follow."

"*Rowan,*" said the marshal. "*Your declaration for the second carry.*"

"*Ember,*" said Perrin, before Kiva could open her mouth. "*Rowan, Ember, declared, single, by her captain's voice.*" He did not look at her. "You'll not need your stance if you're not sitting on it. You'll need your legs."

At the mast, Noa wrote.

The second carry was the best four minutes of lane Kiva had ever stood in, and she could not afterward remember more than half of it.

She remembered the bell. She remembered going for the litter's head with her Ember lit under her breastbone, glad, as it always came, and the low bound it gave her down the stone. She remembered putting her forearms under the head poles, both of them, the left sleeve and the right bandage, crossed at the wrists as one holds out one's arms for a coat. She remembered the weight coming down into the crooks of her elbows, and Pru beside her at the foot with her forearms crooked and her capped fingers spread up and out like the fingers of a girl drying her nails.

She remembered that her hands were in the air, and so were Pru's, and that there was nothing on the poles but cloth and bone.

And she remembered Treen.

Treen was at the Ropewalk's end with her ward low at her hip and her eyes going, quick and dark, from Kiva's arms to Pru's, from Pru's to Jude's fist, and back. Kiva saw her look for hands and not find them. She saw Treen's mouth open on a word and shut again. Carrow was walking down the south side of the pen with her edge lit, waiting to be told, and Treen did not tell her.

"*Walk,*" said Kiva. "*West. Along the north.*"

They walked. They walked the Commodore down the north side of the pen, close against the posts, on their arms, as two washerwomen walk a basket between them. Behind them Anwen had stopped leaning. Lyle was planted two paces off her wall with her shoulder against it, leaning from the floor, and Anwen walked the far end of her wall round. She did not push the near end. She walked the far end in a quarter circle, slowly, as Mace had taught her on a van's line at home, and the near end came after it. Lyle leaned on a wall that was no longer where she was leaning. She leaned on air, from the floor, with her feet planted and all her weight coming up through her heels, and there was nothing there.

She did not fall. She was too good to fall. But she had to shut her stance to keep her feet, and for a count she stood in the middle of the pen with nothing open at all, and Jude was there.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "The Ropewalk."

Lyle knelt. Kiva saw her face as she went down, square and careful, filing a thing away to be thought about later. Then she saw Lyle look across the pen at Anwen and, for the first time Kiva had ever seen, nearly smile.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

It was late. Kiva heard that it was late. Treen had found something to read at last: Jude's feet, coming back from Lyle, wide and slow on the bad side. She said *set* off his feet, and Carrow brought her edge down where Jude was going to be. Jude was not there. He had stopped. He had stopped because he had heard Pru say, quite quietly, *Talley, stay,* and he stayed, a pace short, and Carrow's edge came down on stone.

"*Late,*" said Kiva, under her breath. "*She's late.*"

They were at the hurdle. Anwen had come round the end of her own wall at a run and gone over the hazel, and Jude after her, and the two of them turned on the far side with their good hands up. Kiva and Pru lifted together, on their forearms, which was not a thing anybody had ever done in a lane, and laid the Commodore on the top of the hazel like a coat across a gate. Anwen took the head from under in her two hands. Jude took the foot in his one. It went over. Two to two. Top to top.

"*Over,*" said the marshal. "*Three to Seven. A down. Four to Seven. Four to the Ropewalk.*"

The Graving made a sound that Kiva had not heard it make all week. It was not the warm sound and it was not the long sound of breath going out. It was somebody high up, a long way off, shouting *elbows!* in a voice that cracked, and then a great many people laughing, and some of them were in green.

They lost the third carry by the glass, and it was Kiva's fault, and Pru's, and nobody's.

The Ropewalk had learned. Kiva watched Treen learn it in the pause, at the far hurdle, with Carrow bent beside her and the two of them talking very fast and low. When the bell went, Treen did not look for hands. She looked at feet. She had watched Jude's feet in the second carry and found that she could read them, and now she was reading everybody's: Anwen's heels, Pru's quick small steps, Kiva's own boots coming down after the Ember's bound.

She had taken that off Kiva too. Kiva watched feet. Everybody on the Circuit knew it. Pike had said it on a tailboard in front of half Graywater. *You watch where people put their feet.*

It was a strange thing to see your own habit in somebody else's eyes, being used against you.

Seven went for the litter on their arms again. The Ropewalk did not let them get it moving. Tolver planted at the head before Kiva got there and sat on it, as Kiva had sat on it on Thursday and again at the start of today, with his long arms flat along the canvas and his feet in the stone. Lyle planted at the foot. Carrow walked.

"*Feet,*" said Kiva. "*She's on feet. Talley, don't move them—*"

"*Hands,*" said Pru, at the same instant, from the other side of the litter. "*Tolver's hands, he's—*"

Jude stopped between the two words.

He had been told two things at once, by two voices, about two places. He stood a pace from the litter's middle with his edge lit and his weight on neither foot, and he did not know whose word to take, and for a whole count he took neither.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

Carrow came down. She did not touch Jude. She did not need to. She put her edge across the gap between Jude and the litter's head, lit, a bar of heat the length of her forearm, and held it there, and Jude could not reach Tolver without walking into it. And while he stood there, and Kiva stood there, and Pru stood there with her hands up, Lyle leaned.

She did not lean on anybody. She leaned on the litter. She was planted at the foot with her shoulder against the canvas, and she put her weight down into the floor and let it come back up through her heels, from the floor, as Anwen had taught her; and the Commodore went east under her shoulder a hand's breadth, and another, with a scrape of canvas on stone, until its middle lay across the chalk.

"*Glass,*" said the marshal. He came down off his box and looked, and walked round it twice, as the marshal had walked round it on Saturday against the Tarn. "*In the Ropewalk's half. By a hand.*" He went back up. "*A point to the Ropewalk. Five to the Ropewalk. Four to Seven.*"

The Graving let out its breath.

Carrow came down the pen afterward, as she had come down it in the Ropewalk, at a walk.

She stopped on the far side of Seven's hurdle and looked at them over it. She was breathing hard. There was sweat in her hairline, and her quick blunt face had a look on it that Kiva had not seen there before, which was the look of somebody who has just done a thing they hate and done it well.

"*Elbows,*" she said.

"Finch's," said Kiva.

"Finch's." Carrow looked at Pru. "She took Treen's eyes off her. I'd not have thought of it in a year. I'd not have had to. I've always had my own eyes." She was quiet a moment. "I had to read your feet in the third. Treen did. She's had that off you."

"I know. I saw."

"And I had that off you as well." Carrow lifted her right hand, with the edge dark on it. "The word. *Set.* I heard it twenty-two times on the lip of the Inner Lock on Tuesday, and on Wednesday night I asked Treen to say it to me while I walked." Her face did not change. "It's the worst thing I've ever done. I've never in my life lit a door on anybody's word but mine. I feel like a carthorse with somebody else's hand on the reins."

"It gets easier," said Kiva. "That's not the same as better."

Carrow looked at her for a long moment.

"No," she said. "It isn't." She glanced at Lyle, who had got up off her knee and was standing by the Ropewalk's hurdle with Treen, quite still, looking at Anwen. "Your wall taught mine to lean, and then walked away from her. She'll not forget that. Nor shall I." She turned to go, and turned back, as she always did. "There's a thing they're saying on the quay. That you'll not cross this week. Dray's four had it from your healer, in the loft."

"It's so."

"Why?"

Kiva thought about whether to say. She found that she did not mind saying it to Carrow, here, over a hurdle, with the whole town talking about elbows.

"It's spoken for," she said. "For Wednesday, if anybody asks."

"The basin." Carrow's face did not change at all. "They're going to stop our bank."

"They're going to try."

"I know they are. Half the Ropewalk's fitters' children, and my mother says the low town will be wet for eleven minutes and she's moving the coal up a stair." Carrow looked past Kiva, past the Graving's gate, at the harbour, which could not be seen from the dock floor. "If they try, I want to be on the wall. Not on the tiers. Not in the Mercury. On the wall." She looked back. "That's not a thing I'd say to anybody from Meridian. I'm saying it to you because you kept a crossing for it."

She went away up the pen, at a walk, with one door and nothing open.

Kiva went to find Hart at half past two, when the basin was still.

She did not go to the pump house to ask him anything at first. She went because it was the slack, and the Board's sitting was at seven, and she had never in her life stood up in a hall full of strangers and asked to be heard about a thing that was not a door, and she wanted to stand somewhere quiet first. The pump house's outfall walk was the only quiet place she knew in Saltmere. For twenty minutes twice a day, if a ruined hand was right, it was the only quiet place in the town.

He was on the iron landing over the grating, as he always was, with his book of slacks open in his left hand and his watch on the inside of his wrist. His bad hand was not in his jacket. It was out, resting on the landing's rail, with the fingers half curled.

"Quiet," he said, without looking round. "Since nineteen past. It's the neap. It came when the sea gate said it would, and twenty-three minutes behind it, like a dog." He turned the watch. "On Wednesday it'll not."

"I know. Mara says."

"Your aunt says what I tell her. I tell her what the hand says." He looked round at last. "You've something on your face. You've had it since the shop door, by the look of it."

She stood on the outfall walk below him with her bound hand against her coat.

"The Board sits at seven," she said. "On the Chair's three sheets. Mara's going to stand up and say what she'll do on Wednesday. The Harbour Committee's going to stand up and say why she can't. And Halden's counsel's going to stand up and say whose ash it is." She looked at his hand on the rail. "Nobody in that hall will say what the hand says. They'll say *a fitter's hand,* and they'll say it the way Polglase says *it's the harbour.*"

Hart did not move.

"I'd like you to say it," said Kiva. "Under your own name. The held slack. The dog. That on Wednesday it'll not walk, and nobody can say why, and you've written it every spring since the ninth month." She made herself go on. "I'm asking a thing of a ruined hand. I know I am. I don't know what you're let ask it."

He looked at her for a long time from the landing, with the brown water still and flat below him and the six outfalls pouring into it on the count, and the count not going anywhere.

"It's let be asked one thing," he said at last. "*Quiet,* or *not.* That's all it does. It doesn't know why, or what, or how long. It knows *quiet.*" He turned his bad hand over on the rail, palm up, as Lyle had held her hand at her hip with nothing in it. "It was the best hand at the Bench in 2660. Your sponsor will tell you. It could read a cell through a wall. Now it reads one thing, and aches near everything else, and a girl from Meridian with two dead fingers has come down a walk to ask it to stand up in a hall." He was quiet a moment. "Nobody's asked anything of it in twenty years, but *is it quiet yet, Jess.*"

"I'm asking."

"I heard you." He shut his book of slacks. "Your sponsor will be in that hall."

"Yes."

"Then so will I." He put the book in his jacket. "I'll say what the hand says, and I'll say it's ruined, before anybody else does. And then I'll sit down." He looked at her. "You've not come here to ask me that. That's the second thing. What's the first?"

She had not known it showed. She stood on the walk and found that it did, and that she did not mind it showing to him.

"I'm going to stand up as well," she said. "After Mara. About a thing that isn't a door."

Hart did not ask what. She had thought he might. He looked at her face for a long time, and at her hands, and then he turned his watch on his wrist, once, and looked at the water.

"Ache," he said quietly. "Thirty-nine past. That's twenty minutes. It's going out again." He did not look round. "Same hour every day, Rowan. Never on purpose. You'll have done it already, today."

"Not yet. A quarter to seven."

"Then you'll be in a hall at a quarter to seven, standing up." He nearly smiled. "Lay them on the rail of the Salt Hall's gallery. It's iron. It'll say *yes.* Everything in this town says *yes.* Write it down anyway."

She went back along the walk toward the low town. Behind her, on the landing, she heard him open his book again and write, in his slow left hand, and she did not look back to see what.

# Chapter 36 — Kiva: The Three Sheets

Kellan Renn did the sum for the third time at four on Friday afternoon, on the back of a Halden claims slip, at the corner table of the Halden technical office on the second floor of the Counting House, and it came out the same.

He did not need to do it again. He had done it on Wednesday night standing at the gate, reading a ledger upside down over a clerk's shoulder. He had done it on Thursday morning at the comb table, with the Board's own tallies open in front of him, which anybody might read who asked. He had done it on Thursday night in the loft by the south window with a candle, while the rest of Halden License slept and Seven's beds at the north end were empty because Seven was down in Quiller's shop with the door shut. It came out the same every time. A sum did.

The office was a long room with eight desks under eight windows, and lamps at every desk, white and steady. Through the floor, faintly, he could feel the house's count. He had stopped noticing it in the eighth month. He noticed it now.

Pettit was standing at the end of the table with the card in his hand.

"Counsel asked me to give it you," said Pettit. "In person. He said you'd want it from your captain and not from a clerk." He laid it on the table, face up, beside the claims slip. "It's the one on the board. With a line on the back that isn't on the board."

Kellan had read the front on Thursday at the baize. *The Halden Foundation, technical office. Renn, K., Fire, blade, Meridian, Halden License. Junior assessor, on the Proprietor's roll, at a Fire's wage, from the autumn. A card is not a seat.* It was the best card on the board. Hesketh had said so at breakfast, not quite kindly. It was better than anything Kellan's mother had ever been offered in thirty years of grading other people's ash.

He turned it over.

*On the understanding that Mr. Renn's figures, if the Board calls for them, are given through the foundation's counsel, as the foundation's.*

That was all. It was in counsel's hand, which was small and very neat and had never once been unkind to anybody on paper.

"He's not wrong," said Pettit. He said it heavily. "That's the trouble. I read it twice on the stair. He's not asking you to lie. He's not asking you to leave anything out. He's asking you to say it with the house's name on it and not yours, because you read the house's own clerk's ledger to get half of it, and the house is in front of the Board tonight asking for its claim, and a house doesn't send its own blade in to say a thing against it on his own card." Pettit put his hands in his pockets. "If you were counsel, you'd ask the same."

"I know I would."

"Then?"

Kellan looked at the claims slip, where the sum was, in his own figures.

*Combed at the gate, 1st to 16th: 1,106 tins, jars and bags. Seventh reed sang: 1,102. Silent: 4. The Hunt, the 16th: 412.4 dr. combed off the Longshore. Red: 371.3. Clean: 41.1, every dram of it dry. Nine-tenths.* And under it, in a column of its own: *Claimed under the patent of 2391, by the house's own index. 2391–2600, all from its own yards: 109 dr. 2680, Meridian district alone, outside its yards: 1,175.1. The Measure to date: 371.3.*

And at the foot, the line he had written on Wednesday night and not liked.

*If the claim is good and the comb is right: every dram killed in this country, but the dry tenth.*

"If I give it through counsel," he said, "counsel will read it. He'll read every figure, because he's honest. And then he'll stand up and say what the figures mean, and he'll say they mean the house's claim is large. He'll not say they mean the claim is the whole country. He'll not have to. He'll leave the last line off, because it isn't a figure. It's what the figures come to." Kellan put his finger on the last line. "That's the line nobody else in that hall can say. Bexley won't, because it's a line between two columns. Adlam won't, because it's the house's patent and he's the house's clerk. Counsel won't, because it's his job not to." He took his finger away. "I did the sum. It's mine to say what it comes to."

Pettit was quiet for a long moment.

"You'll lose the card," he said.

"I'll lose the card."

"You might lose the yard. Tuesdays and Thursdays."

"I might."

"And you might lose me," said Pettit, not loudly. "I'm captain of a four that's on the house's licence, standing for the house's name, and tomorrow I've a lane with the National Measure on it, against whoever the bag gives us, and tonight my blade stands up in a hall and tells the Board that the house wants the whole country's ash." He looked at Kellan. "I'll not stop you. I'd not. I'm saying I'll have to think about it, after. That's all."

"I know you will."

Somebody had come up the office stair. Kellan heard the step and knew it before he looked, because it was the step of a man who always seemed to have just been told something surprising and had decided to take it calmly.

Mr. Adlam stood in the office door with his hat in his hand and a black tin box under his arm.

"Renn," he said. "Pettit." His long face had its astonished look, which Kellan had learned in the autumn was not astonishment at all. "I've been to counsel. I've told him I'm reading the bench books tonight myself. All of them. 2402, 2431, 2604, the ninth month, and Saturday's." He shifted the tin box under his arm. "He asked me not to. He asked me very civilly, and I said no very civilly, and we both felt better." He looked at the card on the table, face down, with counsel's line showing. "I'll not tell you what to do. I've no right. I came up to say that I'll be in the same hall reading figures against the house's interest under the house's own name, and that if you read yours under yours, you'll not be the only one."

"Why?" said Kellan.

Adlam considered it. He seemed to think it was a fair question.

"Because I've always said the patent should be defended truthfully or not at all," he said. "And this week I found I meant it. It's an uncomfortable thing to find at my age." He put his hat on. "Seven o'clock. I'll be on the second bench from the front, on the left, if you want somebody to look at."

He went down the stair.

Kellan picked up the card. He turned it over to the front, and read it once more, *junior assessor, on the Proprietor's roll, at a Fire's wage,* and he thought of his mother at her grading bench at home with her glass in her eye, signing *A. Renn, grader* at the foot of other people's tins. Then he turned it back over, to counsel's line, and took his pencil, and wrote under it in his own small square hand.

*No. I'll give my figures under my own name, and the house may have them after. With thanks. K. Renn.*

He gave it to Pettit.

"Pin it back up," he said. "Face to the board. Like Cade's."

Pettit took it. He looked at it, and at Kellan, for a long moment, with his heavy face quite still.

"Cade wrote *with thanks* on his as well," he said. "I saw it at the baize." He put the card in his pocket. "You're all of you mad, the lot of you on that book. I'd expect nothing else." He went to the door, and stopped there, and did not turn round. "Read it clearly. If you're going to cost us the card, I'll not have you mumble."

The Salt Hall had been built to keep salt dry, and at seven on Friday evening it was full of people who had come to watch a thing that was not a board.

The six banks were gone from the floor. The Board's carpenters had taken them out on Tuesday and swept the flags, and now there was a long deal table across the floor at the harbour end, under the load board, which hung dark, every needle lying against its pin. Three chairs stood behind the table. In front of it, in rows, stood the benches that had been carried down from the gallery, and on the benches sat everybody who had a reason to be there. Up in the gallery, on the iron columns, sat everybody who had not.

Kiva had come at twenty to seven. She had gone up the gallery stair before anybody else was there, to the bench at the rail where she had sat on Monday with Aske, and she had laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron of the rail, lightly, without leaning, at a quarter to seven by the clock over the great door.

*It keeps time.*

Everything in this town said *yes.* She had written it anyway, with her left hand. *Friday the 18th. A quarter to seven. The rail of the Salt Hall's gallery. Yes.* Then she had gone down the stair again to the floor, because she did not mean to sit in the gallery tonight.

Now she sat on the third bench from the front, on the right, between Mara and Perrin. Anwen was beyond Perrin. Jude and Pru were on the bench behind, and Aske at the very back by the door, with his bag on his knees, where he had said he would be.

The hall filled round them.

Halden's counsel sat on the front bench on the left, a small grey neat man with a folder on his knees and the Halden mark on its cover. Mr. Adlam sat behind him, on the second bench, with the black tin box on the bench beside him, and Kellan beside the box in his black and gold, very upright. Pettit and the rest of Halden License were three benches further back. The Harbour Committee had the front bench on the right: six men and one woman in good coats, with a chairman in the middle who was a ship's chandler, Jude said, and who held a gold watch open in his palm and looked at it every few minutes as though it might run away.

Peverell sat at the end of the Committee's bench, apart, with her spectacles on their cord and her apron off for once, and her small crabbed hands folded on a book in her lap. Hart sat behind her. He had his book of slacks in his jacket and his bad hand on his knee, and he was not looking at anybody.

Orla sat on the far end of the fourth bench with her stick across her knees. She was not looking at anybody either. She was very carefully not looking at the back of Hart's head.

Yeo was there, and Ivey beside her with her square dark hands in her lap. Coombe was there with her rope. Rusk was there with his thumbs in his waistcoat. Noa sat at a small table of his own beside the long one, with a roll of paper and his ledger, and Polglase on a stool behind him. On the other side, at another small table, sat a woman in plain grey with her hair cut short as a boy's and her hands folded at her waist, whom Kiva had last seen at the gate of a stone house on a bluff over a river.

The sister. The Order's.

"She came on Wednesday's boat," said Mara, very low. "She's been at the Works' lodging asking for me every day. I've been at the pump house." She did not look at the sister. "She's not said what for."

Selka Dray sat on the end of the Meridian bench, behind Halden, on two feet, with her face like a shut door. The Mercury's young man sat at the very end of the front row with three pencils.

At seven exactly, the Clerk of the Measure came out of a side door and walked down the hall between the benches to the long table, and the two Board members came after her and sat on either side. One was a heavy grey man with spectacles pushed up on his forehead. The other was a thin woman with ink on her cuff. The Clerk did not sit. She stood behind the middle chair with her gold chain on her coat, the smallest thing in the hall, and when she spoke, everybody heard her.

"The Charter Board sits," she said, "on three sheets that came to it in the autumn under the seal of the Placement Chair at Meridian, with Mr. Adlam's name second and a line of Officer Yeo's. The Board took them up at its sitting on the 1st. It has not answered them. It will answer what it can tonight." She looked at the Harbour Committee's bench. "The Harbour Committee has asked the Board by four letters whether it will stop the harbour bank on Wednesday. The Committee wants its answer by midnight, so that it may go to the magistrate in the morning if it doesn't like it. The Board will rise at ten. The Committee will have its answer before that." She glanced at the clock over the great door. "Three hours. The Chair."

Ysra Crest stood up from a chair set by itself at the end of the long table.

Kiva had seen the Chair perhaps a dozen times in her life, and every time the Chair had been sitting behind a desk with a page in front of her and a pen beside it. She had never seen her stand in a hall. She was a lean woman in a plain dark coat, older than Kiva remembered, with grey hair pulled back and the level eyes of somebody who had held a ward on a Bench in 2660 and had never since needed to raise her voice.

"Three sheets," said the Chair. "Mr. Bexley's columns. People: what the class of 2680 measured, against forty years. Rifts and spawn: how many tears, of what kind, since the thaw. And Saltmere: a bank that went into step at twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month, in the same minute as four husks stood a square in a pit at Meridian." She did not look at a paper. "I sent them because a Circuit can't ask a nation to look at its banks. A Chair can ask who else has a keeper with a book. I asked. Mr. Bexley has the returns."

She sat down. It had taken less than a minute.

Noa stood up at his little table. He unrolled his sheet and held it up, and two of the Board's clerks came and took the corners and held it high for the hall.

There were four columns on it now. Kiva saw the hall see that. People, and rifts and spawn, and Saltmere, in his small square hand, as they had been on the Rowan kitchen table in the autumn; and a fourth, newer, ruled in fresher ink. *Ash. What the comb says.* Along the bottom there was one rule for the weeks. Above it four lines went up, each in its own column, slowly from the thaw and then faster from the fifth month, and there was not a mark between any of them.

"Four," said Noa. "I've added ash. How much of every month's yield sang on the seventh reed, from every comb on the Board's roll that would send me its book." His voice was thin and very clear. "They go up together. They went up faster from the fifth month of last year. I've drawn them on one sheet with one rule along the bottom, and I've drawn nothing between them."

"Why not?" said the heavy Board member.

"Because I don't know that anything goes between them, sir. I drew a line between two things once that I'd added together, and it was wrong for four months." Noa did not look down. "If the Board wants a line, the Board can draw it. I'll not."

The heavy man looked at him over his pushed-up spectacles for a long moment. Then he wrote something, and nodded.

"The returns," said the Clerk.

Noa read them. He read the eight *nothing found* first, quickly, by house, and the hall shifted on its benches. Then he read Westerley. *At three the stepped lamps on the quay white. Town lamps out half hour. Keeper ill of a fever, his wife sat up. He said in the morning the roof was off. Not fault.* The hall went quiet. He read Tarnfoot. *The four drums stood up together a little after three and lay down.* Then he read Holloway.

*Night lamp-man took ill that evening, a fever two nights. Asked by the warden what he saw in it. Would not have it written. Said he would be laughed at. A fever is not a fault.*

Somebody in the gallery laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of somebody who had been holding his breath for a long time and let it out in the wrong place. It stopped at once. But it had been heard, by everybody in the hall, and Kiva felt it go through her as the edge had gone through her shoulder that morning, flat and light and exactly where it was aimed.

She did not look up at the gallery. She looked at Noa, who had stopped reading and was standing with the yellow form in his hand.

"That's the return," said Noa, after a moment. "I've read it as it came. I'll not add to it." He sat down.

The Clerk let the silence stand for a count of five. Then she looked at the left-hand front bench.

"The foundation," she said.

Halden's counsel stood. He did not hurry. He laid his folder on the bench, and opened it, and took out one sheet, and held it as a man holds a thing he has read so often that he no longer needs to look at it.

"The foundation's patent of 2391," he said. His voice was pleasant and dry. "*All ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.* The foundation has entered that claim against every red bag combed at this Measure, as it entered it against the red rows at Meridian last year, and as it's entered it since 2391, quietly, in its own index, and nobody's said it can't. The Compact's court has the Weighbridge bag before it and hasn't ruled." He laid the sheet down. "The foundation asks the Board tonight to note the claim against the Hunt's bags in its own record. It asks nothing else. It doesn't ask to have the ash. Red ash can't be used. It asks only that the claim be written down where the Board writes things, so that when the court rules, the record is there."

It was reasonable. Kiva heard the hall hear that it was reasonable. The heavy Board member nodded slowly, and the thin woman wrote.

"Mr. Adlam," said the Clerk. "You're the foundation's clerk. You've a box."

Adlam stood up, with the black tin box in both hands.

Halden's counsel did not turn round. But Kiva saw his shoulders change. They did not move. They went still, as a man's shoulders go still when he hears a step behind him that he had asked not to hear.

"The foundation's bench books," said Adlam. He opened the box on the bench in front of him. Inside it there were five books, old and new, in calf and in cloth, with straws in them. "The technical office keeps a count of every stepped row that's fallen in on a bench in this house, and the rate it fell in at. A man with a watch writes it down. He's always written it down. Nobody's ever asked him for it." He took out the first book, which was very old, and opened it at its straw. "2402. *Fell in at forty-eight to the minute.*" He laid it down and took out the next. "2431. *Forty-eight.*" The next. "2604. *Fifty.*" The next, which was cloth and not calf. "The ninth month of last year. *Fifty-five.*" The last, which was a clerk's notebook with a pencil still in its spine. "Saturday the 5th of this month, at the door of the harbour pump house, by my own watch. The harbour bank. *Fifty-seven.*"

He put the notebook down on top of the others.

"The foundation patented a count," he said. "In 2391. It didn't make the count. It found it, in ash that already kept it, and learned to give it to cells. Before the patent the Wren House called it counting ash." His long face was quite calm. "The count isn't the foundation's. It's got quicker. It's got quicker faster since the ninth month. I don't know why. I'd not have brought these if I'd known why; I'd have brought the why." He looked at the back of counsel's head. "I've always said the patent should be defended truthfully or not at all. These are true. I'll leave them on the table."

He carried the box to the long table and set it down in front of the Clerk, and went back to his bench, and sat down beside Kellan.

The hall was very quiet. The chairman of the Harbour Committee looked at his gold watch.

"Mr. Renn," said the Clerk. "You wrote to the Board on Thursday. You said you had figures."

Kellan stood up.

Kiva had heard Kellan do a great many sums aloud. She had heard him do them at the Rowan kitchen table and in the Old Yard and on a frozen tram road at three in the morning. She had never heard him do one in a hall full of strangers with his own house's counsel sitting six feet in front of him, and she found that she was holding her breath.

He did not hold a paper. He had it in his head.

"The Board's own tallies," he said. "Anybody may read them. Combed at the gate from the 1st to the 16th, one thousand one hundred and six tins, jars and bags. The seventh reed sang on one thousand one hundred and two. It was silent on four." He did not look at Seven's bench. "One of the four was a team's sealed medical, given by the Order. One was a jar of the Tarn's that had been cut with soot. One was a Westerley healer's tin from before the thaw. One was the Board's own reference jar."

The thin Board member had stopped writing and was looking at him.

"The Hunt," said Kellan. "Four hundred and twelve drams and four-tenths combed off the Longshore on Wednesday. Three hundred and seventy-one and three-tenths red. Forty-one and a tenth clean. Every dram of the clean was dry. Nine-tenths." He paused. "That's the Board's. This is the house's." He did look at counsel's back then, once. "I stood at the gate on Wednesday and read the claims clerk's ledger over his shoulder. He's a good clerk. He was doing his work. Every red tag in the Hunt, *claimed, under the patent of 2391.* Three hundred and seventy-one drams and three-tenths. And the house's own index, which I've read at the yard on Tuesdays, because I've the yard on Tuesdays and nobody's ever told me not to read the index." He was quite white. "From 2391 to 2600, the house claimed a hundred and nine drams of ash exhibiting the Step. All of it from its own yards. Every grain of it the house had stepped itself. In 2680, in the district of Meridian alone, outside its yards, the house claimed one thousand one hundred and seventy-five drams and a tenth. Off tears in the street. Off a pit. Off a sluice."

He stopped.

"Those are the figures," said the Clerk. "Is there more?"

"There's what they come to," said Kellan. "It isn't a figure. I'll say it, because I did the sum, and nobody else here will." He did not raise his voice. "If the claim's good, and the comb's right, then on this week's figures the house has a claim on every dram of ash killed in this country, except the tenth that happens to be dry when it's bagged. And if Mr. Bexley's fourth column goes on the way it's going, by next spring it's every dram." He took a breath. "I'm not saying the house wants that. I've sat at the Proprietor's table four times and I don't think he wants it. I'm saying it's what the patent says, now. It didn't say it in 2391, because in 2391 ash didn't keep time unless somebody taught it. Now everything keeps time." He looked at the Clerk. "I'm on the house's licence. I'm on its yard. I was on its card until four this afternoon. I'd like that written next to the figures, so that nobody thinks I'd a grudge."

He sat down. Adlam, beside him, did not look at him. But Kiva saw the old man's hand move on the bench, an inch, and stop near Kellan's, and stay there, not touching, as Rhea's hand had stayed near Pike's on a tailboard.

Halden's counsel stood up.

He stood for some time without saying anything. Then he picked up the single sheet off the bench, the patent's line, and looked at it, and put it back in his folder.

"The foundation withdraws its request," he said. "For the present. It'll not ask the Board to note the claim against the Hunt's bags tonight." His voice was as pleasant and dry as before. "It'll put its case to the Compact's court, where it belongs, with the bench books, which are true, and Mr. Renn's figures, which are also true, and which I'd have read myself, more slowly." He sat down. Then, without turning round, he said quite clearly, "Well done, Renn."

Kiva looked along the benches at Pettit. He was sitting three rows back with his arms folded and his heavy face quite still, and he did not look at Kellan, and he did not look away from him either.

The chairman of the Harbour Committee shut his gold watch with a snap.

"It's eight," he said. "With respect to the foundation, and to Mr. Renn, and to the young man's lines, which are very nicely drawn, the Committee didn't come here to hear about ash." He stood up. "It came to hear whether a reader from Meridian is going to stop the pumps that keep the low town dry, on Wednesday, on the strength of a paper in a box and a fitter's hand." He turned toward the end of his own bench. "Mrs. Peverell. It's your house."

Peverell did not stand up at once. She sat with her hands folded on the book in her lap. Then she stood, and took her spectacles off their cord and put them on, and looked at the chairman through them.

"It's my house," she said. "Nobody's stopping my pumps."

The chairman began to speak. She went on over him, not loudly.

"My pumps will run on Wednesday. They'll stop for eleven minutes at the slack, by my hand, at my word, and start again on my word, each on its own eight cells, because that's what the method says, and I've read it. I've written thirty-six names on my fault paper. My own isn't on it." She did not look at Mara. "If the bank comes out, it'll be because thirty-six people put their hands on thirty-six cases on a word, and I'll write that in seven columns. If it doesn't, it'll be found wrong, under seal, and I'll write that too." She took her spectacles off. "I'll not be the keeper who stopped the pumps. I'll be the keeper who let it be tried. That's a different line in my book."

She sat down.

"Eleven minutes," said the chairman. "On a spring tide. With the low town under the sea."

"A foot of water in Ropery Row," said Peverell, without standing. "For eleven minutes. I've told them to move the coal up a stair. They've been moving the coal up a stair every spring since before you were born, Mr. Chairman, every time a gale took a pump. They know how."

Somebody in the gallery laughed again. This time it was a different laugh.

"And the fitter's hand," said the chairman. He had not sat down. "Since we're to stake the low town on it."

Hart stood up behind Peverell.

He did not take his book of slacks out. He stood with his bad hand at his side, the right one, the fingers half curled, and his good hand on the back of the bench in front of him, and he looked at the chairman of the Harbour Committee, and then at the Board.

"Jessamy Hart," he said. "Fitter, the harbour pump house. Nineteen years." His voice was quiet and level, like a gauge. "I'll say it's ruined first, before anybody else does. It was turned out at the Bench in 2660. It can't hold a door. It aches near anything that keeps time, and in this town that's everything." He did not lift it. "It's quiet at the slack. That's the one thing it says now. At the neaps the quiet walks with the sea gate's table, twenty-three minutes behind. At the springs, at dawn, since the ninth month, it doesn't walk. It comes near a quarter past six, whatever the sea's doing." He paused. "I don't know why. I've written it every morning in a book. Mrs. Rowan's read the book. The prediction in the Clerk's press is off the book."

"So it's a fitter's book and a fitter's hand," said the chairman.

"It's a fitter's book and a ruined hand," said Hart. "The hand's the one that reads. The good one never did." He sat down.

Kiva did not look along the bench at Orla. She did not need to. She heard Orla's stick, which had been lying across her knees, move very slightly on the wood.

Mara stood up at a quarter past eight.

Kiva had known she would. She had known since the shop door at Coper Street, and the knock, and the two wires folded into an apron pocket. She had not known what it would be like to sit on a bench in a hall full of strangers and watch her aunt stand up.

Mara did not take anything out. She stood with her reading hand at her side and her other hand on the bench back, and she looked at the Board.

"Mara Rowan," she said. "Grade Two. Reader to the Municipal Works, Meridian." Her voice was dry and exact and carried to the gallery without trying. "On Wednesday the 23rd, at nine minutes past six by the pump house clock, if the basin's still as Mr. Hart's hand says it will be, I mean to take the Saltmere harbour bank out of step. With thirty-six hands that aren't mine, at a word from the landing. Each cell quenched, and filled with a fresh can salted with a jar of plain, and given a count of its own, not two alike in the house. Each pump restarted on its own eight cells, by the keeper." She did not pause. "I'll not touch a case. I've a reading hand that's been starved once, and I'll not make the thing that takes that bank out of step into another one engine, with my hand for the count."

"And if you're wrong?" said the heavy Board member.

"Then the low town's wet for eleven minutes, the bank goes back in, and it's on my name." Mara's face did not change. "Not the Works'. Mr. Rusk's told me the Works doesn't believe anything that hasn't happened, and he's right not to. Not the keeper's. Not Mr. Hart's. Mine." She looked at the Committee's bench. "I'd like that written down, so that the Committee has a name to go to the magistrate with, if it wants one."

The chairman opened his mouth, and shut it.

"There's one thing more," said Mara. "It needs thirty-six jars of plain ash. Ash that's never heard a beat. One to a cell. There isn't any in Saltmere. There's none in Meridian. The soot-cut stock's spoilt, and every tin that came through this Board's gate sang on the seventh, but four." She looked at Kellan, briefly. "Mr. Renn's said so. Without thirty-six jars there's no Wednesday. I'd sooner the Board knew that tonight than found it out at six in the morning on the landing."

"One of the four," said the chairman of the Harbour Committee, at once, "is Meridian's. That team's. Their sealed tin. Two drams of plain." He turned on his bench and looked along the rows to Seven. "I read it in the Board's tally myself. If the town's to be wet for eleven minutes, the town might ask what's in their tin."

Every head in the hall turned.

Perrin stood up. He did it in two stages, with his hand pressed to his side, and Kiva saw what it cost him, and saw Aske at the back half rise and sit down again.

"Perrin Cade," he said. "Captain. Team Seven. It's under my name." His voice cracked a little and he did not seem to care. "It's for a hand. It's written in two books, under two names, and one of the books is the Order's. *For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.* Mr. Adlam offered us twelve drams for it last week and took no for an answer. I'm sorry, sir. It's for a hand. If a hand's hurt on Wednesday that plain will ease, it's there. It's not for a bank."

"It's two drams against a town."

"It's two drams against a word," said Perrin. "We gave it in a courtyard. I'd sooner the low town were wet than that word was."

He sat down. His face was scarlet to the hair.

The sister of the Order stood up at her small table.

She had not moved since she sat down. She stood now with her hands folded at her waist, in plain grey, with her hair cut short as a boy's, and the hall turned to her as it had turned to Perrin, and went very quiet.

"The Order of the Even Hand," she said. Her voice was low and quite calm. "That tin is ours as well as theirs. They've kept our word. The Master will be glad to hear it, and not surprised." She looked at the Board. "The Master has had Mrs. Rowan's wire of the 12th. The ferryman carried it up to the gate. It asked for thirty-six jars. The Master doesn't answer such a thing by wire. She left the house on Wednesday by the ferry, and she's on the coast boat now, and she'll be in Saltmere tomorrow at five. She'll speak for the Order's cellar herself." The sister paused. "She's not been past the house's gate in eleven years. I'd ask the Board to remember that, when it decides where it would like her to stand."

She sat down.

The hall did not make any sound at all for a long moment. Then everybody began to talk at once, in the gallery and on the benches, and the Mercury's young man broke the point of his first pencil and took up his second.

Mara had sat down beside Kiva. Her reading hand was flat on her knee. Kiva looked at it and saw that it was quite steady, and that her aunt's face was white.

"You didn't tell me," said Kiva, under the noise.

"I didn't know. I wired the ferry office for her on the 12th and had nothing back." Mara did not turn her head. "I thought she'd say no. I'd have said no, in her place." She was quiet a moment. "I said it on my name. The low town. I'd not thought it would feel like that."

"What did it feel like?"

"Like standing on a landing with a watch," said Mara, "and the whole sea on the other side of a door."

The Clerk let the noise go on for a count of ten. Then she said, "The Board," without raising her voice, and the hall stopped.

"The Harbour Committee will have its answer now," she said. "It's twenty to nine." She looked at the chairman. "The Board does not stop banks. It does not keep them. It has no power over the pumps of the Saltmere harbour pump house, and it'll not pretend to one tonight to please the Committee or to frighten it. The keeper keeps her house." She did not look at Peverell. "If the keeper lets a thing be tried in her house on Wednesday, the Board will want it written down in seven columns, and it will read them. If the Committee goes to the magistrate in the morning, the magistrate will find the same thing in the keeper's own book, which is older than the Committee." She folded her hands. "That's the answer. It's the only one the Board has."

The chairman sat down. He did not open his watch again.

"The plain ash," said the Clerk, "the Board will hear when the Master of the Order's here to speak for it, and not before. The Board's not going to decide what's to be done with another house's cellar in that house's absence." She glanced at the clock. "The three sheets. The Board has heard the returns, and the bench books, and Mr. Renn's figures, and Mr. Bexley won't draw a line." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, and was gone. "The Board will consider what it's heard. It will—"

Kiva stood up.

She had not decided to do it at that word. She had decided on the boards of the timber pond the night before, between the mast and the wall. But she found that her body had chosen the moment, as it chose a moment on a floor, and that she was standing on the third bench from the front with her bound right hand against her coat and the whole of the Salt Hall turning to look at her, and that her voice had already started.

"Ma'am," she said. "Before the Board considers. I've a thing to put on the table."

The Clerk looked at her. Her small grey face did not change at all.

"Name," she said.

"Kiva Rowan. Team Seven, Meridian. Catcher." She heard her own voice go up the stone walls into the gallery, flat and clear, and let it. "The returns that Mr. Bexley read. The keeper at Westerley, who said the roof was off. The keeper at Ferren in 2604, whose book Mrs. Rowan has sent for, and the keeper here, on the same night. Master Annick, in her notebook at the Library, who called it the sky unroofed. And the lamp-man at Holloway, who wouldn't have it written, because he'd be laughed at."

Somebody in the gallery drew in his breath.

"I've had it," said Kiva. "With a fever. At thirteen, when I was Kindled, and on Classification Day, and on the ferry below the Order's house last month. The same thing. No roof, and nothing over, and the nothing paying attention." Her hands were hot. She did not look at them. "I'm not saying what it is. I don't know. Nobody does. I'm saying I've had it, under my own name, in this hall."

The hall did not make a sound.

"Anybody can say that," said Kiva. "A girl can read a notebook in a library and come out of a fever saying anything. That's why my aunt wrote mine down, the same night, last spring, in my own words, and sealed it, and had the Reader at the Compact Library put her wax over it with the hour written through the join, before I'd ever opened Master Annick's box." She made herself go on. "It's the only page in the country that can't have been borrowed. It's in the Library's press. It comes out to my aunt's hand. She sent for it last night. It'll be here on the transit tomorrow night." She looked at the Clerk. "I'd like the Board to open it."

She stopped. She found she had nothing else to say, and that she was still standing, and that her knees had not decided anything yet.

The Clerk of the Measure looked at her for a long time.

At the back of the hall, Aske had stood up, and was standing by the door with his bag in his hand, and was not coming forward. Beside her, Perrin had put his hand on the bench beside her boot, flat, not touching. Anwen had stood up as well. Kiva did not know when. She was standing at Perrin's shoulder with her hands out of her sleeves, at her sides, saying nothing, being there.

"Sit down, Miss Rowan," said the Clerk.

Kiva sat.

"The Board has had a great many things put on its table in thirty years," said the Clerk. "In a great many rooms. It's not often had a sealed page offered to it by the person whose page it is, before anybody asked." She turned her head and spoke to the thin woman with ink on her cuff, very low, and the woman nodded. "The Board will open it. Not tonight, and not on a Saturday night, in a dock, with the Measure being read. The Board will sit again on Sunday, at two, in the Graving, where there's room, because there's going to be a great deal of town." She looked at Mara. "Mrs. Rowan. The page comes to your hand, you said."

"To my hand or a magistrate's," said Mara. "The Reader's bringing it. And Master Annick's box, in her own custody, if the Board will ask for it. And the Ferren keeper's book for 2604."

"The Board asks for all three," said the Clerk. "Write it down."

The thin woman wrote it down.

The side door of the hall opened. A boy in Halden's livery came in, very quickly, and walked down between the benches to the long table, and gave the Clerk a folded paper, and went out again. The Clerk unfolded it. She read it standing, with her small grey face giving nothing away. Kiva saw that the paper was thick, and cream-coloured, and that the hand on it was large and upright and old.

She looked along the second bench on the left. Mr. Adlam's seat beside Kellan was empty. She had not seen him go.

The Clerk read the paper a second time. Then she folded it and laid it on the table in front of her, under her hand.

"The Board has a letter," she said, "received at four minutes to nine, from Mr. Ambrose Halden, the Proprietor of the Halden Foundation. It's in his own hand." She did not unfold it again. "He asks to be heard by the Board on the matter Miss Rowan has put on the table, under his own name. He says he was a bench clerk in the Counting House on the night of the 11th of the third month, 2604, and he's the last person living who was in this town that night." The Clerk paused. "He says he can't come to the Board. He's not lifted his hand from his glass case for longer than a minute since 2611, and he'll not begin now. He asks the Board to come to him."

The hall was so quiet that Kiva could hear the harbour against the Long Quay's granite through the stone wall behind the load board, and under it, faint, the town's count.

"The Board will go," said the Clerk. "On Sunday, at nine, to the Counting House. *THE FOUNDATION WITHDRAWS.*

*Whether any of this means anything, the Board will say on Sunday, at two, in the Graving. The Board has never yet said that a fever was a fault.*

She folded the sheet and put it under her pillow, and found that she did not want to read it again.

She saw Perrin's mouth shut. She saw Kiva's head move. And she understood, and her face did something that Kiva had never seen it do. It did not open. But for an instant it was not a door. She stepped across instead, with her right foot, the long way, and it was a count and a half and Kiva was a pace and a half off with a hundredweight on her arms and nothing open but the Ember, and that was not enough. She had lost the exchange. She had known she would, since a quarter past seven, at three feet, across two beds. She had chosen to. It did not feel like a thing that had been done to her. It felt like the other thing she had done this week: a thing set down where anybody could see it, Dray and the tiers and Perrin at the hurdle with his mouth shut, and that was all.

"Maybe," said Kiva. "Maybe not. I've been in it. It's short."

"That's a thing I'll not know now," she said. "Ever. Not on this coast. Not next year. You'll be on one in fourteen by then, if your healer's right, and you'll not spend it on a lane." Her voice was quite flat. "You chose that. At three feet, this morning. And on the floor, with your head."

"Yes."

"I'll not thank you for it."

"I didn't ask you to."

"No." Dray looked down at the Commodore, at the foot of canvas lying across the chalk on Seven's side. "You knelt by the head. Nobody touches a downed fighter. I couldn't reach it past you."

"I know you didn't. That's what makes it worse." Dray turned to go. Then she turned back, as Carrow always did, and as Dray never had. "It was declared," she said. "All of it. Before the bell, and at the box. *No change.* I heard your captain say it." She paused. "That's the fairest lane I've ever lost."

She went away up the pen on two feet, with her weight on the right.

Pru was standing at the litter's foot with her capped hands held up and her face scarlet.

"Six," she said. "Six to five."

"You called it," said Kiva.

"I called it. You didn't say a word." Pru looked at her. "Not even when she was on Talley's arm and I didn't see it for a count. I saw you see it. You shut your mouth so hard I heard your teeth."

"I promised."

"I know you did." Pru put her hands down. "That's the first lane I've ever called that nobody called over me." She did not go any redder; she could not. "It's not a favour. You should know that. I'm not grateful. I'm only good."

"I know you are," said Kiva. "I watched."

Rhea Sorn sat on the fourth tier above the floor with her hands in her pockets and her slate on her knee, and watched the girl kneel by the litter's head and not look up, and did not write anything on the slate at all.

Pike was beside her. His hands lay on his knees, closed halfway, as they had lain for a fortnight, and would very likely lie now for the rest of his life. He had not said a word through three carries. He said one now.

"Cade," he said.

"I saw."

"He'd his mouth open on the count. She shook her head." Pike's voice was low and dry. "He shut it. That's a captain. I'd have said the count, at his age. I'd have thought it was mine to say." He nearly smiled. "She called nothing all lane. Not a word. Finch had it."

"I saw."

"Did you know she would?"

"No," said Rhea. "I'd have bet against it. I'd have bet my van."

She took her hands out of her pockets and looked at them. They were a Torch's hands, square and scarred across the knuckles from twenty years of cutting from behind a skin, and they had written a great many cards. They had written one in the autumn that said *catcher's second, to Pike,* with *either of us may say no* at the foot of it, and *no seat is promised,* and a date. The date was the 30th. That was eleven days off, and one more night on her van.

She allowed herself one piece of advice a season to anybody she trained, and no more. She had spent the winter's on the girl in the first month, and the spring's on a mud hard at Gannet Creek at one in the morning. She had held to that rule with every corner-hand and second she had ever had. It was the rule that kept them from being hers.

She had kept to the letter of it. She had broken the rest. She had broken it at the Company yard on Wednesday, telling Yeo to ask the girl for her columns before she slept, and calling it *a thing I know about her.* She had been knowing things about the girl since the first month and saying them aloud to people who would act on them. She had told herself it was only a second's due.

It was not. She knew what it was. It was the thing she had burned a card in the autumn to keep from writing. *Mine.*

"You're not writing," said Pike.

"There's nothing to write. It's not my lane."

"You've written every lane she's stood since the gate. On the back of the slate, small, where you think I can't see." He did not look at her. "*Watches feet.* *Called the far edge from sixty paces.* *Kept the week's crossing.* I can read, Rhea. I've been reading your slate since you first ruled it."

Rhea did not answer that.

On the floor, Selka Dray had turned back to say something to the girl over the head of the litter, and the girl was answering, and the Graving was talking to itself in a long low rumble about what it had seen. Rhea watched the girl's face. It was not a face that had lost anything. It was the face of somebody who had put a thing down on a table where anybody could see it, and was standing back from it.

"Twice in a week," said Rhea. "She's done that twice. In a hall last night, and on a floor this morning. Put a thing down and stood back from it." She took up the slate. "I've never in my life done that once."

She did not write on the back of it. She turned it over to the front, where the six columns were, and below them, in the clear space at the foot where she wrote a card before she copied it out fair, she began to write a card.

It was not the girl's card. It was not time for the girl's card, and it was not hers to write until the girl had been asked, and the girl had answered, and either of them had said no. That was the 30th. She would not write it a day before.

She wrote the other one.

*Graywater. Pryce, A., Hold, Meridian, Team Seven. To Doran Mace's ward line on the night van, in her own place and not a relief's, from the first of the fourth month, at a ward-hand's wage. Mace asked for her by name in the autumn and left her name where it was. A card is not a seat. R. Sorn.*

She read it over. She thought of the Pryce girl on the Ropewalk's boards on Thursday morning, teaching a laid Hold with no ward left how to lean from the floor, knowing it would be used against her own four, and doing it anyway. Mace had told her that on Thursday night at the van, laughing. *She'll give it away to anybody who asks properly. That's the best thing about her and the worst.*

Rhea copied the card out fair, onto a white card from her pocket, in Graywater's slate blue, and blew on it.

"That's not hers," said Pike, reading upside down.

"It's Pryce's."

"I know whose it is. I said it's not *hers.*" He looked at the floor, where the girl was walking back to Seven's hurdle with her bound hand against her coat and the Finch girl beside her. "You'll write hers on the 30th."

"I'll ask her on the 30th. If she says no, I'll not write it."

"And if she says yes?"

Rhea put the card in her pocket.

"Then I'll find out what I write," she said. "I don't know yet. That's the truth, and it's the first time I've said a thing like that to you." She stood up, with her slate under her arm. "I'll pin this at the baize. Come on. You can watch me do a thing I've never done, which is give somebody's Hold away to somebody else's line without asking her captain first."

"Cade'll mind."

"Cade'll mind for an hour and then he'll say he'd like it known that he's glad," said Rhea. "I've watched him all week as well."

She went down the tiers toward the floor's edge, a step at a time, and Pike came after her with his half-closed hands held a little away from his sides, as a man carries a thing he has been asked to carry and does not mean to drop.

# Chapter 38 — Kiva: The Measure Is Read

Halden License lost to Holloway's second four at one o'clock on Saturday by five points to two, and Kiva watched every carry of it from the sixth tier with her right hand in fresh linen and her knees still stiff from the stone.

She had not meant to watch it so closely. She had come up the tiers after Seven's lane to sit with Aske and eat something, and Aske had put a pasty in her good hand and said *eat,* and she had eaten it. Then Halden had come out of the side door in their black and gold, and she had stopped eating.

Kellan was on the floor. She had half thought he would not be. She had lain awake for an hour last night wondering whether Pettit would stand him down after the Salt Hall. But Pettit had declared him at the box in his heavy flat voice, *Renn, edge,* the same as every other day, and Kellan had gone to his place on the north side of the pen and stood there with his edge dark on his fist and his face giving nothing away.

He fought well. Kiva could see that from the sixth tier. He took Holloway's carry with a touch in the first, as clean as anything she had seen all week, and he read their Hold's feet in the second and was there before she moved. It did not matter. Holloway's second four had been ninth on the Bench and fourth on the Hunt, and nobody in the Graving had watched them all week, and they had watched everybody. They had watched Seven's elbows on Thursday and Friday, and Carrow's word, and the hen, and Lyle's lean. In the third carry their catcher sat on the litter's head with both forearms along the canvas, and their Hold leaned on Halden's wall from the floor, and Holloway's blade stood off Kellan's left side and waited for a count he could not see coming.

"*Five to Holloway,*" said the marshal at the glass. "*Two to Halden.*"

The Graving made the long sound of breath let out. It went round the tiers twice. Everybody in it could add.

"That's the Measure," said Aske, beside her, quite quietly. "Isn't it."

"It's the Ropewalk's," said Kiva, "if they beat Holloway's first four at three."

"And if they don't?"

"Then it's still not Halden's." She had done the sum on the tiers, with her left hand on her knee, as Kellan would have done it. "Halden's sixty-five and the Lane. They'd have needed to win today and stand fourth or better. They've lost a lane. They'll be ninth or tenth in the Lane, on points. Eighty, or eighty-one." She looked down at the floor, where Kellan was walking back to Halden's hurdle with his edge dark and Pettit's hand on his shoulder. "The Ropewalk's fifty-eight. If they win at three, they're first in the Lane, or near it. Eighty-two."

"You'd make a grader," said Aske.

"His mother's a grader," said Kiva.

Below them, at Halden's hurdle, Kellan had stopped. He stood with Pettit's hand still on his shoulder, and he looked up at the tiers, at the Meridian knot, and found Seven in it, and found her. He did not wave. He did a thing Kiva had seen him do once before, at a gate, with a pencil: he held up one finger, and then a second, and then let his hand fall.

Two. By two marks. He had done the sum on the floor, before the glass had stopped running.

Old Four lost to Westerley's first four at two, five to two, and Lowen Brask walked off the floor afterward with his notebook open and wrote in it all the way to the side door, as he always did, without looking up.

At three, the Ropewalk's first four came out of the side door for the last lane of the National Measure, and the Graving stood up for Carrow as it had stood up on Friday.

Kiva stood with it. She found that she wanted to.

Holloway's first four had been third on the board after three, and they were good: a big quiet four in dark red who had found in two minutes on the Bench what Seven had needed four to find. They did not lay. They sequenced, as Meridian did, with a still door who planted on his captain's word and a blade who did not move until she was sure. Kiva had watched them beat the Ropewalk's second four on Friday and thought they were the best four in the Measure that nobody talked about.

Carrow declared at the box. Her voice went up the stone walls, low and flat and pleasant.

"*Carrow, edge. Declared. Single.*" She paused. Then, quite deliberately, she added a thing Kiva had never heard a Saltmere captain say. "*And no change.*"

The Graving made a sound. It was very small. Down at the mast on the north side of the pen, Noa looked up from his desk, and then he looked along the tiers until he found Seven, and Kiva saw him find her. Then he looked down and wrote.

"She's had that off Cade," said Aske. "This morning."

"Off Perrin," said Kiva. "Yes."

She had heard it from the Meridian knot in the middle tiers, where Perrin was sitting with his hand pressed to his side. She heard him make a sound that was not quite a word.

The lane went to the Ropewalk, five to three, and it was not close. Kiva watched every carry. Carrow walked on Treen's word, *set,* as she had walked on Friday, and Treen read feet and hands both now, as she had learned on Friday to read them. In the second carry the Ropewalk carried the Commodore down the pen on Tolver's forearms and Treen's, crossed at the wrists, with Lyle leaning from the floor on Holloway's wall. In the third carry, with the Measure on the glass, Holloway's blade came for Carrow at the litter's head, and Treen said *far edge,* two words, once. Carrow turned to her right as if she had been called by name, and Holloway's blade was there, coming round on the side Carrow had not been watching, and Carrow's edge came round and touched her.

"*Down.* Holloway."

"She's had that off you," said Aske.

"Off Pike," said Kiva. "Through me. Through Ivey, I expect, and the Company's yard, and the Marshal's page." She watched Carrow walk back to the Ropewalk's hurdle with one door and nothing open. "It's not anybody's now. It's on a page in every catcher's pocket on the coast."

She looked at Lyle, who was standing by the Ropewalk's hurdle with nothing at her hip. Then she walked across to her and put her hand on her shoulder, and stood there.

Mara had asked Kiva at noon to come to the Long Quay at five, and Kiva came. She came alone, because Mara had asked for her alone, and because she found she did not want anybody else.

The coast boat came in under the sea gate at ten to five, out of a grey sea with the light going yellow behind it, and Kiva saw her before the boat was alongside.

She was standing at the bow, by herself, with her hands folded at her waist. She was in plain grey, with a grey cap, tall and thin and very upright, and she was not looking at the quay or the town or the crowd of green that had gathered along the Long Quay's edge because the Mercury had said in the morning that she was coming. She was looking back over her shoulder, past the boat's wake, past the Mole and the arch of the sea gate with its black iron hanging in it. She was looking at the sea.

Kiva felt her doors lie down.

She was forty paces off, on the quay's edge by the third bollard, with Mara beside her and Peverell and Yeo and the sister from the Order a little way along. Forty paces. On the stair of the Order's house, a month ago, she had felt nothing from the doorway, and had felt her doors lie down only in the small room with the fire. Now they lay down at forty paces, across a strip of brown water, all three of them, the Ember under her breastbone and the hand door under her palms and the still door under everything. They did not shut. They lay down where they were, as dogs lie down when somebody comes into a room whom they do not need to watch.

She put her left hand on the bollard. The first two fingers lay on the iron, lightly.

*It keeps time.*

It did, as it always did on this quay, four and four, up through the iron from the granite. Nothing under her fingers had changed. Only her own doors had lain down, and at forty paces, which they had not done at the Order's gate.

"You've felt her," said Mara, very low. Her own left hand was out of her pocket, flat against her side. "So have I, in my hand. From here." She did not take her eyes off the boat. "In her own house she'd nothing to hold against. Here she has the whole town."

The boat came alongside. The gangway went down. The Master of the Order of the Even Hand did not come down it at once. She stood at the bow a little longer, looking at the sea, and the crowd on the quay waited and did not push, because she was not looking at them.

Then she turned and came down the gangway, slowly, a step at a time, with her hands at her waist.

At the foot of it, on the granite of the Long Quay, she stopped. She stood quite still. Kiva saw her feel the town. She saw it go through the long pale calm face like a wince that is not allowed to become one, and stay there for a moment, and go.

Behind her, on the boat's deck, two sisters in grey were handing a cart down off the boat by its shafts, with great care. It was a low flat cart with iron wheels. On its bed, packed in straw, under a grey cloth, there were jars. Kiva could see the shapes of them through the cloth, small and round, in rows. She could see the grey seals on their necks where the cloth had slipped, each with a mark on it like two hands laid flat side by side.

Thrale looked along the quay. Her grey eyes went over the crowd and the white lamps and the green doors and the bills on them, *THE REGIMENT, NOT A STROKE DROPPED,* and came to rest on Mara, and then on Kiva, forty paces off at the bollard. They rested there without searching, as a hand rests on a table.

She did not come nearer.

"Mrs. Rowan," she said. She did not raise her voice. It came along the quay as though there were nothing in between. "Miss Rowan. I'll not come closer. The last time you were in a room with me, you took a fever on my ferry. The ferryman told the sister at the gate that you were carried off his boat on a box." She looked at Kiva. "You've a board to hear read tonight. I'd not have you hot for it."

"Thank you," said Kiva.

"I've seen the sea," said Clemency Thrale. "I said I'd like to, before I was very much older. I've stood at that boat's bow for a day and a half and looked at it." She was quiet a moment. "It doesn't keep time. Nothing in it keeps time. It's every count at once. I'd forgotten." Her folded hands did not move. "And this town keeps one count, in everything, and I can feel it from my feet up, and it's the loudest place I've ever stood."

"There's a hut at the end of the Mole," said Yeo, beside the sister. Her voice was brisk, and a little too careful. "The Company's watch hut. On the sea side, past the gate. It's on the Mole's stone and not in the harbour's water. There's no cell in it. I've had it swept."

"Thank you, officer. I'll lodge there." Thrale looked along the Mole, out toward the sea gate and the arch and the long curved wall going out into the grey. "I'll come into the town for an hour at a time, and no more. An hour in this town is a day's work for me. Tomorrow I'll come for the Board, when it sits, and I'll stand where it puts me, and I'll speak for the cellar." She looked down at the cart, where the two sisters were settling the cloth back over the jars. "I've brought thirty-six. I've not given them. I've brought them. That's a different thing, and the Board will hear the difference tomorrow." She looked at Peverell, whom she had never met, and seemed to know her. "And you'll hear what they cost, keeper. You'll hear it first."

Peverell took her spectacles off their cord.

"I'll hear it," she said. "I've heard a good many prices this fortnight."

"Not this one," said Thrale.

She turned and walked away along the Long Quay toward the Mole, with the two sisters and the cart behind her and the crowd of green opening in front of her without being asked. She walked slowly. Kiva watched her go, and felt her doors get up, one at a time, as she went, and stand, and shake themselves, and the town's count come back up through the iron under her fingers to where it always was.

"She's not a hundred," said Kiva.

"Fifty, by her face," said Mara. "Older than that by her letters." She put her hand back in her pocket. "She left her house without a Master for the first time in eleven years, to bring thirty-six jars to a bank she's never seen, for a town that's never heard of her." She was quiet. "I wrote and asked. I'd not have come, in her place."

"You did come. In the third month. Two hundred miles."

"For a bank I'd been asked to read," said Mara. "Not to give away the only thing my house has."

*It keeps time.*

She wrote it. *Saturday the 19th. A quarter to seven. The rail at the Graving's lip. Yes.*

She read from the twenty-fourth.

It was the sound of a girl in sea blue, a Hold by her shoulders, who had been on the national roll a moment ago, and was not, and had not yet understood it. *The Hunt.* The board that had put Twenty-Eight on the roll by a tie was the one that had been read on Wednesday night under a line in a clerk's hand. *D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company, reserve to the officer, lost. The Board records it.* Dray had stood under that line and said *I'll take it. I'd not want anybody to think I was pleased.*

She was not pleased now. Kiva could see that from two rows back.

She was aware of Pru behind her making a sound like somebody who has been holding their breath for a week. She was aware of the Graving making a sound as well, a long sound, from the Meridian knot in the middle tiers outward, that was partly the warm sound and partly something else.

*Sixty-nine.* Nineteen on the Bench, sixth, without her. Twenty-four on the Carry, first, on Perrin's back, up a ladder that he had climbed with a cracked rib on her word. Ten on the Hunt, fifteenth, with clean work on their stow and a pass in three that had held for a girl and been too late for a man. Sixteen on the Lane. Seven had been put on the roll by a four and a method, by things with handles, that anybody in the Graving had watched and could do next year. Holloway's second four had done the hen to Halden this afternoon. The Ropewalk had done the elbows. Treen had called *far edge.*

She stood in the ninth row with her bound hand against her coat and did not cry, because it was not that kind of thing.

She read *third, Westerley, first four, seventy-nine.*

"*Second,*" said the Clerk. "*Halden License, Meridian. Eighty.*"

Kellan was in the front row. He did not move. Pettit beside him put his hand on Kellan's shoulder, quite heavily, as he had at the hurdle, and left it there.

The Graving stood up and shouted, and went on shouting.

She turned her head and looked along the rows until she found Seven. She found Kiva. She inclined her head, an inch, as a fact about the weather.

# Chapter 39 — Kiva: The Last Witness

Orla Dane knocked on the Chair's door at the officers' lodging at six on Sunday morning with the head of her stick, because her knee would not let her stand long enough to wait for an answer to her knuckles.

Ysra Crest opened it herself. She was dressed. Orla had known she would be. She had a pen in her hand and a sheet of paper under it, and behind her on the lodging's narrow table there was a lamp turned low and the Chair's ledger lying open beside the thick cream letter that had come to her from the Long Quay a week ago in a large, upright, old hand.

"Dane," said the Chair.

"Crest." Orla did not come in. "Two minutes. Then I'll go down to the pump house and you'll go to a dining room on the quay with a man who can't stand up. I'd sooner say it before than after."

The Chair stood aside. Orla came in, stick first, and sat down on the room's one hard chair without being asked, because she would have fallen over otherwise. The Chair did not sit. She stood by the table with the pen in her hand.

"You've answered him," said Orla, looking at the cream letter.

"An hour ago." The Chair turned the sheet under her pen round on the table so that Orla could read it. It was very short, in the level hand that Orla had read on every condition Kiva Rowan had ever been given.

*To Mr. A. Halden, the Counting House. You asked me on the 12th, in your own hand, to ask Miss Rowan for the sequence of three doors, on any terms I set. I set none. The sequence is not to be asked of her. That stands as I wrote it in the spring. If the country wishes to unwrite it, the country may ask me in a room in front of everybody, and I shall say no in the same room. I thank you for asking properly. Y. Crest, Placement Chair, Meridian.*

Orla read it twice, with her lips shut.

"She said that at his table," she said. "In nearly those words."

"I know. Adlam told me what she said, in the street, the morning after." The Chair took the sheet back. "I'd like it said twice, in two hands. Once by the girl, at a dinner. Once by the Chair, on paper. Then nobody can say the first was a child being rude to an old man, or the second a clerk hiding behind a rule." She blotted it. "What are your two minutes?"

Orla looked at her stick for a moment.

"My line," she said. "On her certificate. The sponsor's. *Recovery only, dark hands; and one crossing.* My standing rides on her record. Every condition you've written since the spring has my name somewhere under it." She did not look up. "I'd like you to strike it."

The Chair did not move.

"Not today," said Orla. "When it's no longer needed. I want it asked now, in this room, and written down, so it's been asked before and not after. I've held things back in case I was wrong. A card for twenty years. I'll not hold this one."

"When is no longer needed?"

"When she makes a crossing in the field, inside her certificate, on her own word, with nobody's standing riding on it but hers." Orla's crooked face did not change. "I'll know when. So will you. It may be Wednesday. It may be never. If it's never, leave my name where it is and I'll be glad of it."

The Chair looked at her for a long time across the narrow room, with the lamp between them. They had stood on opposite sides of a Bench in 2660, Orla at the rail with her candidate and Crest on the ward, and they had both watched a boy's hand go, and they had never once spoken of it in twenty years.

"Why?" said the Chair.

"Because she stood up in a hall on Friday and put a thing on a table under her own name that she'd been keeping since she was thirteen." Orla's voice was rough and brief. "And on Saturday she knelt on a floor in front of the whole town on one door, because she'd decided what her crossing was for, and nobody had told her. My line says she's mine to answer for. She's not. She's hers." She put both hands on her stick. "A line that says otherwise when it isn't true is a lie in your ledger. I'd not have that, at my age."

The Chair turned to the ledger and took up her pen and wrote, standing, at the foot of the page that had Kiva Rowan's conditions on it. Orla watched the pen move. When it stopped, the Chair turned the ledger round.

*Requested by the accountable sponsor, O. Dane, Sunday the 20th of the third month, 2681, at six in the morning: that the sponsor's line be struck when the certificate is able to stand in the candidate's own name. To be decided by the Chair on evidence. Y. C.*

"On evidence," said Orla.

"Always." The Chair capped her pen. "You'll be at the Graving at two."

"On a bench at the back. Where I can get out."

"Then I'll see you there." The Chair hesitated, which Orla had never seen her do. "He'll be at the Graving as well. Hart. He's said he'll stand and say what the hand says, if he's asked again."

"I know he will," said Orla. "I'm going to the pump house now. Before. Not after."

He was on the gallery at a quarter to seven, oiling the second pump, with his book of slacks in his jacket and his bad hand at his side.

It was the middle of a neap ebb, and the basin was going out quietly under the sea gate, brown and slow. "Dane," he said.

"Jess."

Nobody had called him that in this building, she thought, but Peverell, and Peverell said it like the name of a tool. Orla had not said it since 2660. It came out of her mouth rough and strange, like a word in a language she had stopped speaking.

He put down the oil can.

They stood on the gallery with the cells between them. She had come here a fortnight ago, on a Sunday, and said the wrong thing first and not found the right one after. He had told her so. She had walked down this stair afterward and told the girl it had gone badly, and the girl had told her that Hart had said the same thing to her. *It went badly on both sides.* She had not known whether to be glad of that. She was not, she found, glad of it now. She only wanted to say a true thing to him and go down the stair.

"I'll say one thing," she said. "I've had it ready since the boat. I'll say it badly. It's true."

He waited. He had always been good at waiting. She had taught him that, on a floor, when he was fifteen.

"I didn't write after the Bench," said Orla, "because I was afraid you'd write back and tell me it was my fault, and it was."

The pumps went up and down. One of the cells in the north gallery flickered, as a cell does, and steadied.

Hart did not say anything for a long time.

"It was mine as well," he said at last. "I went onto that Bench with a door I knew wasn't ready, because I wanted you to see me do it. I'd have gone if you'd forbidden me." He looked at his bad hand, at his side, with the fingers half curled. "I've known that for twenty years. I've not said it to anybody."

"That doesn't make it not mine."

"No. It makes it both." He picked up the oil can again. "I'll not say it's all right, Dane. It's not. I'd have liked a letter."

"I'd not believe you if you said it was."

"I know you wouldn't. That's why I'm not." He bent to the pump's crank and put a drop of oil on it, very carefully, with his left hand. "Go down. You've a girl standing up in a dock at two, and I've a hand to stand up beside her. I'll see you there."

She went down. At the foot of the stair she stopped and looked back up at the gallery, where he was oiling the pump with his back to her, and she thought that nothing had been mended. Nothing was going to be mended. It had been said, by both of them, in a building full of things keeping time, and it would stay said.

That was not nothing. She had thought, for twenty years, that it would be.

The Counting House was lit at nine on Sunday morning, every window, as it had been lit at eight on a Friday night a week ago, white and steady all the way up.

Kiva came along the Long Quay with Mara beside her and the Chair a pace behind. The Clerk of the Measure was already on the step, small and grey, with her gold chain on her coat and the two Board members flanking her: the heavy man with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, and the thin woman with ink on her cuff and a ledger under her arm. Halden's counsel stood a little apart in his neat grey coat with his folder against his chest. Nobody else had been let come. The Clerk had said so in the Salt Hall, and she had said it again on the step, to the Mercury's young man, who had followed them along the quay with three pencils and stood now at the bottom of the step looking as though he might cry.

"Two o'clock," the Clerk told him. "In the Graving. You'll hear all of it read."

"No," said the Clerk. "It's not. That's why the room's not large."

On the fourth step, her heart began to slow.

She had known it would. She felt it come down, and come down again on the tenth step, and by the top of the stair it was going slowly and evenly and heavily, like a pump. She put her left hand in her coat pocket and kept it there. She did not lay her fingers on the banister. She knew what the banister would say.

Mara, on the stair beside her, had her left hand in her pocket too.

"You've gone white," said Mara, very low.

"I know."

"So have I." Mara did not look at her. "Fifty-seven, I'd say. I've never been fifty-seven in my life."

The long room was as it had been. The windows at both ends, one over the harbour and one over the high town. The fire. The long dark table, polished, with nothing on it but a white cloth at the far end and two candlesticks and a glass case with a brass frame. Mr. Adlam stood by the harbour window with his hat in his hand and his long face astonished, which it was not.

And at the head of the table, with his left hand flat on the case, sat the Proprietor.

He did not get up. He looked at them as they came in, one after another, with his lean face and his dark hair grey at the temples, a man of fifty-five by his face and a hundred and three by every other measure. He looked at the Clerk and the Board, and the Chair, and his own counsel, and Mara, and Kiva last.

"Forgive me for sitting," he said. His voice was pleasant and dry, as it had been. "I can't rise without lifting my hand, and I don't lift it. Miss Rowan knows."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"Then she'll know where to sit." He did not smile. "Miss Rowan. I asked you to sit further off a week ago, and you didn't, and I was sorry. I'll not ask today. You'll sit where you choose."

Kiva went to the far end of the table. All the way. She sat down in the last chair, thirty feet from him, with the harbour window behind her and the white cloth and the two candlesticks between them, and felt her heart come up a beat, two beats, as though somebody had let go of a rope. Her Ember settled under her breastbone. Her still door, which had wanted to beat on the stair, lay still.

"Thank you," said the Proprietor. "That's the right chair. I was too proud to say so last time."

Mara sat beside her. The Chair sat on the window side. The Clerk sat at the middle of the table, halfway between, with the two Board members on either side, and the thin woman opened her ledger and dipped her pen. Halden's counsel stood behind his master's chair and did not sit.

"The Charter Board," said the Clerk, "takes the evidence of Mr. Ambrose Halden, at his request, under his own name, at his own table, because he can't come to the Board. What's said will be written down by the Board's clerk and read in the Graving at two, in full." She folded her hands on the table. "Mr. Halden."

He told it to the harbour window.

He told it as he had told it to Kiva a week ago, at the same table, in nearly the same words, and she found that she could hear the places where the words had worn smooth from being said to himself for seventy-seven years. A bench clerk on the second floor, twenty-six, four years in the house, who had written nothing in his life but counts. *Fell in at fifty to the minute.* A fever on the evening of the 11th of the third month, 2604. A window at three in the morning. To anybody. In seventy-seven years."

Kiva sat very still at the far end of the table.

"Some go white," said the Proprietor. "Some go out. And on the Long Quay in 2604, the lamps that went white were the lamps that kept time together, and the lamps that went out were the lamps that kept their own." He did not raise his voice. "That's my reading. I've had it since I was twenty-seven. If whatever comes, comes again, I want this country's lamps white and not out. I want every bank stepped, every pump house, every box in every tenement, all keeping one time, so that whatever comes finds them lit." He looked at the Clerk. "And you're certain," said the heavy Board member.

"I'm sure of my reading," said the Proprietor. "I've been sure of it for seventy-seven years. I'm not certain I'm right." He said it quite simply, as he might have said the time. "Those are two different things, sir. I've been a clerk. I know the difference between a count and what it means." He turned his head and looked down the table at Kiva again. "Which is why I asked the Board to bring Miss Rowan. I'd like to ask her one question. Through the Board, if the Board allows it. She's not obliged to answer."

The Clerk looked at Kiva.

"You're not obliged," she said.

"I know," said Kiva.

"Then ask it, Mr. Halden."

The Proprietor looked at her for a long moment, down thirty feet of polished wood, past the two candlesticks and the white cloth. Her heart was going steadily. Her still door was still. Her hands were hot in her lap.

"The ferry," he said. "Below the Order's house. Last month. The Mercury says you were lower than before, and that the lights were nearer, and that some were going out." He did not lean forward; he could not. "Were any of them white?"

She had known he would ask. She had known since the shop on Thursday night, when she held the ferry back from her four and told them she did not yet know whom it was for. She had known on a gallery in a pump house, and on the quay with Kellan, who had said *every time he gets to white his voice goes quiet.* She had known all night, lying awake by the north window with the Mercury under her pillow.

It was the one thing she had seen that would please him.

If she said *yes,* every word of it would go into the thin woman's ledger, under the Proprietor's own line, and be read at two in the Graving to the whole town, and printed in the Mercury in the morning beside *some go white* in a Glory master's hand. Halden's counsel would have it in his folder by tonight. It would be a stone in the foundation's wall. It would be read by every man in the country who wanted his lamps white and not out, and it would mean, to every one of them, that the Proprietor had been right.

She did not know that it meant that. She did not know what it meant at all.

If she said *no,* or said nothing, he would never know. Nobody would. It was hers alone. She had told Mara everything for four years at a kitchen table, and she had not told Mara this. It was the one piece of the dream she had kept, even from the page.

She thought of a lamp-man in Holloway who would not have it written, because he would be laughed at. She thought that he had not been the only one keeping a thing back for fear of what it would be taken to mean. The Proprietor had kept his for seventy-seven years, from shame. She had been about to keep hers for the opposite reason. She would be keeping it because she did not like whose side it would be put on.

That was not a reason. That was choosing a side by leaving a thing out of a column, and she had watched her aunt, in the autumn, refuse to hide a line on a mast that she did not understand.

"Yes," said Kiva.

The room was very quiet.

"Some had gone white," she said. "On the ferry. Not on Classification Day, and not when I was thirteen. Only the last time. I was lower than I'd been, and the lights on the roads were nearer, and I could see them better. Some were bright and some were dull. Some were going out. And some had gone white." He looked at his own hand on the case. "I've known for seventy-seven years that white is what I saw. I've never once known what white is. Nor have you. Nor did she."

"No," said Kiva.

"And you told me, knowing what I'd do with it."

"Yes."

"You might have kept it."

"I'd have been keeping it from you," said Kiva. "That's not the same as keeping it."

The Proprietor looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, as he had laughed a week ago at the same table, short and real, and it went across his lean face and stayed there.

"No," he said. "It isn't. I've spent seventy-seven years not learning the difference." He turned to the Clerk. "That's all I have, madam. The Board may go to its dock."

Kiva thought about it, honestly, because she had been asked.

It was not on Mara's page, because she had never told Mara; she had tried once, at thirteen, and found she had no word for it, and had never tried again. It was in Annick's notebook too, in one line. "That's in your aunt's page?"

"No," said Mara, beside her. Her voice was dry and exact, and only Kiva, who had known it all her life, could have heard what it cost. "It's not. She's never told me it."

The Clerk looked at the two of them for a moment longer.

"Then it's left off," she said. "Write that, please. *A thing she has no word for. Not put on the table.*"

The thin woman wrote it.

The Proprietor had not moved at the head of the table. But Kiva saw his eyes, which had been on the harbour window, come round to her and stay there, very still. He knew the line in Annick's notebook. He had read it in 2610. He did not say anything.

And I'll break its rule on a dock floor in front of a town I've never seen."

She looked along the dock at Peverell, on the first bench.

"Keeper. I said on the quay you'd hear the price first. Here it is." She did not raise her voice. "My house breaks its rule, and writes in its own book that the Master broke it, on this day, in this dock, and why. That's mine to pay, and I'll pay it. And four jars are what's left for every hand that's hurt on the road south, for as long as there's no more plain in the country. That's my house's to pay." She was quiet a moment. "And this is yours. If those thirty-six jars go into your cells on Wednesday, those thirty-six cells are never stepped again. Not by Mr. Halden's house, nor the Works, nor the Committee, nor you, nor any keeper after you, while your pump house stands. Each on its own count, not two alike, for good. Written in your book and in mine, under your name and the Committee's." She looked at the Committee's bench. "You'll keep a slower bank, and a dearer one, and your fitters will curse it every winter for the rest of their lives. That's the price. It's not ash. It's that."

Peverell stood up.

She stood for a long moment at the end of the first bench with her spectacles on their cord and her apron on, because she had come from the pump house, and her small crabbed hands at her sides. Then she took the keeper's book for 2604 off the Board's table, where she had laid it, and opened it at the back, at a clean page, and took the pencil out of her apron pocket.

"I'll write it," she said. "In this. My father's father's book. The page after the night the lamps went white." She wrote, standing, against the table, in her small crabbed hand. "*Received from the Order of the Even Hand, thirty-six jars of plain. In return, the harbour bank never to be stepped again while the house stands. Each cell its own count. Peverell, keeper.*" She held the pencil out to the chairman of the Harbour Committee without looking at him. "Mr. Chairman."

The chairman looked at the pencil.

"A slower bank," he said. "A dearer one."

"And the low town dry on its own count, and not on anybody's," said Peverell. "Sign it or don't. If you don't, it's my house, and I'll sign for the house, and you can take me to the magistrate in the morning." She held the pencil out. "I've not slept since Tuesday. I'd sooner you signed."

The chairman of the Harbour Committee took the pencil. He signed. His hand was not quite steady.

At the gate, Thrale bowed her head an inch.

"Then they're given," she said. "Not brought. Given." She looked at the sister on her left. "Write it in our book tonight, at the hut. In my words. *Given to a bank, by the Master, who broke the house's rule to do it, and said the price first.*" She was quiet. "And write that I'd do it again."

It was ten to three by the clock on the Board's table. Kiva saw that the Master's long pale face had gone grey, and that her folded hands were no longer quite still. One thumb crossed the other, and went back, and crossed again.

"Master," said the Clerk. "You've ten minutes."

"I've five," said Thrale. "I'll go now, if the Board's done with me. I'd like to walk back to the Mole while I can still walk it." She unfolded her hands for the first time since she had come down the steps, and let them hang at her sides. "Miss Rowan."

Kiva stood up on the second bench. She did not decide to. Her knees decided.

"Your lips counted," said Thrale, down the length of the dock, "when you read her. The sister wrote it down. I've thought about it since the summer. I said at my house that I'd not decided what I thought." She looked at Kiva across three hundred paces of stone with her grey eyes, which did not search. "I've decided. I think you were counting the roads. I think that's all. I think it's a good thing to count, and a hard one to stop."

She turned and walked out of the Graving by the seaward ramp, with the two sisters a step behind her, and the empty cart left standing by the gate, and the thirty-six jars on it in their straw.

Kiva's doors got up, one at a time, as she went, and stood, and shook themselves. The town's count came back up through the bench to where it always was. And the heat in her hands, which had been held, she understood now, by the same thing that had held her doors down, came up all at once like water when a hand is taken off a pipe.

She sat down rather quickly.

"Rowan," said Aske, behind her, very low. He had her wrist already. "A hundred and two."

"I know."

"Sit. Don't stand again." He did not let go. "I'll take you out when the Board's done. Not before. You'd never forgive me."

The Clerk had not moved through any of it. She sat at the middle of the Board's table with her hands folded and her small grey face giving nothing away, and when the Master had gone up the ramp and out of sight, she let the silence stand for a count of ten.

"Mrs. Rowan," she said. "The Works. You stood on Friday and said what you'll do on Wednesday. The Board's heard the Proprietor's reading and the Master's. What's the Works'?"

Mara stood. She still had the page folded under her reading hand.

"The Works hasn't got one either," she said. "The Works doesn't believe anything that hasn't happened. Mr. Rusk will tell you so." She did not look at Rusk. "I'll tell you mine, since you've asked. It's the Home Line's as well; a foreman told me so in the autumn, on a hill, about a drum." Her voice was dry and exact. "I don't know what's coming. Nobody here does. The Proprietor thinks it'll find stepped lamps white. The Master thinks it'll find nothing to answer in a house with one door. I don't know which of them is right, or whether either is." She looked at the Board. "I know one thing, and I've known it since a bank on a hill went into step with a pump through the water. Whatever's coming, don't be one engine when it comes. Not one count, in everything, that one hand can drive and one fault can stop. Thirty-six counts, not two alike, each with a hand on it that isn't mine." She sat down. "That's not a reading. It's a fitter's rule. It's all I've got."

The Clerk of the Measure stood up.

She stood behind the middle chair with her gold chain on her coat, and when she spoke everybody heard her, from the Board's table to the lip, eighty feet up, where people were leaning over the rail.

"The Board will say what it can," she said. "It'll not say more."

She did not look at a paper.

"The Board records that a thing has been dreamed, with a fever, by people who never heard of each other. By the Master Annick of the Even Hand, between 2446 and 2450, in a notebook she showed nobody. By the keeper at Ferren and the keeper at Saltmere, on the same night in 2604, in their remarks columns, because a fever isn't a fault. By the keeper at Westerley, that night, in the morning, to his wife. By Mr. Ambrose Halden, that night and eleven times since. By the Master Thrale, three times. By a lamp-man at Holloway on the 17th of the ninth month last year, who's asked his name not be printed, and who'll have his wish. And by Miss Kiva Rowan, of Meridian, at thirteen and since, whose account the Board has had read to it from a page that was shut before she could have borrowed it from anybody." The Clerk paused. "The Board believes that page. It's had thirty years of pages in a great many rooms. It knows a shut one."

The Graving did not make a sound.

"The Board can't say what was dreamed," said the Clerk. "It's heard three readings of it this afternoon, and they don't agree, and none of the people who gave them say they're certain. The Board isn't going to be certain on their behalf." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, and was gone. "The Board has no name for it. It's been called a fever and a fault and a dream and a remark. None of those is right. The Master Annick had a phrase for it. The Board heard it read this afternoon from her own notebook, and it'll borrow it, for want of a better, and give it back the moment anybody finds one." She folded her hands. "What the Master called the sky unroofed. The Board will call it the unroofed sky, and it'll write that down, and it'll not say what it is, because nobody in this dock can."

Kiva sat on the second bench with Aske's hand on her wrist and her own hands burning in her lap, and heard four thousand people take a breath at once and not let it out.

"The Board warrants a Barrel response," said the Clerk, in exactly the same voice, "at the harbour pump house, at the dawn slack on Wednesday the 23rd, on the keeper's leave, which she's given. Under the Saltmere Company's officers. With the Municipal Works' reader on the landing, and the keeper's fitters on the cells, and the Order's thirty-six jars, given, at the price the Master said, which is written in the keeper's book and the Committee's name. Any four on the national roll may stand in it, by name, on the Board's warrant and the Board's ash, if it chooses. No four is obliged." She looked round the dock. "The sealed prediction in the Board's press will be opened at noon on Wednesday, as it was always going to be, in front of whoever Mrs. Rowan names and whoever the Board does. It'll be found right or wrong. The Board will write down which."

She looked at the clock.

"The foundation's claim is for the Compact's court. The Board has Mr. Renn's figures and Mr. Adlam's books, and it'll send both to the court tonight, with a line saying it read them." She looked at Noa. "Mr. Bexley's four columns go up together. The Board's looked at them for three days and it won't draw a line between them either. It'll send them to every pump house and lamp office on its roll, as they are, without a line." She paused. "The Board rises."

The Chair did not rise with it.

She sat at the end of the long table where she had sat all afternoon, with her ledger open in front of her, and while the Graving stood up round her in pieces from the top down and began to talk all at once, Kiva saw her turn to a page near the back of the ledger and take up her pen.

It was a page that had only two lines on it. Kiva had seen it once, in the autumn, upside down, across the Chair's desk. It was headed *Witnessed,* because there had been no column for her, and there was still no column for her.

The Chair wrote a third line. She wrote it sitting, in her level hand, while the Graving stood up around her, and blotted it, and turned the ledger round on the table, and held it so that Kiva could read it from the bench.

*Sunday the 20th of the third month, 2681. Put it on the table herself, under her own name, before anybody asked. Y.C.*

Kiva read it twice.

Then Aske stood up behind her, and put his hand under her arm, and said *now,* and she went.

---

The fever came up along the Long Quay, in the white lamps, as it had come a week ago.

She had expected it. She found that expecting it did not help. By the third bollard she was shaking, and by Quiller's door she could not have climbed the stair if Jude had not taken one arm and Perrin, against every card he had, the other, until Aske said *Cade* in a voice Kiva had never heard him use, and Anwen took Perrin's place without a word.

Aske had her on the bed by the north window inside a minute, with the candle lit and his watch out and Pru on her knees on the other side with her capped fingers on Kiva's wrist, very lightly, because she could not grip.

"A hundred and four," said Aske. "Going up." He put the back of his hand against her cheek, as her aunt used to. "Two of them. In one day. A man with his hand on a glass in the morning, and a woman with her back to the sea in the afternoon." He wrote it on the card, standing, against the window frame, and Kiva watched him write it and could not read it for the heat. "*The third this year. Two Glory masters inside six hours.* That's all I'll write. It's all I know."

"I knew," said Kiva. Her teeth were together. "On the quay. Before."

"I know you did. You sat in the far chair this morning and you stood up for her this afternoon anyway." He sat down on the upturned bucket. "I'll not say you shouldn't. I'll count."

She lay with her eyes open and her jaw shut and waited for the roof to go.

She knew how it would come, if it came. She had felt it come on a ferry with her hands on the rail. She lay with the candle burning and Aske counting and Pru's light fingers on her wrist and the quay lamps in a row along the black water outside the window, and the fever climbed through her like a tide coming in over flats, and she waited for the nothing and the roads and the lights, and for whatever was paying attention to pay it to her.

It did not come.

There was the loft's ceiling, dark, with its beams. There was the candle. There was the count, under the floor and the chandlery and the quay, four and four, going on. There was Aske's voice, saying a number every few minutes, and Pru's breathing, and somewhere at the far end of the loft somebody turning over who was not asleep.

At a quarter to seven, by Aske's watch, with the fever at a hundred and four and her teeth together, she took her left hand out from under the blanket and laid the first two fingers on the iron rail of the bed. She did it on purpose. It was the hour.

*It keeps time.*

"Write it," she said. "Please. I can't."

Pru took the book off the sill and the pencil on its string. She wrote it, with her capped fingers, very badly, worse than Kiva's left hand. *Sunday the 20th. A quarter to seven. The bed rail, the loft. Yes. Written by Finch.*

The fever broke a little after eleven, in a long sweat that soaked the blanket through, as it had on the barge and on the night after the Counting House. Aske said, "There," and wrote it, and Pru sat back on her heels with her hands held up in front of her face.

"Nothing," said Kiva.

She said it aloud, as she had said it a week ago. Her voice was very hoarse.

Aske looked at her over his spectacles in the candlelight.

"Nothing came," he said. It was not a question.

"Nothing came." She shut her eyes. "That's all. I'd have told you if it had. I'd tell anybody now."

He did not say anything to that for a while. Then he wrote one more line on the card, small, and this time he turned it round and showed her, because she had asked nobody to keep anything from her any more, and he seemed to know it.

*Fever, the third. Nothing came. Said so.*

---

At ten o'clock, while the fever was at its height, the Mercury's boy had come up Quay Street with his bag, and Jude had gone down and bought the late sheet, and stood at the window with it under the candle for a long time, reading, with his good arm on the frame.

He did not read it to her. He folded it and laid it on the sill. She saw it there when she opened her eyes at midnight, with the fever gone and the blanket soaked and Aske asleep at last on his bucket with his chin on his chest.

The headline went across the top of the folded sheet in letters as big as her thumb, and the candle lay on it.

*THE UNROOFED SKY.*

And under it, smaller: *The Board Names What Nobody Can Say. Two Masters and a Catcher. The Order Gives Its Cellar to the Bank. The Board Warrants Wednesday.* And under that, in a column with a line round it: *"Some Go Out. Some Go White." "I'll Not Have It Said I Saw More."*

She looked at it for a long time.

It was a name. It was not hers. It had been a Glory master's phrase in a notebook nobody had been let read, and a clerk had borrowed it in a dock for want of a better, and now it was in letters as big as her thumb on a penny sheet that a boy with ink on his thumbs had sold up and down Quay Street. By the morning it would be in every town on the coast. By the end of the week it would be in Meridian, on Coper Street, in the shop.

Everybody in this city knew that she dreamed. Everybody would know by Wednesday. Nobody knew what it was. The Board had said so, and so had the Proprietor, and so had the Master, and so had she.

She found that she did not mind it. She had thought that she would, for four years. She had kept her mouth shut for four years so that she would never have to lie in a bed and look at a sheet like this. And now she was lying in a bed looking at a sheet like this, and it was only a name. It did not say what the thing was. It did not say what she was. It said that a thing had been put on a table where anybody could see it, and that nobody owned it now, not her, not the Proprietor, not the Order, not the Board. It belonged to the lamp-man at Holloway as much as to anybody.

She put her hand inside her shirt and found the disk.

It was cold. It had been cold at the far end of the Proprietor's table that morning, and cold on the bench in the dock while the Master stood with her back to the sea, and cold through the fever. She had looked each time. She would write it in the morning, on the left, with her left hand. *Cold. Two masters. As before.* There was no crossing, so the fourth column was owed nothing. She would write it anyway.

At the far end of the loft somebody was still not asleep. She could see the shape of him at the south window, sitting up. Kellan. He had a card in his hand, a white one with a Halden mark, and he was looking at it in the light from the quay. She did not know what was on it. She would ask him in the morning. She thought he would tell her, and that it would come out as a sum.

Beside her bed, on the next one, three feet off, Selka Dray was lying on her back with her eyes open, looking at the ceiling. She had been there all evening. She had not said anything. Now, as Kiva turned her head, Dray turned hers.

"Wednesday," said Dray, very low. "If they ask the roll."

"Yes."

"I'll be there." Dray looked back at the ceiling. "On my feet. I'd sooner you heard it from me."

She shut her eyes.

Kiva lay with her hand on the cold disk and her eyes open.

Outside the window, under the white lamps on the Long Quay and the yellow ones in the low town, Saltmere went on keeping its count. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster. At the end of the Mole, in a swept hut on the sea side where nothing kept time, a woman in grey who had broken her house's only rule was lying awake with her hands folded, and two sisters were writing it in a book. On a cart at the seaward end of the Graving, under a grey cloth, thirty-six jars stood in their straw, given. On the second floor of a pump house a keeper had written a line in her great-grandfather's book and gone to bed for the first time since Tuesday. In a tall grey house on the Long Quay, every window lit, a man of a hundred and three sat with his left hand flat on a glass case and his right over his eyes, and nobody saw him.

In the Board's press, in a black iron box with three locks, there was a paper with a seal on it and the hour, which would be opened at noon on Wednesday. At dawn on Wednesday, if two people were right, the basin would go still at nine minutes past six. There were three days.

And after that there was Rhea Sorn's last night on the van, and the last of the third month, and a card that had not been written yet, and either of them might say no.

The Measure was read. Seven was seventh. The unroofed sky had a name and no meaning.

She slept at last with her hand on the disk, and dreamed of nothing at all.
