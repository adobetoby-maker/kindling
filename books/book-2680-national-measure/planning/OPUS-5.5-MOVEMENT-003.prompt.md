## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,
  consistent with an action/interiority-forward book rather than a dialogue-driven one.
- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —

- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult

- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a
  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

## Where we enter

It is the end of the second month of 2681. Kiva Rowan, seventeen, seated and unclassified,
is on a coast boat with Team Seven, Healer Aske, and a fever just broken; Perrin has counted
the days off the shed door ("Sixteen"). **The gate fortnight at Saltmere begins Tuesday the
1st of the third month; the National Measure's first board is Monday the 14th.** Saltmere
itself has not been seen on the page. Team Seven is Kiva (catcher), Perrin Cade (captain,
carry, still door), Anwen Pryce (hold), Jude Talley (kill, arm in a sling and shin still
mending), with Pru Finch reserve and second catcher; Kellan Renn stands for Halden License
and keeps Seven's Meridian book. The team has a Flask license in its own right, a method
(the handed crossing) rehearsed on a yard and never live, a book holding **6.23 drams**
(about 5.63 uncommitted), and a sealed medical tin of **2.0 plain ward-trace** bound by
the Order of the Even Hand: *For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.*

Kiva has four of Orla's six hand-to-Ember crossings (Sundays the 1st, 8th, 15th, 22nd of
the second month, each on the Circuit's mast, read by Noa), and the hand sulks longer each
time (last: cold to mid-forearm, warm in two nights). The Chair's ledger enters the fifth and
sixth for the Saltmere gate mast, read by Noa with Orla present, "one in seven days and not
faster; not certified until the sixth." Aske's card holds: single doors, declared; one
crossing in seven days at the most; the hand seen next morning; no three-door. Kiva has not
told anyone that she dreams; the dream returned on the ferry and she kept her mouth shut. The
disk is cold. Her left fingers say that a thing keeps time and never what time.

Orla Dane and Mara Rowan have each decided, in scenes Kiva did not witness, to go south:
Orla by the Circuit's transit to find Jessamy Hart and "not use her to say it"; Mara by the
Works' transit in the first week, with Examiner Rusk, having written Officer Yeo "I will not
promise to read your bank." Kiva has been told neither. Rhea Sorn's Graywater van is coming
south; her card for Kiva has **two nights left, her card ends on the 30th,** and Pike's hands
are due out of their dressings about the 4th. Dinah Coombe will be on the Longshore.

## Pressure and what this movement is for

Saltmere is proud of its harbour bank. Thirty-six cells have been in step since twenty past
two on the 17th of the ninth month and have not dropped a stroke in six months. The Works'
people from Meridian arrive as guests telling their hosts that their best machine is a
hazard; Mara's twelve sheets already failed there, because a harbour is not a storm drain.
That is a fair position held against a fair position. Many sensible people like the stepped
bank.

The movement widens the ladder from Meridian's regional circuit to a national one and lets
Kiva meet, for the first time, **schools that solved the same problem a different way.** The
Ropewalk School teaches two doors **laid**, open together and agreeing, which Meridian calls
a stack and forbids; it is not foolish, it was built against people and the spawn of forty
years ago, it wins lanes, and its circuit has a column for it. Selka Dray's sealed mast rule
(any candidate with more than one door read from a sanctioned mast by a reader of the
Meridian office) now makes the host's best candidates an incident, and Meridian is disliked
for it. Let the lay beat Seven fairly in the seeding rounds, and let Seven learn from losing.

Underneath, a second problem is quietly turning: **Jessamy Hart** (Orla's candidate of 2659,
hurt at the Bench in 2660, now the fitter with the watch at the harbour pump house) turns out
to be the man who can tell the Works when the harbour is still. The harbour keeper's book
(the pump house's keeper is **Peverell**, who does not want to be the keeper who stopped the
pumps) has an entry for the night of the 11th of the third month, 2604, the same night as the
Ferren keeper's entry that Kiva and Mara have read. **Something stands in the basin at each
spring tide, and stands a little taller each time.** Hart and Mara seal a prediction about
it, to fall due on the morning of Wednesday the 23rd.

Orla and Hart meet once, badly. Kiva meets Hart, and learns from him what a ruined hand can
still be asked.

Midway the movement leaves the lanes. **Graywater's van is called south on a coast night
under a Charter Board warrant** — a professional call on tidal ground, where the forms work
the flood and the ebb and no sheet lies still. Kiva rides as Pike's second and speaks only
the far edge, two words, once; Rhea goes short on something (see the card: *if T7 is called
on a night of mine, she is T7's and I go short*). Show what a coast call teaches that the
Pit and the yard could not, and let it bear on the harbour.

**Ambrose Halden, the Proprietor** (Glory, alive in 2604, holder of the Halden foundation's
pattern) asks to see the plate and is answered by a watch's vote. He then asks Kiva for the
three-door, on a Halden mast, for a price. **She says no.** He is not a villain. He has
believed for seventy-seven years that stepped lamps are the ones that go white and not out,
and he has a defensible reading of what the dream means. Let him be the most interesting
adult Kiva has yet argued with, and let the argument cost him nothing he has not already
paid. A Glory master's presence is a place he cannot leave, a time he cannot exceed, or a
thing he must hold (bible §9).

**Exit.** Seven is seeded in the middle of twenty-four. The prediction is under seal. Orla and
Hart have spoken once, badly. The Measure's first board is on the doorstep, and what stands in
the basin has been seen by more than one pair of eyes.

## Craft lessons from Movement Two (measured, simulated, same-family)

- The prose came out bimodal: short beats plus a few 60–90 word "and…and" chains, with the
  middle band thin (median 6 against the formula's 11; 44.6% at five words or fewer against
  27.7%; narration long-tail 6.2% against 3.3% after repair). Write the middle: clear
  8–25 word narration sentences carrying one thought, a few longer compound-complex sentences
  where hierarchy stays easy to follow aloud, short beats for contrast. Do not chain
  semicolons into 60-word breaths.
- Ration mannerisms. "I'd like it known" is Perrin's alone; Coombe's knots belong to her
  entrances; "slowly," "scratched his jaw," "pale eyes," "something moved at the corner of

# Chapter 16 — Kiva: The Even Hand

The fourth crossing went on Sunday the 22nd, at seven, in the Old Yard, with a thaw coming up the hill and the frost going to water under the posts.

*Hand, to Ember. Rowan, changing on three.* Orla's stick on the ward, once. The fold. The shut. Nobody, in the hands, and the cold coming into them as it came now, at once and a long way up. The Ember. Six paces, onto a white cross that was half washed out.

"One cliff," said Noa. "Twenty-six. Thirty in all."

"*Four,*" said Orla.

The hand was white to the middle of the forearm. Kiva did not hold it up. She put it inside her coat against her ribs, where the disk was, cold, and stood on the cross while the thaw dripped off the shed roof. She knew, before Aske had ever seen it, that it would not be warm in the morning.

It was not warm in the morning. It was not warm at noon, or at four, or at nine on Monday night, when she went up the Wren House stair with her aunt behind her and Aske held the wrist for two minutes by his watch and wrote, and did not say anything until he had written.

"*Cold to the middle of the forearm at seven on Monday. To the wrist at nine at night.*" He capped his pen. "I said I'd say so before and not after. I'm saying it. The card's held. There's no fifth until I've seen this hand warm on a morning, and there's no mast on a road in any case."

"I know there isn't."

"I know you know. I'm writing it so that your sponsor can't say that I didn't." He looked at her over his spectacles. "Noon. Four o'clock. A night. Two nights, now, by the look of it. I don't know what it's sulking about. I don't know whether it'll sulk three nights at Saltmere after the fifth, or a week after the sixth." He was quiet for a moment. "I'll say a thing that isn't on any card. I'm coming south with you. By the road, since that's how you're going."

"The Circuit's paying for you to go on the transit with the officers."

"The Circuit's paying for me to go to Saltmere. It didn't say how." Aske put the pen away. "I'll not have that hand looked at by a ferryman."

On Tuesday at seven it was cold to the knuckle. On Wednesday at seven, in the dark, by a lamp in the Wren House yard with the carrier's cart standing at the gate and the horse blowing, Aske held it for a minute by his watch and said, "Warm."

He wrote it on the card, standing against the wall. *Warm at seven on Wednesday. A sulk of two nights. Four of six, hand to Ember.* Then he got up onto the cart beside Pru, with his bag on his knees, as though he had been doing it all his life.

Jude had read the month out on Monday night, standing by the dresser at the Rowan kitchen table, because Perrin had asked him to and because, he said, a book that went south ought to go south with everybody having heard what was in it.

"*Carried, the first of the second month: held, nine and sixty-four. Sealed, medical, W, plain: eight-tenths.*"

Then he read every line that the month had put under it, the crossings and the floors and the lane and the moor, each of which they had heard him read once already on a shed step or a hearth. It took a quarter of an hour, and nobody interrupted.

"*A purse sheet and no pole, to replace Finch's, frosted at Garside's mill: 2.40, today, at the chandler's on the Hythe.*" He looked up. "*Held, six and twenty-three.* Spent this month, ten and eighty-one; credited, seven and forty."

"That adds," said Anwen.

"It adds. I've done it four times." Jude did not close the book. "*Spoken for: two crossings at the Saltmere gate, if the hand lets them; six-tenths, near enough. Uncommitted, five and sixty-three.* That's every door that four people light on four boards in front of a Board. It's not a great deal. I'd want everybody to know that it's not a great deal."

"And sealed?" said Perrin.

"Eight-tenths. Not opened. Not made up." Jude scratched his jaw. "There's one more line. It's for tomorrow. I've left room."

The line for tomorrow came at eleven on Tuesday morning, across the ash office counter, in a stone jar the size of a fist with a brown card round its neck.

A woman in widow's black had brought it up from the Brickfields on the tram. Her name was Mrs. Ottery. Her husband had been a carter to the wardens of the Levee end for thirty years and had died in the twelfth month. She had found the jar on the top shelf of his tool cupboard with his winter boots, and somebody at the chapel had told her that there were students at the Circuit who would give anything for plain.

*Handful, plain,* said the card round its neck. *Tinned the 3rd of the eleventh month, 2669. Winter stock.*

"Half a dram," said Mrs. Ottery. "He always said it was half a dram. I'd take two and a half for it. That's what the chapel said it was worth now. I'd not ask more than it's worth."

Anwen had the team's tin and the book. Pru had the comb. Kiva stood at the end of the counter with her left hand in her coat, and she looked at the jar, and at the woman's hands on it, which were red and cracked and held it as one holds a thing that belonged to somebody.

"May we comb it?" said Pru.

"I'd want you to."

Pru held the comb over the jar's open mouth. The third reed sang, thin and faint.

The seventh did not.

There was a moment in the ash office when nobody breathed. Kiva felt it go along the counter like a draught: the clerk, and Anwen, and the woman in black, and two juniors who had come in for a thimble and stopped in the door. Then Kiva heard what Pru had already heard. The third reed was sounding yet, and it was thin.

Pru tipped the jar, very slightly, toward the window, and looked into it.

She did not say anything. She held it out to Kiva, without a word, and Kiva looked.

The ash in the jar was grey, as ward-trace is grey. But it was not all grey. There was black in it, fine and dull, in streaks, like pepper stirred into flour and not stirred enough; and where the black was, it lay heavier, and did not shine at all.

"Soot," said Pru, quite quietly.

"It's plain," said Mrs. Ottery. "It didn't sing. You heard it."

"It's plain, Mrs. Ottery. The seventh's right; it's old enough never to have heard a beat. It's the third that's wrong. It sang thin, because there's half as much in it to sing as there ought to be." Pru said it gently, for her, and went scarlet. "Soot doesn't sing. Soot doesn't do anything. It's been stretched. Somebody's put soot in it to make it weigh half a dram. There's a quarter of a dram here, perhaps less. I'd not put it on a hand. It might do nothing. It might do worse."

The woman stood at the counter with her red hands on the jar.

"He'd not have done that," she said.

Nobody answered her; the clerk looked at the ledger. Kiva thought about a can of civic stock in the Works' store, in the autumn, that somebody had found cut with soot and put down and nobody had taken up, and about a carter who kept his winter boots on the top shelf of a cupboard, and about thirty years of hard winters on the Levee end. She did not say any of it, because it was not hers to say to a woman in black at a counter.

"We'll not buy it," said Anwen. "I'm sorry. I'd want it said that we're sorry."

"I'll not sell it, then." Mrs. Ottery put the lid back on with great care, and put the jar in her bag. "I'll not sell it to anybody. I'll put it back on his shelf." She went to the door. At the door she turned round. "He was a good carter," she said, and went out.

Jude wrote it in the book that night, on the line that he had left room for.

*Tuesday the 24th. The ash office. Offered: half a dram of "plain" ward-trace, winter stock of 2669, for 2.5. Combed by Finch: silent on the seventh, thin on the third; cut with soot to about half. Refused. Nothing spent.*

He looked at it for some time after he had written it.

"I'd want it said that I've never been so sorry not to spend anything," he said.

She showed it to Perrin, because it was under his name as much as hers, and he read it twice and said that he would like it known that he had never written to an Order in his life and did not mean to begin, and signed under her.

"I had six questions from that house in the summer. I said that I'd answer two of them. I said it to you, in this room, and I've not done it." Her aunt did not look up. "I'll not have you go through that door on a latch that I left open and never closed. I'll write tonight. It'll go with yours by the Line runner in the morning."

"Four of them are yours," said Mara. "If you ever want them. The other two are mine. I'll not tell you what I say." Her pencil tapped once on the oilcloth. "I've found that I'm a woman with a sealed page in a press at the Library. I'd not have you think that I'd lost the habit."

Mara Rowan wrote her letter at half past eleven on Monday night, at the kitchen table, alone, with the lamp turned down, her reading hand flat on the oilcloth, and her niece asleep in the next room.

It was the second letter that she had written that night. The first had gone into an envelope already, sealed and addressed, and lay on the dresser beside the brass weight with the Works' stamp on its corner. It was to Officer Yeo of the Saltmere Company, and it was four lines long, and it had taken her an hour.

*You asked twice for the Works' reader for the spring. I have read your fitter's six minutes against your harbour's table every night for six weeks and cannot make them agree, and I cannot do it from here. I will come in the first week of the third month, by the Works' transit, with Examiner Rusk. I will not promise to read your bank. M. Rowan, Grade Two.*

She had not told Kiva. She would tell her on the cart, perhaps, on Wednesday morning, or she would write it to Saltmere, where it would arrive after the girl had already had a week of standing in a strange country on one door. She had not decided. She had found, since the autumn, that she was a woman who decided a great many things in the middle of the night at a kitchen table and then did not tell anybody until it was too late for them to argue, and she did not altogether like it in herself, and had not stopped.

She had been afraid of it for six weeks. She was still afraid of it. She had one hand that read, and thirty-six cells on a harbour that did not lie still, and every night she had laid that hand flat on the oilcloth and felt the grain of the wood through it and thought, *that's what I'll lose.* She had sat across this table from a girl who had lost the feeling in two fingers in the Pit in the autumn for a method that nobody else could do, and who had spent the whole of this month making it into a method that three people could, and Mara had watched her do it.

She had not been brave about it. She was going anyway. She thought that perhaps that was all that brave had ever meant.

The second letter was harder, and shorter.

She took the Order's letter out of the dresser drawer, where it had lain since the summer under the license. It was on thick grey paper, and it had six questions on it in a small upright hand, numbered, and a line at the bottom that she had read perhaps forty times: *We ask nothing of the girl. These are for whoever will answer them. The door is on the latch. C.T., for the house.*

She wrote the numbers of two of the questions at the top of a clean sheet. She wrote an answer under each.

They were not long. The first took three lines and the second took one. She did not read them over when she had done, because she knew what they said, and because she had found that if she read a true thing over she began to make it tidier, and she did not want to make these tidy.

Under them she wrote: *The other four are my niece's to answer, if she ever chooses, and not mine. She is coming to your door on Thursday for a hand and not for a year. I would ask you to take her at her word. She means it. M. Rowan.*

She sealed it. She put it beside Kiva's card on the dresser, and beside Yeo's letter, and stood for a moment looking at the three of them under the brass weight in the lamplight: a card in a square hand, and two envelopes in her own.

Then she turned the lamp out and went to bed, and did not sleep for some time.

The road went round the Sag.

It went round it for the whole of Wednesday and the first half of Thursday, on the carrier's cart, at the pace of a tired horse. The great shallow bowl of the Sag was always on their left hand, brown and grey and enormous, and the black line of Cowl Edge went away behind them on its far side until it was a thread and then nothing. It was raining by Wednesday noon, a thin cold rain off the hills, and the five of them and Aske sat under a tarpaulin on the cart's bed among their sheets and poles and the team's tin and the warrant's papers, and nobody said very much.

Perrin had the coil of tarred rope over his shoulder. He had not taken it off since the moor.

"Nineteen," he said, at Wednesday's dusk, at the inn where the carrier stopped. He had no shed door to chalk it on. He said it to the window. "Nineteen days. It's the twenty-fifth."

"Eighteen," said Jude. "Tomorrow's eighteen."

"I know what tomorrow is. I was saying what today is."

They came down off the Sag's south rim on Thursday a little after two, and the rain stopped. The country opened out below them into a long green valley with a river in the bottom of it, broad and brown and slow, going away south toward a haze that Kiva did not know the name of. On the far side of the river the road went on, south; on the near side it came down to a long stone slip with a flat ferry-boat tied to it and a bell on a post.

Above the slip, on a bluff over the river, there was a house.

It was long and low and built of grey stone, with a slate roof and a great many chimneys, and a wall round it, and inside the wall the tops of bare trees. It stood on its bluff with its back to the Sag and its face to the river and the south, quite plain, like a farm. There was smoke going up from three of its chimneys. There were no lights.

"That's it," said the carrier. "The ladies' house. I'll put you down at their gate, and I'll be at the slip at eight in the morning for the first ferry. I'll not come in. I never do. I've nothing against them," he added, "only it's very quiet."

A woman opened the gate before they had knocked.

She was perhaps forty, in a plain grey dress and a grey wool cap, with her hands folded at her waist; and Kiva knew her. She knew her at once, from across a reading room in the Library in the summer, a table and a green notebook and a box that had been disputed for seventy years. The sister had sat at the end of that table, with a pencil, and had written that the reader's lips moved, counting, from the third line to the last.

The sister knew her too. She looked at Kiva for a moment with a perfectly calm face, and inclined her head an inch.

"I sat by you in the summer," she said.

"I know."

"Come in. All of you. Your healer as well." She stood aside. "The Master has your card, and Mrs. Rowan's letter. She'll see you after supper. We eat at dusk."

"What time's dusk?" said Jude.

"When it's dark," said the sister.

She took them across a courtyard paved with flags and into the house by a low door, and along a stone passage with doors on either side, all shut, and into a long room with a fire at one end and a table down the middle and candles in iron holders on the walls, not yet lit. It was very clean and very plain. There was nothing on the walls at all.

Kiva stopped in the doorway.

She did not know at first what she had stopped for. Then she did. She was listening for a thing, as one listens for a clock in a strange house, and there was no clock. There was no tick anywhere. There was the fire, and the rain dripping off the eaves outside, and nothing else. Under the fire and the rain there was not the faint low hum that came up through the floor of every house in Meridian from the box in the cellar, and through every pavement from the drum under the street.

She put her left hand flat on the door's stone jamb, light, with the first two fingers on it.

*Nothing.*

She held it there. Pru came up beside her, without being asked, and took her right wrist, and looked at the window, because there was no clock to look at, and counted under her breath.

"Sixty-eight," said Pru. "You're quite calm."

"Then it's nothing," said Kiva. "There's nothing in this house keeping time."

She had had her fingers say *nothing* once before in her life, six days ago, on a stone on a moor, by a kiln, and the thing in the kiln had sung on the seventh reed. She took her hand off the jamb, and put it in her coat, and found the disk there. It was cold, as she had known that it would be.

They ate at dusk, at the long table, with eleven sisters in grey who did not speak during the meal and did not seem to mind that their guests did. After supper the sister from the gate came and stood at Kiva's elbow and said, "The Master."

Kiva went. She did not ask whether the others might come. Perrin got up as she did, without asking either, because it was under his name. The sister looked at him and nodded, and the two of them went after her along the stone passage to a door at its end.

The room behind it was small, with a fire and two chairs and a window over the river, black now, with one light on the far bank where the ferryman lived. There was a woman standing at the window with her back to them.

Kiva felt her before she saw her face.

She had been near a Blaze for a month. She had stood on a cinder floor while a Hunt Marshal drew back a ward over a cage with one hand, as one draws a curtain, and she had felt it from the rail like a stove from a doorway. This was not like that. There was nothing to feel from a doorway. The air in the small room was quite still and quite ordinary, and the fire burned in the grate, and the woman at the window was only a tall thin woman in grey with her hands folded at her waist.

Kiva's doors lay down.

All three of them. She felt it happen, and could not have stopped it, and did not want to. The Ember under her breastbone, which was always awake, which had come gladly to every call since she was thirteen, went still. The hand door, close, under the skin of her palms, went still. The still door, heavy and slow under everything, which she had thought could not go any stiller, went still in a way that she had no word for, as a pond goes still when the wind drops. They did not shut. They had not been asked to shut. They lay down where they were, as dogs lie down in a room when somebody comes into it whom they do not know and do not need to watch.

She had stood in a frame in the Measure Hall in the spring and felt it go through her like a hand through a drawer, calling every door it found. This called nothing. It did not look. It was simply there, enormous, very close, in a small room with a fire; and it was the quietest thing that she had ever been near in her life, and the largest, and she could not have said why she knew that it was both.

The woman turned round.

She might have been fifty. Her hair was quite white and cut short under a plain cap, and her face was long and pale and very calm, with deep lines at the eyes. Her eyes were grey, and they came to rest on Kiva and stayed there, without searching, as a hand rests on a table.

"Miss Rowan," said Clemency Thrale. "Mr. Cade. Sit down. I'll stand, if you'll let me. I've been sitting with your aunt's letter for two days and I find I've no more use for chairs."

Kiva sat. Her knees had decided, as Anwen's had at a kitchen table.

"You came for a hand," said the Master.

"Yes."

"You wrote that it was not for a year. You wrote it in the third line, and you said that you'd want it said first, and you didn't say it first; you put the hand first." The grey eyes did not move. "I'd like to know why."

Kiva had not expected that. She thought about it, honestly, because she had been asked, in a room where she could not have done anything else.

"Because the hand's why we're here," she said. "The year's why we might not be. I'd sooner you knew what we wanted before you knew what we didn't."

"That's a fair order." Thrale's hands stayed folded at her waist. "I'll say what we are, once, and then I'll say what I'll do, and then you may say no to either. That's the rule of this house. It's the only rule we have that isn't about doors." She looked at the window, at the one light on the far bank. "We keep one door each, for life, and every other shut. We keep no clock and no cell and no pump. We've stepped nothing; we never have. There's plain ash in our cellar that was tinned before the Halden patent was sealed, and some that was tinned before there was a Halden, and it's never been near a thing that kept time. There are forty-one jars of it. There have been forty-one jars since I was made Master, and before that there were forty-four." She paused. "I can hold a great deal, Miss Rowan, and for a very long time. I can't make a jar more than it is. Nobody can. When you go down to the ferry in the morning, there'll be forty."

Kiva did not say anything.

"We don't sell," said the Master. "We've never sold. We've been offered a great deal this winter, by people I'd not have expected to write to us, and we've sold nothing, and we won't. But we've always made good a hand." She looked back at Kiva. "Your aunt's letter says that in the tenth month your team's tin of plain eased her reading hand. One dram and two-tenths, out of two, for a hand that was starved between the cells of a bank on a hill, so that she might read again."

"Yes."

"Then your tin eased a hand. We'll put back what it gave. One dram and two-tenths of plain ward-trace, from our cellar into your tin, so that your tin's what it was before it did a kindness." Thrale's voice did not change. "Not for your team. Not for a floor or a crossing or a bag or a gate. Not for a bank. Not to be sold, whatever you're offered for it; and you will be offered a great deal. For a hand. Whoever's hand is hurt next, in your four or out of it, if it's a hand that plain will ease. It'll be written so in our book and in yours, in your hand and your captain's, and if it's ever spent on anything else, I'd want to be told."

Perrin had gone very red.

"That's binding," he said. "Ma'am. On us. On the tin."

"It's binding. That's why I'm saying it to you, and not only to her. It's under your name, I'm told." The grey eyes went to him for the first time. "You may say no. I'd think none the worse. A tin that's bound is a tin you can't use when you'd most like to, and I'd not pretend otherwise."

Perrin looked at Kiva. Kiva looked back, and did not help him, as she had not helped him on a frozen yard; and she saw him see that she was not going to, and saw him decide.

"Yes," he said. "Under my name. For a hand."

"Miss Rowan?"

"Yes," said Kiva. "For a hand."

"Then it's done." Thrale did not move. "The sister will bring it to you in the morning, in your own tin, under our seal and yours, and you'll both sign. Tonight you'll sleep under this roof, and you'll keep our rule while you do, which is no door lit and no clock wound, and you'll find that it's very easy, since there's nothing here that wants either." She paused. "That's all that's asked. A night. Not a year."

Kiva sat in the chair with her doors lying down inside her like sleeping dogs, and found that she could not think of a single thing to say.

"Your aunt answered two of my six questions," said the Master. "She answered them shortly, and I think truly. She wrote that the other four were yours, if you ever chose." The grey eyes rested on her, quite still. "I'll not ask them. Not tonight, and not by letter, and not if you come back. If you ever want them, the latch is on your side of the door. It always was."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I've only said what's so." For the first time her folded hands moved: one thumb crossed the other, and went back. "I'll say one thing more, since you've a long road and I've a short memory for what I've decided not to say. Your sister at the gate wrote, in the summer, that you counted when you read. I've thought about it since. I've not decided what I think. I'd not have you go south believing that I'd decided."

"Nor have I," said Kiva.

"No," said Clemency Thrale. "I didn't suppose that you had."

"I've not been past that gate in eleven years," she said, to the glass. "The Master keeps the house. When she goes out, the house has no Master, and it's never been without one long. I've gone out twice in my life." She was quiet a moment. "I'd like to see the sea again before I'm very much older. I don't expect to. Good night, Miss Rowan. Mr. Cade."

Kiva slept that night in a narrow white room off the stone passage, under a grey blanket, with Pru in the other bed and the rain on the window. She slept as she had not slept since she was small, without waking once, without dreaming of anything at all.

The third reed sang.

The seventh did not.

It did not sing at all. It was not that it sang faint. It was still, a thin bar of metal in a row of thin bars of metal, with the third ringing full beside it like a spoon on a glass. Kiva stood on the wet flags with her hands at her sides and listened to it not sing. It was a flat broad boat with a rail round it, worked by a cable and a windlass, and it took the carrier's cart and his horse and the six of them and their sheets and poles and the team's box, with room over. The ferryman wound the windlass, and the bank with the slip and the grey house on its bluff went away behind them into the morning, and the brown water went by under the boat's flat bottom with a sound like somebody shushing a child.

Kiva was hot by the middle of the river.

She did not notice it at first. She was standing at the rail with her hands on it, looking south, down the long valley toward the haze where the river went, and the coast. She thought that it was the sun, which had come out over the Sag behind them and was warm on her back for the first time in weeks. Then she found that her hands on the rail were hot, both of them, the eight fingers that reported and the two that did not, and that the wind off the water, which was cold, was not cooling them.

She took them off the rail. She put the left one inside her coat against her ribs, where the disk was, and the disk was cold, and her ribs were not.

"Rowan," said Pru, beside her. "You've gone red."

"It's the sun."

"It's not the sun. You've gone red from the collar." Pru took her wrist without asking. She looked at the far bank, since there was no clock, and counted under her breath; and Kiva saw her face change.

"A hundred and twelve," said Pru. "You're not frightened. You're not *doing* anything." She did not let go. "Aske."

Aske came along the rail. He took the other wrist and held it for a minute by his watch, with his face quite still, and then he put the back of his hand against her cheek, as her aunt used to when she was small.

"Fever," he said. "Sit down. Here, on the box. Talley. Your coat."

She sat down on the team's box, against the rail, in Jude's coat. The far bank came nearer, very slowly, on the cable. She heard Perrin ask whether it was the hand, and Aske say that he did not think it was the hand; and Anwen say something that she could not hear; and the windlass creak, round and round, slow, and the water shush under the flat bottom of the boat.

Then the roof went.

---

It was not a roof. It was not the sky over a river valley in the second month, grey and white with the sun coming through it. It was nothing.

She had known it would be. She knew it as she knew the order of her own doors. It came as it had come when she was thirteen and on Classification Day, all at once and from every side, and there was nothing over her at all; and the nothing was not empty, and it was not dark. It was enormous. It was paying attention.

There were roads. There were lights on them, going along them, a great many, unlike, from every side, small and far off and moving; and she was above them, as she always was, looking down.

She was not so high as she had been.

She did not know how she knew it. There was nothing to measure it by. But the lights on the roads were nearer than they had been, by a little; she could see that some were bright and some were dull and some were going out, and that some had gone white; and she had not been near enough to see that, before, she thought. She did not know what it meant. She did not want to know what it meant. She was lower, and the nothing over her was paying attention, and she could not have said to what.

She kept her mouth shut.

She found that she was doing it. She had a hand on her wrist and a voice somewhere counting, and something in her that had learned in a van, at a hem, with a cloth under her hands and two words owed and nothing else, had shut her teeth. She did not say anything. She did not say what she saw. She held it behind her teeth, as she had held *Hobday's proud* behind her teeth on a road in the frost, because there was nobody on that boat whose word it was, and it was not her place to say it.

And then there was a bank, and a boat bumping against a slip, and a horse being led off up a ramp, and the sky was a sky again, grey and white, with the sun coming through it.

---

She was on the river by noon, on a long low barge going south with the current, under a canvas awning, on a straw mattress that Aske had bought off the bargeman for a shilling. The fever stayed all that day and all that night, and broke on Saturday at about four in the morning, in a long sweat that soaked the mattress through.

Aske counted it. He sat beside her on an upturned bucket with his watch in his hand and took her wrist every hour, and wrote what he found on her card in pencil, and did not ask her anything at all.

At about two on Saturday morning, when it was worst, he said, without looking up from his watch, "Have you had one of these before?"

"Yes," said Kiva.

He wrote it.

"I'll not ask what," he said. "I'll count."

Pru held her other wrist through most of the night, on her knees on the barge's boards, and did not ask her anything either. Once, when the fever was high and Kiva's teeth were shut so hard that her jaw ached, Pru said very quietly, "You can say it, if you want. Whatever it is. I'll not tell anybody." And Kiva shook her head, once, on the wet straw; and Pru said, "All right," and went on counting, and did not ask again.

By Saturday noon she could sit up. By Saturday evening she could eat. The barge went on down the river, slow, between green banks that she did not know, with willows on them and cattle standing in the shallows and here and there a village with a church. The river got wider, and browner, and slower, and the air began to smell of something that she had never smelled before and could not put a name to, flat and cold and clean, coming up from the south on the wind.

"Salt," said Jude.

He was sitting at the barge's bow with his strapped arm across his knees, looking south. He had been sitting there since noon.

"It's salt. The bargeman says. It comes up the river on a south wind, thirty miles." He did not turn round. "I'd want it said that I've never smelled it."

She wrote her book that evening, under the awning, by the bargeman's lantern, sitting up on the dried straw with Jude's coat round her shoulders.

It went to Orla on Sundays. It would go to Orla at Saltmere, at the gate, on a crate, on the first Sunday of the third month. It had lain on the kitchen table every night for Mara for a spring and a summer and an autumn and a winter, and it had never once had a page torn out of it, and it had never once had in it what she had seen when there was no roof.

She wrote on the left, where the things went that she had seen.

*Friday the 27th. The ferry below the house of the Even Hand. Fever, from the middle of the river. A hundred and twelve, by Finch. Aske counted it all night. Broke Saturday at four. I can sit up.*

*Cold, the disk. Before and after. I looked.*

On the right, where the reasons went, she wrote nothing for a long time.

Then she wrote one line, small.

*I kept my mouth shut. I'd learned how.*

She shut the book, and laid it on the straw beside her, under her hand. The barge went on down the river in the dark, with the lantern swinging at its bow and the salt coming up on the wind, and somewhere ahead of it, three days off, a gate that would open on Tuesday. In the team's box under the awning, in a tin with two seals on it, there were two drams of plain ward-trace that were for a hand and for nothing else.

Perrin was asleep against the box, with a coil of tarred rope over his shoulder.

Before he slept, he had looked at the bank going by in the dark for a long time, and said, to nobody, "Sixteen."

It was the twenty-eighth. Nobody had told him to count it. He had counted it anyway.

## 1. Book promise

Kiva Rowan ended her first collegiate season seated, unclassified, with eight fingers that
report and a company's card that promises no seat. Book Two takes her two hundred miles to the
coast. She spends a winter with her hands dark, kneeling at a professional's hem with her
mouth shut. She trains for Riftspawn that no longer die by the book. She stands the National
Measure at Saltmere among twenty-four fours and schools that solved her problem a different
way. And she finds that the thing Meridian did once, by hand, to one bank on one hill, has to
be done on water that never lies still.

The title carries three meanings, and the book pays all three: the competition; the nation
counting what it has (keepers' books, banks, fevers, plain ash); and **the beat itself**, the
count of four that more and more of the country's ash is keeping.

## 2. The argument

Book One asked why the post is crooked and answered: keep the reason with the method. Book
Two asks what happens when a method has to **travel.** Mara's eleven sheets reach the coast and
fail there, because a harbour is not a storm drain. Kiva's three-door sequence cannot travel
at all; it lives in one damaged hand. What can travel is the part she has been made to learn
all winter: say what the piece is, who has it, and when it changes hands.

Her earned capability in this book is not a fourth three-door. It is the **handed crossing**
(working term): catch, carry, and plant done by three people with three unlike doors, with
one caller who has been inside both gaps and can say when the load passes. It costs almost no
ash. It costs trust, rehearsal, and a count that is not hers.

## 3. Complete ending

**The Measure is read in full.** No board is struck. The Ropewalk School's first four wins the
National Measure. Halden License is second. **Team Seven finishes seventh of twenty-four and
makes the national license roll, which takes eight, by one mark.** Seven wins one board
outright, the Carry, on Perrin Cade's back. Selka Dray beats Kiva in a lane, on a declared
count, with a needle on her, exactly as promised; Seven wins the lane on the other three.

Kiva loses that exchange by choice. One crossing in seven days is all her card allows, and by
then she knows what the week's crossing is for. She fights Dray on one door and says why
beforehand, to Dray's face.

**The dream becomes a public fact.** Before the Charter Board, Kiva has Mara's sealed page
opened: timed and sealed before she ever read Annick, it proves that her account was her own.
Beside it lie Annick's entry, the Ferren keeper's line of 2604, the Saltmere harbour keeper's
line for the same night, and the first returns of a national reading of remarks columns. Two
living Glory masters then say under their own names that they have dreamed it: Clemency
Thrale, Master of the Order of the Even Hand, and Ambrose Halden, the Proprietor, who was
alive on the night in 2604. A Board clerk borrows Annick's phrase, and the papers print **the
Unroofed Sky.** Nobody can say what it means. The two masters read it in opposite ways.

**The Slack.** At the dawn low-water slack of Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, a mixed
cohort from the Measure's roll and three professional crews take Saltmere's harbour bank of
thirty-six cells out of step. The water has to be still, and harbour water is still for about
twenty minutes. The thing standing in the basin has to come off the water first. It is the
first Barrel-class expression on the page, and it is keeping the harbour's time.

> Built by TAC

## 1. Rules that do not move (`ESTABLISHED`)

- One source, three traditions: Ember, rift-granted magic (the hand door: Ward, Sustain), and
  internal cultivation (the still door). The soul fills. Ash is ownerless, finite fuel.
- The rank ladder: Kindled, Ember, Flame, Fire, Torch, Blaze, Glory. Rank and breadth are one
  axis; skill and control are another. Hold, Draw, Call, yield, grade, resonance, license,
  and standing are separate measures, and none pays for another.
- **Draw** is ash spent on a piece of work over the book expectation for it: 1.0 is standard,
  lower is better.
- A kill does not raise rank. Ash funds the next attempt. Learning is what makes it survivable.
- Ash is recorded by **yield** (Handful, Flask, Barrel, Well), **grade**, and **resonance.**
  The comb's third reed sings for ward-trace (W). The seventh (I) is cut to the Step.
- Glory stops ordinary aging, is rare, and cannot be granted, bought, or stolen. It widens
  what one person can hold: throughput, duration, precision. It never makes ash free.
- Riftspawn expressions echo worked powers with nobody home to direct them. Spawn anatomy
  as trained: breath; posts or load paths; knots; a cord; a hem; the tear; closure.
- Students close no tear. Licensed crews do. Radiation work and licenses not held are not
  taken over in a crisis.
- Classification is physical: rigs, masts, pens, tapes, projected tables, human rulings. No
  private overlay.
- **Doors, for Kiva.** A competition is all doors open and fighting. A stack is two open
  together and agreeing: soft, cheap, and open to anything near; forbidden to her. A sequence
  shuts each door before the next opens, and each gap costs about a quarter of a dram with
  nothing open. A crossing is one gap. Three doors are two gaps.
- **The disk** does not teach, speak, map, choose, open a door, or answer. Two bearers have
  written *warm, once,* after a first crossing, and *cold* ever after.
- **The dream** is a fever, not a prophecy that can be consulted. It cannot be summoned. Each
  recurrence has a bodily cost. Triggers on record: fever, ash overload, filter instability,
  close contact with Glory.
- **The Step** (Halden's foundation patent, 2391): a row of cells salted late with one pinch
  of ash already made to keep a beat thereafter beats as one and can be driven by one
  operator. Used in Halden's yards, refineries, lift yards, and big tram sheds. Pump houses
  and tenement boxes were never meant to be stepped. The claim covers "all ash exhibiting the
  Step, however produced." The caution: "Not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under
  load." Before the patent the Wren House called it *counting ash.*
- Shown, not explained, in Book One: stepped pumps make water rise and fall on a count of four
  and four; a cell joined to a stepped bank by nothing but that water goes into step; a tear
  wants "the thing that keeps time" and not the bright thing; a string with an arm in the
  water is itself a pump; cut cords spill open ash that can cook a bank.

## 4. Spawn in a national season (`PLANNED`)

All new behavior obeys trained anatomy and combines or redirects it. None is a bigger Flask.
Working descriptions follow; the crews on the page give the names, as Book One's did.

- **Packs.** Husks that keep a count among themselves and move on it. Cutting one out of time

- **A folding hem.** A hem that folds from its far edge, as a catcher's ward does, and so
  comes off a purse sheet.
- **An entering form.** A cord that goes to an open stack as a string goes to water that

  sheet can be laid and ash goes into the water.

- **Yield** is higher on average and nearly all of it reads I above trace. Rich ash that

## 5. Ash in a national season (`PLANNED`)

- **Plain ash is disappearing.** Natural yield carried the Step in trace by the spring of 2680
  (`ESTABLISHED`: "it's all had the hair since the thaw"). By the winter no fresh kill reads
  plain, in Meridian or in the hills.
- **Plain ash is what un-stepping needs:** a jar a cell, of ash that "has never heard a beat."
  Old stock is finite: reference shelves, wardens' winter stores, sealed medical reserves, and
  the cellars of an Order that never stepped anything.
- So the price of ash turns over. The richest red bags are close to worthless for safe work,
  and eight-tenths of a dram of plain ward-trace is worth an argument.
- A healer can ease a **starved** hand with plain ward-trace and cannot ease one that is
  **turned out** (`ESTABLISHED`). Stepped ward-trace is no use for it.

## 6. The handed crossing (`PLANNED`; the working term may be replaced on the page)

- **What it is.** Catch, carry, and plant, each done by a different person through one door,
  with a caller. A hand door lifts and folds. An Ember door takes the folded load across. A
  still door plants it. Between people there is no quarter-dram gap, because nobody shuts and
  opens.
- **What it costs instead.** For an instant at each pass the load belongs to nobody. A pass
  called half a count wrong drops it or nips someone (`ESTABLISHED`: Pru's fingers). Each
  holder must take a count that is not theirs. It needs rehearsal with those exact people.
- **Why it was not already done.** Schools teach one door and declared roles; a pass between
  unlike doors belongs to nobody's curriculum. The Home Line had a word for it once. A
  single-door fighter cannot feel the far side of a gap. Somebody who has been through both
  can call it.
- **Limits.** It is slower to set than a three-door sequence. It fails if any one of three
  people is hurt, late, or unwilling. It cannot be done by strangers. It does not close a
  tear, kill anything, or mend a bank.
- **Kiva's part** is the call, the ground test, and the written columns. Under her certificate
  she may also stand as one link by making one declared crossing on a sanctioned mast.

## 7. What Book Two establishes about the Step, the filter, and the dream (`PLANNED`)

Each line is to be earned by evidence on the page and said by somebody who can be wrong.

1. **The Step is a mark.** It is what rising pressure from beyond the filter leaves in ash.

2. **Tears come to it.** A stepped bank is a worked pattern keeping time at scale, and spawn
   expressions echo worked patterns. That is why a hold stood, and why a string went to the
   drain.
3. **People and spawn are answering one pressure.** Noa's columns get a fourth, for ash, and
   it rises with the other three.
4. **It is quickening.** Old Halden bench books give the Step a slower count than today's
   four and four. (The figures are the author's, after reading the patent scene in Book One.)
5. **It is not uniform.** The notch that follows Kiva's cliff by eleven seconds at Meridian
   follows it sooner on the coast. Nobody can say what that distance is a distance to.
6. **The dream is shared.** Annick (Glory, 2431; her entry undated); the Ferren keeper and the
   Saltmere keeper on one night in 2604; Kiva in 2676, proved independent by a sealed and timed
   page; Ambrose Halden in 2604 and since; Clemency Thrale; and returns from other cities'

7. **A T-0 can be local and can be wide.** When two Glory fields from unlike traditions touch

8. **The readings disagree.** Halden: step the country, so that its lamps go white and not
