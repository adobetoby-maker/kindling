# Chapter 25 — Kiva: One Water

The Salt Hall had been built to keep salt dry, and it still smelled of it.

It stood on the Long Quay between the Company's office and the customs steps, a long stone warehouse with a slate roof and no windows on the harbour side. Inside, a gallery ran round three walls on iron columns, twenty feet up, where men had once stood to tip sacks down into the bins. The bins were gone. The floor below was flagstone, swept and chalked, and on it, in two rows of three, stood six banks of cells.

Kiva saw them from the gallery stair at twenty to nine on Monday morning, with Aske behind her and a crowd in green behind him.

Each bank was six cells in a row. Each cell was a tall glass case on an iron stand, with a red handle at its side and a can slot under the handle, and inside each case a slow light turned, yellow-white, like a lamp turned low. A wooden skirt painted the Board's grey ran round the foot of every bank, waist-high, so that from the floor nobody could see what the stands were standing in. Over the great door at the end of the hall hung the load board. It was a black frame as big as a barn door, with thirty-six dials in six rows, and every dial had a needle, and every needle was moving.

"Sit," said Aske.

"I'm going to."

"Sit at the front, then, where you can see. I'd sooner you sat at the front than stood at the back on your toes." He found two places on the bench at the gallery rail and sat down in one of them with his bag on his knees. "You're not at the Bench. You're watching a Bench. That's a different thing, and it's on your card, and nobody'll ask you to do anything but look."

Kiva sat. The rail was iron, cold, worn smooth on top by a century of elbows. She did not put her fingers on it.

The gallery filled behind and beside them. It was mostly Saltmere green, with the brown and buff of the Tarn in one corner and a knot of Meridian's colours near the stair. There were people in coats she did not know: a dark red, a sea blue, a grey with a white collar. Those were Holloway and Westerley, Jude had said at breakfast, and the third that nobody could pronounce. At the far end, by themselves, there were three men in black coats with notebooks open on their knees, writing already, though nothing had happened.

"The papers," said Orla.

She had come up the stair behind them on her stick, a step at a time, and she sat down on Kiva's other side without asking anybody to move. Somebody moved anyway.

"The *Mercury*," she said. "And two from inland. They'll want a name by tonight. They always want a name." She hooked her stick on the rail. "Don't give them yours."

"They don't want mine. I'm not at the Bench."

"That's why they'll want it," said Orla.

---

The Clerk of the Measure came out onto the floor at nine exactly. She was a small grey woman with a gold chain on her coat, and she stood in the middle of the six banks without a box or a step, so that she was the smallest thing in the hall. When she spoke, the whole hall heard her.

"The Bench," she said. "The first board of the National Measure. Six fours to a heat, four heats. The Board's rules, which are the Circuits' rules, with one change, which I'll read."

She did not look at a paper. She said it as a clerk says a thing she has said in a great many rooms.

"Each bank has a fault planted in one cell of the six. A four finds it and declares it. It quenches that cell and no other. It tunes it clean and brings the bank up. That is the board as it's always been." She paused. "This year's fault is this year's trouble. The planted cell in every bank was salted from a red bag. It keeps a count, and it does not keep its own. When the bank comes up again, it will be read on the Board's mast for ten minutes. Two cells keeping one count is a step. A step ends your hold. A four is marked by the time to find and by the hold, and by nothing else."

A sound went round the gallery. It was not loud. It was the sound of several hundred people deciding that they had heard right.

"She's put the harbour on the floor," said Orla, very low.

"A bank of six," said Aske.

"A bank of six with a cell in it that's been told what time it is." Orla did not take her eyes off the Clerk. "That's what they've got down the quay. Thirty-six of them, and nobody's ever found the one that was told first."

Kiva looked down at the nearest bank. Its six lights turned in their cases, slowly, each at its own pace. One of them was not turning at its own pace. She could not tell which. From up here they looked the same.

Behind the Clerk, in the hall's far corner, there was a mast. It was a short one, twelve feet of black iron with a single drum, and there were two reading desks at its foot. Noa stood at one with his pencil behind his ear. Polglase stood at the other in his steel spectacles. Neither of them looked up.

"Heat one," said the Clerk. "To your banks."

Halden License came out of the crowd at the door in black and gold.

Kiva had known that they would be in the first heat; the draw had been on the tent pole since Saturday. She had not known how it would feel to see Kellan walk out onto a floor in somebody else's coat. He walked behind Pettit, with Yarrow and Hesketh, to the second bank in the near row. He did not look up at the gallery. She had not expected him to.

The other five fours went to their banks. One was in the dark red of Holloway; one was a Westerley four in sea blue, which Jude had said was their second; the other three Kiva did not know. They stood behind their banks with their hands at their sides, as fours stand behind a hurdle.

"*Find,*" said the Clerk, and a man in grey turned a glass on the table beside her.

Hesketh was Halden's Hold, and she had the drum. Kiva watched her go down the bank with her palm flat on the glass of each case in turn. It was as Anwen did it, and as Anwen's mother had once done it down a line of shirts. Hesketh did not hurry. At the first case she kept her hand there a long breath. At the second she lifted it almost at once. At the third she stopped.

She kept her palm on the third case. She took it off and put it back. Then she said one word to Pettit, so low that the gallery could not hear it, and Pettit lifted his hand to the marshal.

"*Halden License,*" said the marshal. "Three. Declared."

"A minute and forty," said Orla.

Kiva had not looked at the glass. She had been watching Hesketh's hand.

"How did she know?"

"Wren House palm. She's had it since she was twelve, like Pryce." Orla rubbed her bad knee. "It's not hard to find a cell that's beating too hard. It's hard to be sure. She was sure."

Halden quenched. Pettit pulled the red handle on the third case, and the light in it went out like a lamp blown out. Kellan was at the can slot with a box, and something grey came out of the slot into the box, and he shut it. Then Hesketh was at the case with a fresh can in her hands. Yarrow was beside her with a card, holding it up where she could see it, and she looked at the card and filled the can and slid it home with her eyes on Yarrow's watch.

The third light came up again, yellow-white, turning.

"*Up,*" said Pettit.

The marshal turned a second glass. At the mast, Noa and Polglase bent over their desks.

Kiva watched the load board. There were six dials in Halden's row, and six needles. Five of them went on as they had been going, each at its own pace, a little uneven, the way the needles of any bank went. The sixth, the third dial, went at a pace of its own that was nothing like any of the others. It was faster, and lumpier. It jerked twice and paused and jerked three times. It was keeping the count off Yarrow's card, which was no count that Kiva had ever seen.

"He's given it a limp," said Orla.

"Is that allowed?"

"The rule says tune it clean. It doesn't say what to." Orla almost smiled. "That's Halden. They know more about the Step than anybody alive, and they know what it can't get hold of. It can't get hold of a limp."

The other five banks found their cells. The Holloway four found theirs in two minutes. The Westerley second four took four and a half, and one of the three that Kiva did not know took six, and the gallery made a long low noise at it, not unkindly. They all quenched. They all tuned. Four of them, Kiva saw, had a card held up like Yarrow's. She could not see what was written on any of them.

And Kiva, who could not stand at the Bench, sat at the rail and watched the load board.

It was a strange way to watch. On a floor she had always watched close things: a heel, a pole, a square of clean dirt, the edge of a sheet. Up here she could see the whole of the floor at once, the six banks laid out like a game on a table, and the load board over the door with its thirty-six needles all moving. She did not watch any one of them. She let her eyes go loose and watched the whole board, the way she had watched the Tarn's square come down a pen on Saturday.

That was how she saw it.

On the Westerley bank, at the fourth minute of the hold, two needles were going together. They had not been, at the start. They had been going as any two needles went, near each other and not the same, the way two people walk side by side and are never quite in step. Now they were in step. And a third was coming. She could see it come, as you see a man fall into step with the two in front of him without meaning to, a little faster for a pace, and then the same.

"Orla," she said.

"I see it."

At five minutes and twenty seconds the marshal by the Westerley bank lifted his hand.

"*Stepped,*" said the Clerk. "Westerley, second four. Five and twenty."

The Westerley four looked at their bank. One of them, a tall girl in sea blue, put her hand flat on the glass of the third case as if she could stop it with her palm. The marshal shook his head at her, quite kindly, and she took it away.

"They tuned it to the table," said Orla. "I'd put money on it. The count in the Board's book, the one everybody's tuned a cell to since they were twelve. That's what clean means, at home."

"It wasn't the one they tuned that stepped."

Orla turned her head.

"It was the fourth and the fifth," said Kiva. "And then the second. Not the one they filled fresh. The ones they didn't touch." She found that she was gripping the cold rail with her right hand, and let go. "They'd started going together before the hold. I saw the fourth and fifth on the board before Westerley had even found their cell. Not in step. Leaning. The way you lean when you're about to fall in with somebody."

"From up here," said Orla.

"From up here you can see all six at once. On the floor you look at one."

Orla was quiet. Below them, the Holloway bank went on through its eighth minute with its needles uneven and its four standing very still. A Halden needle went on limping.

"There's something else," said Kiva.

She had not meant to say it until she was sure. She looked down past the rail, past the Westerley four and their dead bank, at the grey wooden skirt that ran round the foot of the stands. From the floor it was a skirt. From twenty feet up, looking down at an angle, it was a box without a lid. Inside it, under the stands, under all six cells, there was water. It lay in one long iron trough the length of the bank, dark and still, with the light of the cases on it.

"They're standing in water," she said. "All six. In one trough."

"It's the jacket," said Aske, on her other side. It was the first thing he had said since the glass turned. "To keep them cool. Every bench bank has one."

"One water," said Kiva. "Under all of them."

Aske did not answer that. Orla took her stick off the rail and laid it across her knees.

"Say it plainly," said Orla. "As if I didn't know anything."

Kiva thought about it. She did not want to say it the way she would write it in a column. She wanted to say it so that somebody standing at a bank with their hands full could use it.

"A cell joins a stepped bank by the water, if the water's all there is between them," she said. "That's Ferren. Everybody in Meridian knows that; it's in my aunt's sheets. So the fault cell's been sitting in that trough telling the water its count, and the water's been telling the other five. That's why they lean. Then you quench the fault and fill it fresh, and the five that are left are already half in step with each other. If you tune the new one to the table, it's a seventh voice saying the same thing. If the cans have the hair in them as well, and every can's had the hair since the thaw, then that's everything in the trough leaning the same way. And it falls in."

"And Halden?"

"Halden found it in a minute and forty. The others hadn't had time to lean. And they gave the new one a limp." Kiva looked at the third needle, jerking twice and three times. "It can't get hold of a limp."

"No," said Orla. "It can't."

At ten minutes the marshal by the Halden bank lifted his hand, and the Clerk said, "*Held.* Halden License," and the gallery made the warm low sound that it had made on Saturday in the Graving, the sound of a thing going as it was expected to go. Holloway held as well. A four Kiva did not know stepped at eight minutes, and another at six.

Kellan, below, put both hands flat on the grey wooden skirt of his bank and looked at them. He did not look up at the gallery.

---

Seven's heat was the third, at two o'clock.

Kiva spent the hour between in a corner of the gallery with her book on her knee, writing, while the second heat ran below her. The Ropewalk's first four was in it. She watched Lyle find their cell with her palm, laid, both doors open and her feet in the flagstones, and the gallery's Saltmere green went very quiet to watch her. It took Lyle two minutes and ten. They tuned it with a card. They held all ten minutes, laid, with Carrow standing behind the bank with her arms folded and Treen's quick eyes on the load board. They did not touch the water.

They held because they were quick, Kiva thought. Not because of the water. She wrote that down in the left-hand column, where the things went that she had seen.

On the right, where the reasons went, she wrote three lines, and crossed one of them out, and wrote it again more plainly. Then she tore the page out of the back of the book, where the clean pages were, and folded it in four.

It was the first page that she had ever torn out of that book. She looked at the stub for a moment. Then she put the book away.

"That's for Pryce," said Aske, who had been watching her and pretending not to.

"It's for whoever has the drum. It'll be Pryce." She turned the folded page over in her fingers. "There's a minute at the rail, between the quench and the tune. The Clerk read it out this morning. A four can go to the rail and speak to anybody."

"And you'll be at the rail."

"I'll be at the gallery rail. I'll not be on the floor." She looked at him. "That's on my card. Not at the Bench. I'm not at it. I'm above it, holding a piece of paper."

Aske considered that for some time.

"I'd want to read the card again before I said yes," he said at last. "I've read it four times this week. It says you don't stand at a Bench and you don't touch a cell, and you light nothing but one door, declared." He rubbed his chin. "It says nothing about paper."

"No."

"Then I'll sit beside you while you hold it, and if you so much as lean over the rail I'll pull you back by your coat." He settled his bag on his knees. "That's not on the card either. That's mine."

---

Perrin declared at the marshal's table at five to two, in the flat clear voice that he had found at the Graving.

"*Team Seven. Cade, carry. Pryce, ward, drum. Talley, edge. Finch, ward, catcher. Rowan stands down, by the Chair's condition.*"

There was a murmur in the gallery. It was smaller than Saturday's. People had heard it before.

Seven went to the end bank in the far row, the one nearest the mast. From the gallery they looked very small: Perrin with the quench box under his arm, Jude limping a little with his left arm out of its linen and held away from his side, Anwen with her sleeves rolled up, and Pru at the end with her cord at her belt and her face already going red.

Kiva watched Pru's hands. They were steady.

"*Find,*" said the Clerk.

Anwen went down the bank with her palm.

She went slowly. She went more slowly than Hesketh or Lyle, and Kiva knew why. Anwen did not trust a thing until she had tried it twice. At the first case she held her hand on the glass for a long breath, and lifted it, and moved on. At the second she held it. She lifted it. She put it back. Then she went on to the third, and the fourth, as if the second had been nothing, and Kiva's stomach went tight.

"She's past it," said somebody behind them, in a coast voice. "It's the second. Anybody could see that."

"Hush," said Orla, without turning round.

Anwen went down all six. Then she came back. She came back to the second case and put her palm on it and held it there, a long time, with her head bent. Then she put her other hand flat on the fourth case at the same time, and held them both, one palm on each, with her arms out wide.

She was reading two at once. Kiva had never seen anybody do that.

"*Second,*" said Anwen, quite loudly, so that the gallery heard her. "And the fourth's leaning on the fifth."

Perrin's hand went up.

"*Team Seven. Two. Declared.*" The marshal looked at the glass. "Four minutes and ten."

It was slow. It was the slowest in the heat by a minute. Kiva heard somebody in green behind her let a breath out through their teeth. But Anwen had said the other thing out loud, in front of everyone. *The fourth's leaning on the fifth.* She had found it with her own hands.

They quenched. Perrin pulled the red handle on the second case, and its light went out. Pru was at the can slot, kneeling, with her ward up at her hip, low and small, a tray the width of a doormat. The quenched charge came out of the slot as a little grey spill, like flour out of a split sack. It did not reach the flagstones. It came down onto the ward. Pru tapped it once with her right hand and folded it from the far edge, quickly, to a penny, and the penny went into the quench box in Jude's hands, and Jude shut the lid.

"Clean," said the marshal.

Pru was scarlet to the ears. She stood up.

"*The rail,*" said the Clerk, to the whole heat. "One minute."

Pru came to the rail.

It was not the gallery rail. It was the rope at the foot of the gallery, where the floor ended and the crowd began, under the iron columns. Kiva leaned forward, and felt Aske's hand take hold of the back of her coat, quite lightly, and stopped leaning. She held the folded page out between two fingers of her right hand over the gallery rail. Pru looked up and saw it.

"Drop it," said Pru.

Kiva dropped it. It went down twenty feet turning over and over, and Pru caught it in both hands against her chest as if it had been a rung, and unfolded it, and read it.

She did not read it aloud. She read it with her lips moving, the way Orla read the book. Then she looked up.

"Say it," said Pru. "I've got this. I want it in your voice, so I've got it twice."

Kiva put both hands on the gallery rail. The fingers of the left lay on the cold iron and said *keeps time,* because everything in Saltmere kept time, and she paid them no attention.

"There's water under them," she said. "All six, in one trough, under that skirt. You can't see it from where you are. I can. The fault's been telling the water, and the water's been telling the rest, and that's why Pryce felt the fourth leaning. If you tune the new one to the table, the rest will pull it in. The cans have the hair. Everything in that trough will lean the same way."

Pru's eyes were on her mouth.

"Give it a count of its own," said Kiva. "Nothing like the table's. Nothing like the others. Halden gave theirs a limp. And if the rule lets you, get the water out from under them."

"Does the rule let us?"

"I don't know. Ask."

"Thirty seconds," said the marshal.

Pru did not say thank you. She did not say anything at all to Kiva. She turned round with the page in her fist and went back to the bank at a walk, which was very nearly a run, and Kiva watched her go to Anwen and say it.

She said it with her hands. Kiva saw that from above. Pru's right hand went down flat, low, at her hip: *water, under.* Then it went out along the length of the bank: *all six.* Then she held up the page, and Anwen read it, and looked at Perrin, and Perrin looked at the marshal.

"Sir," said Perrin. "Is there anything in the rule about the jacket?"

The marshal looked at him for a moment. He looked at the Clerk. The Clerk, who had heard, looked at nothing at all.

"There's nothing in the rule about the jacket," said the marshal. "Nothing says the water goes. Nothing says it stays." He turned the glass in his hand. "If your cells run hot, that's your hold, not mine."

"*Tune,*" said the Clerk.

Jude was already on his knees at the end of the bank. He had the skirt's little hatch open, the one that Kiva had seen the Board's men use that morning to fill the trough, and his good hand was inside it on the cock. He turned it. Kiva could not hear the water go. She could see it. From the gallery the dark line of it in the trough went down, slowly, past the iron feet of the stands, and the light of the six cases lay on wet iron instead of water.

The gallery noticed. A sound went along the rail. Somebody said, "They're draining it," and somebody else said, "They can't," and the first said, "They are."

Anwen had the fresh can. Perrin held up a card. Kiva could not see what was written on it. She knew Perrin's hand. He would have written it big.

Anwen filled the can with her eyes on Perrin's watch, and her lips moving, and slid it home.

The second light came up.

"*Up,*" said Perrin.

Kiva watched the load board.

Seven's row was the last. Six dials. The second needle came up off its pin and went into a count that was nothing like a bank's. It went three quick and a slow, three quick and a slow. It was Anwen's own. Kiva knew it before she knew how she knew it; it was the rhythm that Anwen tapped on a table with her fingers when she was thinking. It was nobody else's count in the world.

The other five went on as they had been, uneven, near and not the same.

One minute. Two. On the bank beside Seven's, a Holloway second four had a needle wavering. On the near row, the Tarn's bank was going well; Trewin stood behind it with his hands clasped and his gatepost face quite still. Three minutes. Four. The trough under Seven's bank was dry. Kiva could see the stands' iron feet on dry iron, and nothing under them now but air and the grey skirt.

At five minutes the Holloway second four stepped. Their marshal's hand went up and the Clerk said it. At six, one of the three Kiva did not know.

Seven held.

Kiva did not breathe very much. She watched the whole board with her eyes gone loose. The second needle went three quick and a slow. The first and the sixth went on alone. The third went on alone.

The fourth and the fifth were near each other.

They had always been near each other. Anwen had said so. They were nearer now. They were not in step. They were walking side by side, a little out of time, the way two people walk who have been walking together a long while. Kiva saw the fifth hesitate, and catch up, and go on. Seven minutes. Eight.

"Come on," said Orla, under her breath. It was the first time Kiva had ever heard her say it.

At eight minutes and forty seconds, the fourth and the fifth needles fell into step with each other. They did it quite gently. One moment they were two. The next they were one count, in two dials, and they stayed that way.

The marshal's hand went up.

"*Stepped,*" said the Clerk. "Team Seven. Eight and forty."

There was a long sound from the gallery. It went up at the end, and Kiva could not tell whether it was disappointment or something else.

Below her, Anwen took her hands off the bank and looked at them. Perrin put the quench box down on the flagstones. Jude, still on his knees at the dry cock, put his forehead against the grey skirt for a moment and then got up.

Pru looked up at the gallery.

She did not look for comfort. She looked as she had looked on Saturday, from behind a hurdle, asking a question. Kiva put her right hand up, flat, and held it there for a moment, the way a referee holds up a hand for a call that was right.

Then Pru put the folded page in her pocket and went to shake hands with the Holloway catcher.

---

"It was the fourth and the fifth," said Anwen, at the foot of the gallery stair, at a quarter to five. "I felt them leaning before we'd even found the second. I said so."

"You said so," said Kiva. "I heard you. The whole hall heard you."

"I should have said it to the marshal. I should have asked to quench them as well."

"Quench that one and no other," said Perrin. "It's the rule. They'd have struck us." He had his hand pressed to his side, which he did now without noticing. "We did the board. We found it slow, and we drained it, and it held eight and forty. Two cells stepped. Not the one we filled."

"Because they'd been leaning for four minutes before we found it," said Anwen. "If I'd been quicker—"

"If you'd been quicker you'd have been Hesketh," said Jude. "And then you'd have been in black and gold, and none of us would like you." He had the book open on his good arm. "Pryce, sixteen hundredths, for the drum. Finch, nine, for the catch. Cade, six, for the quench and the box. Thirty-one." He wrote it. "*Held, three and eighty-five.*"

Pru was standing a little apart. She had the folded page in her hand again, and she was smoothing it flat against her thigh.

"You tore it out," she said. "Out of the book. You never tear anything out."

"It was the back. The clean ones."

"Still." Pru looked at the page, and then at Kiva. Her face had gone back from scarlet to its own colour, which was rare enough that Kiva noticed. "I want to say a thing and I want you not to answer it."

"All right."

"I could carry that," said Pru. "What you said. I didn't have to understand all of it. I had to get it from the gallery to the bank and say it to Pryce in a way she could use with her hands full, and I could." She folded the page in four again. "That's all. That's the thing. Don't answer it."

Kiva did not answer it. She stood at the foot of the stair, with the smell of salt and hot glass coming out of the hall, and thought about it.

All winter she had been told that she would have to learn to say things. Pike had made her say what a master did that he never said aloud. Coombe had made her write the handed crossing in seven columns, with every hand in it by name. She had thought that the point of it was to get the thing out of her own head. She had thought it was a kind of insurance, in case her hand went for good.

It was not insurance. It was the thing itself. She had sat twenty feet above a floor that she was not let stand on, with her doors shut and her hands in her lap, and she had seen a thing, and said it plainly, and a girl had caught it in both hands and carried it to a bank and made it work for eight minutes and forty seconds. That was what a catcher did when she could not do the thing. She said what it was. Then she let it go to whoever could use it.

It had not won them the board. That was all right. She found that she did not mind as much as she had expected to.

The Bench went up on the Board's tent pole at the timber pond at six, under the lamp, with the seal on it.

*1. Halden License, Meridian. A minute and forty; held.*

*2. Holloway, first four. Two minutes; held.*

*3. The Ropewalk School, first four. Two minutes and ten; held.*

*4. Old Four, Meridian. Three minutes and five; held.*

*5. Westerley, first four. Three minutes and thirty; held.*

*6. Team Seven, Meridian. Four minutes and ten; stepped at eight and forty, two cells, not the cell tuned.*

*Five fours held. Nineteen stepped.*

Under it, in a clerk's newer hand, there was a line that had not been on any board Kiva had ever read.

*Team Seven drained the jacket. Noted for the Board.*

"*Noted,*" said Jude. "Is that good?"

"It means somebody wrote it down," said Kellan.

He had come up behind them in his black and gold, with his hands in his pockets. He did not stand with them. He stood a pace off, the way a man stands at a gate he is not sure he is welcome through.

"First," said Perrin.

"First." Kellan looked at the board. "A minute and forty, and a limp. Pettit wrote the limp on Thursday night. He's been tapping it on the loft table for four days, and Yarrow's been throwing his boots at him." He was quiet a moment. "Nineteen stepped. Nineteen fours out of twenty-four. Do you know what that is?"

"Near enough four in five," said Jude.

"It's four in five of the best students in the country who can't keep a bank of six out of step for ten minutes on a dry floor with the Board standing over them." Kellan did his sum aloud, as he always did. "Five held. Three of those were quick, and two had watched Halden. Nobody who held drained anything. One four drained it and stepped anyway, and it stepped two cells that it hadn't touched." He looked at Kiva. "That was yours."

"Pru carried it. Pryce put her hands on it. Talley turned the cock."

"I'd expect nothing else." Kellan almost smiled. "I'm saying who saw it."

He turned to go. Then he turned back.

"The Regiment's thirty-six," he said. "In a harbour. You can't drain a harbour." And he went away across the boards toward the loft before anybody could answer him, which was just as well, because nobody had an answer.

# Chapter 26 — Kiva: The Inner Lock

Perrin was not in the loft at supper, and he was not on the Long Quay, and he was not at the Board's tent reading the Bench again. Kiva found him at twenty to seven, sitting on the lip of the Inner Lock with his legs over the edge and twenty-four feet of empty air under his boots.

The Inner Lock lay behind the Long Quay, where the old wet dock opened into the basin. It was a granite trench sixty paces long and twelve wide, with a pair of black timber gates at each end. The gates at the head held back the wet dock, which was full and flat and green and smelled of weed. The gates at the tail held back the basin, which was falling. Between them the chamber was empty. Its floor was wet stone, with weed in the cracks and a long puddle down the middle where the last of the water had not drained, and a man in a waistcoat was walking along it with a lantern, kicking at things.

"The lockkeeper," said Perrin, without turning round. "He's looking for anything that might trip somebody tomorrow. He's found a bottle and a crab."

Kiva sat down beside him. The granite was cold through her coat.

There was a notice pinned to the door of the lockkeeper's hut, behind them, under a little tin roof to keep the rain off it. She had read it on her way past. Everybody in Saltmere had read it; it had been up since five, and the Mercury had printed it.

*THE CARRY. The Inner Lock, Tuesday the 15th, from nine. The casualty lies at the head of the chamber, under a weighted cloth, with a spar lashed across its shins. A four starts on the floor at the tail. At the bell the head sluices are opened. Time stops when the casualty lies on the mark. The north ladder or the tail stair; no other way up. One rope and the Board's sling. Water at the casualty's face, ten seconds. A drop, twenty. Nothing lit on the casualty. Reverse order of seeding.*

"Twelfth seed," said Perrin. "We go thirteenth. After eleven fours have shown us how, and before eleven who'll have seen us."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know. I've been sitting here since half past five trying to decide." He pointed down the chamber, toward the head gates. "That's where it'll lie. Under the sluices. Do you see the culverts?"

She saw them. There were two square black holes low in the head wall, either side of the gates, each with an iron shutter. When the shutters went up, the wet dock would come through them.

"The water comes in at his end," said Perrin. "Not ours. He's lying right under it. You have to run forty paces to him through it coming the other way, and by the time you've got the cloth off him and the spar off his legs, he's lying in it." He pointed again, to the north wall, near the head. "And that's the ladder. Ten paces from where he lies. Twenty-two rungs, I've counted them twice. And that—" He pointed back the other way, toward the tail, where a flight of stone steps went down into the chamber from the quay. "—is the stair. Fifty paces from where he lies, back through the water, and then up."

"Everybody will take the stair."

"Everybody will take the stair who can't get him up a ladder." He was quiet for a moment. "The Ropewalk will take the ladder. They'll put a girl up it with the rope, and haul him from the top. Treen's light, and she's quick, and Tolver can hold anything you like laid at the bottom. That's what I'd do, if I were the Ropewalk."

"And Halden?"

"The stair. On a litter. Sequenced, and quick, and nobody wet above the knee." Perrin rubbed his side. "And the Tarn will take the stair because Trewin will carry him on his shoulders, slow, and never drop him, and they'll be good. They're always good."

"And us?"

He did not answer at once.

Down in the chamber the lockkeeper had reached the head gates. He held up his lantern to the north wall. The light went up the iron rungs one by one, black and wet and very close together, set into the granite in a straight line from the floor to the lip.

"I went down it at six," said Perrin. "He let me. I asked him." He nodded at the lantern. "I went down with nothing open, and then I came up with my door open. A still door goes down through whatever you stand on, into the ground under it. You know that. That's all it does." He held out one big hand and looked at it. "A rung's iron. It's set a foot into granite. The granite goes down to the bottom of the basin. So when I put my foot on a rung and open, I'm stood on the ground. I'm not on a ladder at all. I'm on twenty-two little pieces of the earth, one above the other."

Kiva looked at the rungs.

"And between them?"

"Between them I shut, and I'm a man on a ladder. Hanging by my arms. With whatever I'm carrying." He put the hand back on his knee. "Twenty-two times. And then I open again, and I'm the ground, and nothing can shift me."

"With him on your back."

"In the sling. Crossed on my chest, the straps." He said it quite flatly. "I've worked it out. I can carry him. I carried three hundred and eighty kilos for thirty counts on a gate in the autumn. He's eighty-five. He's nothing, as long as I'm planted. It's the gaps. Twenty-two gaps, where I've only got arms."

She thought of his ribs. She thought of them in the autumn, when Aske had said *sprung, not broken,* and in the winter, when he had said they were telling him things, and on Sunday, when he had said that if they went on the Bench or the Carry there would be no plant.

She did not say any of it. He knew it better than she did.

"I'd like it known," said Perrin, "that I know."

"I know you know."

"I want you to say it. Somebody has to." He was still looking at the ladder. "You're the only one who's ever done a thing like it. You did three doors on a yard, and in the Pit, and on the Old Yard, with your arm going white, because there was a thing that needed doing and you were the only one who could. And everybody said the price first. Aske said it. You made him." He turned his head at last and looked at her. "Say the price."

Kiva looked at him for a long time.

"Your ribs," she said. "Sprung in the autumn. Mended, Aske says, and telling you things. Twenty-two times your arms take eighty-five kilos and your own weight with the straps across your chest. One of them goes, likely. Perhaps two. Then you're on a ladder with a man on your back and nothing in your chest, and the water's coming up under you." She made herself go on. "And tomorrow's the Longshore. If they go today, you don't plant tomorrow. Not for us and not for anybody."

"Yes," said Perrin.

"That's the price."

"That's the price." He took a breath, carefully, the way he breathed now. "Thank you. I'll pay it. I'd like that known as well."

Behind them, along the quay, the clock on the customs house struck the quarter. Kiva took her left hand out of her pocket and laid the first two fingers on the iron rail at the lock's lip, lightly, without leaning.

*It keeps time.*

She took her book out and wrote it at the back, in the ruled columns: *Monday the 14th. A quarter to seven. The rail of the Inner Lock, Saltmere. Yes.*

"What's that?" said Perrin.

"The same question at the same hour. Yes or no. Hart's way." She put the book away. "It doesn't tell me anything. That's the point. In a year it might."

"In a year." Perrin looked down at the chamber. "I can't think past tomorrow at ten."

"Then don't." She sat a while longer on the cold granite beside him. "Perrin. There's one thing."

"Say it."

"You'll be shutting and opening twenty-two times, by feel. You'll be feeling for the rung with your boot, and waiting till it's under you, and then opening. Waiting's slow." She looked at the rungs in the lantern light. "Somebody at the foot could see your boot land. They could say when it's on. You'd open on the word, not on the feel."

Perrin was very still.

"That's *nobody,*" he said. "That's the yard."

"It's the other way round. It's *set.*" She shrugged. "It's only a word. I'd be at the bottom anyway, after the cloth. I watch feet. I always have."

He looked at her, and then at the ladder, and then at his own hands.

"I'd have thought of it," he said. "By tomorrow. I'd like that known too."

"I know you would."

"But I hadn't yet." He got up off the lip, in one movement, and then put his hand to his side and stood still until whatever it was had stopped telling him. "*Set.* On the word. I'll open on your word, and you'll not say it till the boot's on, and if you're wrong I'll be the first to hear about it." He looked down at her. "It's still mine. The ladder. You're only the word."

"It's yours," said Kiva. "I'm only the word."

Halden License had come in at eight from a dinner at the Counting House, all four of them, and Yarrow had brought back a bottle with a gold label and was trying to give it away. Old Four had decided that the Carry was theirs and were saying so to the room. Orla was sitting up on the bed by the stove with her bad leg out straight and her stick against the wall. She read Monday in silence. She read Kiva's line under the Bench. *The fault tells the water and the water tells the rest. Pru carried it. It held eight and forty.* And then the other line, on the left, about the street outside the Counting House.

She shut the book and gave it back.

"He wrote to the Chair," she said.

"On Saturday. Adlam told me in the street. He said the Proprietor wanted me to know before anybody else told me."

"He would." Orla looked at the stove. "You told him the road. He's on it. Good."

"Good?"

"It's a long road. It goes through Crest, and Crest goes through your healer, and your healer goes through me, and I go through you." Orla's crooked face did not change. "Every one of us on it has a door we can shut. That's what a road is." She was quiet a moment. "You'll find out now whether you meant it. When you said it at his table."

"I meant it."

"Then when the Chair writes to you, and she will, you'll say to her what you said to him. In the same words, if you can manage it." Orla reached for her stick, and changed her mind, and left it. "That's all. It's not a thing to sit up over. It's a thing to have said once and be able to say again."

Kiva went back up the room. At the north end, by the window, Aske was sitting on Perrin's bed with Perrin's shirt pulled up and his own hands flat on Perrin's ribs, very gently, with his head on one side as if he were listening at a door.

"Sprung," said Aske. "As before. Telling you things, as before." He took his hands away. "I'll not tell you not to. It's not my card. I've been asked to watch the Carry, and I'll watch it, and you'll come to me after and tell me what it said." He stood up. "If you hear it go, stop. That's not advice. That's the only thing I know about ribs that's worth a dram."

"If I stop on a ladder, I'm a man on a ladder with no ribs," said Perrin.

"Then don't hear it," said Aske, and went away to his own bed.

Mara Rowan stood on the outfall gallery at a quarter past eleven on Monday night, with her reading hand in her coat pocket and the basin going down below her in the dark.

It was the neap. Hart had said so at supper, in the keeper's room, with his book of slacks open beside his plate and his bad hand inside his jacket. *Neap tonight. No ring. You'll see nothing. Come anyway.* So she had come, and Peverell had lit the lamp on the landing and gone back to her pumps, and now there were two of them at the rail in the dark: herself, and the tall stooped man with the watch on the inside of his wrist.

The basin was low, but not very low. The neap did not take it far. A long beach of mud showed under the outfall walk, wet and shining in the lamp, and beyond it the water lay brown and nearly flat, with the outfalls pouring into it on the count. Ten paces out from the grating, where the ring rose at the springs, there was only mud with water lying on it a hand deep.

Hart had his right hand on the grating. It lay there grey-white and curled, as it always lay, like something that had been left on the iron by somebody else.

"Ache," he said. "Still running out."

Mara took her watch out and held it to the lamp. Twenty past eleven.

"The table," she said.

"The gate's table says ten past eleven for the bottom of this ebb. Add three-and-twenty for the basin." He did not look at his own watch. "Thirty-three past, near enough. It'll be thirty-four or five tonight. There's wind outside."

"And at the springs."

"At the springs it doesn't add anything." He turned his watch on his wrist, once, the way he did before he answered anything that mattered. "I've been meaning to show you that since Friday. I didn't know how to say it so you'd not think I was mad."

He took the book out of his jacket with his left hand and opened it on the rail under the lamp. Mara had seen it before. It was a fitter's notebook, black, with the corners gone, and every page was ruled in four columns in his left-handed writing. *Date. Slack, by the watch. Hand. Staff.* He turned back a few pages and held it open with his thumb.

*The 8th. 6.12. Quiet. 4' 6½".*

*The 9th. 6.13. Quiet. 4' 6½".*

*The 10th. 6.13. Quiet. 4' 5".*

*The 11th. 6.14. Quiet. 4' 2".*

"The springs," said Hart. "The dawn slack, four mornings running. A minute in four days." He turned the page. "Now the gate's table for the same four mornings."

He had written it underneath, in pencil. *Gate, low water: 5.49. 6.38. 7.27. 8.15.*

Mara read it twice.

"That's fifty minutes a day," she said. "That's a tide."

"That's a tide. That's the sea, outside the Mole, going out and coming in as it always has, fifty minutes later every day." He put his finger on the inked column. "And this is the basin. At the springs. Holding at a quarter past six every morning, while the sea outside goes on walking round the clock without it."

"That can't be."

"No," said Hart. "It can't. I didn't write it down because I thought it could. I wrote it down because it did." He turned the page back again and showed her an older one. "Look. The neaps in the second month. The basin keeps the table. Three-and-twenty minutes behind it, every day, walking with it like a dog. It's only at the springs that it stops walking. Four days, five. Every spring since the ninth month." He shut the book. "And only the dawn slack. The evening one walks."

Mara stood with her hand in her pocket and looked at the dark water.

She had found the twenty-three minutes herself, on her first night, with a watch and the gate's table and Peverell's staff, and had been pleased with it. She had not gone on to the springs. She had not thought to.

"Held," she said.

"That's what I'd call it. If I had to call it anything." Hart's voice was quite flat, the way it always was, like a gauge being read. "At the springs, at dawn, something holds the slack. Holds it to an hour. As if the basin had an appointment."

"With what?"

"I write what the hand says." He took his hand off the grating and put it back inside his jacket. "I don't know what it's an appointment with. I know the ring stands up at the same hour every spring morning, near enough, and it's been getting taller. And I know that on the 23rd, if it keeps the appointment, it'll be at nine past six. That's what we wrote. That's why we could write it a fortnight off."

Below them, the basin went on going down. The outfalls poured. The lamp on the landing burned steady and yellow, an oil lamp, Peverell's, not the Board's white.

At twenty-six minutes to midnight by Mara's watch, Hart said, "Quiet."

His hand was not on the grating. He had laid it there again without her seeing, and it lay there now, grey on the black iron. The water below did not change. The outfalls went on pouring. But Mara found that she could not hear the count in the rail any more, through her sleeve. She had been hearing it for a fortnight. It was gone.

"No ring," said Hart. "At the neaps there's no ring. The count goes out of the harbour at the slack, as it always does, and I don't know where it goes." He looked out at the place ten paces from the grating, where there was nothing but mud and a hand of water. "At the springs I can see where it goes. At the neaps I can't. It's worse at the neaps."

They stood for a while. Mara found that her left hand had come half out of her pocket, of its own accord, and was resting on the rail beside Hart's. She looked at it. It was the hand that read. It had read the Ferren bank and twenty-four cells on one hill and seven drums on another, and it had been starved to the wrist once for it, and it had come back.

It had not touched a cell in Saltmere. She had decided that on the first day and kept to it for a fortnight, while Rusk asked her twice and Peverell asked her with her eyes and the Harbour Committee wrote to the Clerk three times to ask whether the Works meant to stop the Regiment. She had kept her hand in her pocket because she did not know enough, and a hand on a cell that does not know enough is how a man loses a hand.

She knew more now. She did not know enough.

"Peverell had a letter from the Committee this afternoon," said Hart. "The fourth. They want to know by Friday whether the Works will stop the bank on the 23rd. They say the low town has a right to know whether it's to be pumped or bailed."

"It has."

"It has." He turned his watch. "Rusk says it's not the Works' bank. It's the harbour's, and the harbour's the Committee's, and the Committee's waiting for the Works. Peverell says she'll not stop her pumps on anybody's word but her own, and she'll not give her own till somebody tells her it's safe." He was quiet a moment. "Nobody's going to tell her it's safe."

"No."

"Somebody's going to have to touch one," said Hart. "Before the 23rd. A cell. Somebody who can read it. Or we'll be standing on this landing at nine past six watching a ring we wrote down, with thirty-six cells behind us that nobody's laid a hand on since the autumn, and we'll have been right, and it won't help anybody."

Mara looked at her hand on the rail.

"I know," she said.

She did not take it off the rail. She did not put it in her pocket. She stood with it there, beside his, in the quiet that had no count in it, until a little after a quarter past twelve, when Hart said, "Ache," and the count came back into the iron under her palm as if it had never been away.

The Inner Lock was full by half past eight.

People stood three deep along both lips of the chamber, and on the gates, and on the roof of the lockkeeper's hut, and on the bollards along the Long Quay, and on each other. There were children on their fathers' shoulders. There were Company men in green on the head gates with their arms folded. The Mercury's men had a place by the hut, with a little table, and one of them had a long glass on a stand. At the tail end, by the stone stair, there was a rope across the quay, and behind it, in a row, the twenty-four fours in their colours, waiting to be called.

Kiva stood with Seven at the rope. Perrin was in front, in his shirt, with the linen under it and his coat over his arm. He was not talking. He had stopped talking at about half past seven.

Down in the chamber the casualty lay at the head end, under the culverts.

The Board called him the Purser. He was a canvas man the size of a big sailor, stuffed with wet sand, with a painted face and a cap sewn onto his head. He lay on his back on the wet stone, with a grey sailcloth spread over him from his chin to his boots, and across his shins there was a spar, an oak baulk as thick as a man's thigh, lashed down to iron rings in the floor with tarred line. The sailcloth was heavy. Kiva could see the line of lead shot sewn into its hem along the far edge, the edge away from the tail, a dark seam like a scar.

"Weighted on the far side," said Anwen beside her. "So when you lift the near edge, it pulls back down on him."

"Like a skin," said Kiva.

"Like a skin." Anwen looked at it. "Somebody on the Board's been to the Longshore."

The Clerk came along the quay in her grey coat with the gold chain on it and stopped at the rope.

"The Carry," she said. "The second board. Reverse order of seeding: the twenty-fourth seed goes first. The bell, and the head sluices open. Time stops when the casualty lies on the mark." She pointed, without looking, at a chalk circle on the quay by the lockkeeper's hut, twenty paces from the lip. "You've read the notice. I'll not read it again." She turned to the first four in the row, a Westerley four in sea blue. "When you're ready."

Kiva did not watch the first run. She watched the water.

The lockkeeper stood on the head gates at a great iron crank with a handle at each side, like a ship's capstan laid on its back. When the bell went, he put both hands to it and turned, and the iron shutters in the head wall went up a foot. The wet dock came through the culverts in two brown spouts and fell onto the stone floor of the chamber with a noise like applause. It spread. It came down the chamber toward the tail in a thin brown sheet, and it pooled first round the Purser, who lay right under it.

At a minute by the clock on the customs house, the lockkeeper turned the crank again, harder, both arms, and the shutters went up all the way. The spouts became two thick pouring columns. The noise doubled. The water round the Purser went from a sheet to a pool, and from a pool to a flood.

Kiva counted.

She counted the first run, and the second, and the third. She stood at the rope with her lips moving, and she did not care who saw her. The Westerley four took the stair and came in at four minutes and two seconds. The second four took the stair and dropped the Purser on the steps, and the marshal said "*Drop,*" and they came in at four and forty. The third four took the ladder with a rope, as Perrin had said the Ropewalk would, and got the Purser's face under while they were tying him on. "*Face,*" said the marshal, and they came in at four and nine.

By the fourth run she had it.

"Half for a minute," she said. "Then full. At half, it reaches his face at a minute and forty. If it's full sooner, a minute and twenty." She looked down at the chamber. "He turns it at the same time every run. By the customs house clock. He's careful."

"So we've a minute and forty," said Jude.

"From the bell. To have him out from under the cloth and up out of the water. Forty paces to run first. Through it."

"Then a minute and forty's long," said Perrin.

It was the first thing he had said for an hour. Everybody looked at him.

"It's long," he said again. "If we're quick at the cloth. Pryce's ward under the near edge, Rowan on the far edge, Talley's edge on the lashings, me on the spar. Then he's on my back. Then it doesn't matter where the water is." He did not look at the ladder. "Then it's twenty-two rungs, and I don't care what the water does."

They watched. The fifth run took the stair with a litter. The sixth took the ladder with a rope and was quick, three and forty-one, the best yet. A Holloway four in dark red took the stair and was clean at three and thirty-eight.

The tenth was the Ropewalk's second four. They ran the chamber at the bell in a body. Their Hold laid a wall under the sailcloth and lifted it up and back in one smooth movement, and it did not fold back, because her feet were in the floor. Their carry, laid, took the spar off the Purser's shins like a man lifting a gate off its hinges. Their catcher went up the north ladder with a rope round her shoulders, very light and very quick, and their blade went up after her. At the top the two of them hauled, while the carry stood laid at the bottom and steadied the Purser up the wall like a sack going up into a loft.

"*Three twenty-seven,*" said the marshal. "Clean."

The quay made its warm low sound.

"That's the time," said Jude. "That's the one to beat. Till the Ropewalk's first."

"That's their second four," said Anwen. "Their first is better."

# Chapter 27 — Kiva: Twenty-Two Rungs

The water came through the culverts with a noise like a crowd standing up.

Kiva was running before she heard it. The four of them went down the chamber together, forty paces of wet stone, with the granite walls going past on either side and the faces all along the top of them going past as well, a blur of pink and green. The water met them a third of the way. It came along the floor toward them in a thin brown sheet, very fast, and it was cold through her boots at once.

At thirty paces she could see the Purser. At twenty she could see the water round him. It had already found his head. It lay round the canvas of his skull in a ring, and in the ring it was going round and round, because the spouts were falling on the stone a yard beyond him and spreading out in every direction.

She went wide. That was the plan. She went past the Purser's feet on the north side and round his head, under the spouts, so close to the head wall that the spray came down on her like rain. The noise was enormous. She could see the leaded hem of the sailcloth lying in the water at the Purser's crown, a dark heavy seam, and she put both boots on it and opened.

Her still door went down.

It went down through the hem and the stone and the granite under the stone, a long way, to wherever the bottom of the dock was. She felt it go and she felt it hold. The far edge of the sailcloth was under her heels, and it was the ground now, and nothing in the world would shift it while she stood there.

"*Far edge,*" she said. "*Mine.*"

Anwen was at the near edge, at the Purser's shins. Her ward came up at her hip, low and flat, silver in the spray, and she put it under the cloth's hem where it lay on the spar and lifted.

The cloth came up off the Purser like the cover of a book.

It wanted to fall back. Kiva could feel it want to. The lead in its far edge pulled, the way the lead in a fishing net pulls, and if her boots had not been on it, the whole grey sheet would have slid down under Anwen's ward and back over the painted face. But her boots were on it, and her still door was in the ground. The far edge did not move. Anwen walked her ward up the cloth and over, as Mace had taught her to walk a wall, the far end first, and the near half of the sailcloth went up and over and came down in a heavy wet fold on the far half, across the toes of Kiva's boots.

The Purser lay in the water with his face to the roof. The water was at his ears.

"*Line,*" said Jude.

He was on his knees at the spar. His left arm was held away from his side, as Aske had told him, and his edge was lit on his right fist, short and bright. He put it to the first lashing where the tarred line went round the spar and through the iron ring in the floor, and the line parted with a sound like a whip. He went to the second. It parted.

"*Spar,*" said Perrin.

He had planted beside the spar's north end. Kiva saw his boots go into the floor, both of them, square, the way he planted on a gate. He bent and put his two big hands under the oak and stood up with it.

It came up. It was as thick as a man's thigh and as long as a man was tall, and he lifted one end of it off the Purser's shins as easily as he would have lifted a gate off its hinges. He held it there, up at his waist, with his arms straight and his whole body planted, and his face went no colour at all.

"*Out,*" he said.

Anwen had shut her ward. She took the Purser by the collar of his painted coat and dragged him backward from under the spar, out of the water's ring, through the water, two paces, three. He was heavy. He was eighty-five kilos of wet sand in a canvas skin, and the water made him heavier. Jude had his feet.

"*Down,*" said Perrin, and set the spar down on the wet stone where the Purser's legs had been. Then he shut.

Kiva shut. The cloth lay folded at her feet. She stepped off it.

Twenty-six seconds, said a voice somewhere above them, very faint, through the noise of the spouts. It was a man on the lock's lip with a watch, calling it for the crowd.

"*Sling,*" said Perrin.

Anwen had it. It was the Board's sling, a band of grey canvas as wide as a hand with a loop at each end and a buckle in the middle. They had practised it twice in the loft on Monday night, with Jude as the Purser, and Jude had complained the whole time that he was being strangled.

They got the Purser up. It was the worst part. Kiva had known it would be. A canvas man full of sand has no stiffness in him anywhere, and when you lift him he folds, at the knees and the waist and the neck, like a sack folding. Anwen took him under one arm and Jude under the other, and Kiva took his legs, and between them they stood him up against Perrin's back with his head lolling on Perrin's shoulder.

Perrin crouched. He had his hands on his knees.

Anwen put the loops over his shoulders, one each side. She crossed the band over his chest, under the linen that Jude had wound round him that morning. She pulled it tight and buckled it.

Kiva saw Perrin's face when the buckle went home. It did not change. Only his breathing changed. It went short, and stayed short.

"*Up,*" he said.

He stood up with the Purser on his back.

The lockkeeper turned his crank. The shutters went up all the way. The spouts became two great columns, and the noise doubled, and the water in the chamber went from the shins to halfway up the calf in the time it took Kiva to look down at it.

A minute. They had a minute's start on the face. It did not matter now. The Purser's face was over Perrin's shoulder, two feet above the water, and it would stay there unless Perrin went down.

Perrin walked to the ladder. He did not run. He walked through the rising water with the Purser on his back and his head bent forward under the weight, ten paces, with the three of them round him, and stopped at the foot of the north wall where the iron rungs went up.

"*Talley,*" he said. "Go."

Jude went up the ladder.

He went up it fast, with nothing lit, the way a boy goes up a tree. He went up on his good arm and his bad one both, though he had been told not to lean on the bad one. At the top he went over the lip on his stomach and turned round at once and lay flat on the granite with his arms down over the edge, both of them. His face looked down at them, white, from twenty-four feet up.

"Ready," he called.

Perrin put his right boot on the first rung.

Kiva stood in the water at the foot of the ladder, with her face a yard from his heel. Anwen was beside her. The water was at their knees, cold, pushing at the backs of their legs as it went down the chamber toward the tail. The spouts roared. Over all of it, from the top of the lock and the quay and the gates and the roof of the lockkeeper's hut, there was the crowd, and the crowd had stopped making any noise at all.

She watched the boot.

She watched it come down on the iron. She watched the heel find the rung and the sole settle on it, and the weight come into it. She watched the ankle stop moving.

"*Set,*" she said.

Perrin opened.

She felt it go down through the rung, through the granite, into the ground. It was the same thing that she had felt a thousand times in a yard and a pen. It was a still door planting. Only it was going sideways out of a boot on an iron bar, into a wall, and down from there.

He was the ground. He let go of the rung above him with his right hand and reached for the next. He shut.

For a moment he was a man on a ladder with eighty-five kilos on his back and nothing under him but his arms. Kiva saw the muscles in his forearms stand out like ropes. His left boot came up off the first rung and found the second.

She watched the heel. She watched it settle.

"*Set.*"

He opened. He was the ground again.

It went like that. It went like that for a long time.

Afterward, people told her how long it had been. Jude had counted it from the top. The man with the watch on the lip had called it for the crowd. Anwen had counted it under her breath, standing beside her in the water. Kiva did not count it. She did not know how long anything was. She knew only the boot and the rung and the word, over and over.

The boot came down. She watched the heel. *Set.* He opened and was the ground, and reached, and shut, and was a man on a ladder.

The water came up past her knees. It came up her thighs. It was so cold that she stopped feeling her feet, and then she stopped feeling her shins, and she did not look down at it. She kept her eyes on the boot.

The boot came down. *Set.*

By the fifth rung he had it. She could see that he had it. He was quicker. He did not wait to feel the iron. He put his boot on it and listened for her, and when she said the word he opened on the end of it, as Trewin had opened on a yard on Sunday a count late, and as Perrin himself had opened on the Low Lane in the second month a quarter early. Only now it was neither. It was on the word. He was opening as her mouth closed on the *t.*

"Three a rung," said Anwen, very low, beside her. "He's doing three a rung."

Kiva did not answer. The boot came down.

At the ninth rung she heard his breathing change.

It had been short since the buckle. Now it went shorter. It went in and stopped and went in again, the way breath goes when it hurts to take it. His left boot came up off the ninth rung and hung in the air a moment over the tenth, and then came down. She watched the heel.

*Set.*

He opened. He reached. He shut. The right boot came up. It came down on the eleventh rung.

She watched the heel find the iron.

And she heard it.

She heard it through the noise of the spouts and the crowd's silence and the cold. It was not loud. It was a small dull sound inside him, like a green stick breaking under a wet cloth.

Perrin stopped.

He did not open. He did not shut. He stood on the eleventh rung with his right boot on it and his left boot on the tenth, with his arms locked on the rung at his chest and the Purser's painted face over his shoulder, and he did not move at all. She could see his back. It had gone quite still under the canvas band, the way a horse goes still when it is deciding whether to fall.

*If you hear it go, stop.*

He had stopped.

Kiva stood in the water up to her thighs and looked at his boot. The heel was on the iron. It was settled. She had not said the word.

She did not say it now. It was not hers to say. The word was for when the boot was on, and the boot was on, and he had not opened. He was hanging by his arms on a ladder with a broken rib and eighty-five kilos on his back and nothing under him, and if she said *set* he would open because she had said it, and she did not know whether that was what he wanted. She did not know whether he could.

"*Cade,*" said somebody above. It was Jude, from the lip, very high and thin.

Perrin did not answer.

Kiva watched his boot. She heard Anwen beside her draw breath to say something, and stop. The water came up another inch. Somewhere at the far end of the chamber, under the lock's tail gates, the marshal was standing on the stair with his glass, watching, and he did not say anything either.

Two counts. Three.

Then Perrin breathed. She heard it. It was a long breath in, careful, all the way down, and a breath out. And his right heel moved on the eleventh rung. It moved a little, settling, an eighth of an inch, the way a man settles his weight on a floor he is about to stand up on.

She watched it stop.

"*Set,*" said Kiva.

He opened on the end of the word.

She felt the ground take him. She felt the whole of his weight, and the Purser's, go down out of his arms through his boot and into the wall and the earth, all at once, and she saw his arms go slack on the rung above him, just for a moment, as a man lets his arms go slack when somebody else has taken the load. He hung there, planted, the ground, with nothing in his arms.

Then he reached for the twelfth rung.

He was slower after that. Four a rung, Anwen said, and then five. Kiva did not hear her. She heard the boot come down, and watched the heel, and said the word, and felt him plant.

At the fifteenth rung, his left hand missed the iron.

It went up past the rung and slapped the granite and came down again and found it. Kiva's heart went into her throat and stayed there. He was shut. He was a man on a ladder. He hung by one hand for the space of a breath, with the Purser swinging out from his back on the canvas band. Then his other hand closed on the rung beside the first, and his boot came up, and came down on the sixteenth.

She watched the heel. She could hardly see it now. He was sixteen feet up and the spray from the spouts was in her eyes.

*Set.*

Carrow had it in her hands. She had been holding it up. But she was holding it with nothing open, because she was laid, and to lift anything she had to walk, and to walk she had to unlay. She had done that. She had shut both doors to hold the head. And the Purser was heavy, and wet, and folding at the neck, and his chin went down onto his chest. It was flat. It was the only flat thing in the whole roar.

Carrow did not look up. She did not stop. She lifted the head again and held it, and Tolver said *up,* and Treen and their blade hauled from the top, and the Purser went up the north wall of the Inner Lock on the end of a rope, steady and fast, with Tolver laid at the bottom keeping him off the stone. It was beautiful. It was the fastest thing anybody had done all morning.

They ran him to the mark.

"*Two minutes and fifty-nine seconds,*" said the marshal. "*Ten for the face. Three minutes and nine.*"

The Mercury's man got to Perrin before anybody could stop him.

He was a young man with ink on his cuffs and a notebook already open. He came pushing through the people round the bollard and stood in front of Perrin and said, in a quick coast voice, "Mr. Cade. The Mercury. How did you think of it, sir? The rungs. Nobody's ever planted a ladder."

Perrin looked at him.

"I sat on the lip," he said. "Last night. For an hour."

"And it came to you?"

"No. I worked it out." Perrin's voice was hoarse. "A rung's iron. It's in granite. The granite's in the ground."

The young man wrote it down. He wrote it with his mouth moving, the way Pru read. "*The granite's in the ground,*" he said. "That's very good. That's a line. And the girl at the foot, sir, calling? That was the catcher? Rowan?"

"That was the word," said Perrin. "The ladder was mine."

The young man looked up from his book. He looked at Perrin's face, and then at Kiva in her blanket against the hut, and something in his own face changed, as if he had found something more interesting than what he had come for.

"Yes, sir," he said. "Of course. *The ladder was mine.*" He wrote that down too. "And how does it feel, sir? To beat the Ropewalk on their own quay? People will be talking about it for years. They're talking about it now." He jerked his head at the crowd. "Half the low town's down here. They came to see the Ropewalk. They'll go home talking about you."

Perrin did not answer.

The young man waited, and then, when he saw that no answer was coming, he touched his hat and went away into the crowd with his notebook, to find somebody who would talk.

Kiva saw them then. She had not been looking. Now she saw how many people were looking at Perrin.

They were all along the quay. They were Saltmere green, and Holloway red, and Westerley blue, and the buff of the Tarn, and Meridian. They were looking at him the way people look at a man who has done a thing that they wish they had done. Some of them were smiling. Some of them were not. A boy in green by the stair said something to the girl beside him, low, and the girl laughed, and they both looked at Perrin, and the girl stopped laughing. By the hut, one of Old Four was saying to another, not quite low enough, *he'll not stand tomorrow, any road. Not with that.*

Perrin heard it. Kiva saw him hear it.

He sat on his bollard with his hands on his knees. He did not look at anybody. He looked at the Purser, lying on the mark with his painted face to the sky and the Board's sling round his chest.

"Kiva," he said.

She got up, with the blanket round her, and went and stood beside the bollard. Her bare feet were on the cold granite.

"I wanted a thing with only my name on it," said Perrin, very low. "All winter. I told you so, out loud, at home. I minded that it was always you."

"I know."

"Now it's got only my name on it." He was still looking at the Purser. "And everybody on this quay's looking at it. And half of them want it." He breathed in, carefully, as far as he could go, and out. "I thought it would feel like having something."

"What does it feel like?"

He thought about it for a long time. He was honest about things. He always had been, even when it cost him, even in the autumn when he had stood in a yard and said *I mind* out loud.

"Like being looked at," he said. "That's all. Like being looked at by people who'd like to be where I am, and think I'm lucky, and don't know about the rib or the lip or the hour." He looked up at her at last. "That's what it was like for you. Wasn't it. All year."

Kiva did not answer at once.

She thought of the gate at home in the autumn, and the Pit, and the Old Yard, and the white arm. She thought of every floor she had stood on since the spring with people looking at her, and of a boy who had stood up in front of his own four and said *I mind,* out loud, and had been Perrin.

"Yes," she said. "It's that."

"It's not better," said Perrin. "Being envied. I thought it would be better than the other thing. It isn't."

"No."

"It's not worse either." He looked back at the Purser. "It's just the other side of the same thing. I'd not have known that. I'd have gone on thinking that you had something I hadn't." He was quiet. "I'd like it known that I know now."

"It's known," said Kiva.

Aske came back through the crowd with a cup of tea in each hand. He gave one to Perrin and one to Kiva, and looked at them both.

"Drink that," he said. "Neither of you is to be looked at by anybody for at least a quarter of an hour. I'll stand here and see to it."

He stood in front of the bollard with his bag in his hand and his back to the quay, a narrow grey man who never raised his voice, and for a quarter of an hour nobody came near them at all.

# Chapter 28 — Kiva: The Second Plant

Aske wrote the card at the long table in the loft at two o'clock, with Perrin sitting across from him in a clean shirt and the four of them standing round.

He wrote it in his small square hand, slowly, as he wrote everything that mattered. Then he turned it round on the table so that Perrin could read it, and then so that the rest of them could.

*Cade, P. The seventh rib on the left, cracked across, not through; the sixth and eighth sprung as before. Tuesday the 15th, at the Inner Lock, under load. No plant. No lift. Nothing carried heavier than a cup. Strapped, to be seen morning and night. A fortnight at the least. W. Aske.*

"A fortnight," said Perrin.

"At the least." Aske capped his pen. "I've written *at the least* so that nobody can come to me on the thirtieth and say I promised."

"The Lane's Thursday."

"The Lane's Thursday, and you'll be at the hurdle with your hands in your pockets, declaring." Aske did not raise his voice. He never did. "You may captain. You may talk. You may stand on the hard tomorrow on the Longshore and watch your four with a glass, if the Board will have you there, and I think it will. You may not go onto the mud. If you go onto the mud and something goes wrong, you'll be one more thing on the mud that somebody has to carry, and you'll be the heaviest."

Perrin looked at the card for a long time.

"I knew," he said. "On the lip. Last night. I said the price. Rowan said it."

"Then you knew," said Aske. "That's the best I can say for it. You knew, and you paid, and you came first." He got up. "I'll see it again at eight."

He went down the room to his own bed. The four of them stood at the table and looked at the card.

"Finch stands," said Perrin.

He said it before anybody else could say anything. He said it as he had said things at the marshal's box, flat and clear.

"Two catchers," said Anwen. "And no still door."

"No still door."

"On the Longshore." Anwen had her hands in her sleeves. "Where the ground's mud, and the water's never still, and you've told us since Coombe that the far edge wants weight on it." She looked at him steadily. "What's behind it? Two catchers and no plant. I'm asking because somebody will."

"Rowan's a still door."

"Rowan's whatever you declare her. One door." Anwen did not look at Kiva. "If you declare her stance, she's our plant, and she can put her boots on a far edge as she did this morning, and we've got weight. That's what a still door is on the Longshore. Coombe said so. On a moving thing, weight on the far edge."

"I know what Coombe said."

"Then what's behind it?"

Perrin did not answer at once. He looked at his own hands on the table.

"Ember," said Anwen.

"Why?"

"Because if somebody goes under a skin tomorrow," said Perrin, "there's one thing I know of on this coast that takes it off them without folding whoever holds it. Coombe said it. She said she'd seen mixed fours pass a load hand to hand on the Longshore, badly, two people grabbing at once and hoping, and twice it killed somebody. She said she'd never seen it called." He looked up at last. "It's three people. Finch folds. Rowan carries. Somebody plants. We've two of them standing tomorrow, and the third was me, and the third's cracked."

Anwen was quiet.

"I'm saying if it's wanted, and we haven't got it, I'll have spent the day on the hard with my hands in my pockets knowing I could have had it declared and didn't. I'd sooner lose the weight on a far edge than that." He put his hand to his side, without noticing. "That's what's behind it. It's a thing that might not happen. I'm declaring for it so that if it does, we're not grabbing."

"I've been thinking about the plant since Sunday," said Perrin. "I asked for a second plant on Sunday, at eight, at this table. You all heard me. I said if my ribs went on the Bench or the Carry, there'd be no method." He almost smiled. "They went on the Carry."

"That's it," she said. "That's the word."

"It is. It gets less horrible."

"Does it?"

"No," said Pru, from the other end of the yard. "It gets more usual. That's not the same."

"What happens in the gap?" she said. "When nobody's got it. When she's let go and you've not got it, and you've let go and he's not got it. What happens to the thing?"

Kiva thought about it. Nobody had ever asked her that. They had asked her how long the gap was, and what it cost, and whether it was a stack. Nobody had asked her what happened to the thing.

"Nothing," she said. "For a quarter of a count, nothing. It hangs. It doesn't belong to anybody, so it doesn't do anything to anybody." She looked at her own right fist. "That's the whole point of it. A thing that wants to fold onto whoever holds it can't fold onto nobody."

Bridie looked at her fist too.

"Our book says you pass a load hand to hand," she said. "Two people holding it at once. So it's never dropped."

"That's the book."

"It sounds safer."

"It sounds safer," said Kiva. "Two people holding a thing at once is two doors on one thing, agreeing. There's a cord on the Longshore that goes to that like a dog to a bone. Our Marshal had one in a cage. It went to two people on a sack." She stopped. She did not want to frighten a girl of fifteen the night before a Hunt. "I'm not saying your book's wrong. I'm saying nobody's is right about everything."

Bridie considered that with her chin up.

"My grandad used to say that," she said. "About his scales." Then her chin did a small thing, not quite a shake. "He said nobody's scales were right about everything, and that's why you weigh twice." She went back to the gate.

*It keeps time.*

She wrote it in the back of the book by the light from the cooper's window. *Tuesday the 15th. A quarter to seven. A stave in a cooper's yard on Quay Street. Yes.*

Perrin did not answer. Kiva did not know the answer.

"No. She's watching." Perrin looked at the black water. "That's how they do it on the coast, I think. They watch you till they're sure, and then they ask once."

It was not much. It was two words, once, to the person who needed them.

She put the hand back in her pocket and went up to bed.

The Longshore began where the Mole ended.

Kiva had walked out along it once before, at two in the morning with Jude and a lantern, and then it had been black flats and a pale line of sea. Now, at half past seven, it was grey. The causeway ran out from the end of the Mole across the flats, an old raised track of stone wide enough for a cart, and on either side of it there was nothing but mud and sky. The mud was grey and wet and shining, and cut everywhere with creeks, some as narrow as a ditch and some as wide as a street, all running seaward full of brown water. The tide was going out. It had been going out since six and would go on going out till noon. Far off, at the edge of everything, there was a line of white where the sea was.

The wind was in the south-west, off the sea. It came across the flats in gusts, cold, smelling of salt and weed, and when it came it pushed the water in the creeks back a little against the ebb, so that the brown surface wrinkled and stood up in little ridges and then lay down again.

"Doran Mace, ma'am. Graywater. At Gannet Creek, in the dark, I watched him."

"Once, ma'am."

At half past eleven the channel was low enough to stand in.

The ebb had taken the creek down. "Yes."

"What's *nobody?*"

She looked at him.

"I heard the Tarn's captain at the comb table," said Penhale. "Saying it under his breath. *Nobody, nobody.* Like a prayer. And your captain said you'd an Ember declared, on a beach, where nobody needs to carry anything anywhere." He scratched his red beard. "I've been wondering."

Kiva thought about how to say it. She did not want to say it the way it was written in the columns. She wanted to say it the way she would say it to anybody who might need it on a beach.

"It's a way of passing a thing," she said. "A thing that folds onto whoever's holding it. One person catches it on a ward and lets go. For a quarter of a count, nobody's got it. Penhale looked at her fist. Then he laughed, loud and cheerful, so that a curlew got up out of the marsh grass on the far bank and went away crying.

"Nobody," he said. "On this coast nobody passes anything to nobody. We hand it, man to man, both hands on it. You'd put half the Company out of a job." He shook his head, still laughing. "I'd like to see it. I'll not, likely. I've been reserve on four Hunts, and never been onto the mud yet."

"I'd sooner you didn't see it," said Kiva. "It's only for when it's wanted."

"Then I'll hope not to." He touched his forehead to her, a little salute, with mud on his fingers. "Coombe's page. The far edge to the second. They say it's at the printer."

"It is."

"We give it the corner-hand. It's in the book. Has been since my dad." He looked down the channel toward the Tarn's stow, where Bridie was standing in the mud beside its posts with a purse over her shoulder, looking at it as if it might bite. *Something's pushing.*

And then, because she held them there, the other thing.

*It keeps time.*

# Chapter 30 — Kiva: Slack

Kellan Renn had the sum done before the rung hit the water, and it did not come out.

He was standing in the mud of Skerry Pill at twenty-five past eleven with the line of Halden's second purse in his left hand and a mallet in his right, driving its pole. The pill was narrower than a creek and deeper, a cut in the mud no more than twenty paces across, running seaward between banks of marsh grass. Two forms stood in it. Halden's was a stow, two grey posts and a sleeve, forty paces above the kiddle. The Ropewalk's was a kiddle, seven posts across the channel nearer the sea, leaning on the ebb with the white plates coming out of its skins. And in the pill's mouth, where it opened onto the flats, there was the lodger.

It lay on the mud at the water's edge where the Company had left it. It was grey, and long, longer than a man, as thick as a wrist, and it did not look like anything. It looked like a cable somebody had dropped off a boat. It had not moved since dawn. Bray had said so at the spur, in her voice like a foghorn, and every one of them had looked at it, and then not looked at it, which was harder.

The gust came off the sea at twenty-five past.

Kellan saw it on the flats, a darkness going over the shining mud like the shadow of a cloud. It came up the pill against the ebb, and the brown water stood up in ridges, and the Ropewalk's kiddle leaned hard inland all at once, all seven posts, and the fourth from the north bank cracked at the top.

A yard of it fell. It was a heap of grey ash as it fell. It hit the water and spread.

"*Maud,*" said Carrow. "*Boom.*"

She said it at once. Kellan saw her say it, and saw that she did not think about it. It was a word she had said a hundred times, on this coast, at this kind of moment, and it came out of her the way *down* comes out of a marshal.

Lyle was on the south bank, twenty paces below the kiddle. She stepped down onto the mud at the water's edge and laid.

Kellan had seen her lay in a lane and at a lock. He had never seen her lay on water. Her ward came up at her hip and went out over the pill, silver, and lay down flat on the brown surface across the whole width of the channel, bank to bank, and her feet went into the mud under it in the same breath. He felt them go. Everybody on the beat felt them go. It was like a post being driven into the ground beside you.

The grey fan came down the pill on the ebb and met the silver and stopped.

All of it stopped. Nothing went under. Graywater's walls had let a third of a rung under at Gannet Creek; Lyle's let nothing under. The grey piled against her laid ward in a long steaming crust from bank to bank, and the ebb leaned on it, and she stood on the mud with her feet in the floor of the pill and her wall in the water and did not move an inch.

It was beautiful. Kellan thought so, and was ashamed afterward that he had stopped to think it.

"*Net,*" said Bray, from the spur.

Then Treen said, "*Run.*"

Kellan turned his head.

Treen was on the north bank, small and dark, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her eyes on the pill's mouth. She had not shouted. She had said it the way she said it in a lane, short and flat, for one person.

He looked where she was looking.

The lodger had lifted its tip.

It lay along the mud at the water's edge as it had lain since dawn, grey, dull, like a dropped cable. But its tip was off the mud now. It was up, a hand's breadth, and turned, and pointing. It pointed up the pill. It pointed at the silver lying flat across the water forty paces away, and at the girl standing on the bank behind it with her feet in the mud.

Kellan had watched a lodger's tip turn in Coombe's cage in the second month, from the rail of the Pit, and had thought that he understood it. He had not understood how fast it would be.

The lodger came off the mud into the water. It went up the pill along the south bank, under the surface, the way an eel goes, straight, without any of the bends a snake makes. Kellan could see it as a ripple and a darkness. It was coming at a man's walking pace, and then faster.

"*Run,*" said Treen again. "*Maud. Run.*"

Lyle did not run.

Kellan saw her hear it. He saw her head turn a fraction toward the north bank. And then he saw her look at the grey crust lying against her wall, forty drams of the Board's ash, steaming, with the whole of the ebb leaning on it. If she shut, it went. It went down the pill on the ebb, straight into the mouth, onto the flats, into the sea. It went into the water where the lodger was.

She looked back at the crust. She did not move.

"*Maud,*" said Carrow. "*Run. Shut and run. Leave it.*"

It was too late for that. Kellan knew it was too late even as Carrow said it. The ripple was ten paces from Lyle's boots.

He dropped the mallet.

He did not decide to. He found afterward that he had done the sum, and that the sum had come out, and that he had not waited for it. The sum was that he was a blade, forty paces off, with an Ember door that would light on his right fist in less than a count, and that the lodger was a cord, and a cord can be cut. And there was nobody else on the beat who was a blade and nearer. Halden's sum was something else. He would do Halden's sum later.

"*Renn,*" said Pettit, behind him.

He was already running.

He ran across the mud of the south bank toward Lyle. The mud took his boots to the ankle at every stride, and the ripple in the water was going faster than he was. He saw it reach the bank below Lyle's boots. He saw it come out of the water onto the mud, grey, dripping, and go up over the toe of her left boot, and lie there.

Its tip went into the place where her ankle met the mud.

Kellan saw her wall go dark.

It did not shut. A ward that shuts goes in on itself, folds, closes like a hand. This went out. He reached her.

And the stow died.

Kiva had never seen a thing die all at once like that. The kiddle at Gannet Creek had been cut three posts at a time, and then four, and had gone to ash in the mud slowly, over the low water, while she was walking away from it in the dark. This went at once. The two stumps went grey and soft and slumped in the mud like candles in a hot room. The mouth went grey. The sleeve, lying in its fold on the bank and across the mud, went grey all along its length, and then thin, and then it was a film of ash lying on the mud like frost on a window.

Only the dark place in the creek bed was left, under where the mouth had been. The tear. The thread of slack water lay round it in a ruffle.

"*Dead,*" said Yeo. "Seven's stow. Twenty-nine minutes past. Two posts, two purses shut, and a rung in the net's tin from the ebb." She shut her notebook. "That's clean work. I'll say so on the slate." She looked down the spur. "Ivey. Your crew. The upper tear's yours."

Ivey came along the spur with the closing crew behind her, four Company men in green and their officer, a short grey man with his sleeves rolled. They went down into the channel past Jude, who was coming out of it on his good leg and his bad with his edge shut and his face white. They knelt in the mud round the dark place in the bed, with their hands out over it. The light under their hands was steady and white.

Kiva sat back on her heels on the bank.

She looked at her hands. They were shaking. The left one, the warm one, with its two fingers that bent and gripped and told her nothing, was shaking as much as the right. She put them both flat on her knees.

"Pru," she said.

"Don't," said Pru, three paces off, still on her knees with the second line slack in her fists. "Don't say anything. I'll go red and I'll not be able to haul anything for an hour."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

Kiva did not say it. She sat on the bank with her hands flat on her knees and looked at the film of grey ash on the mud where the stow had been, and at the Company crew kneeling round the tear with their hands out and the white light under them.

She put the first two fingers of her left hand back on the ash pole. Lightly. Without leaning.

Nothing.

The slack held. It was twenty-nine minutes past twelve, and the slack had been holding for seventeen minutes, and it would hold for twenty minutes more, near enough, if the wind held. The channel lay quite still. Down at the Tarn's half, sixty paces off, the lower stow stood in the bend with its two posts upright and its sleeve lying flat on the mud behind it.

"*Down,*" said Yeo, on the spur. "*A person down. Penhale.*"

Penhale went.

He went off the spur and down the east bank into the channel in two strides, the way he had gone into the mud a hundred times. He went across the thread of water in his thigh boots and up onto the west mud. He did not hurry and he did not hesitate. He went to the fold as a man goes to a gate he has opened every day of his life.

He went to the near edge.

That was the book. Kiva knew it as she watched him. She had never read the Company's book, but she knew it, because it was the same as every other book: when a person is under a skin, the catcher goes to the near edge, and gets his ward under it, and lifts, and the person rolls out under the ward. It was a page and a half. It had been the right page for forty years.

Penhale's ward came up at his hip. It was broad and low and grey, a coast catcher's ward, and he knelt and put it under the near edge of the fold where it lay on the mud, and he lifted.

The fold had two ends.

Kiva saw that too late. Everybody saw it too late. It was a sleeve. It had a mouth, at the post, and a closed end, a long way off down its length. The fold that had come down over Bridie was its middle. The closed end was still coming round. It was still swinging on the post, across the channel, slowly, the last of the long grey sleeve, the far end of it, the end that had been lying furthest downstream on the mud all morning while everybody watched the mouth.

It came round over the near edge. It came round over Penhale's back.

It came down.

It came down on top of the fold and on top of the man kneeling under it with his ward over his head and his hand on a girl's arm. It came down flat, grey, frosting, with a sound that Kiva did not hear, because she was on her feet by then and running, and Pru was running beside her, and somebody on the spur was shouting her name.

The far post of the Tarn's stow stood up straight in the channel's bend.

And under her boots, on the mud of the east bank, as she ran, the slack held. Her fingers were not on anything; she did not need them. The marsh grass on the west bank stood up straight and did not stir. The thread of water in the channel's bottom lay in its bed like a ribbon, running neither way. Nothing on Mallow Creek had turned but the stow.

# Chapter 31 — Kiva: Nobody

Sixty paces along the east bank of Mallow Creek is a long way in mud.

Kiva ran it. She ran it with Pru beside her and the mud taking her boots to the ankle at every stride, and the whole time she was running she was looking at the grey fold lying on the west mud across the channel, flat, still, with nothing under it that moved. It lay there as a sheet lies over furniture in a shut house. At one edge of it, the edge nearest the thread of water, a man's boot stuck out. It was a thigh boot, black, with mud on it to the knee.

She stopped on the east bank opposite it, at the channel's edge, with the thread of slack water between her and the fold. She stopped because the rule said stop.

*A person down on the mud is the officer's crew's. The fours stand off.*

The officer's reserve was under the fold.

"*Ivey,*" said Yeo, on the spur.

Her voice did not rise. It went up the creek, flat and brisk, to the place sixty paces upstream where Ivey and the closing crew were kneeling in the mud round Seven's tear with their hands out and the white light under them. Kiva saw Ivey's head come up. She saw her look down the creek, and see, and get up off her knees.

She would be a minute. A minute through mud, and then she would be at the fold, and she would do what the book said. She would go to the near edge. She would put her ward under it and lift.

The fold's far end lay on top of the near edge, where it had come down across Penhale's back.

"Ma'am," said Kiva.

Yeo did not look at her. Yeo was looking at the far post of the lower stow, standing whole in the channel's bend, with the red-haired boy of the Tarn standing beside it in the punt and the boatman holding the punt against it with his pole. The boy's edge was unlit. He was looking up at the spur with his face like paper.

"*Cut it,*" said Yeo. "*The far post. Now.*"

"*No,*" said Pru.

She said it before Kiva could. She said it loud, from the bank, scarlet to the hair, to an officer of the Saltmere Company who had not asked her anything.

Yeo turned her head.

"If you cut the post, the skin folds," said Pru. Her voice shook and kept going. "From its far edge. Toward the nearest line. We saw it at Gannet Creek, a cut post's skin, it folds onto whatever's nearest. Ma'am. They're the nearest. They're under it."

Yeo looked at her for one breath.

"*Hold,*" she said to the punt. "*Don't cut.*" The red-haired boy took his fist away from the post as if it were hot.

Then there was nothing anybody could do. Kiva stood on the bank and knew it. The book said lift, and lifting brought the far end over the lifter. The post could not be cut, because the skin would fold tighter. Nobody could go onto the mud but the officer's crew, and the officer's crew was Ivey, sixty paces off in the mud, coming, and Ivey would lift.

"Ma'am."

It was Perrin.

He had come along the spur from Seven's half. He had not run; he could not run. He had walked as fast as a man walks with a cracked rib, with his hand pressed to his side and his face grey, and he stood at Yeo's shoulder now on the stone.

"There's a way," he said. "It's three people. Two of them are on that bank."

Yeo looked at him.

"Finch catches the fold on her ward," said Perrin. "Rowan carries it, with her Ember. It's declared; I declared it at eight. The third plants it. On this bank, away from them. Nobody holds it long enough for it to fold on them." He breathed in, as far as he could. "It's been done on a yard. Twice before Coombe. Once on Sunday with him." He pointed. "He's the third."

Trewin was on the east bank below the spur, with the punt's line in his two hands. He had not let go of it. He was looking at the fold across the channel with his gatepost face gone quite white, at the place where a plait of brown hair had come loose from under the grey and lay on the mud.

"I'll plant," said Trewin. He did not look away from the plait. "Ma'am. If you say."

Yeo looked at the fold. She looked at Ivey, forty paces off now, coming down the west mud as fast as anybody can come through mud. She looked at Kiva and Pru on the bank. It was three seconds, perhaps. Kiva watched her face through all three of them, and saw an officer of thirty years on the coast decide a thing that was not in any book she had ever read.

"It's mine," said Yeo. "On my word. Do it."

Kiva went down off the bank into the channel. Pru came after her.

The thread of slack water was a yard wide and a hand deep. They went across it in one stride and up onto the west mud, and Kiva went to the fold. Not to the near edge. To the far end, the closed end of the sleeve, where it lay heaped over the near edge and over whatever was under the near edge, grey and frosting, with the cold coming off it in a breath that she could feel on her face.

She knelt beside it. Pru knelt beside her.

"It's ten paces long," said Pru. Her voice was very small. "I can't fold ten paces. My ward's the width of a doormat. I fold a rung. I fold a cord."

Short, with the swallow.

At the top of the bank, at the edge of the spur, Perrin was waiting.

He was not supposed to lift anything heavier than a cup. Aske had written it on a card. He knelt at the edge of the spur and put both his big hands under the head of the boards as they came up over the lip, and took the weight, and lifted. Kiva saw his face as he did it. She came along the spur from the causeway at a walk that was nearly a run, in her grey Board coat, with the rope in her hands. Kiva watched her come all the way. She came past the place where Seven's stow had been, a film of grey on the mud under brown water now, and past the place where the Company crew had been kneeling at the upper tear, and past the punt drawn up on the stone. She stopped where Yeo was kneeling.

"Yeo," she said.

Coombe looked at the creek too.

Coombe looked at Trewin, standing a little way off by himself with his frosted boots on the stone and his hat in his hands. She looked at the film of grey ash across the channel, stretched from the east mud where Trewin had planted to the stump of the far post in the bend, going under now in the brown flood.

She looked at it for a long time.

Coombe came across the spur to Kiva.

She did not crouch. She stood over her, with the rope in her hands, and the pale eyes looked down at Kiva's white knuckles and at Pru's white fingertips.

"It held," said Coombe.

Coombe looked at her a moment longer. The two of them knelt there with Davy Penhale between them and did not say anything to each other at all.

Kiva had not thought that it would. From the spur she could see the other beats on the Longshore, the flags on their poles standing up out of the marsh at the heads of the creeks, a quarter of a mile apart all the way to the sea. She had ruled it herself, every line of it, every night for six years, and she had written *Lives: none* on it more times than she could count, and twice she had not. She thought of a card in a kitchen in the autumn, slate blue, read aloud, signed on the back by four children. *Then we look again, and either of us may say no.* She thought of the thirtieth, a fortnight off.

"Is there a difference?"

"Not much," said Rhea Sorn, and sat on the tailboard beside the other officer until the light began to go.

# Chapter 32 — Kiva: The Measure Goes On

Aske greased the knuckles of her right hand in the healers' tent at two, with the flap tied back and the Longshore going under the flood outside.

He did it with two fingers and a pot of yellow grease that smelled of sheep, slowly, as he did everything, and then he wound linen round the hand from the wrist to the first joints and tied it off. He did not look up while he did it. He had done Pru's first, all ten fingertips, each in its own small cap of linen, so that she sat on the next stretcher with her hands held up in front of her like a surgeon waiting to be gloved.

"A burn of cold," he said. "Across the knuckles and the backs of the first two fingers. It'll blister tonight and peel by Sunday." He tied the knot. "It's your right hand. It's not the left. I'd like that understood. It's the one that works, and it'll go on working, and in a week you'll not know it happened."

"And the Ember?"

"The Ember's not in your hand. It's under your breastbone, where it's always been, and it's done nothing wrong today." He looked at her at last. "It carried more than I'd have said it could. I watched you come across that channel from the causeway with a glass, the way you'd watch a horse go at a wall you'd told it not to jump. It held. It's tired. So are you."

"Thursday," said Kiva.

"Thursday's the soonest. I said so this morning, and nothing that happened on that mud has moved it." He put the grease pot in his bag. "The left hand's warm. The right's burned. Neither of them's the hand I'm counting days for." He paused. "I'll say the price on Thursday, if anybody asks for one. I'll say it twice."

On the stretcher at the far end of the tent, Bridie Hebden lay under two blankets with her legs bare to the knee on a folded sheet. They were white to the knee, not grey, and the Company healer was laying wet linen on them, cool, very gently, and changing it. Bridie was not talking about fathoms any more. She was looking at the canvas roof of the tent with her plaits down over the stretcher's edge, and she was not crying. Trewin sat on an upturned crate beside her with his boots off and his feet in a basin of water, white to the ankle, and his hat on his knee.

He had been sitting there for half an hour without saying anything. Now he looked up, across the tent, at Kiva.

"You've plain," he said.

The tent went quiet.

"Two drams," said Trewin. He said it slowly and politely, as he said everything. "Sealed. Ward. You declared it at the gate on the first day, in front of the whole queue. Everybody saw it go silent on the seventh. It was the only one." He looked at Bridie's legs. "Plain ward-trace is for the cold. That's what my grandmother said. A healer can ease the cold with it, if it's plain."

Kiva did not answer at once. She looked at Aske.

Aske had not moved. He sat on the end of her stretcher with his bag on his knees and looked at Trewin, and then at the Company healer, and then at Bridie's white legs on the folded sheet.

"It's for a hand," said Kiva. "That's what's written on it. In two books, under two names. *For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.*" She made herself go on. "It's Seven's word. It's my captain's name and mine. If it were wanted, I'd have to go to my four and to the Order, and say it was wanted, and ask."

"I know," said Trewin. "I'm not asking you to break your word. I'm asking whether it's wanted."

"It's not," said Aske.

They both looked at him.

"Plain ward-trace eases a starved hand," said Aske. "A hand that's had the ash drawn out of it through a door, so that the door's hungry and the flesh round it's cold from inside. I've done it. Her aunt's hand was starved once, in the autumn, and plain eased it inside the hour." He nodded at Bridie. "That's not starved. That's burned. It's a burn of cold from outside, off a skin, the same as frost off a gate. It wants grease and wet linen and a fortnight. Trewin looked at him for a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, as though nodding were a thing that had to be done properly.

"You'd sooner know," said Aske. "So would I. So would she." He got up. "Miss Hebden. The linen's to be changed every quarter of an hour till dark, and you're not to stand on them till Friday, and you're to eat something. I'll come at six."

"Nothing, tonight. It's red; it's evidence; it's tagged. Nobody can use it." Kellan put his hands back in his pockets. "I don't know what it does with it next year. I don't know what it does with it if next year it's ten-tenths." He was quiet. "I'm not saying anything's wrong. I'm saying I did the sum, and it came out, and I didn't like the number. I'd expect nothing else of a sum. I thought somebody should know it besides me."

He went back to the clerk's table and stood there, reading upside down.

Noa was at the mast.

The mast stood up black above the timber pond as it had stood every day for a fortnight, forty feet, with its drum and its eight pens. Nobody had stood on its crosses since Monday morning. Noa's desk was at its foot, and Polglase's beside it, and the two readers were standing at them in the last of the light with their tapes unrolled.

Kiva went over. She had not meant to. She found that she had.

"Rowan," said Noa. He had his pencil behind his ear. He looked at her bandaged hand and then, carefully, not at it. "I heard."

"Everybody's heard."

"Yes." He looked down at his tape. "I'd like to show you something. I don't know what it is. I want to show it to somebody who was on the Longshore."

He put his finger on the tape.

It was a day's tape off the mast, a long white strip, with eight lines running along it from the eight pens, flat, because nobody had been on the crosses. The lines ran flat all along it, hour after hour. And at one place, near the middle, there was a mark across them. It was no taller than a hair is wide. It went across all eight lines, top to bottom.

"Twelve forty-four," said Noa. "By the mast clock. All eight pens. A hair." He took his finger away. "Nobody was on the mast. Nobody's been on it since Monday. There's no cliff for it to come after. It's just there."

"There's a hair on this mast most days at the springs," said Polglase, beside him, without looking up from his own tape, which had the same mark at the same place. "I've said so. It's the harbour."

"It's the neap," said Noa.

Polglase did not answer that. He took his spectacles off and polished them.

Kiva looked at the hair on the tape. Twelve forty-four.

"The stow turned at a quarter to one," she said. "On Mallow Creek. The lower one. The Tarn's. Perrin had the glass and the watch, and he wrote it down. A quarter to one, or a minute before." She heard her own voice, quite level. "It turned before the water. The water turned at nine minutes to one. Seven minutes after. My fingers had said nothing the whole time, and the grass stood up straight."

Noa looked at her. Then he took his pencil from behind his ear and wrote in the ledger on his desk's ledge, in his narrow upright hand. Kiva watched him write.

*The 16th. A hair on all pens at 12.44 by the mast clock. Nobody on the mast. At Mallow Creek, by T7's captain's watch, a stow turned at 12.44 or 12.45, seven minutes before the water (Rowan, K., fingers on a pole; the grass). N.B.*

He did not draw a line between them. He did not write *because.* He left a space under it.

"I don't know what that is," he said. "I'm not going to say I do."

"I know you're not."

"Mr. Polglase says it's the harbour."

"Mr. Polglase has been reading this mast for forty years," said Polglase, putting his spectacles back on. "He's begun to look." He rolled his tape. "He'd like that noted as well."

It was a quarter to seven. Kiva took her left hand out of her pocket, the warm one, the unbandaged one, and laid the first two fingers on the iron leg of Noa's reading desk where it stood on the boards over the black water of the timber pond. Lightly. Without leaning.

*It keeps time.*

She wrote it in the back of the book with the pencil in her left hand, badly, because her right was in linen. *Wednesday the 16th. A quarter to seven. The leg of the reader's desk, the gate. Yes.*

Anwen Pryce climbed Tar Lane at seven with tar on her boots and her hands in her sleeves, and did not know what she was going to say.

The Ropewalk was lit. Its windows went away along the ridge in a long line of yellow squares, as they had the evening she had first come up, and the doors at the near end stood open. Inside, the boards were bare. There were no children tonight. There was one lamp hung from the beam over the near end, and under it, on the bench against the shed's wall, Maud Lyle was sitting by herself with a towel in her hands.

On the boards in front of her, Juniper Carrow was walking.

Anwen stopped at the door.

Carrow was walking up and down the near end of the shed, slowly, ten paces and back, in her shirtsleeves, with her edge lit on her right fist. Only her edge. Anwen could feel it. There was no post going into the ground under her, no stance; her feet came up off the boards at every step like anybody's feet. She walked ten paces, and turned, and walked back, and at every step her edge wavered on her fist the way a candle wavers when somebody opens a door.

"It's loud," said Lyle, from the bench, without looking round. "That's what she's finding. I told her." She had seen Anwen in the doorway. "Come in, Pryce. She'll not stop for you."

Anwen came in. She sat down on the bench, a little way from Lyle, and did not say anything.

They watched Carrow walk.

"She shut her stance at five," said Lyle. "In front of the whole school, at the gate, after they combed us. Treen asked her what she was doing and she said, *I'm learning to declare.*" Lyle's square careful face did not change. "She'll declare edge at the Lane. Only edge. On a twelve-pace floor, against people who've watched her lay since the first month. Treen says she'll be nothing. Treen's probably right."

"Why?"

"She called boom." Lyle turned the towel over in her hands. "She says she's called boom a hundred times and never once thought about what she was calling. She says she called *laid,* without saying it, because she'd never once called anything else. She says she'll not stand on a floor again with two doors open together until she knows what it's calling." She was quiet for a moment. "She says it's not because it was wrong. She says she doesn't know whether it was wrong. She says that's worse."

Carrow turned at the end of her ten paces. Her edge wavered and steadied. She did not look at the bench.

Anwen looked at Lyle's hands on the towel.

"Can I see?" she said. "I'm not asking to be kind. I'd want to know. If it were me."

Lyle looked at her. Then she put the towel down on the bench and stood up.

She opened her stance. Anwen felt it go into the boards, a post driven into the ridge under the Ropewalk, solid, sure, the same as it had been in the lane and at the lock. Then Lyle lifted her right hand to her hip, where a ward comes up.

Nothing came up.

She held her hand there. Anwen watched it. It was a Hold's hand, broad, steady, held at the hip with the palm up, the way Anwen held her own a hundred times a day. There was nothing on it. There was nothing under it. There was no tray, no wall, no silver, not even the hesitation of a door that was shut and might open. There was a hand, at a hip, in the lamplight.

"There," said Lyle. "That's all."

She shut her stance. She sat down again and picked up the towel.

"It's quiet," she said. "I always said laid was the quietest thing you'd ever feel. Two hands on a rope. You stop having to think about either." She folded the towel. "It isn't. This is. There's one hand on the rope now, and I can't stop thinking about the other one."

Anwen sat with her own hands in her sleeves.

She had thought, all the way up Tar Lane, that she would not say *I told you.* She had decided it on the causeway, and again at the bottom of the lane, and again at the door. It had been easy to decide. It was harder to sit on a bench beside the girl she had told, twice, and not have it in her mouth.

It was not in her mouth. She found that it was not there at all.

"I want it said that I'm sorry," said Anwen. "That's all I came to say. I'm sorry."

"I know you are." Lyle did not look at her. "You told me. Twice. I heard you both times. I'd have done the same tomorrow." She was quiet. "Forty drams going down the pill onto the flats, wet, red. They'd have gone anyway. Nobody's going to net them. I held a boom for forty drams of red ash that nobody can use, and I'd do it again, and I don't know why."

"Because it was coming down the pill," said Anwen. "And you were the boom."

Lyle looked at her then.

"Carrow said I was the best wall on the coast," she said. "I said I wasn't. I said I was the best laid wall on the coast, and if you took my feet out of the floor I'd be a wall like anybody's." She almost smiled. It did not get as far as her mouth. "They took the wall. I've got my feet. I'm a floor now, like anybody's. Less, maybe."

Anwen looked at the boards. At Carrow, walking. At her own heels.

"You leaned well," said Lyle. "In the first lane, against us. You leaned from the floor; your heels were right. I said so." She put the towel round her neck. "Will you show me? How you lean from the floor. When you've nothing on your hands. I've a floor left. I'd like to be good with it."

"Yes," said Anwen. "Tomorrow. Before the Lane."

"Tomorrow." Lyle nodded. "Thank you, Pryce."

At the end of her ten paces Carrow stopped, and turned, and shut her edge. She stood on the bare boards in the lamplight with her hands at her sides and nothing open at all, and she looked at the bench for the first time.

"Gapper," she said to Anwen. Not unkindly. As a fact about the weather. "How long did it take you? To stop hearing it. One door."

"I've never stopped," said Anwen. "That's how you can hear it. That's how you know it's there."

Carrow considered that for a long moment, with her quick blunt face giving nothing away. Then she lit her edge again, alone, on her right fist, and began to walk.

Orla Dane sat on a hard chair in the corner of the Board's printer's shop on Quay Street at a quarter past ten, with her stick across her knees, and watched Dinah Coombe take a page off a hook.

The printer had come down in his shirt, with a candle, grumbling. He had opened his door to the Board's Marshal because she had knocked on it as if it were on fire. Now he stood behind his counter in the smell of ink and oil and paper with the candle in one hand and his other hand flat on the counter, and he said, for the third time, "The Board said after the Measure, ma'am. In writing. The Clerk's own hand."

"I know what the Board said," said Coombe. "I'm the Board's Marshal. I'm saying now."

"The Clerk—"

"I've seen the Clerk. At eight, at her tent." Coombe laid the page on the counter. "She said the Board's printer takes the Marshal's word for the Marshal's page. She said it took her thirty years in a great many rooms to say a thing like that, and she said it." She smoothed the page with the flat of her hand. "I want it set tonight. I want it on every officer's beat on the Longshore by six in the morning, and on every van in the Company's yard, and in every catcher's pocket in this town who can read."

Orla had walked with her from the causeway. She had not been asked. She had come out of the healers' tent at half past seven and found the Marshal standing on the causeway with her rope in her hands, looking at Mallow Creek under the flood, and she had stood beside her, and when Coombe turned to walk back to the town she had walked with her, a step at a time, on her stick. Neither of them had said anything for a mile.

The printer looked at the page. He held the candle to it.

"*On a Thing That Folds from the Far Edge,*" he read. "*The Marshal's page.*" He looked up. "It's only one page, ma'am."

"It's one page. It's been one page since the second month." Coombe took a pencil out of her coat. "I'm changing one line."

She bent over the counter and wrote. Orla could not see what. She saw Coombe's hand move across the head of the page, slowly, in the candlelight, and stop, and write again.

"Read it back," said Coombe.

The printer read it. "*After D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company, at Mallow Creek, the 16th of the third month, 2681, who went in by the book.*" He stopped. He put the candle down. "Davy Penhale?"

"Davy Penhale."

"I knew his father." The printer looked at the page for a long time. "I printed his father's notice. The Company's. Ten years gone." He took the page off the counter. "I'll set it tonight, ma'am. I'll set it myself. You'll have it by five."

He went into the back with the candle and the page, and they heard him begin to move type in the dark.

Coombe stood at the counter with her hands on it. She did not sit down. Orla watched her from the hard chair. She had known Dinah Coombe by name for twenty years and by sight for a fortnight, and she had never once seen her hands still on her rope. They were still now, flat on the counter.

"You held it back," said Orla. "The page. In case it was wrong."

"I did."

"It wasn't wrong."

"I didn't know that." Coombe did not turn round. "I'd one man on the Longshore who called a hem ground and laid on it. He's a lock-keeper. I wrote a page. It said make the thing carry its sheet uphill, and if that fails, weight on the far edge, and the far edge to the second, as a place. I'd seen it work twice. Once on a wharf, from a slate, and once on a frozen yard in Meridian with a bedsheet." She was quiet. "Twice is not a page. I thought. I gave it to the printer and said hold it till the Measure's done, and if it's wrong, nobody's read it."

Orla looked at her stick.

"I held a card a season," she said. "From a boy I put on a Bench twenty years ago, and never wrote to after. I didn't show it to anybody, in case I was wrong about him, and in case I was right." She did not look up. "The girl found him in a week. By walking down a quay."

Coombe turned her head.

"It's not the same," said Orla. "I'm not saying it's the same. I'm saying that holding a thing back in case it's wrong is a thing I know about. That's all." She put her hand on the stick. "Your page wasn't wrong. You didn't know. Now you've put his name at the top, and it's going out at five, and somebody will read it on a creek tomorrow who'd not have read it otherwise."

"He'd have read it," said Coombe. "He said so on the causeway this morning, in the queue, to anybody who'd listen. *When it's printed, I'll read it. I'm not one for reading.*" Her hands were still on the counter. "That's what I can't get past. He was going to read it."

Orla did not answer that. There was no answer to it. She sat on the hard chair in the printer's shop with her bad knee out straight and listened to type being set in the dark, a letter at a time, small hard clicks, like somebody counting.

After a while Coombe took her hands off the counter and picked up her rope. She began, slowly, to tie a knot in it.

Mara Rowan stood on the bank gallery of the harbour pump house at half past ten, with Peverell beside her and Hart behind, and took her reading hand out of her pocket.

The thirty-six cells stood along the two galleries, eighteen a side, in their tall glass cases. Their lights turned. They turned together, every one of them, at the same pace, four and four, fifty-seven to the minute, and the six iron pumps below went up and down on the same stroke, and the whole hall hummed with it like a held note. Mara had stood in this hall every day for a fortnight. She had not once put her hand on a case.

She held it out now. She held it an inch from the glass of the first cell on the north gallery, where the light inside turned slow and yellow-white, and she did not lay it on.

Peverell watched her. She had her spectacles on their cord and her apron on, and her small crabbed hands folded on the rail. Hart stood a pace behind with his watch on the inside of his wrist and his bad hand in his jacket.

"A Company man," said Peverell. "On the Longshore. Davy Penhale." She said it quite flatly. "I knew his mother. She takes in washing in Ropery Row, below the high-water line. Her kitchen's dry because of these." She did not look at the cells. "His mother's kitchen."

"I know," said Mara.

She held her hand an inch from the glass. She could feel the count in it, through the air, faint. Four and four. She could feel what her hand would tell her if she laid it on. She had felt it on twenty-four cells on a hill and seven drums on another, and a trunk of drums under a city, and she knew what it would cost and how long it would take to come back.

"I'll not," she said.

Hart did not say anything. Peverell turned her head.

"I've been deciding for a fortnight," said Mara. "Whether to touch one. Rusk has asked me twice. You've asked me with your face every morning. Jessamy said on Monday night that somebody would have to, before the 23rd, or we'd be standing on the landing watching a ring we'd written down." She did not take her hand away from the glass. "He's right. Somebody will have to. Somebody will have to touch all thirty-six of them at once, on one word, at nine minutes past six, with the water still. If anybody's going to do it at all."

"Then touch one," said Peverell. "Now. And tell me what it says."

"If I touch one," said Mara, "then I'm the one who reads them. Then on the 23rd there are thirty-six cells and one hand, and the hand's mine, and it's been starved once already, and if it goes on the third cell, or the tenth, there's nobody." She looked at her hand, an inch from the glass. "That's what this bank is. One engine. Thirty-six cells and nobody can do anything alone. I'll not make the thing that takes it out of step another one engine, with my hand for the count."

"Then who reads it?"

"Thirty-six hands," said Mara. "Not mine. Your fitters. Six of the Works'. Rusk's men, if he'll send them, and he will, because I'll ask him tonight. The Company's closing crews, if Yeo will lend them, and she'll lend them now." She took her hand away from the glass at last and put it, not in her pocket, but on the gallery rail, flat. "I'll teach them. Every one, a cell each, by the 22nd. Jessamy will teach them when the water's still; his hand knows it and mine doesn't. You'll teach them your cells. And on the 23rd I'll stand on that landing with a watch and say *now,* and thirty-six people who aren't me will put their hands on thirty-six cases."

Peverell looked at her for a long time.

"I'll not be the keeper who stopped the pumps," she said.

"You'll be the keeper whose fitters read them," said Mara. "That's a different line in your book."

Peverell said nothing. Then she took a pencil out of her apron pocket and a fold of the pump house's fault paper, and she began, slowly, in her small crabbed hand, to write names.

Hart had not moved. Mara turned her head and looked at him.

"Well," she said. "You've been quiet."

He turned his watch on his wrist, once.

"Thirty-six," he said. "On one word. With the water still." He looked at the cells. "That's the right number. I'd not have said it. I'd have said one good hand." He was quiet a moment. "I've one hand that reads and one that doesn't, and the one that reads is the one that's ruined. I'll teach your thirty-six when the water's still. I'll teach them what quiet feels like." He looked at her. "You've decided."

"I've decided."

"Then I'll write it down," said Hart, and took his book of slacks out of his jacket with his left hand.

Ivey looked up.

"The stow. The Tarn's. It turned on its post at a quarter to one. The water turned at nine minutes to. Seven minutes after." Kiva made her voice plain. She did not want it written. She wanted it said, once, to somebody who could use it. "I had my fingers on a purse pole. They tell me the slack; they tell me nothing at the slack and something when it ends. They said nothing the whole seven minutes. The grass stood up straight. The water was still. And the stow turned anyway, as if it had heard the turn coming before the water had."

"Things like that don't hear," said Ivey.

"That's what Penhale said. At half past ten, when the upper one leaned before a gust." Kiva looked at the sheets on the counter. "I don't know that it heard. I know it moved first. So on the coast the far edge isn't when the tide turns. It's when the thing decides to. Whoever has the far edge can't watch the water for it. They have to watch the thing. The far end of it. All the way through the slack, even when the slack's holding." She was quiet for a moment. "And a stow's a sleeve. It's got two ends. The book's for a sheet."

Ivey did not say anything for a long time. She looked at the sheet on the handed crossing, and at Kiva's bandaged hand on the counter, and at the lantern.

"I turned him over," she said at last. "On the mud. I'd my hands on him for the count of three fathoms. I wasn't reading anything. I just had my hands on him." She put one square dark finger on the column headed *Before.* "He'd have been in this column. If anybody had written him. *A Company catcher, reserve, who goes to the near edge by the book.*"

"Coombe said to put him there," said Kiva. "In the first column. On the spur."

"Then put him there." Ivey squared the sheets together. She did it carefully, the way Penhale had squared a purse's iron ring into the mud. "I'll copy these tonight, if you'll let me. All of them. And I'll read them to Yeo's crew at six, before the printer's boy comes round with the Marshal's page. And at the next Hunt, I'll have a second." She tied the tape. "I've never had one. Nobody on this coast has. I'll have one. She'll have the far end of the thing, and she'll say it once."

"It's a place," said Kiva. "Not a person."

"I know. It's in your column." Ivey picked up the lantern. "He'd have liked that. He'd have laughed. *Nobody,* he said to me this morning on the spur. *She's got a thing called nobody. Nobody passes anything to nobody on this coast; she'd put half the Company out of a job.*" Her face did something, and stopped. "He laughed so hard a curlew got up."

"I heard it."

"I'll bring them back by the morning," said Ivey. "Thank you, Rowan."

She went out into Quay Street with the sheets under her coat and the lantern swinging, and Kiva stood in the dark shop and listened to her boots go away toward the low town, until there was nothing to hear but the gratings in the gutters rattling on the count.

---

Kiva went up the stair at half past eleven.

The loft was dark except for the candle on the sill by the north window. Aske had gone to his own bed. Perrin was asleep on his back with his hand on his ribs, breathing short. Pru was asleep at last, with her capped hands on the blanket. Anwen's bed was empty; she had not come back from Tar Lane. Jude was at the window.

He was standing where he always stood, with his good arm on the frame, looking out at the harbour. Kiva went and stood beside him. Below them the Long Quay's white lamps burned in a row along the black water without a flicker, all the way to the Counting House. Beyond the Mole, the sea was breathing, every count at once.

"Yeo was at the board," said Jude. "At nine. Behind us. I saw her." He did not turn his head. "She didn't say anything. She looked at me and she didn't say anything."

"She will."

"I know she will. I'd want it said that I know." He was quiet a long time. "I asked everybody since the autumn what it's for. The ash. I asked a Marshal and a foreman and my own four. I asked Yeo on the Ropewalk steps. I asked the sea last night, from the quay." He looked at the dark water. "Today I carried a man up a bank on a board who'd counted fathoms to a girl under a skin to keep her still. That's what it's for. I think. Not the ash. The bank. Carrying it up the bank." He stopped. "I don't know. I'd want it said I don't know. I'll stay anyway. When it's done. If she asks."

Kiva did not answer. There was nothing to say that would not have been smaller than what he had said. She stood beside him at the window.

After a while he went to bed.

She took her book out with her left hand and sat on the edge of her own bed by the candle. She opened it to the back, where the clean pages were, past the stub where she had torn one out on Monday for Pru. She ruled a fresh sheet into seven columns, badly, with the pencil in her left hand, because her right was in linen. At the head of the first she wrote the word that went there.

*Before.*

Under it, in the first line, she wrote what Coombe had told her to write, and what Ivey had told her to put there, and what she would have put there herself without being told.

*D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company. Reserve to the officer. Went to the near edge by the book. Counted fathoms under it to keep a girl still. Stopped at three.*

She looked at it for a long time. She did not cross anything out.

Then, though there had been no crossing and the fourth column was owed nothing, she did a thing she had no need to do. She took the disk out of her coat on its cord and laid it on the page, on the line she had just written, and looked at it in the candlelight.

It was cold.

She had known it would be. It had been cold through every crossing since the first, and through three doors and a fever and a Glory master's table, and it was cold now. She did not write that down. There was no column for it tonight. She put the disk back inside her coat.

Down the room, Aske turned over in his bed and said, without waking, or perhaps not, "Thursday. Soonest."

Kiva blew out the candle.

Outside the window, under the white lamps and the yellow ones and the dark, the town went on keeping its count. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster. On the Longshore the flood was at the top of its neap, quiet, over the flats and the creeks and Mallow Creek, over the place where a stow had stood and a man had knelt. In a shop on Quay Street a printer was setting a page, a letter at a time, with a dead man's name at the top of it. In a pump house a keeper was writing thirty-six names on fault paper. In a long tarred shed on the ridge, a girl with one door was walking up and down by lamplight, ten paces and back, learning to hear it.

The Measure went on. In the morning there would be the Lane.

She lay down in the dark with her bandaged hand on her chest and her warm hand at her side, and the first two fingers of it, without her meaning them to, came to rest on the iron rail of the bed.

*It keeps time.*

It was not a quarter to seven. It was not on purpose. She did not write it down.
