# Chapter 36 — Kiva: The Three Sheets

Kellan Renn did the sum for the third time at four on Friday afternoon, on the back of a Halden claims slip, at the corner table of the Halden technical office on the second floor of the Counting House, and it came out the same.

He did not need to do it again. He had done it on Wednesday night standing at the gate, reading a ledger upside down over a clerk's shoulder. He had done it on Thursday morning at the comb table, with the Board's own tallies open in front of him, which anybody might read who asked. He had done it on Thursday night in the loft by the south window with a candle, while the rest of Halden License slept and Seven's beds at the north end were empty because Seven was down in Quiller's shop with the door shut. It came out the same every time. A sum did.

The office was a long room with eight desks under eight windows, and lamps at every desk, white and steady. Through the floor, faintly, he could feel the house's count. He had stopped noticing it in the eighth month. He noticed it now.

Pettit was standing at the end of the table with the card in his hand.

"Counsel asked me to give it you," said Pettit. "In person. He said you'd want it from your captain and not from a clerk." He laid it on the table, face up, beside the claims slip. "It's the one on the board. With a line on the back that isn't on the board."

Kellan had read the front on Thursday at the baize. *The Halden Foundation, technical office. Renn, K., Fire, blade, Meridian, Halden License. Junior assessor, on the Proprietor's roll, at a Fire's wage, from the autumn. A card is not a seat.* It was the best card on the board. Hesketh had said so at breakfast, not quite kindly. It was better than anything Kellan's mother had ever been offered in thirty years of grading other people's ash.

He turned it over.

*On the understanding that Mr. Renn's figures, if the Board calls for them, are given through the foundation's counsel, as the foundation's.*

That was all. It was in counsel's hand, which was small and very neat and had never once been unkind to anybody on paper.

"He's not wrong," said Pettit. He said it heavily. "That's the trouble. I read it twice on the stair. He's not asking you to lie. He's not asking you to leave anything out. He's asking you to say it with the house's name on it and not yours, because you read the house's own clerk's ledger to get half of it, and the house is in front of the Board tonight asking for its claim, and a house doesn't send its own blade in to say a thing against it on his own card." Pettit put his hands in his pockets. "If you were counsel, you'd ask the same."

"I know I would."

"Then?"

Kellan looked at the claims slip, where the sum was, in his own figures.

*Combed at the gate, 1st to 16th: 1,106 tins, jars and bags. Seventh reed sang: 1,102. Silent: 4. The Hunt, the 16th: 412.4 dr. combed off the Longshore. Red: 371.3. Clean: 41.1, every dram of it dry. Nine-tenths.* And under it, in a column of its own: *Claimed under the patent of 2391, by the house's own index. 2391–2600, all from its own yards: 109 dr. 2680, Meridian district alone, outside its yards: 1,175.1. The Measure to date: 371.3.*

And at the foot, the line he had written on Wednesday night and not liked.

*If the claim is good and the comb is right: every dram killed in this country, but the dry tenth.*

"If I give it through counsel," he said, "counsel will read it. He'll read every figure, because he's honest. And then he'll stand up and say what the figures mean, and he'll say they mean the house's claim is large. He'll not say they mean the claim is the whole country. He'll not have to. He'll leave the last line off, because it isn't a figure. It's what the figures come to." Kellan put his finger on the last line. "That's the line nobody else in that hall can say. Bexley won't, because it's a line between two columns. Adlam won't, because it's the house's patent and he's the house's clerk. Counsel won't, because it's his job not to." He took his finger away. "I did the sum. It's mine to say what it comes to."

Pettit was quiet for a long moment.

"You'll lose the card," he said.

"I'll lose the card."

"You might lose the yard. Tuesdays and Thursdays."

"I might."

"And you might lose me," said Pettit, not loudly. "I'm captain of a four that's on the house's licence, standing for the house's name, and tomorrow I've a lane with the National Measure on it, against whoever the bag gives us, and tonight my blade stands up in a hall and tells the Board that the house wants the whole country's ash." He looked at Kellan. "I'll not stop you. I'd not. I'm saying I'll have to think about it, after. That's all."

"I know you will."

Somebody had come up the office stair. Kellan heard the step and knew it before he looked, because it was the step of a man who always seemed to have just been told something surprising and had decided to take it calmly.

Mr. Adlam stood in the office door with his hat in his hand and a black tin box under his arm.

"Renn," he said. "Pettit." His long face had its astonished look, which Kellan had learned in the autumn was not astonishment at all. "I've been to counsel. I've told him I'm reading the bench books tonight myself. All of them. 2402, 2431, 2604, the ninth month, and Saturday's." He shifted the tin box under his arm. "He asked me not to. He asked me very civilly, and I said no very civilly, and we both felt better." He looked at the card on the table, face down, with counsel's line showing. "I'll not tell you what to do. I've no right. I came up to say that I'll be in the same hall reading figures against the house's interest under the house's own name, and that if you read yours under yours, you'll not be the only one."

"Why?" said Kellan.

Adlam considered it. He seemed to think it was a fair question.

"Because I've always said the patent should be defended truthfully or not at all," he said. "And this week I found I meant it. It's an uncomfortable thing to find at my age." He put his hat on. "Seven o'clock. I'll be on the second bench from the front, on the left, if you want somebody to look at."

He went down the stair.

Kellan picked up the card. He turned it over to the front, and read it once more, *junior assessor, on the Proprietor's roll, at a Fire's wage,* and he thought of his mother at her grading bench at home with her glass in her eye, signing *A. Renn, grader* at the foot of other people's tins. Then he turned it back over, to counsel's line, and took his pencil, and wrote under it in his own small square hand.

*No. I'll give my figures under my own name, and the house may have them after. With thanks. K. Renn.*

He gave it to Pettit.

"Pin it back up," he said. "Face to the board. Like Cade's."

Pettit took it. He looked at it, and at Kellan, for a long moment, with his heavy face quite still.

"Cade wrote *with thanks* on his as well," he said. "I saw it at the baize." He put the card in his pocket. "You're all of you mad, the lot of you on that book. I'd expect nothing else." He went to the door, and stopped there, and did not turn round. "Read it clearly. If you're going to cost us the card, I'll not have you mumble."

The Salt Hall had been built to keep salt dry, and at seven on Friday evening it was full of people who had come to watch a thing that was not a board.

The six banks were gone from the floor. The Board's carpenters had taken them out on Tuesday and swept the flags, and now there was a long deal table across the floor at the harbour end, under the load board, which hung dark, every needle lying against its pin. Three chairs stood behind the table. In front of it, in rows, stood the benches that had been carried down from the gallery, and on the benches sat everybody who had a reason to be there. Up in the gallery, on the iron columns, sat everybody who had not.

Kiva had come at twenty to seven. She had gone up the gallery stair before anybody else was there, to the bench at the rail where she had sat on Monday with Aske, and she had laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron of the rail, lightly, without leaning, at a quarter to seven by the clock over the great door.

*It keeps time.*

Everything in this town said *yes.* She had written it anyway, with her left hand. *Friday the 18th. A quarter to seven. The rail of the Salt Hall's gallery. Yes.* Then she had gone down the stair again to the floor, because she did not mean to sit in the gallery tonight.

Now she sat on the third bench from the front, on the right, between Mara and Perrin. Anwen was beyond Perrin. Jude and Pru were on the bench behind, and Aske at the very back by the door, with his bag on his knees, where he had said he would be.

The hall filled round them.

Halden's counsel sat on the front bench on the left, a small grey neat man with a folder on his knees and the Halden mark on its cover. Mr. Adlam sat behind him, on the second bench, with the black tin box on the bench beside him, and Kellan beside the box in his black and gold, very upright. Pettit and the rest of Halden License were three benches further back. The Harbour Committee had the front bench on the right: six men and one woman in good coats, with a chairman in the middle who was a ship's chandler, Jude said, and who held a gold watch open in his palm and looked at it every few minutes as though it might run away.

Peverell sat at the end of the Committee's bench, apart, with her spectacles on their cord and her apron off for once, and her small crabbed hands folded on a book in her lap. Hart sat behind her. He had his book of slacks in his jacket and his bad hand on his knee, and he was not looking at anybody.

Orla sat on the far end of the fourth bench with her stick across her knees. She was not looking at anybody either. She was very carefully not looking at the back of Hart's head.

Yeo was there, and Ivey beside her with her square dark hands in her lap. Coombe was there with her rope. Rusk was there with his thumbs in his waistcoat. Noa sat at a small table of his own beside the long one, with a roll of paper and his ledger, and Polglase on a stool behind him. On the other side, at another small table, sat a woman in plain grey with her hair cut short as a boy's and her hands folded at her waist, whom Kiva had last seen at the gate of a stone house on a bluff over a river.

The sister. The Order's.

"She came on Wednesday's boat," said Mara, very low. "She's been at the Works' lodging asking for me every day. I've been at the pump house." She did not look at the sister. "She's not said what for."

Selka Dray sat on the end of the Meridian bench, behind Halden, on two feet, with her face like a shut door. The Mercury's young man sat at the very end of the front row with three pencils.

At seven exactly, the Clerk of the Measure came out of a side door and walked down the hall between the benches to the long table, and the two Board members came after her and sat on either side. One was a heavy grey man with spectacles pushed up on his forehead. The other was a thin woman with ink on her cuff. The Clerk did not sit. She stood behind the middle chair with her gold chain on her coat, the smallest thing in the hall, and when she spoke, everybody heard her.

"The Charter Board sits," she said, "on three sheets that came to it in the autumn under the seal of the Placement Chair at Meridian, with Mr. Adlam's name second and a line of Officer Yeo's. The Board took them up at its sitting on the 1st. It has not answered them. It will answer what it can tonight." She looked at the Harbour Committee's bench. "The Harbour Committee has asked the Board by four letters whether it will stop the harbour bank on Wednesday. The Committee wants its answer by midnight, so that it may go to the magistrate in the morning if it doesn't like it. The Board will rise at ten. The Committee will have its answer before that." She glanced at the clock over the great door. "Three hours. The Chair."

Ysra Crest stood up from a chair set by itself at the end of the long table.

Kiva had seen the Chair perhaps a dozen times in her life, and every time the Chair had been sitting behind a desk with a page in front of her and a pen beside it. She had never seen her stand in a hall. She was a lean woman in a plain dark coat, older than Kiva remembered, with grey hair pulled back and the level eyes of somebody who had held a ward on a Bench in 2660 and had never since needed to raise her voice.

"Three sheets," said the Chair. "Mr. Bexley's columns. People: what the class of 2680 measured, against forty years. Rifts and spawn: how many tears, of what kind, since the thaw. And Saltmere: a bank that went into step at twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month, in the same minute as four husks stood a square in a pit at Meridian." She did not look at a paper. "I sent them because a Circuit can't ask a nation to look at its banks. A Chair can ask who else has a keeper with a book. I asked. Mr. Bexley has the returns."

She sat down. It had taken less than a minute.

Noa stood up at his little table. He unrolled his sheet and held it up, and two of the Board's clerks came and took the corners and held it high for the hall.

There were four columns on it now. Kiva saw the hall see that. People, and rifts and spawn, and Saltmere, in his small square hand, as they had been on the Rowan kitchen table in the autumn; and a fourth, newer, ruled in fresher ink. *Ash. What the comb says.* Along the bottom there was one rule for the weeks. Above it four lines went up, each in its own column, slowly from the thaw and then faster from the fifth month, and there was not a mark between any of them.

"Four," said Noa. "I've added ash. How much of every month's yield sang on the seventh reed, from every comb on the Board's roll that would send me its book." His voice was thin and very clear. "They go up together. They went up faster from the fifth month of last year. I've drawn them on one sheet with one rule along the bottom, and I've drawn nothing between them."

"Why not?" said the heavy Board member.

"Because I don't know that anything goes between them, sir. I drew a line between two things once that I'd added together, and it was wrong for four months." Noa did not look down. "If the Board wants a line, the Board can draw it. I'll not."

The heavy man looked at him over his pushed-up spectacles for a long moment. Then he wrote something, and nodded.

"The returns," said the Clerk.

Noa read them. He read the eight *nothing found* first, quickly, by house, and the hall shifted on its benches. Then he read Westerley. *At three the stepped lamps on the quay white. Town lamps out half hour. Keeper ill of a fever, his wife sat up. He said in the morning the roof was off. Not fault.* The hall went quiet. He read Tarnfoot. *The four drums stood up together a little after three and lay down.* Then he read Holloway.

*Night lamp-man took ill that evening, a fever two nights. Asked by the warden what he saw in it. Would not have it written. Said he would be laughed at. A fever is not a fault.*

Somebody in the gallery laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of somebody who had been holding his breath for a long time and let it out in the wrong place. It stopped at once. But it had been heard, by everybody in the hall, and Kiva felt it go through her as the edge had gone through her shoulder that morning, flat and light and exactly where it was aimed.

She did not look up at the gallery. She looked at Noa, who had stopped reading and was standing with the yellow form in his hand.

"That's the return," said Noa, after a moment. "I've read it as it came. I'll not add to it." He sat down.

The Clerk let the silence stand for a count of five. Then she looked at the left-hand front bench.

"The foundation," she said.

Halden's counsel stood. He did not hurry. He laid his folder on the bench, and opened it, and took out one sheet, and held it as a man holds a thing he has read so often that he no longer needs to look at it.

"The foundation's patent of 2391," he said. His voice was pleasant and dry. "*All ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.* The foundation has entered that claim against every red bag combed at this Measure, as it entered it against the red rows at Meridian last year, and as it's entered it since 2391, quietly, in its own index, and nobody's said it can't. The Compact's court has the Weighbridge bag before it and hasn't ruled." He laid the sheet down. "The foundation asks the Board tonight to note the claim against the Hunt's bags in its own record. It asks nothing else. It doesn't ask to have the ash. Red ash can't be used. It asks only that the claim be written down where the Board writes things, so that when the court rules, the record is there."

It was reasonable. Kiva heard the hall hear that it was reasonable. The heavy Board member nodded slowly, and the thin woman wrote.

"Mr. Adlam," said the Clerk. "You're the foundation's clerk. You've a box."

Adlam stood up, with the black tin box in both hands.

Halden's counsel did not turn round. But Kiva saw his shoulders change. They did not move. They went still, as a man's shoulders go still when he hears a step behind him that he had asked not to hear.

"The foundation's bench books," said Adlam. He opened the box on the bench in front of him. Inside it there were five books, old and new, in calf and in cloth, with straws in them. "The technical office keeps a count of every stepped row that's fallen in on a bench in this house, and the rate it fell in at. A man with a watch writes it down. He's always written it down. Nobody's ever asked him for it." He took out the first book, which was very old, and opened it at its straw. "2402. *Fell in at forty-eight to the minute.*" He laid it down and took out the next. "2431. *Forty-eight.*" The next. "2604. *Fifty.*" The next, which was cloth and not calf. "The ninth month of last year. *Fifty-five.*" The last, which was a clerk's notebook with a pencil still in its spine. "Saturday the 5th of this month, at the door of the harbour pump house, by my own watch. The harbour bank. *Fifty-seven.*"

He put the notebook down on top of the others.

"The foundation patented a count," he said. "In 2391. It didn't make the count. It found it, in ash that already kept it, and learned to give it to cells. Before the patent the Wren House called it counting ash." His long face was quite calm. "The count isn't the foundation's. It's got quicker. It's got quicker faster since the ninth month. I don't know why. I'd not have brought these if I'd known why; I'd have brought the why." He looked at the back of counsel's head. "I've always said the patent should be defended truthfully or not at all. These are true. I'll leave them on the table."

He carried the box to the long table and set it down in front of the Clerk, and went back to his bench, and sat down beside Kellan.

The hall was very quiet. The chairman of the Harbour Committee looked at his gold watch.

"Mr. Renn," said the Clerk. "You wrote to the Board on Thursday. You said you had figures."

Kellan stood up.

Kiva had heard Kellan do a great many sums aloud. She had heard him do them at the Rowan kitchen table and in the Old Yard and on a frozen tram road at three in the morning. She had never heard him do one in a hall full of strangers with his own house's counsel sitting six feet in front of him, and she found that she was holding her breath.

He did not hold a paper. He had it in his head.

"The Board's own tallies," he said. "Anybody may read them. Combed at the gate from the 1st to the 16th, one thousand one hundred and six tins, jars and bags. The seventh reed sang on one thousand one hundred and two. It was silent on four." He did not look at Seven's bench. "One of the four was a team's sealed medical, given by the Order. One was a jar of the Tarn's that had been cut with soot. One was a Westerley healer's tin from before the thaw. One was the Board's own reference jar."

The thin Board member had stopped writing and was looking at him.

"The Hunt," said Kellan. "Four hundred and twelve drams and four-tenths combed off the Longshore on Wednesday. Three hundred and seventy-one and three-tenths red. Forty-one and a tenth clean. Every dram of the clean was dry. Nine-tenths." He paused. "That's the Board's. This is the house's." He did look at counsel's back then, once. "I stood at the gate on Wednesday and read the claims clerk's ledger over his shoulder. He's a good clerk. He was doing his work. Every red tag in the Hunt, *claimed, under the patent of 2391.* Three hundred and seventy-one drams and three-tenths. And the house's own index, which I've read at the yard on Tuesdays, because I've the yard on Tuesdays and nobody's ever told me not to read the index." He was quite white. "From 2391 to 2600, the house claimed a hundred and nine drams of ash exhibiting the Step. All of it from its own yards. Every grain of it the house had stepped itself. In 2680, in the district of Meridian alone, outside its yards, the house claimed one thousand one hundred and seventy-five drams and a tenth. Off tears in the street. Off a pit. Off a sluice."

He stopped.

"Those are the figures," said the Clerk. "Is there more?"

"There's what they come to," said Kellan. "It isn't a figure. I'll say it, because I did the sum, and nobody else here will." He did not raise his voice. "If the claim's good, and the comb's right, then on this week's figures the house has a claim on every dram of ash killed in this country, except the tenth that happens to be dry when it's bagged. And if Mr. Bexley's fourth column goes on the way it's going, by next spring it's every dram." He took a breath. "I'm not saying the house wants that. I've sat at the Proprietor's table four times and I don't think he wants it. I'm saying it's what the patent says, now. It didn't say it in 2391, because in 2391 ash didn't keep time unless somebody taught it. Now everything keeps time." He looked at the Clerk. "I'm on the house's licence. I'm on its yard. I was on its card until four this afternoon. I'd like that written next to the figures, so that nobody thinks I'd a grudge."

He sat down. Adlam, beside him, did not look at him. But Kiva saw the old man's hand move on the bench, an inch, and stop near Kellan's, and stay there, not touching, as Rhea's hand had stayed near Pike's on a tailboard.

Halden's counsel stood up.

He stood for some time without saying anything. Then he picked up the single sheet off the bench, the patent's line, and looked at it, and put it back in his folder.

"The foundation withdraws its request," he said. "For the present. It'll not ask the Board to note the claim against the Hunt's bags tonight." His voice was as pleasant and dry as before. "It'll put its case to the Compact's court, where it belongs, with the bench books, which are true, and Mr. Renn's figures, which are also true, and which I'd have read myself, more slowly." He sat down. Then, without turning round, he said quite clearly, "Well done, Renn."

Kiva looked along the benches at Pettit. He was sitting three rows back with his arms folded and his heavy face quite still, and he did not look at Kellan, and he did not look away from him either.

The chairman of the Harbour Committee shut his gold watch with a snap.

"It's eight," he said. "With respect to the foundation, and to Mr. Renn, and to the young man's lines, which are very nicely drawn, the Committee didn't come here to hear about ash." He stood up. "It came to hear whether a reader from Meridian is going to stop the pumps that keep the low town dry, on Wednesday, on the strength of a paper in a box and a fitter's hand." He turned toward the end of his own bench. "Mrs. Peverell. It's your house."

Peverell did not stand up at once. She sat with her hands folded on the book in her lap. Then she stood, and took her spectacles off their cord and put them on, and looked at the chairman through them.

"It's my house," she said. "Nobody's stopping my pumps."

The chairman began to speak. She went on over him, not loudly.

"My pumps will run on Wednesday. They'll stop for eleven minutes at the slack, by my hand, at my word, and start again on my word, each on its own eight cells, because that's what the method says, and I've read it. I've written thirty-six names on my fault paper. My own isn't on it." She did not look at Mara. "If the bank comes out, it'll be because thirty-six people put their hands on thirty-six cases on a word, and I'll write that in seven columns. If it doesn't, it'll be found wrong, under seal, and I'll write that too." She took her spectacles off. "I'll not be the keeper who stopped the pumps. I'll be the keeper who let it be tried. That's a different line in my book."

She sat down.

"Eleven minutes," said the chairman. "On a spring tide. With the low town under the sea."

"A foot of water in Ropery Row," said Peverell, without standing. "For eleven minutes. I've told them to move the coal up a stair. They've been moving the coal up a stair every spring since before you were born, Mr. Chairman, every time a gale took a pump. They know how."

Somebody in the gallery laughed again. This time it was a different laugh.

"And the fitter's hand," said the chairman. He had not sat down. "Since we're to stake the low town on it."

Hart stood up behind Peverell.

He did not take his book of slacks out. He stood with his bad hand at his side, the right one, the fingers half curled, and his good hand on the back of the bench in front of him, and he looked at the chairman of the Harbour Committee, and then at the Board.

"Jessamy Hart," he said. "Fitter, the harbour pump house. Nineteen years." His voice was quiet and level, like a gauge. "I'll say it's ruined first, before anybody else does. It was turned out at the Bench in 2660. It can't hold a door. It aches near anything that keeps time, and in this town that's everything." He did not lift it. "It's quiet at the slack. That's the one thing it says now. At the neaps the quiet walks with the sea gate's table, twenty-three minutes behind. At the springs, at dawn, since the ninth month, it doesn't walk. It comes near a quarter past six, whatever the sea's doing." He paused. "I don't know why. I've written it every morning in a book. Mrs. Rowan's read the book. The prediction in the Clerk's press is off the book."

"So it's a fitter's book and a fitter's hand," said the chairman.

"It's a fitter's book and a ruined hand," said Hart. "The hand's the one that reads. The good one never did." He sat down.

Kiva did not look along the bench at Orla. She did not need to. She heard Orla's stick, which had been lying across her knees, move very slightly on the wood.

Mara stood up at a quarter past eight.

Kiva had known she would. She had known since the shop door at Coper Street, and the knock, and the two wires folded into an apron pocket. She had not known what it would be like to sit on a bench in a hall full of strangers and watch her aunt stand up.

Mara did not take anything out. She stood with her reading hand at her side and her other hand on the bench back, and she looked at the Board.

"Mara Rowan," she said. "Grade Two. Reader to the Municipal Works, Meridian." Her voice was dry and exact and carried to the gallery without trying. "On Wednesday the 23rd, at nine minutes past six by the pump house clock, if the basin's still as Mr. Hart's hand says it will be, I mean to take the Saltmere harbour bank out of step. With thirty-six hands that aren't mine, at a word from the landing. Each cell quenched, and filled with a fresh can salted with a jar of plain, and given a count of its own, not two alike in the house. Each pump restarted on its own eight cells, by the keeper." She did not pause. "I'll not touch a case. I've a reading hand that's been starved once, and I'll not make the thing that takes that bank out of step into another one engine, with my hand for the count."

"And if you're wrong?" said the heavy Board member.

"Then the low town's wet for eleven minutes, the bank goes back in, and it's on my name." Mara's face did not change. "Not the Works'. Mr. Rusk's told me the Works doesn't believe anything that hasn't happened, and he's right not to. Not the keeper's. Not Mr. Hart's. Mine." She looked at the Committee's bench. "I'd like that written down, so that the Committee has a name to go to the magistrate with, if it wants one."

The chairman opened his mouth, and shut it.

"There's one thing more," said Mara. "It needs thirty-six jars of plain ash. Ash that's never heard a beat. One to a cell. There isn't any in Saltmere. There's none in Meridian. The soot-cut stock's spoilt, and every tin that came through this Board's gate sang on the seventh, but four." She looked at Kellan, briefly. "Mr. Renn's said so. Without thirty-six jars there's no Wednesday. I'd sooner the Board knew that tonight than found it out at six in the morning on the landing."

"One of the four," said the chairman of the Harbour Committee, at once, "is Meridian's. That team's. Their sealed tin. Two drams of plain." He turned on his bench and looked along the rows to Seven. "I read it in the Board's tally myself. If the town's to be wet for eleven minutes, the town might ask what's in their tin."

Every head in the hall turned.

Perrin stood up. He did it in two stages, with his hand pressed to his side, and Kiva saw what it cost him, and saw Aske at the back half rise and sit down again.

"Perrin Cade," he said. "Captain. Team Seven. It's under my name." His voice cracked a little and he did not seem to care. "It's for a hand. It's written in two books, under two names, and one of the books is the Order's. *For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.* Mr. Adlam offered us twelve drams for it last week and took no for an answer. I'm sorry, sir. It's for a hand. If a hand's hurt on Wednesday that plain will ease, it's there. It's not for a bank."

"It's two drams against a town."

"It's two drams against a word," said Perrin. "We gave it in a courtyard. I'd sooner the low town were wet than that word was."

He sat down. His face was scarlet to the hair.

The sister of the Order stood up at her small table.

She had not moved since she sat down. She stood now with her hands folded at her waist, in plain grey, with her hair cut short as a boy's, and the hall turned to her as it had turned to Perrin, and went very quiet.

"The Order of the Even Hand," she said. Her voice was low and quite calm. "That tin is ours as well as theirs. They've kept our word. The Master will be glad to hear it, and not surprised." She looked at the Board. "The Master has had Mrs. Rowan's wire of the 12th. The ferryman carried it up to the gate. It asked for thirty-six jars. The Master doesn't answer such a thing by wire. She left the house on Wednesday by the ferry, and she's on the coast boat now, and she'll be in Saltmere tomorrow at five. She'll speak for the Order's cellar herself." The sister paused. "She's not been past the house's gate in eleven years. I'd ask the Board to remember that, when it decides where it would like her to stand."

She sat down.

The hall did not make any sound at all for a long moment. Then everybody began to talk at once, in the gallery and on the benches, and the Mercury's young man broke the point of his first pencil and took up his second.

Mara had sat down beside Kiva. Her reading hand was flat on her knee. Kiva looked at it and saw that it was quite steady, and that her aunt's face was white.

"You didn't tell me," said Kiva, under the noise.

"I didn't know. I wired the ferry office for her on the 12th and had nothing back." Mara did not turn her head. "I thought she'd say no. I'd have said no, in her place." She was quiet a moment. "I said it on my name. The low town. I'd not thought it would feel like that."

"What did it feel like?"

"Like standing on a landing with a watch," said Mara, "and the whole sea on the other side of a door."

The Clerk let the noise go on for a count of ten. Then she said, "The Board," without raising her voice, and the hall stopped.

"The Harbour Committee will have its answer now," she said. "It's twenty to nine." She looked at the chairman. "The Board does not stop banks. It does not keep them. It has no power over the pumps of the Saltmere harbour pump house, and it'll not pretend to one tonight to please the Committee or to frighten it. The keeper keeps her house." She did not look at Peverell. "If the keeper lets a thing be tried in her house on Wednesday, the Board will want it written down in seven columns, and it will read them. If the Committee goes to the magistrate in the morning, the magistrate will find the same thing in the keeper's own book, which is older than the Committee." She folded her hands. "That's the answer. It's the only one the Board has."

The chairman sat down. He did not open his watch again.

"The plain ash," said the Clerk, "the Board will hear when the Master of the Order's here to speak for it, and not before. The Board's not going to decide what's to be done with another house's cellar in that house's absence." She glanced at the clock. "The three sheets. The Board has heard the returns, and the bench books, and Mr. Renn's figures, and Mr. Bexley won't draw a line." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, and was gone. "The Board will consider what it's heard. It will—"

Kiva stood up.

She had not decided to do it at that word. She had decided on the boards of the timber pond the night before, between the mast and the wall. But she found that her body had chosen the moment, as it chose a moment on a floor, and that she was standing on the third bench from the front with her bound right hand against her coat and the whole of the Salt Hall turning to look at her, and that her voice had already started.

"Ma'am," she said. "Before the Board considers. I've a thing to put on the table."

The Clerk looked at her. Her small grey face did not change at all.

"Name," she said.

"Kiva Rowan. Team Seven, Meridian. Catcher." She heard her own voice go up the stone walls into the gallery, flat and clear, and let it. "The returns that Mr. Bexley read. The keeper at Westerley, who said the roof was off. The keeper at Ferren in 2604, whose book Mrs. Rowan has sent for, and the keeper here, on the same night. Master Annick, in her notebook at the Library, who called it the sky unroofed. And the lamp-man at Holloway, who wouldn't have it written, because he'd be laughed at."

Somebody in the gallery drew in his breath.

"I've had it," said Kiva. "With a fever. At thirteen, when I was Kindled, and on Classification Day, and on the ferry below the Order's house last month. The same thing. No roof, and nothing over, and the nothing paying attention." Her hands were hot. She did not look at them. "I'm not saying what it is. I don't know. Nobody does. I'm saying I've had it, under my own name, in this hall."

The hall did not make a sound.

"Anybody can say that," said Kiva. "A girl can read a notebook in a library and come out of a fever saying anything. That's why my aunt wrote mine down, the same night, last spring, in my own words, and sealed it, and had the Reader at the Compact Library put her wax over it with the hour written through the join, before I'd ever opened Master Annick's box." She made herself go on. "It's the only page in the country that can't have been borrowed. It's in the Library's press. It comes out to my aunt's hand. She sent for it last night. It'll be here on the transit tomorrow night." She looked at the Clerk. "I'd like the Board to open it."

She stopped. She found she had nothing else to say, and that she was still standing, and that her knees had not decided anything yet.

The Clerk of the Measure looked at her for a long time.

At the back of the hall, Aske had stood up, and was standing by the door with his bag in his hand, and was not coming forward. Beside her, Perrin had put his hand on the bench beside her boot, flat, not touching. Anwen had stood up as well. Kiva did not know when. She was standing at Perrin's shoulder with her hands out of her sleeves, at her sides, saying nothing, being there.

"Sit down, Miss Rowan," said the Clerk.

Kiva sat.

"The Board has had a great many things put on its table in thirty years," said the Clerk. "In a great many rooms. It's not often had a sealed page offered to it by the person whose page it is, before anybody asked." She turned her head and spoke to the thin woman with ink on her cuff, very low, and the woman nodded. "The Board will open it. Not tonight, and not on a Saturday night, in a dock, with the Measure being read. The Board will sit again on Sunday, at two, in the Graving, where there's room, because there's going to be a great deal of town." She looked at Mara. "Mrs. Rowan. The page comes to your hand, you said."

"To my hand or a magistrate's," said Mara. "The Reader's bringing it. And Master Annick's box, in her own custody, if the Board will ask for it. And the Ferren keeper's book for 2604."

"The Board asks for all three," said the Clerk. "Write it down."

The thin woman wrote it down.

The side door of the hall opened. A boy in Halden's livery came in, very quickly, and walked down between the benches to the long table, and gave the Clerk a folded paper, and went out again. The Clerk unfolded it. She read it standing, with her small grey face giving nothing away. Kiva saw that the paper was thick, and cream-coloured, and that the hand on it was large and upright and old.

She looked along the second bench on the left. Mr. Adlam's seat beside Kellan was empty. She had not seen him go.

The Clerk read the paper a second time. Then she folded it and laid it on the table in front of her, under her hand.

"The Board has a letter," she said, "received at four minutes to nine, from Mr. Ambrose Halden, the Proprietor of the Halden Foundation. It's in his own hand." She did not unfold it again. "He asks to be heard by the Board on the matter Miss Rowan has put on the table, under his own name. He says he was a bench clerk in the Counting House on the night of the 11th of the third month, 2604, and he's the last person living who was in this town that night." The Clerk paused. "He says he can't come to the Board. He's not lifted his hand from his glass case for longer than a minute since 2611, and he'll not begin now. He asks the Board to come to him."

The hall was so quiet that Kiva could hear the harbour against the Long Quay's granite through the stone wall behind the load board, and under it, faint, the town's count.

"The Board will go," said the Clerk. "On Sunday, at nine, to the Counting House. Miss Rowan, Mrs. Rowan, the Chair, and the foundation's counsel will come with it, and nobody else, because the room's not large. What's said there will be read in the Graving at two." She took her hand off the letter. "The Board rises."

---

The Long Quay was full of people when they came out, and every one of them turned to look.

Kiva walked between Perrin and Anwen with her head up and her bound hand against her coat. She did not look at anybody. She felt them look at her, in green and in grey and in the colours of six circuits, under the white lamps that did not flicker. She felt it as a weight on the side of her face, and her neck, and the backs of her hands. It was not unkind. It was worse than unkind. It was the way people look at a thing that has been sent for.

Perrin had said it would be like that, in the shop. He had been right.

Somebody fell into step on her left. It was Selka Dray, on two feet, with her face like a shut door, walking at Kiva's pace as though she happened to be going the same way.

"You'll not cross this week," said Dray. "Your healer said so at the window. I heard."

"No."

"And you've just stood up in a hall and told the country you dream." Dray did not turn her head. "And tomorrow you're in a lane. Against whoever the lot gives you."

"Yes."

"The Clerk's put the draw back to nine, for the sitting," said Dray. "I'll be at the pole." She walked three more paces. "That was declared. What you did in there. All of it, out loud, before anybody asked." Her flat voice did not change. "I've not changed my mind about you. I'd want you to know I saw it."

She turned off toward the gate at the Graving's end of the quay, where the draw would go up, and did not look back.

At Quiller's door, Mara stopped. She had walked the length of the Long Quay without a word, with her reading hand in her coat pocket. Now she took it out and looked at it under the white lamp, as she had looked at it on a gallery at the pump house a fortnight ago.

"I said it on my name," she said. "And then you stood up and said yours."

"I said I would."

"I know. I heard you knock." Mara put her hand back in her pocket. "I watched you do it. I sat on a bench and watched you, and I couldn't put a hand on it, and I didn't." She looked at Kiva under the lamp, and her dry exact face did something that Kiva had not seen it do since she was thirteen and lying in the dirt of a yard. "That was the hardest thing I've done this year. Harder than the low town. Go to bed. You've a lane in the morning."

She went away along the quay toward the Works' lodging.

Kiva stood at the foot of Quiller's stair. Down the quay, at the end of the timber pond, the clerk was standing on his crate under the lamp at the tent pole with the canvas bag in his hands, drawing Saturday's lanes out of it by lot, one at a time, in front of a crowd that had gone very still.
