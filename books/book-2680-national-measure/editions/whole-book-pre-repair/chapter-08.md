# Chapter 8 — Kiva: Under a Captain

Ruddock had cut the cord out of his fist on Saturday, a thread at a time, with a pair of nail scissors.

It lay on the bench under the window on Sunday afternoon, in eleven short pieces, beside nine ruled sheets and a laundry ticket. Pike had asked for it to be kept.

"I waxed that in the tenth month," he said. "I'd like to know that it finished as a thing with ends."

He was in the chair by the stove, with his arms laid along a pillow, bound white to the elbow; he held them a little away from himself, as one holds a pair of boots that belong to somebody else. Greta Stroud had gone to her dinner, and Pru sat on the box in her place, with the cup, and was not allowed to say that she minded.

"You've your paper," said Pike.

"Yes."

"Then you'll write. I can't, and I've a thing or two that I'd sooner were in my words than in Sorn's." He moved his head against the chair. "You found the one that you'd missed."

Kiva had found it on the wharf, at about the fourteenth count, and had not known that she had until she lay awake afterward.

"Your hands never go inside the cloth."

"Never."

"Not on any of the three at the kiln. You work the neck with your knee and the cord. You touch the outside of it, to hear whether it's loud. You don't put a finger past the hem."

"Not in six-and-twenty years. I couldn't have told you so on Tuesday week. I'd have said that I caught as anybody catches." He looked at the stove. "A cloth's got open ash in it. Whatever's in there with the ash takes what the ash takes. My first master had three fingers on his left hand, and he never told me how he came to be short. He only never let me reach."

"Shall I write that?"

"Write that the catcher's hands stay outside. Under *why,* put that anything inside a cloth with open ash belongs to whatever the ash is listening to." He waited while she did it. "Now the next column. *Went wrong.*"

She had the pencil on the line, and she did not want to write it.

"He reached," said Pike. "Two words. I'll spell them if you like."

"I was a count late."

"So you've said. So Sorn's said, on a slate, and taken it for hers." He was not gentle about it. He had never been gentle, and he did not begin now. "I'll tell you what a count's worth. I looked up at the fourteenth and it was a pace nearer. If you'd spoken at the thirteenth, I'd have looked up and it would have been a pace farther off. And I'd have had my corners clear, and thought, there's my rung running to a string, and gone in after it just the same."

"You don't know that."

"I know what I did with the time that I had. I'd no more of an answer at thirteen than at fourteen. The book had run out." He drew a breath. "You spent a count keeping my rule. I'd have spent it. Don't make a wound of it. I've two already, and I'll not be put to the trouble of minding a third."

Pru made a small sound into the cup.

Kiva wrote it. *Went wrong: he reached.*

It was the plainest line on the sheet, and it looked like a meter reading.

"And *why,*" said Pike. "That's mine, and I've not got it. I've been at it since four yesterday morning. *A catcher doesn't leave a rung.* That's what I'd have told a corner." He shook his head. "It's not a why. It's what I did. Put that I don't know. Put that the book stopped, and that I went on without it as though it hadn't."

She put that.

"Now a fresh sheet."

She ruled one on the last of the Works paper that her aunt had given her in the autumn.

"What's it called?"

"Ruddock's named it. A purse string."

"Then that's the first column. *A purse string. At Meridian, on the coal wharf below the Hythe.*" He shut his eyes. "*What.* A string on end that lays its skin on the ground behind it, for a cloth. It is not hem. It lies under a rung, and it folds the rung home from the far edge. Have you that?"

"Yes."

"*How.* That's how it's taken, and it's not been taken properly yet; we did it once, badly, with coal. Dead weight along the far edge before a cloth goes near it. Not a ward. Not iron. As much as will lie shoulder to shoulder. Then the cloth, over all. Then somebody at the left hem who has the far edge and nothing else, and says so before the cut, and has it said back."

"That's on the Torch's slate."

"I know where it is. I was looking at it for an hour last night; she's hung it where I can't help." He opened his eyes. "*Cost.* A catcher's hands, for six weeks, if Ruddock can count. Thirteen sacks. And a rung and a quarter that was sealed outside its sixty and will weigh short."

"*Second try.*"

"The weight. And I'll have it written whose that was. I'll not have it go about the Hythe that Graywater thought of coal."

"It was the Torch who thought of coal."

"It was a laundress who thought of a foot on a hem. Sorn only looked round a wharf for the heaviest foot that she could find." He nearly smiled. "Put her name."

So it went into the sixth column of a chartered company's craft, in a catcher's second's square hand. *Mrs. Pryce, laundress, Coper Street: a sheet with a foot on its far hem does not fold for anybody.*

"She'll want it struck out," said Kiva. "She says that it's only folding."

"Then she and I shall get on."

There was one square left, at the right.

"I can't fill that," said Pike. "Why it lays a skin down. Why a thing with nobody in it should do what Finch does with a ward. I've no more notion than that stove has."

"Nor have I."

"Then leave it white. I've seen a deal of old lane sheets in my time, and it's the ones with every square filled that I never believed."

She left it white.

She squared the ten sheets on her knee. They were not hers: they were a man's whole trade, as far as she had been able to see it from a hem, and the last of them had been made up on a wharf, the night before last, by a Torch and a laundress and a boy with a barrow. It was the first thing that she had ever written down from beginning to end that had nothing whatever to do with her own hands.

She found that she did not miss them in it.

"The second month," said Pike. "Two nights. Imrie's lending Varley for the cloth; he's a decent pair of hands and he's never been made to say why. I ride. You kneel at my left, which will be his left, and you've the far edge." He looked at her along his nose. "And you'll still not open your mouth. Bar two words."

"Once."

"Once. I read the slate." He shifted his arms on the pillow, and his face went grey, and came back. "Finch. That cup's been empty for ten minutes. You're holding it at me like a collection plate."

"I wasn't going to say."

"No. That's why I'm telling you."

---

They were six at the Rowan kitchen table on Monday evening, and there was not a chair for the sixth.

Jude stood by the dresser. He had always stood there. Pru had the stool. Kellan had come up the shop stair last, in his own coat, and had taken the place with its back to the window.

Kiva told them the wharf.

She told it as a slate is read. She gave them the ground and the gate and the white patch, and a heel put on it twice. She gave them the heap that leaned straight down. She did not tell them about two fingers on a canvas, since that had proved nothing; but she told them that she had been a count late, and why.

Nobody interrupted. Mara was at the far end of the room, with the tide table across the dresser top, and she did not turn round.

"I'll say what's ours in it," said Kiva. "That was the best crew in Meridian. It had a catcher of six-and-twenty years. He put his boot on the thing and called it ground, because the book does." She looked along the table. "If it had been us on that wharf, Pru would have thrown on it. I'd have called the corners at *one;* I always have. Kellan would have cut on my word. We'd have had somebody's hands in it by the twentieth count."

"Mine," said Pru. "It'd have been mine."

"And we're going to hunt," said Anwen. "On purpose. On tidal ground, with a marshal's comb at the gate."

"Mace has told his line," said Kiva. "Nobody's to stand a wall on a white patch until it's been weighted. Sorn's written it into her *before* column. That's one company. I don't know who's told Saltmere."

"Then we train for it," said Perrin.

"On what?" said Jude.

"I don't know on what. On the thing."

"There isn't a thing. There's one string in a cage in this city, and it's the Works', and it's never laid so much as a handkerchief." He scratched his jaw. "I'm asking, since I'd sooner know. What in Meridian folds from the far edge and won't take your fingers off?"

There was a short silence.

"A sheet," said Anwen slowly.

"I was being serious."

"So am I. I've folded ten thousand of them. There's a drying yard behind my mother's scullery, forty feet by twenty, and a double that's been turned sides-to-middle so often that she'll not put it on a bed." She had the look that she wore when a wall had no thin place in it. "Somebody throws a cloth on it. Somebody drops Gerald. And two people take it by the far corners and bring it home, as fast as they can walk, and whoever's got a hand inside loses the bag."

"That's a game," said Kellan.

"So was *post, left* until a hill had to be cleared with it." She did not look at him. "I want it said that I know it's a sheet. It doesn't frost. It doesn't keep time. But it comes from where nobody's looking, and it's the only one that we've got."

"The Marshal has floors," said Perrin. "In the second month. She's a Blaze. She'll have the real thing."

"At a dram a head," said Kellan.

He had his pencil out. He had been waiting, Kiva thought, for somebody to say a number, so that he could be in the room.

"I'll do it once, aloud, and then it's done. We hold twenty-four and sixty-four hundredths. The warrant's three a head. Five heads: Rowan, Pryce, Cade, Talley, and Finch for the reserve. Fifteen." He wrote it on the oilcloth, in a column, upside down to himself so that they could read it. "That leaves nine and sixty-four."

"That's not nothing," said Jude.

"It's not. Now the Marshal. Four heads on a floor, at a dram. If she gives each four one floor, that's four drams. I asked Bexley, and Bexley asked the clerk, and it's one floor a four; she's six circuits to get round." He wrote. "Five and sixty-four."

"Go on," said Anwen. "There's more. You've got your *more* face."

"Miss Dane's asked for six goes. Hand to Ember. She says that a catcher who can only cross away from her own ward has learned the wrong half of it, and she wants the other certified before the gate." He glanced at Kiva. "A crossing's three-tenths. Rowan's thimble is half a dram, and it won't stand a crossing and a floor in the same week; we found that in the summer. So if she's to have it, it's the book's. Six at three-tenths. One and eight-tenths."

"I've not asked for that," said Kiva.

"No. I'm only saying where it would come from." He drew a line under the column. "Three drams and eighty-four hundredths. That's what this team has to its name, on the twenty-fifth day of the first month, with a board called the Hunt in front of it. I've not put down a call reserve. I've not put down a cord, or a lamp, or what a purse sheet costs when a string's frosted it."

"I said in the tenth month that we'd something over," said Jude. "You said so. You said that you'd never had a figure come out that way."

"I had it for three months. I was rather fond of it."

"And the sealed?"

"Eight-tenths. It can't be made up and it can't be spent. I've left it off; it isn't ours to count. It's somebody's hand that hasn't been hurt yet."

Perrin had been adding on his fingers, under the table.

"Then the hunt in the hills has to pay," he said. "Before we go. Or we're at the gate with a cord and a clean conscience."

"What hunt in the hills?"

"There's got to be one. It's in the small print. A four with a Flask license in its own right hunts on its own entry. We've a second step. We haven't the license. Somebody's going to have to take us out and let us find a thing." He looked round. "I read it. I can read."

Nobody had an answer to that either. It lay on the oilcloth with the figures.

"There's a line that I didn't read out," said Kellan.

He had put the pencil down. He laid both hands flat on the table, on either side of his column, and Kiva saw that his ears had gone white already.

"A week ago Friday. I read you the conditions and I stopped at one of them. *A candidate stands for one four only, declared at entry.*" He was looking at the oilcloth. "I've five heads in that sum. In the tenth month I said five and I meant myself."

"We noticed," said Anwen.

"I thought that you had." He drew a breath. "I'm standing for Halden License. I told Birch on Thursday. He'll have my name tomorrow, on the line that says *blade.* I've come to say it here first, at this table, since I'd not have you read it off a board."

The kitchen was very quiet. Kiva heard her aunt's pencil stop.

It was Perrin who spoke. He had gone a dull red.

"You declared for us."

"I did."

"In front of eleven hundred people. Under rule nineteen. You took that jacket off on a struck floor and walked across to us, and I thought, well, there's Renn, he's done the one thing that I'd never have had the nerve for."

"I'd do it again. If it were the four or the yard, I'd do it tomorrow morning. I wrote that to Birch in the autumn and it's still so." Kellan's voice was quite level. It had the levelness of something being carried carefully. "It isn't the four or the yard. Nobody's asked me to wear anything. I'm on this book for calls, if you'll leave me on it. If a bell rings in Meridian, I'm yours. It's six days at Saltmere."

"What's behind it?" said Anwen.

"The Works pays its own warrant. That's three drams that this book needn't find."

"That's a sum. I asked what was behind it."

"It's a true sum."

"I know that it's true. You'd not bring me a false one." She had the team book under her hand, shut. "I want it said, Kellan. Not what it saves. What you want."

He was silent for so long that Kiva thought he would not.

"I want to know what I am," he said.

He said it to the table. It came out without any shine on it at all.

"I'm the best blade in the Third Division. I've been told so since I was fourteen. At this table I'm the one whom everybody looks at when a thing wants cutting, and I've liked that a good deal more than I've let on." His hands had not moved. "That four was first at the Regional. It'll go to Saltmere as one of the three or four in the country that could win. I want to stand in it and find out whether I'm the weak corner. I'll never learn that here. Here I'm asked."

"That's it?" said Perrin.

"That's all of it. I looked for something better. I spent a week looking." He lifted his eyes, at last, and they went to Jude. "And you'll not be worse at the cut. I've seen you on a tenth knot. You come down heavy, and there's work at a hunt for something that comes down heavy."

Jude had his thumb against his jaw.

"I asked to be asked," he said. "In the seventh month. Three things, on a slip." He was in no hurry. "I've a fourth. If I'm the Kill on that entry, I'm not on it by name and a head's share. I'm on the book. I sign under the others, and when it's short I'm short with it."

"That's the wrong way to ask for a rise," said Kellan.

"I know which way it is."

"Write it," said Anwen. She had opened the book. "*Talley, J., Kill. On the book, from the entry.* I'd have asked you in the autumn. I thought that you liked the door."

"I liked being able to see it. I've stopped looking."

She wrote. Then she put the pen down, and looked at Kellan, and Kiva saw her decide what she thought.

"I mind," said Anwen. "I'll say that, since Cade's only gone red. I minded a card from Sorn with one name on it, in the spring, and I was wrong about that, and I may be wrong now. But you asked a catcher *now?* over your shoulder, and it took you a season to learn, and you're taking it to a floor where the catcher lays a cloth by the book and a bank cooks."

"I ask Yarrow."

"Does Yarrow know?"

"Not always." Something moved in his face. "No. That's the part that I shall miss. I'd sooner you knew that I know it."

Kiva had not spoken.

She had been turning over what she felt, as she turned over a stance before she stood on it. She had wanted to be asked. She had stood at a board on a Friday and been ashamed of how much. And he had not asked her, or any of them. He had gone and found out by himself, on a blue brick floor, what he wanted, and had come and said it in a kitchen with his ears white.

It was what she had done with a card from the south coast. She had not asked anybody what a stranger might read.

"It's yours," she said. "I wanted you to ask me. I'd have told you to stay, and it would have been for me. I'm glad you didn't."

"I thought of it."

"I know. You've been sulking for a fortnight."

"That was thinking."

"It looked a good deal like sulking from the bench," said Jude, and for the first time that evening somebody laughed. It was Pru. She clapped her hand over her mouth.

Kellan did not laugh. He turned back his left cuff instead, where there were two figures in pencil that Kiva could not read from her side of the table.

"There's a thing that I owe this book," he said. "I've had it since Thursday. I didn't know whom I'd be saying it to." He turned the cuff toward them. "Nine rungs on the license yard since the new year. Nine red tags. It's a stepped floor; there's a bank under the brick. Whatever falls on it lies open for sixty counts with that under it, and comes up loud. Halden doesn't care. It doesn't tin what falls there."

"The Hunt's marked on clean," said Anwen.

"The Hunt's marked on clean. And that four has never bagged a rung anywhere else." He buttoned the cuff. "I've not said it to Birch. I shall have to. I thought that you might as well hear what I'm walking into."

Mara had turned round.

She did not say anything. She looked at a boy of seventeen with two figures on his cuff, as she might have looked at a reading that she had not asked for and could use. Then she turned back to her tide table.

---

"*A captain shall be a classified candidate of the four, in a declared role,*" said Anwen.

She had the Board's sheet. Noa had copied it for her, fair, in his small square hand, and she had it flat beside the book.

"*The captain is the one voice that a referee or a marshal hears. The captain declares every role and every change, and calls a withdrawal.*" She looked up. "Rowan's not classified. Talley's on the book as of ten minutes ago, and a clerk would laugh. Finch is the reserve. Renn's gone."

"I've not gone," said Kellan. "I'm sitting here."

"You're a candidate of another four. Hush." She laid her finger on the line. "That leaves two."

"It's you," said Perrin promptly. "It's obviously you. You've the book, and the wax, and a face like a shut ledger. A referee would take one look."

"It's you."

"It is *not.*"

"*The one voice that a marshal hears.* I'm in a wall. I look at the curb. I've stood forty paces of Mace's line now, and I can tell you where every holder's boots are and nothing whatever about what's in front of us; that's what a wall is." Her voice was exact. "*Calls a withdrawal.* That's *off.* It's been yours since the seventh month. You said *no* to six-tenths, and *yes, with a brake,* and you kept the word on a hill from the end of an ash pole with two ribs out. The Board's only given it a hat."

"I can't be a captain. I mind things."

"That's what one's for," said Jude.

"I stand on marks for people! I carry old men in chairs! I was a *clock!*" Perrin had risen, and found that there was nowhere in the Rowan kitchen for a person of his size to pace, and sat down again. "A captain's somebody who knows what to do. I've never once known what to do. I only know when a thing ought to stop."

"You held a mark for me," said Kiva. "A fortnight ago, with dirt down your neck. Nobody asked you. You said *test it under me,* and it was the one thing all afternoon that Brask hadn't got written down."

"That was only being heavy in a useful place."

"You thought of it. I didn't. It was my own notion, off a litter, and I'd not seen that it would go."

He looked at her helplessly.

"And you'll have to say every role and every change," said Pru. She had gone pink. "Aloud. To a marshal. Rowan's one door, before each board. You'd be the one who says it for her. I'd sooner it was somebody who'd mind getting it wrong."

That finished him. Kiva saw it go home.

"I'd like it known," said Perrin, with great bitterness, "that I didn't want it. I'd like it written on the entry."

"One can't write that on an entry."

"Then in the book."

Anwen wrote it. She read it out as she did so, since there was nobody at that table who could have borne her not to.

"*Captain, for the National entry: Cade, P., carry. Chosen by the four, the reserve and the Kill. He minds. Entered at his own request.*"

"That's handsome," said Perrin. "I've a place in my Hall's second team, and a figure in a stone, and now I've a hat." He put his head in his hands. "I got everything that I wanted in the autumn. I'd no notion that it would be so much work."

Jude had come away from the dresser.

He did not sit. There was no chair. He stood at the end of the table with his long forearms folded, and he looked at Kiva.

"I asked a thing in the tenth month," he said. "On a cold floor. You said that if you'd no better answer by the time there was an entry to sign, I was to ask again. At the table."

"I did."

"There's an entry. What are we taking you for?"

She had known that he would. She had kept the place for him.

It was a strange thing to have ready, since it was so small. In the tenth month she had put away an answer about three doors, on a hill, at a price. She still had it. It was still true. It was also not what she had been for, on any day since.

"I'm a catcher with one door," said Kiva. "Declared. That's what goes on the sheet, and it's a rank with a clause in it, and I'll be read from a mast. I can plant a corner. I can put a ward under a thing for a tap. I can't do both, and I'll say which before every board."

"That's what you are. I asked what for."

"I look where the others aren't looking. And I say what it is, and whose." She found that she could say it without her mouth going dry. "A thumb. A place over a wall where nobody had the litter. My own habit, that Brask was reading like a signpost. The far edge of a cloth. I don't mend any of them. I can't. I've a hand that doesn't know a thread from two. I only say it so that somebody else can."

"It's not much."

"No. There's a slate at Graywater with a line on it. That's all it comes to."

Jude scratched his jaw.

"It's four things from where there was nothing to do," he said. "I've been counting. And there's a first-year with ten fingers." He nodded, once, as a man nods who has been paid. "I'll tell a clerk that. I'd not have known what to tell him in the tenth month."

"I want it said," said Anwen, "that that's an answer."

She did not write it. She only shut the book.

"You've all been a great while," said Mara, from the dresser, without turning round. "There's bread. Somebody will have to cut it. I've a reading to finish, and my niece chases a loaf."

---

The ash office weighed fifteen drams out of Team Seven's press on Friday, at four, and it took the clerk a quarter of an hour.

He did it in five lots, a head at a time, into five sealed tins with the Charter Board's label. He held the Circuit's comb over each. The seventh reed stirred, and touched its neighbor, and came away.

"Trace," he said, five times. "Clean. You'd be astonished what I've had across this counter for a warrant."

"Nobody's astonished any longer," said Perrin. "There's a man at the Works who's given it up."

He was standing very upright, in the grey jersey. He had been a captain since Monday. He had asked the clerk what he was to do, and had been told that he was to sign, and had seemed to feel that there ought to have been more.

The entry lay on the counter. It was a printed sheet with a seal at the head, and somebody had written it out fair.

*T7, of the Meridian Collegiate Circuit. Captain: Cade, P., carry. Catcher: Rowan, K. Hold: Pryce, A. Kill: Talley, J. Reserve: Finch, P., catcher.*

They signed under it. Kiva signed second, with her right hand, and Pru put a finger on the corner of the sheet for her without being asked.

"*Friday the 29th,*" said Kellan.

He had the team book. He had asked for it at the foot of the stairs. *I'm on it for calls,* he had said. *I can still add.*

"*Warrant, the National Measure, at Saltmere. Five heads at 3.0, the reserve included. Paid with the entry: 15.0. Held: 9.64. Sealed, medical, W: 0.8.*" He did not look up. "*Spoken for, as at the table: 5.8. Uncommitted: 3.84.*"

"That adds," said Anwen.

"It always adds." He signed it, under four names and a new fifth. He blotted his own. "It'll be somebody else's to read from tomorrow. I'd give it to Talley. He counts everything and trusts none of it."

"I'd sooner have had the something over," said Jude. But he took the book.

There was a second sheet pinned to the board outside, beside theirs. It was in black and gold at the head.

*Halden License. Captain: Pettit, carry. Catcher: Yarrow. Hold: Hesketh. Blade: Renn, K.*

Kiva read it once. It was a very ordinary line. It had a name in it that she had been used to seeing somewhere else.

"The Chair's countersigned yours," said Noa.

He had come down the passage with a paste pot. He put up a third sheet, under the two, in a clerk's fair copy of a level upright hand.

*Entered by the Chair. Rowan, K., catcher, T7. Her conditions for 2681, as for 2680, re-entered line by line.*

*One door declared to the referee or the marshal before every board, and any change declared; at Saltmere, by her captain's voice. Her book to her sponsor weekly. She is not at the Bench. She is read from a sanctioned mast by a reader of this office. One crossing is certified, Ember to still; a stack so read is an incident. The longer thing is not to be asked of her.*

*One incident at a sanctioned board ends her season. I have had that line in front of me since the autumn. I have left it in. Y. Crest.*

"She thought about it," said Perrin.

"She says so. She doesn't say which way."

"*By her captain's voice.*" He had read it twice. "So if I say *Ember* and you've declared the still, that's an incident. That's your whole year. On my tongue."

"Yes."

"I'd like it known that I've stopped enjoying the hat."

Somebody was coming along the passage on two sticks. She came slowly. She had climbed the basement stairs by herself, since Kiva could hear Wilf Marten three steps behind her, not helping, by instruction.

Selka Dray read the three sheets from the top.

"Cade," she said.

"Yes."

"He'll call *off* early."

"He'll call it when it's wanted," said Kiva. "He kept the word for me on a hill. I'd not have anybody else."

"I didn't say that it was a fault. I said early." The grey eyes went to the second sheet, and stayed there a moment. "And Renn's gone to the Works. I'd have wagered on that in the ninth month."

"He told us himself. At a table."

"Did he. That's more than I'd have looked for." Her jaw moved. "Twenty-Eight's is Marten. They'd not have a captain who couldn't walk to the referee. I came up those stairs to argue it, and I shall, and I shall lose."

She went on toward the Chair's stair. It took her some time.

"Tomorrow's the thirtieth," said Kellan. He had his pencil out again; he could not help it. "The gate's the first of the third month, so we're on the road before the second month's out. That's thirty days at the outside. And the Bench is the fourteenth. Forty-four."

"Forty-four days," said Pru.

"To the first board. On three drams and eighty-four hundredths, with a captain who minds and a catcher on one door."

"And a Kill who comes down heavy," said Jude.

"And a sheet," said Anwen. "I've asked my mother. She said that she'd never heard such nonsense, and then she said that we might have it on Saturday if it came back clean."

Kellan put the pencil away. He stood for a moment in front of the board, with one name on each of two sheets.

"I'd expect nothing else," he said. He did not say of whom.

He went up the stairs. He did not take them two at a time.

---

Perrin Cade called his first floor for three on Saturday, in the Old Yard, and he opened it by apologizing.

"I've asked nobody," he said. "I'm told that's what the hat's for. If it's a stupid floor, it's mine, and I'd like that understood before anybody's cold."

It was the stupidest floor that Kiva had ever seen laid.

There was a double sheet spread on the packed dirt, between the two rows of black posts. It was grey with washing, and it had a seam down its middle where it had been turned. Mrs. Pryce had sent it up on the tram in a basket. She had also sent Dilys.

"I'm the far edge," said Dilys. She was thirteen, and she had never been inside a school of the Circuit in her life, and she looked at the posts as one looks at the masts of a ship. "Me and her. We're to take the corners and bring them home."

"As fast as you can walk," said Anwen, at the other corner. "Faster. You're a thing with nobody in it."

Gerald hung from a pole across two trestles, in his hat.

"It's a rung to a bag," said Perrin. "On a skin. Finch throws on it. Talley lets go the rung. And those two fold it, from down there, as soon as they like and without telling anybody. If there's a hand inside the cloth when the far edge comes home, that hand's lost, and I say *off,* and we've all been killed by a laundry."

"And me?" said Kiva.

"You've the left hem. You've one thing. I'm told that there's a line on a slate."

There were four sandbags by the south row. Jude had carried them down from the Low Lane, two under each arm. They were to be coal.

She knelt on the east side of Pru's cloth, a pace and a half down from the corner.

It was a clear cold afternoon. The bar hung on its two hooks on the bottom wall. Orla was on her crate, a pace north of where she had sat for one-and-twenty years, with her stick between her boots, and she had said nothing whatever since they laid the sheet. She had only looked at it as though it were the first new thing in that yard since a lid was dug up.

There was somebody on the shed step.

He had come by the side gate, with Greta Stroud to open it. He sat with his arms laid across his knees, bound white to the elbow, and his square grey face was turned to the cloth.

"I'm not here," said Pike. "I've no card for this yard. I was passing."

"You were passing a school on a hill," said Orla. "On a Saturday. In a tram that doesn't come within half a mile."

"I'd a notion to see a sheet folded."

"Then you may have my crate. It's got a back to it, and you've nothing to hold on by."

He looked at her. He got up, with care, and crossed the lane, and she moved her stick to make room. They sat side by side on a crate that was a pace out of its place: a sponsor who had once had a boy hurt, and a catcher with his hands in linen.

"Pru," said Kiva. "The far edge is mine. I'll say if it moves."

"Yours."

"*Go,*" said Perrin.

Jude let go the rung. A hundredweight of sand came down in the upper middle of the cloth with a sound like a dropped mattress.

"*Dark! One!*"

Kiva did not look at it.

She had her right hand on the hem. She had turned her head, and she was looking down the lane at the bottom of a grey sheet, where a laundry girl and a ward-holder stood with a corner each. They were grinning at one another. Dilys had her tongue out.

It was absurd. A carter would have laughed. There was a sandbag in a hat, and a bedsheet, and a boy in a jersey with a turnip watch in his fist.

It was also the only place in Meridian, that afternoon, where five people were kneeling on a cloth with somebody set to watch the edge of it.

"*Four,*" said Pru. "*Five.* I'm waiting my ten. He does."

"Who told you that I waited ten?" said a thick voice from the crate.

"It's in a column. She read it to me."

The far corners lifted. They came up together, an arm's width apart, as true as a rule.

"Far edge," said Kiva.

"*Off the cloth!*" That was Perrin. He did not say *off;* he had been told that the word was for a withdrawal, and he was keeping it clean. "*Hands out! Talley, the weight!*"

Jude slung a sandbag. It landed on the bottom hem with a thump, and Dilys, hauling, found that she had a sheet with a foot on it and sat down hard in the dirt.

"It doesn't fold," she said, very much pleased. "I told you. I did that in the ironing room and got shouted at."

"Again," said Perrin. "The weight was late. It was three counts after she said. I want it before she's finished saying."

"I can't sling before I'm told."

"Then be standing where you can."

They did it again. They did it eleven times before the light went, and on four of them somebody would have lost a hand.

Kiva said two words each time. She did not say anything else. It was all that she had, on that floor, and for once it did not feel like less than the others. Pru had a cord and a second pin. Anwen had the corner of a sheet, and was learning what a wall looked like from the far side. Jude had the weight. Perrin had a watch, and a word that he was saving, and a look of acute suffering.

Between the seventh and the eighth she sat back on her heels to rest her knee.

There was a sheet of paper in her coat. It was not Works paper; that was gone. It was the back of a laundry order, ruled in seven with a pencil, and she had headed the first column on the tram.

*A purse string. Drill for a four, with a sheet. At Meridian, in the yard under the south wall.*

She had put *what,* and *how.* She had put *went wrong:* the weight three counts late, since the Kill waited to be told. She looked at the last column.

"Mr. Pike."

"I'm not here."

"Why does the weight go on before the cloth? On the wharf we threw the cloth over the sacks. Perrin's had Jude sling after."

There was a pause. On the crate, a square grey man shut his eyes.

"Since it folds when there's something on it worth folding," he said slowly. "And not before. It lay there under my heel and never stirred. If your weight's down before your rung is, it's got a foot on its hem before it's a mind to move. If you wait until it's lifting, you're racing it." He opened his eyes. "I don't know that. I've seen the thing once. Put that it's what I'd try."

She wrote it in his words. *What P. would try. Not known.*

It was a little thing to be doing on the last day of a month, with dusk coming and a frost behind it. She was putting down somebody else's craft in a column, for four people and a reserve to use, off a method that nobody had yet got right. It had a laundress's name in it. It had a square left white.

Perrin had chalked a figure on the shed door. He had done it before anybody came, and had said nothing about it. It was a foot high.

*44.*

"Tomorrow that's forty-three," said Pru.

"I know what it is tomorrow. I'm the one with the chalk."

Beyond the bottom wall the long hill went down, straight as a rule, with a lamp at every twentieth pace, to the Brickfields road and the river. There was a boat on that river every Monday. There was a road that went round the Sag, and doubled at a ferry.

She had thirty days, at the outside, before she was on it. She had one door at a time, and a card that said one crossing in seven days at the most, and two fingers that were a reason to look. She had a team that was three drams and eighty-four hundredths from having nothing, and a captain who had not wanted the place.

"Rowan," said Perrin. "Your knee's had its minute. I'd like it known that I'm being firm."

She put the paper in her coat. The pocket had a button. She left it for whoever came by.

"Pru. The far edge is mine."

"Yours."

Jude let go the rung.
