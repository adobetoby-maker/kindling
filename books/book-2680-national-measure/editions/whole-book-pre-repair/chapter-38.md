# Chapter 38 — Kiva: The Measure Is Read

Halden License lost to Holloway's second four at one o'clock on Saturday by five points to two, and Kiva watched every carry of it from the sixth tier with her right hand in fresh linen and her knees still stiff from the stone.

She had not meant to watch it so closely. She had come up the tiers after Seven's lane to sit with Aske and eat something, and Aske had put a pasty in her good hand and said *eat,* and she had eaten it. Then Halden had come out of the side door in their black and gold, and she had stopped eating.

Kellan was on the floor. She had half thought he would not be. She had lain awake for an hour last night wondering whether Pettit would stand him down after the Salt Hall. But Pettit had declared him at the box in his heavy flat voice, *Renn, edge,* the same as every other day, and Kellan had gone to his place on the north side of the pen and stood there with his edge dark on his fist and his face giving nothing away.

He fought well. Kiva could see that from the sixth tier. He took Holloway's carry with a touch in the first, as clean as anything she had seen all week, and he read their Hold's feet in the second and was there before she moved. It did not matter. Holloway's second four had been ninth on the Bench and fourth on the Hunt, and nobody in the Graving had watched them all week, and they had watched everybody. They had watched Seven's elbows on Thursday and Friday, and Carrow's word, and the hen, and Lyle's lean. In the third carry their catcher sat on the litter's head with both forearms along the canvas, and their Hold leaned on Halden's wall from the floor, and Holloway's blade stood off Kellan's left side and waited for a count he could not see coming.

"*Five to Holloway,*" said the marshal at the glass. "*Two to Halden.*"

The Graving made the long sound of breath let out. It went round the tiers twice. Everybody in it could add.

"That's the Measure," said Aske, beside her, quite quietly. "Isn't it."

"It's the Ropewalk's," said Kiva, "if Westerley beat Old Four at two, and the Ropewalk beat Holloway's first four at three."

"And if they don't?"

"Then it's somebody else's. It's still not Halden's." She had done the sum on the tiers, with her left hand on her knee, as Kellan would have done it. "Halden's sixty-five and the Lane. They'd have needed to win today and stand fourth or better. They've lost a lane. They'll be ninth or tenth in the Lane, on points. Eighty, or eighty-one." She looked down at the floor, where Kellan was walking back to Halden's hurdle with his edge dark and Pettit's hand on his shoulder. "Old Four's sixty-four. If they win at two, they've won three, and it's theirs. If they lose, it's the Ropewalk's fifty-eight, and if the Ropewalk win at three, they're first in the Lane, or near it. Eighty-two."

"You'd make a grader," said Aske.

"His mother's a grader," said Kiva.

Below them, at Halden's hurdle, Kellan had stopped. He stood with Pettit's hand still on his shoulder, and he looked up at the tiers, at the Meridian knot, and found Seven in it, and found her. He did not wave. He looked at her for a moment, and then at the side door, where Old Four would come out at two, and Kiva knew that he had begun the same sum and that it would not come out until three.

---

Old Four lost to Westerley's first four at two, five to two, and Lowen Brask walked off the floor afterward with his notebook open and wrote in it all the way to the side door, as he always did, without looking up.

At three, the Ropewalk's first four came out of the side door for the last lane of the National Measure, and the Graving stood up for Carrow as it had stood up on Friday.

Kiva stood with it. She found she wanted to.

Holloway's first four had been third on the board after three, and they were good: a big quiet four in dark red who had found in two minutes on the Bench what Seven had needed four to find. They did not lay. They sequenced, as Meridian did, with a still door who planted on his captain's word and a blade who did not move until she was sure. Kiva had watched them beat the Ropewalk's second four on Friday and thought they were the best four in the Measure that nobody talked about.

Carrow declared at the box. Her voice went up the stone walls, low and flat and pleasant.

"*Carrow, edge. Declared. Single.*" She paused. Then, quite deliberately, she added a thing Kiva had never heard a Saltmere captain say. "*And no change.*"

The Graving made a sound. It was very small. Down at the mast on the north side of the pen, Noa looked up from his desk, and then he looked along the tiers until he found Seven, and Kiva saw him find her. Then he looked down and wrote.

"She's had that off Cade," said Aske. "This morning."

"Off Perrin," said Kiva. "Yes."

She had heard it from the Meridian knot in the middle tiers, where Perrin was sitting with his hand pressed to his side. She heard him make a sound that was not quite a word.

The lane went to the Ropewalk, five to three, and it was not close. Kiva watched every carry. Carrow walked on Treen's word, *set,* as she had walked on Friday, and Treen read feet and hands both now, as she had learned on Friday to read them. In the second carry the Ropewalk carried the Commodore down the pen on Tolver's forearms and Treen's, crossed at the wrists, with Lyle leaning from the floor on Holloway's wall. The Graving laughed out loud. It was the warmest sound Kiva had heard it make all week, and some of it was for the Ropewalk and some of it, she thought, was for Pru.

In the third carry, with the Measure on the glass, Holloway's blade came for Carrow at the litter's head, and Treen said *far edge,* two words, once. Carrow turned to her right as if she had been called by name, and Holloway's blade was there, coming round on the side Carrow had not been watching, and Carrow's edge came round and touched her.

"*Down.* Holloway."

"She's had that off you," said Aske.

"Off Pike," said Kiva. "Through me. Through Ivey, I expect, and the Company's yard, and the Marshal's page." She watched Carrow walk back to the Ropewalk's hurdle with one door and nothing open. "It's not anybody's now. It's on a page in every catcher's pocket on the coast."

"*Five to the Ropewalk,*" said the marshal at the glass. "*Three to Holloway.*"

The Graving did not stand. It was already standing. It shouted, all of it, for the first time all week. It shouted for a long time, and Carrow stood in the middle of the pen with her edge dark and her hands at her sides and did not look up at any of it. She looked at Lyle, who was standing by the Ropewalk's hurdle with nothing at her hip. Then she walked across to her and put her hand on her shoulder, and stood there.

Down at the floor's edge by the side door, among the black and gold, Kellan looked up at the tiers and found Kiva. He did a thing she had seen him do once before, at a gate, with a pencil: he held up one finger, and then a second, and then let his hand fall.

Two. By two marks. He had done the sum before the shouting stopped.

---

Mara had asked Kiva at noon to come to the Long Quay at five, and Kiva came. She came alone, because Mara had asked for her alone, and because she found she did not want anybody else.

The coast boat came in under the sea gate at ten to five, out of a grey sea with the light going yellow behind it, and Kiva saw her before the boat was alongside.

She was standing at the bow, by herself, with her hands folded at her waist. She was in plain grey, with a grey cap, tall and thin and very upright, and she was not looking at the quay or the town or the crowd of green that had gathered along the Long Quay's edge because the Mercury had said in the morning that she was coming. She was looking back over her shoulder, past the boat's wake, past the Mole and the arch of the sea gate with its black iron hanging in it. She was looking at the sea.

Kiva felt her doors lie down.

She was forty paces off, on the quay's edge by the third bollard, with Mara beside her and Peverell and Yeo and the sister from the Order a little way along. Forty paces. On the stair of the Order's house, a month ago, she had felt nothing from the doorway, and had felt her doors lie down only in the small room with the fire. Now they lay down at forty paces, across a strip of brown water, all three of them, the Ember under her breastbone and the hand door under her palms and the still door under everything. They did not shut. They lay down where they were, as dogs lie down when somebody comes into a room whom they do not need to watch.

She put her left hand on the bollard. The first two fingers lay on the iron, lightly.

*It keeps time.*

It did, as it always did on this quay, four and four, up through the iron from the granite. Nothing under her fingers had changed. Only her own doors had lain down, and at forty paces, which they had not done at the Order's gate.

"You've felt her," said Mara, very low. Her own left hand was out of her pocket, flat against her side. "So have I, in my hand. From here." She did not take her eyes off the boat. "In her own house she'd nothing to hold against. Here she has the whole town."

The boat came alongside. The gangway went down. The Master of the Order of the Even Hand did not come down it at once. She stood at the bow a little longer, looking at the sea, and the crowd on the quay waited and did not push, because she was not looking at them.

Then she turned and came down the gangway, slowly, a step at a time, with her hands at her waist.

At the foot of it, on the granite of the Long Quay, she stopped. She stood quite still. Kiva saw her feel the town. She saw it go through the long pale calm face like a wince that is not allowed to become one, and stay there for a moment, and go.

Behind her, on the boat's deck, two sisters in grey were handing a cart down off the boat by its shafts, with great care. It was a low flat cart with iron wheels. On its bed, packed in straw, under a grey cloth, there were jars. Kiva could see the shapes of them through the cloth, small and round, in rows. She could see the grey seals on their necks where the cloth had slipped, each with a mark on it like two hands laid flat side by side.

Thrale looked along the quay. Her grey eyes went over the crowd and the white lamps and the green doors and the bills on them, *THE REGIMENT, NOT A STROKE DROPPED,* and came to rest on Mara, and then on Kiva, forty paces off at the bollard. They rested there without searching, as a hand rests on a table.

She did not come nearer.

"Mrs. Rowan," she said. She did not raise her voice. It came along the quay as though there were nothing in between. "Miss Rowan. I'll not come closer. The last time you were in a room with me, you took a fever on my ferry. The ferryman told the sister at the gate that you were carried off his boat on a box." She looked at Kiva. "You've a board to hear read tonight. I'd not have you hot for it."

"Thank you," said Kiva.

"I've seen the sea," said Clemency Thrale. "I said I'd like to, before I was very much older. I've stood at that boat's bow for a day and a half and looked at it." She was quiet a moment. "It doesn't keep time. Nothing in it keeps time. It's every count at once. I'd forgotten." Her folded hands did not move. "And this town keeps one count, in everything, and I can feel it from my feet up, and it's the loudest place I've ever stood."

"There's a hut at the end of the Mole," said Yeo, beside the sister. Her voice was brisk, and a little too careful. "The Company's watch hut. On the sea side, past the gate. It's on the Mole's stone and not in the harbour's water. There's no cell in it. I've had it swept."

"Thank you, officer. I'll lodge there." Thrale looked along the Mole, out toward the sea gate and the arch and the long curved wall going out into the grey. "I'll come into the town for an hour at a time, and no more. An hour in this town is a day's work for me. Tomorrow I'll come for the Board, when it sits, and I'll stand where it puts me, and I'll speak for the cellar." She looked down at the cart, where the two sisters were settling the cloth back over the jars. "I've brought thirty-six. I've not given them. I've brought them. That's a different thing, and the Board will hear the difference tomorrow." She looked at Peverell, whom she had never met, and seemed to know her. "And you'll hear what they cost, keeper. You'll hear it first."

Peverell took her spectacles off their cord.

"I'll hear it," she said. "I've heard a good many prices this fortnight."

"Not this one," said Thrale.

She turned and walked away along the Long Quay toward the Mole, with the two sisters and the cart behind her and the crowd of green opening in front of her without being asked. She walked slowly. Kiva watched her go, and felt her doors get up, one at a time, as she went, and stand, and shake themselves, and the town's count come back up through the iron under her fingers to where it always was.

"She's not a hundred," said Kiva.

"Fifty, by her face," said Mara. "Older than that by her letters." She put her hand back in her pocket. "She left her house without a Master for the first time in eleven years, to bring thirty-six jars to a bank she's never seen, for a town that's never heard of her." She was quiet. "I wrote and asked. I'd not have come, in her place."

"You did come. In the third month. Two hundred miles."

"For a bank I'd been asked to read," said Mara. "Not to give away the only thing my house has."

---

At a quarter to seven Kiva laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron rail at the landward lip of the Graving, where she had stood on the first day and looked down eighty feet to a floor with men chalking lines on it.

*It keeps time.*

She wrote it. *Saturday the 19th. A quarter to seven. The rail at the Graving's lip. Yes.*

Then she went down the steps to the floor, because the Board had asked every four that had stood the Measure to stand on the floor of the Graving while it was read.

---

There were ninety-six of them, and the reserves behind, and they stood on the stone at the bottom of the dock in twenty-four rows of four, in the order of the standing after three boards, with Halden License at the front and the twenty-fourth at the back by the floating gate. The tiers went up round them on three sides, full to the iron and glass roof, and the lamps had been lit along every tier, white and steady. At the seaward end, behind the twenty-fourth, the harbour breathed against the gate's black timber.

Seven stood in the ninth row. Perrin had been let stand, by Aske, on condition he did not stand long. He stood at the end of the row with his hand pressed to his side, and Anwen next to him, and Jude, and Kiva at the other end. Pru stood behind them with the reserves, with her capped hands folded in front of her as Thrale folded hers.

The Clerk of the Measure stood on the chalk of the pen's middle line, alone, the smallest thing in the Graving. She did not have a box. She did not have a paper. When she spoke, everybody heard her.

"The National Measure," she said. "At Saltmere. Four boards. Twenty-four fours. No board struck." She waited for the sound that went round the tiers, and it went round and stopped. "The Lane first, because it's the board that's not been read. Then the Measure, from the bottom."

She read the Lane. She read it from the top, because that was how a board was read. *First, the Ropewalk School, first four. Won three. Second, Westerley, first four. Won three. Third, Holloway, second four. Won three.* She read the nine who had won two, in their order, by points for less points against, and Kiva heard Seven in the middle of it, *ninth, Team Seven, Meridian, won two, lost one, seventeen for, twelve against,* and Halden after it, tenth, two points to the good, and Old Four eleventh, one to the good. She read down to the twenty-fourth, which was a four from the sixth circuit that had lost three and scored four points in three days and stood at the back of the dock by the gate with their heads up.

Then she was quiet for a count of five.

"The Measure," she said. "Marked by place on every board, twenty-four to the first and one to the last, and added. The national license roll takes the first eight. They're licensed for the year to stand a Barrel response under a company officer, whenever the Board warrants one. I'll read from the twenty-fourth."

She read from the twenty-fourth.

It took a long time. She did not hurry it. She read every four's name and its circuit and its marks, and after each one she waited a breath, and the four she had named made whatever sound it made, or none. Kiva stood in the ninth row with her bound hand against her coat and listened to the names go past, twenty-fourth, twentieth, fifteenth, and the Tarn's first four among them, with Trewin standing in the front of his row on frosted feet in boots two sizes too large, and his captain's hat in his hand.

"*Eleventh,*" said the Clerk. "*The Tarn, second four. Fifty-nine.*"

The thin dark girl who was Bridie Hebden's cousin did not move. But her four put their hands on each other's shoulders, all four, in a line.

"*Tenth. The Ropewalk School, second four. Sixty-six.*"

The green in the tiers made the warm sound, not loud.

The Clerk waited. Then she said the next thing in exactly the same voice.

"*Ninth and eighth. There's a tie.*"

The Graving went quiet. It was not the quiet of people waiting. It was the quiet of four thousand people who had all, at the same moment, begun to add.

"*Westerley, second four. Sixty-eight. Team Twenty-Eight, Meridian. Sixty-eight.*" The Clerk did not look at either. "The Board's rule. A tie on marks goes to the four with the best single place on any board. Westerley's second four stood third on the Hunt. Team Twenty-Eight stood first on the Hunt." She paused, for a breath. "*Eighth, Team Twenty-Eight. Ninth, Westerley, second four.*"

There was a sound from two rows in front of Kiva, in the seventh row. It was not a word. It was the sound of a girl in sea blue, a Hold by her shoulders, who had been on the national roll a moment ago, and was not, and had not yet understood it. The boy beside her put his arm round her and turned her face into his coat.

Kiva did not look at her. She looked past her at the back of Selka Dray's head, in the fifth row, four rows in front.

Dray had not moved. She stood in Twenty-Eight's row with her weight on her right foot and her face, Kiva supposed, shut. Marten beside her had put his hand over his eyes. Dray did not put her hand anywhere. She stood, and the Graving's lamps lit the side of her face, and Kiva saw her jaw move once.

*The Hunt.* The board that had put Twenty-Eight on the roll by a tie was the one that had been read on Wednesday night under a line in a clerk's hand. *D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company, reserve to the officer, lost. The Board records it.* Dray had stood under that line and said *I'll take it. I'd not want anybody to think I was pleased.*

She was not pleased now. Kiva could see that from four rows back.

"*Seventh,*" said the Clerk. "*Team Seven, Meridian. Sixty-nine.*"

Kiva did not hear anything for a moment after that.

She was aware of Perrin at the other end of the row taking his hand off his side. She was aware of Anwen's shoulder against hers, quite hard, and of Jude, between them, very still, with the book under his good arm. She was aware of Pru behind her making a sound like somebody who has been holding their breath for a week. She was aware of the Graving making a sound as well, a long sound, from the Meridian knot in the middle tiers outward, that was partly the warm sound and partly something else.

*Sixty-nine.* Nineteen on the Bench, sixth, without her. Twenty-four on the Carry, first, on Perrin's back, up a ladder that he had climbed with a cracked rib on her word. Ten on the Hunt, fifteenth, with clean work on their stow and a pass in three that had held for a girl and been too late for a man. Sixteen on the Lane. Seven to two with no fingers, four to five against Carrow's one door, six to five on the glass, with her on her knees beside the litter's head and Pru calling.

Not one mark of it was three doors. She had not opened three doors since the fifth of the tenth month, and she had not crossed once since Thursday the 10th. She had stood every lane of the Measure on one door. Seven was on the national roll for the year by one mark, and it had got there with a ladder, a stow, a hen, a pair of elbows and a girl who called the pen.

She found this mattered more than she could have said. She had come two hundred miles with a thing in one hand that nobody else could do, and she had not used it. Seven had been put on the roll by a four and a method, by things with handles, that anybody in the Graving had watched and could do next year. Holloway's second four had done the hen to Halden this afternoon. The Ropewalk had done the elbows. Treen had called *far edge.*

She stood in the ninth row with her bound hand against her coat and did not cry, because it was not that kind of thing.

The Clerk went on reading. She read *sixth, Holloway, second four, seventy-six,* and *fifth, Holloway, first four, seventy-seven,* and *fourth, Old Four, Meridian, seventy-eight,* and Kiva heard Lowen Brask's flat voice say *thank you* to nobody from the second row, as a man says it to the ground. She read *third, Westerley, first four, seventy-nine.*

"*Second,*" said the Clerk. "*Halden License, Meridian. Eighty.*"

Kellan was in the front row. He did not move. Pettit beside him put his hand on Kellan's shoulder, quite heavily, as he had at the hurdle, and left it there.

"*First,*" said the Clerk of the Measure. "*The Ropewalk School, Saltmere, first four. Eighty-two.*"

The Graving stood up and shouted, and went on shouting.

Carrow did not move. She stood in the fourth row with Lyle on one side of her and Treen on the other and Tolver behind, with her edge dark on her right fist and her quick blunt face giving nothing away, and the whole town shouting for her from every tier. The nation's favourite, the Mercury had called her on the first day, a laid blade from a school with a column for two doors. She had won it with one door shut and a catcher's word.

She turned her head and looked along the rows until she found Seven. She found Kiva. She inclined her head, an inch, as a fact about the weather.

Kiva inclined hers.

"*The roll,*" said the Clerk, when the Graving would let her. "*The Ropewalk School, first four. Halden License. Westerley, first four. Old Four. Holloway, first four. Holloway, second four. Team Seven. Team Twenty-Eight.*" She did not read the marks again. "Licensed for the year. If the Board warrants a Barrel response and you go, you go by name, on the Board's warrant and the Board's ash." She paused. "The Board has been asked to warrant one for Wednesday morning, at the harbour pump house. It'll say tomorrow, at its sitting, at two, in this dock, after it's heard what it's asked to hear." She looked round the twenty-four rows. "No four on the roll is obliged to go. A license is not a warrant, and a warrant is not an order. I'll read it once more so that nobody can say they didn't hear it: no four is obliged to go."

She looked down the rows.

"The National Measure is read," she said. "The Board thanks the town of Saltmere for its dock. And for a great deal else." She did not say what else. "The Board records a man lost on the Longshore on Wednesday, in the course of the Measure, making the recovery the book prescribes. It'll go on recording him." She folded her hands. "That's all."

She walked off the chalk and went out by the side door.

---

Jude wrote it at the foot of the dock steps, under the first lamp, with the book on his arm and the crowd going up the steps round them.

He wrote the Lane first, because it was the day's. *Saturday the 19th. The Lane: Twenty-Eight, six to five. Pryce 0.15, Talley 0.11, Finch 0.12; Rowan her own thimble.* He did the sum in the margin, twice. *Lanes, three: thirty-one, forty-two, thirty-eight. A dram and eleven. Held, five and fifteen.*

"Five and fifteen," said Anwen.

"The most we've had at the end of anything." Jude did not look up. "And the tin, sealed. Two drams. For a hand. Not touched." He went on writing, under the ledger line, in his careful hand.

*The National Measure, at Saltmere. Bench, sixth. Carry, first. Hunt, fifteenth. Lane, ninth. Sixty-nine. Seventh of twenty-four. The national roll, by one mark.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he scratched his jaw with his right hand.

"I'd want it said," he said, "that I don't know what it's worth yet. A license. I'll know on Wednesday." He shut the book on his finger. "I'll write it tonight anyway."

Perrin was sitting on the bottom step of the dock with his hand pressed to his side and his face grey. Aske was standing over him. Neither of them was saying anything. Perrin looked up at Kiva as she came, and his face did the thing it did when he was going to say something he had been holding.

"One mark," he said. "One. Westerley's second four were on the roll till she read the tie. I watched the girl in sea blue."

"So did I."

"I'd thought it would feel like the top of the ladder." He breathed carefully. "It feels like that girl's face." He was quiet a moment. "I'll take it. I'd not give it back. I'm only saying."

"I know," said Kiva.

"And we won the Carry," said Perrin. "Outright. One board out of four, outright, on the national. That's ours." He let Aske take his wrist. "That's the ladder. That's the one with my name on it."

"It's got all our names on it," said Anwen.

"I know. That's why I like it."

---

The transit came into the high town's station at twenty-five to ten, and Mara was on the platform, and Kiva was beside her, because the page was hers.

It was a long low iron shed at the top of the town, lit white. The transit came in under it with a long sigh of brakes, three carriages, and stopped, and the doors opened, and people got down who had come two hundred miles in a day and a half and looked it. Kiva watched them go past. A man in Works grey. Two women with a basket. A clerk with a satchel.

Then a small woman got down from the second carriage with two pairs of spectacles, one on her nose and one pushed up into her grey hair, and a leather satchel held in both arms against her chest as though it were a baby. Behind her a porter carried a grey box tied with tape, with a number on its end and a red card in its holder, and behind him a second porter carried a black calf book under one arm, with a straw in it.

The Reader of the Compact Library looked up and down the platform with her bright eyes, and found Mara, and came straight to her.

"Mrs. Rowan," she said. "Miss Rowan." She did not put the satchel down. "I've not let go of this since eight o'clock yesterday morning. I slept with it on my lap. The guard asked whether it was a cat." She looked at Mara over the lower spectacles. "It's sealed as it went in. My wax over yours. The hour through the join. I've looked at it every hour since the Library and it's not moved." She opened the satchel, and took out a single sheet folded in three, with brown wax across the fold and black wax over the brown, and figures written across the join so that half of each lay on either side. *Eight minutes to two. Monday the 25th. Received shut. L. Arkwright.*

She held it out.

"To your hand," she said. "As I said. Not your niece's. Yours."

Mara took it. Her reading hand closed on it, and Kiva saw the hand not shake, and saw what it cost.

"And the box," said the Reader. "Annick, Personal. Under my custody. The settlement says it may be produced to a magistrate. I've decided that a Board sitting in a dry dock with half a town watching is near enough a magistrate for a reading room." She almost smiled. "If the Order's Master objects, I'll take it back to Meridian unread, and say so, and nobody will think the worse of either of us."

"She'll not object," said Mara. "I don't think."

"Nor do I. I've read her letters to the Library about those boxes for twenty years." The Reader nodded at the second porter. "And Mr. Lumb's book, with his compliments, which were not very civilly put. He says it's never been out of the pump house since 2604 and he'd like it back before the eleventh month." She looked at Kiva at last, properly, with her bright eyes. "You read it in the room. In the summer. Your lips counted."

"Yes."

"I wrote that down. I write everything down." The Reader shut the satchel. "Tomorrow they'll read it aloud in a dock. All of it. Yours and hers and the keepers'." She looked at Kiva for a long moment. "I've kept that page in the dark for nine months so that it could be believed. I'd like you to know I've never once been curious what's on it. I'll be curious tomorrow."

---

Anwen was waiting at the foot of Quiller's stair at a quarter past ten, with her hands in her sleeves.

She had not gone up. The loft's window was lit above, and Kiva could hear Old Four by the stove arguing about the Lane. Anwen stood on the bottom step in the dark of Quay Street with the gratings rattling in the gutter on the count, and when Kiva came along from the Long Quay she did not move off the step.

"Pryce," said Kiva.

"I've a card," said Anwen. "Rhea Sorn pinned it at the baize this morning, after our lane. I read it at one. I took it down at two." She took one hand out of her sleeve with the card in it, slate blue, and held it out. "Read it. I want you first. Before Perrin, before my mother. I've decided, and I want it said plainly, and I want you to hear it before anybody tells you."

Kiva took it in her left hand and read it by the light from Quiller's window.

*Graywater. Pryce, A., Hold, Meridian, Team Seven. To Doran Mace's ward line on the night van, in her own place and not a relief's, from the first of the fourth month, at a ward-hand's wage. Mace asked for her by name in the autumn and left her name where it was. A card is not a seat. R. Sorn.*

On the back, in Anwen's hand, square and pressed hard into the card: *Yes. A. Pryce.*

"It's not a relief," said Anwen. "It's a place. Mine. Mace's line. At home." She did not go red. Anwen never went red. "I'll be on the van from the first of next month, two nights a week, and the Hall the rest. I'll not be in Seven's lanes after the summer. Not every one." She looked at Kiva in the dark. "I'm not leaving. I'm going somewhere. I wanted you to know the difference, from me."

Kiva stood on the cobbles with the slate-blue card in her hand.

She thought of a stair in the Hall a year ago, and a girl who would not look at her. She thought of a wax seal and a sealed tin, and a wall walked from its far end round a planted girl in the Graving that morning, and Lyle on her knees nearly smiling. She thought of Anwen saying in a shut shop *I'll be at your back with my wall up.*

"You taught Lyle to lean," she said. "On Thursday. And she leaned on us on Friday."

"I know she did."

"Mace told Rhea you'd give it away to anybody who asked properly."

"Mace says that about everybody he likes." Anwen took the card back, carefully, and put it inside her coat. "I'd give it away again. That's what it's for. A wall nobody else knows how to walk is only a wall." She was quiet a moment. "You'd know. You've been saying that about yourself all winter."

"I'm glad," said Kiva. "And I'll miss you in the lanes. Both."

Anwen looked at her for a long moment. Then she did a thing she had not done since a kitchen table in Coper Street in the autumn. She put her arms round Kiva, quickly and hard, on the bottom step of Quiller's stair, with the gratings rattling in the gutter, and let go before either of them had to say anything about it.

"Go up," she said. "You've the Board in the morning, and a man with his hand on a glass case."

"Nine o'clock."

"Nine o'clock." Anwen went up the stair ahead of her. At the top she stopped with her hand on the door. "Kiva. Whatever's on that page. I'll be there at two."

"I know you will," said Kiva.
