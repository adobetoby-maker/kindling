# Chapter 48 — Kiva: Either of Us

Jude gave Pru the book on Tuesday afternoon, in Quiller's shop, among the coils of rope, with the door shut.

He did it without any ceremony at all. He took it out from under his arm, where it had been since the autumn, the team's book with its cloth spine gone soft and its pages thick with figures in four hands and a fifth. He held it out to her across the counter, and Pru looked at it and did not take it.

"I'm staying," said Jude. "In Saltmere. On the closing crew, from Thursday. Six months, and then they look again." He kept holding it out. "Pryce is going north on Thursday with Graywater. Cade's going to the Brickfields in the autumn, and till then he's a captain with no lift in him and a card that says *cup.* Renn's going to count Halden. Rowan's going wherever she's sent, with one hand, in three weeks." He looked at Pru. "You're the only one of us who's going back to the Crooked Line next season to stand on a floor."

"I'm the reserve," said Pru.

"You've not been the reserve since the Bench. You caught a national board. You called a lane on your own. You called the far edge on a cask of held water at a dock's sill with a still door going down beside you." Jude did not lower the book. "Seven's not a four any more. It's a name on a book and eight-tenths of a dram in a tin. Somebody has to keep the name. I'd rather it was you than the Circuit."

Pru looked at the book for a long time. She had gone scarlet from the collar, slowly, and she did not look away, which was new; Kiva had noticed it on the dock floor and noticed it again now.

"Ivey asked me on Sunday," said Pru. "To the Company. Yeo's crew, with Ivey and the girl. A catcher's place, and a second's place when I wanted one, and the sea."

"I know she did," said Jude. "She told me at the yard."

"I said no." Pru took the book. She held it in both capped hands against her chest, the way Loveday Hosken had held a jar. "I'd have been Ivey's catcher. I'd have been good. I'd have been the girl from Meridian on Ivey's crew who used to be Rowan's cord." She looked at Kiva. "I'm on a sheet. My own, with my name on the hem in ink. I'm going back to the Crooked Line and I'm going to find three people who've never heard of the Pit, and I'm going to catch for them, and nobody's going to say whose I was." She held the book tighter. "I'm not grateful to anybody. I'm only good. I'd like that written in the book, on the first page I write on."

Jude found the pencil on its string and gave it to her.

She wrote it, standing at the counter, with her capped fingers, very badly. Kiva read it upside down. *Tuesday the 29th. Seven's book, from Talley to Finch. Held 5.15. Sealed 0.8, bound as before. Finch is not grateful. Finch is good.*

"There," said Pru. She shut the book. "That's mine now."

---

Twenty-Eight went home on Tuesday evening by the station transit, because Marten's father had paid the fares, and Kiva met Dray on the Long Quay at six with her bag over her shoulder and her healed leg under her and the white lamps not yet lit.

Dray stopped. She did not say anything for a moment. Her face did what it always did.

"Your hand," said Dray.

"Three weeks."

"And the card's at fourteen. I heard Aske say it in the loft to the stove." Dray looked at the dead hand hanging against Kiva's coat. "You'll not spend that on a lane either. Not this year."

"No."

"Good." Dray shifted her bag on her shoulder. "I called it. Every pass. From the wall, on my feet. It's in the Board's press under my name, Hart says, every minute of it, fifty-seven." She did not look away. "That's all I wanted out of Wednesday. My name on it, so it was mine if it was wrong."

"It wasn't wrong."

"No. It wasn't." Dray was quiet a moment. "Bannock's legs will be all right by the summer. Aske says. I asked him on Saturday. I'm telling you I asked, because you'll not hear it from Bannock; he'll say they're fine now, and they're not." She looked along the quay toward the transit's shed. "I've not changed my mind about you, Rowan. I said so in the autumn and at the Hunt and in the Lane, and I'll not say it was wrong to have said it. You've three doors nobody can learn and a hand you'll spend on a thing because you've decided it's worth spending. I'd not do either." She paused. "But I'd call it again. If you were on the mud and I was on the wall. I'd call it."

"I know you would."

"That's not friendship," said Dray. "I'd want that clear."

"It's clear."

Dray nodded, once. Then she went away along the Long Quay toward the transit's shed with her bag on her shoulder, walking on her healed leg without a stick, and she did not look back, and Kiva had not expected her to.

Carrow was sitting on the third bollard from the shed with her arms folded, watching Twenty-Eight go. She looked up as Kiva came level.

"She called it well," said Carrow. "I struck on it for four minutes on that seam and never once had to look at her." She unfolded her arms. "Yeo carded me. On Saturday. The Company's wall crews, from the summer, on my own harbour." Something moved in her wind-brown face. "I'd wanted it since I was twelve. I thought I'd get it by winning the Measure. I got it by standing on a wall cutting on a girl from Meridian's count, read on a mast, one door." She looked at Kiva. "I asked Polglase for the tape. The bit where it says one line, two people. I've got it in my coat."

"Dray's in that line too."

"I know she is. That's why I wanted it."

"Your lay's how the planks kept her count," said Kiva. "Four of them agreeing on one voice, and holding till it was time. I had that off your floor."

Carrow looked at her for a moment. "Then we're square," she said, and stood up off the bollard. "Go home, Rowan. Come back to my harbour when your hand's done, and I'll show you the Ropewalk's floor when nobody's on it, and you can tell me what you'd have done different in the second carry on the Friday." She went off toward the Ropewalk steps and the high town, light and quick, and turned round at the corner, as she always did, and lifted one hand, palm out.

---

Rhea Sorn sat on the tailboard of Graywater's van at eleven on Wednesday morning, the 30th of the third month, with a sheet of the Charter Board's paper on her knee and a pen in her hand, and did not write anything for a quarter of an hour.

The sheet had come at nine, by a Board clerk, from Dinah Coombe. It was headed in the Board's print: *THE CHARTER BOARD. TRAVELLING COMMISSION, UNDER THE ORDER OF THE 25th OF THE THIRD MONTH, 2681.* Under that, in Coombe's square, unhurried hand, there was a list of names, a short one. A reader from the Works at Ferren, lent by Lumb with what Coombe called *a letter of some rudeness.* Two officers from the Saltmere Company, lent by Yeo. A fitter from Peverell's house, Hart's mate, the heavy man with the burn scar, lent by Peverell for a year and paid by the Board. Polglase, who had asked. And then a line with nothing on it but a number, and at the foot, in Coombe's hand: *Sorn. You've had her all winter. Write her in, if you'll write her, under my hand and not yours. Say what she is. Say what we'll do. D. C.*

Rhea looked at the empty line.

She had written a card once, in the autumn, slate blue, in this hand, on this knee, in a kitchen in Meridian with four children round the table. She had written *No seat is promised* on it, and *either of us may say no,* and she had read it aloud to all four and watched them sign the back. She had burned a card that said *mine* the week before she wrote it. She had not told anybody that. Pike knew, because Pike knew everything about her that she did not tell him.

On Monday night in a cellar in the Tanyard, under her columns, below the slate's last ruled line, where there was no column for it, she had written one more thing by the light of Mace's lamp on a copper. She had not shown it to the girl. She looked at it now, on the slate on her belt, in her own small square chalk.

*The second called the far edge, once, before the bag. Not mine to keep.*

It was true. It had been true since the Lane, when she had sat on the fourth tier with Pike and watched the girl lose an exchange on purpose and thought *mine* in her own head, and known it for what it was. She had been an officer for twenty years. She knew what it looked like when an officer started to keep a girl. It looked like a seat that was never quite offered, and a card that was always renewed, and a van that the girl could never quite leave, because there was always one more night. The officer was always good to her, and it was always kind. At the end of five years the girl was a very fine second to somebody else's catcher on somebody else's van, and nothing at all of her own.

Rhea had had a second like that once. Before Pike. She had been the second.

She uncapped the pen.

*Rowan, K.,* she wrote, on Coombe's empty line. *Catcher. Caller of the pass in three: a ward to an Ember to a still door, called, nobody holding it twice. One door declared at every response. Two crossings certified, Ember to still and hand to Ember; one in fourteen days, by her healer's card; the three doors not to be asked. Dark hands till her healer's card is up, from the 15th of the fourth month. Under D. Coombe's hand. Until the last of the ninth month.*

She stopped. She looked at it for a long time.

Then she wrote the last line, which was the only line on it that was hers.

*Then we look again, and either of us may say no.*

She blotted it on her sleeve. She did not sign it. It was under Coombe's hand, and Coombe would sign it, and the girl would, and the girl's aunt beside her because she was seventeen. Rhea's name was not on it anywhere. She had been careful about that.

Pike came round the side of the van with two cups of tea held against his chest in his half-closed hands, and sat down on the tailboard beside her, and gave her one, and looked at the sheet on her knee.

"Under Coombe," he said.

"Under Coombe."

"Not under you."

"Not under me." Rhea drank her tea. "I'd have kept her, Pike. I'd have been very good to her, and kept her."

"I know you would." Pike looked at the sheet a moment longer. "So would I. That's why it's Coombe's." His long brown face did the thing it did, nearly. "You've no advice left, have you. For the spring."

"I spent it at Gannet Creek."

"Then you'll have to say it some other way," said Pike, and drank his tea.

---

Kiva came to the van at noon, as she had been told, with her left hand in her coat and her right in her pocket, and found Rhea on the tailboard with a sheet of paper on her knee and Pike beside her with an empty cup.

"Sit down," said Rhea.

Kiva sat on the cobbles with her back against the van's wheel, because there was nowhere else, and Rhea looked down at her from the tailboard, as she had looked down at her from a hundred tailboards since the first month, slate blue, very straight.

"The card ends today," said Rhea. "It said we'd look again. I've looked." She did not look at the sheet on her knee. "I'll ask you first, because I'm the officer. Graywater's van goes north tomorrow. Pike's hands are coming back. Aske says by the summer they'll close two-thirds, which is more than anybody thought in the first month. He'll want a second at his other hem through the winter, the way he's had one this winter." She paused. "I'd not write you a seat. I've said so from the start, and I'll not unsay it. I'm asking if you'd want me to ask Graywater for you to be his second again next winter. When your hand's back."

Kiva looked at Pike on the tailboard. He was looking at the cobbles.

"No," she said.

Rhea did not move.

"Because I'd stay," said Kiva. She heard her own voice and kept it level, because it was a thing she had decided, and decided things are said levelly. "If I came back to the van next winter, I'd be good at it, and you'd be good to me, and Pike would nearly smile at me once a night. And I'd come back the winter after. And I'd be Graywater's second for five years, and I'd never call anything." She looked at Pike. "He doesn't need a second next winter. He needs somebody at his far edge. Nairn's watched me do it for six nights. Nairn can do it."

"Nairn can do it," said Pike, to the cobbles. "I've told him so."

"That's no, then," said Rhea. Her face did not change at all. "Good." She was quiet. "I'd have said no too, if you'd asked me to write it. You should know that." She put her hand flat on the sheet on her knee. "Ask me something. It's either of us. You've a no of your own to take, if you want it."

Kiva thought about it, honestly, because she had been asked.

"Come on the commission," she said. "Coombe's. As an officer. You've been in more cellars than anybody on that list. I want you there."

"No," said Rhea.

She said it at once, as Kiva had known she would, and as Kiva had needed to hear her say it.

"Because I'd be your officer again," said Rhea. "On somebody else's commission, under somebody else's hand, and in a month I'd be standing behind you on a dock floor with my slate thinking *mine.*" She looked down at Kiva steadily. "I thought it on the Saturday of the Lane, on the fourth tier, when you shook your head at your captain. In my own head. It's not a thing an officer should think about a second. It's the start of keeping somebody." She took her hand off the sheet. "Graywater's mine. You're not. That's the no."

Pike, beside her on the tailboard, had shut his eyes.

Rhea picked up the sheet off her knee and held it down to Kiva, over the edge of the tailboard, the way she had once held a slate-blue card across a kitchen table.

"I've written you in," she said. "Not to Graywater. To Coombe. The Board's travelling commission, under her hand, from the 15th of next month to the last of the ninth. It goes to every municipal bank on the Board's roll in two years and puts it on its own count, and takes out the ones that have stepped as plain ash can be found, and writes down in seven columns what was got wrong first." She did not look at what she had written. "No seat. A line. Your name on it under another officer's hand, and not mine anywhere."

Kiva took the sheet with her right hand. She read it on the cobbles with her back against the van's wheel. She read it twice, as Anwen read a card before she signed it.

She read *Caller of the pass in three.* She read *one in fourteen days,* and *the three doors not to be asked,* and *under D. Coombe's hand.* And at the foot, in a different ink, as though it had been written a little after the rest, *Then we look again, and either of us may say no.*

"You've put *either of us* on it," she said.

"It's Coombe and you, this time. Not me." Rhea's face did not change. "I'd want it on every card anybody ever writes you. It's the only advice I've got left, and it's not advice, it's a line." She drank the last of her tea, which was cold. "You'll take it, or you won't. If you take it, you take it on whatever you say now, and Coombe writes it under. She's said so. She's coming along the quay; I can see her rope."

Kiva looked up. Coombe was coming along the cobbles of the Company yard in her grey Board coat with her rope over her shoulder, unhurried, the way she came to everything.

Kiva got up off the cobbles. She did it one-handed, and nobody helped her, and she did not need them to.

"I'll take it," she said, when Coombe stopped in front of the van. "On two conditions. Mine."

Coombe took the rope off her shoulder and began, very slowly, to tie a knot in it.

"Say them," she said. "Once. I'll write them."

"Every bank I call a pass at," said Kiva, "I teach the call to two people there who aren't me, before we leave. Out loud, on that bank, with whatever's to hand, a sack or a tun or a rope's end. And I leave it in seven columns in that house's book, in their hand as well as mine. And the next bank we go to, if somebody who isn't me can call it, they call it, and I keep the count, or the far edge, or nothing."

She held the sheet in her right hand. "My sheet said the caller had to have been inside both gaps. It was wrong. Ivey called it in the Company yard on Thursday with one door and a girl of sixteen counting. I'll not be the only one who can. If the commission needs me to be the only one who can, then it's not a method, it's me, and I said no to that on a quay last Wednesday to a man in a chair."

Coombe's hands stopped on the knot.

"That's one," she said.

"The dream's not the commission's." Kiva did not look away from her. "Nobody on it asks me about it. Not the officers, not the readers, not the Board. If I've a thing to put on a table, I'll put it there myself, under my own name, the same night, the way I did in the Salt Hall. If I haven't, nobody asks." She was quiet. "I've a thing I've no word for. It's mine. It stays mine on the commission, the same as it's mine in a loft."

Coombe looked at her for a long time with her pale eyes.

Then she finished the knot, and took the sheet out of Kiva's hand, and laid it on the van's tailboard beside Rhea's knee, and took a pencil out of her coat, and wrote under the last line, standing, in her square unhurried hand.

*On her two conditions, said first. One: she teaches the call at every bank to two who are not her, and leaves it in seven columns in that house's book, and the next bank is called by somebody else where somebody else can. Two: the dream is hers and not the commission's; she will put a thing on a table herself if there's a thing; nobody asks. Accepted. D. Coombe, Hunt Marshal.*

"There," said Coombe. "That's a better first line than mine was." She handed the sheet back. "Your aunt signs it beside you. You're seventeen."

"She'll sign it."

"I know she will. She'll not ask you anything about it either. I've met her." Coombe put her pencil away and her rope back over her shoulder. "The 15th. At the Board's office at Meridian. Bring your book. Bring that hand, if it's back, and if it's not, bring it anyway, and we'll put you at a far edge till it is." She turned to go, and stopped, and looked back at Rhea on the tailboard. "Sorn. You wrote her well."

"I wrote what she is," said Rhea. "I don't write good."

"No," said Coombe. "That's why I asked you."

She went away along the yard with her rope. Pike, on the tailboard, opened his eyes and looked at Kiva standing on the cobbles with the sheet in her right hand.

"You've two noes and two conditions and a line," he said. "That's a good day's work for a girl with one hand." His face did the thing it did, and then, as it had in the cellar, it went the whole way. "Go and get your aunt's name on it before you lose it."

Mara signed it at four, at the table in the Works' lodging, with her reading hand.

She read it first. She read every line of it, Rhea's and Coombe's, the way she read a fault report across a counter, dry and exact, with her lips shut. She read the two conditions twice. Then she took the pen from the inkstand and signed beside the space where Kiva would sign, *M. Rowan, Grade Two, guardian,* in her own small exact print, and blotted it, and turned the sheet round.

She did not ask anything. Kiva had known she would not.

"The 15th," said Mara. "At the Board's office."

"Yes."

"The Works' transit goes on Friday. Rusk's been asked by the Board to write the Saltmere sheet, and he's asked me to write it with him, *How the Saltmere bank was taken out of step, with what was got wrong first,* and he says the first thing got wrong was a number I let a keeper say in a Salt Hall. Peverell's already put her seven columns on it in front of the Board, as she said she would, and he's copying them in first." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "You'll come home on the transit with us. On the Board's fare. Coombe's paid it; she says commission hands travel on the Board." She looked at Kiva. "A fortnight in the shop, with a hand that's no use and your aunt at the counter. You'll hate it."

"I'll like it."

"You'll like it for three days and hate it for the rest." Mara put the pen back in its stand. "Then you'll go. And I'll have the shop, and the Works, and Orla being rude to me on Tuesdays, and a sheet on the dresser that's longer than the Ferren one." She looked at the signed paper on the table. "I took it on my name, on Wednesday. You took yours. Neither of us owned the other's. I'd like that to go on being so."

"It will."

"Good." Mara stood up. "Then sign it. I can't hold the paper still for you all evening; I've a sheet to write."

Kiva signed it right-handed, under her aunt's name, *K. Rowan,* worse than anything she had ever signed, and blotted it on her sleeve.

Anwen went north at seven on Thursday morning, the 1st of the fourth month, on Graywater's van.

The van stood at the head of the Long Quay with its horses in and its lamps lit against the grey. It was slate blue. Mace was on the box and Nairn beside him, with Ruddock and Tibb and Stiles behind, and Pike on the tailboard where he always sat, and Rhea standing at the horses' heads with her slate on her belt. Anwen's bag was already up. Anwen was not. She was standing on the quay with her cap on and her hair pinned under it and her hands in her sleeves, and Kiva and Pru and Jude and Perrin stood round her, because that was how they stood.

"I've a thing for you," said Kiva.

She had written it on Wednesday night, right-handed, at the table in the loft, three times, until it was legible. One sheet, in seven columns. She held it out.

*The pass in three. Second edition. What was got wrong first: the first line of the limits. "A caller who has been inside both gaps." Wrong. A caller who can hear the count, and somebody who keeps it who isn't carrying.*

Anwen took it and read it, all of it, as she read a card before she signed it. It took her a long time.

"For Mace's line," said Kiva. "If you want it."

"I want it." Anwen folded it once and put it in her sleeve. "I want it said I'll teach it. On the van. To the line, and to whoever Rhea puts at the far edge." She took her hand out of her sleeve again, and there was something in it, small and dark: a stick of sealing wax, red, half used, with the end of it melted and shaped by her thumb. "And this is Seven's. I've had it since the autumn. Every seal on the book and the tin. It's not mine." She held it out to Pru.

Pru took it in her capped hand and held it as though it were still warm.

"I'll seal things," she said.

"Seal them properly," said Anwen. "Thumb in the middle. Not the edge." She looked round at the four of them. At Perrin with his hand pressed to his side, at Jude with his white knuckles and his card from the Company in his coat pocket, at Pru with the book under one arm and the wax in her capped hand, at Kiva with her left hand in her coat. She did not go red. Anwen never went red.

"I'm not going to say anything about the year," she said. "You all know it. I'd only say it wrong."

"Say one thing," said Perrin.

Anwen looked at him.

"I was the best student wall in Meridian," she said, "and I stood in the way of things for three years, and I thought that was what I was for." She looked at Kiva. "On Wednesday at ten past six I put a wall under a thing that wanted me, on a count that wasn't mine, and held it, and let it go into the air on a word. That's not standing in the way. I don't know what it is. I'm going to find out on a van." She took Kiva's right hand in hers, briefly, and held it. "I want it said that I'd not have had it any other way. Write and tell me what you get wrong first."

"I'll write it."

"Every bank," said Anwen. "I'll read them on the van."

Then she let go, and turned, and went up onto the van's tailboard beside Pike, and sat, with her hands back in her sleeves and her face toward the quay. Rhea went up onto the box. Mace shook the reins. The van went away along the Long Quay at a walk, between the white lamps on their fluted posts that the lamp-men were putting out one after another in the grey morning, and turned at the end of the quay toward the hill road and the north, and was gone.

Perrin let out a breath.

"I'd like it known," he said, "that I've never once in my life seen her cry, and I'd not have wanted to just then, and she didn't, and nor did I."

"Nobody did," said Jude.

"Pru did," said Perrin.

"I did not," said Pru, scarlet, with her face wet.

At a quarter to seven on Thursday evening Kiva laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron rail at the end of the Mole, with her right hand holding it there by the wrist, because it was the hour. She had let the morning's go by on the Long Quay, at Anwen's van, and the clock had a quarter to seven twice a day.

No answer.

She wrote it in her book, right-handed, leaning on the rail. *Thursday the 1st of the fourth month. A quarter to seven. The rail at the end of the Mole. No answer.* It was the ninth day of *no answer.* She did not mind it. Hart had told her what it was for. In three weeks there would be a morning when it said something else, and she would know it was new by setting it against these.

Jude was beside her. He had come out along the Mole with her after supper because he wanted to see the sea, he said, and he had not seen it from the end of the Mole at dusk yet, and he was going to be living beside it for six months and thought he ought to start. He leaned on the rail with his good hand and looked out past the sea gate's arch at the grey water going dark.

Behind them, along the Long Quay, the lamp-men were lighting the lamps, one after another, white and steady on their fluted posts. In the low town the yellow lamps came on in the windows of Ropery Row. At the head of the basin, the pump house's six outfalls poured into the evening flood on six counts, every one its own, and none of it came up through the stone of the Mole into Kiva's boots. She had stopped noticing that it did not. She noticed now that she had stopped noticing, and was surprised by it.

Noa came along the Mole at five to seven with a sheaf of yellow forms in his hand and his pencil behind his ear.

He did not hurry. He never did. He came along the top of the Mole between the bollards in his grey coat with the forms held against his chest, past the Company's swept watch hut at the end where the Master of the Order of the Even Hand had lain on Wednesday night with her hands folded, and gone home on Friday's boat without coming into the town again. He stopped beside Kiva at the rail.

"The coast came in last Thursday and Friday," he said. "Every lamp office on the Board's roll from here to Westerley, and the ships in the roads between: the same nine seconds, every lamp to a point. That's in my column already." He touched the yellow forms. "These are new. The Board's wire office has had them since four. From over the water, through the cable office at Westerley. The Clerk's read them and said I could." He did not give them to her. He held them. "I'll read you three lines. I've written them in my column. I've drawn nothing under them."

"Read them," said Kiva.

He read them in his thin clear reader's voice, as he read returns, without looking up.

The first was from a harbour master in a port over the water whose name Kiva had seen once, on the Board's big map in the Salt Hall, and could not have said aloud. *HARBOUR LAMPS AND SHIPS' LAMPS IN THE ROADS ALL TO A POINT OF LIGHT AND BACK. NINE SECONDS. RECKONED TO SALTMERE TIME TWENTY-TWO MINUTES PAST SIX MORNING 23RD THIRD MONTH. ENTERED. NOT FAULT.*

The second was from another port, further south, in another tongue, put into the Board's words by the cable office at Westerley with a note at the foot to say so. It said the same thing. It gave the same minute.

The third was from the port over the water that a ship's engineer had come from, off a hull, in the autumn. He had said, on a green-edged sheet from the Saltmere Company's hall that Noa had read aloud at a table in Meridian before Kiva ever came south, at the foot, *as a thing is written that the writer will not vouch for,* that his own port's bank had fallen into step at the same hour as Saltmere's, reckoned back. It said: *POINT OF LIGHT AS ABOVE, SAME MINUTE. AND IN ANSWER TO THE BOARD'S QUESTION: OUR HARBOUR BANK FELL INTO STEP 17TH NINTH MONTH LAST AT AN HOUR WHICH RECKONED TO SALTMERE TIME IS TWENTY PAST TWO AFTERNOON. AS OUR ENGINEER REPORTED. VOUCHED.*

Noa lowered the forms.

"Vouched," he said. "I've had that line in my column since the autumn with *not entered as fact* beside it. I've struck the note out this afternoon. Neatly, so it can still be read." He put his pencil behind his ear again. "That's three ports over the water in the same nine seconds. And a bank over the water that fell in in the same minute as ours. I've written them. I'll send them to every pump house and lamp office on the Board's roll, as they are, with the rest." He looked at the sea. "I'll not draw anything between. I drew one wrong once."

Jude had not taken his eyes off the water.

"Rowan," he said.

She looked where he was looking.

There was a hull standing in from the south.

It was a long way off, out beyond the sea gate's arch and the end of the Mole, on the grey sea going dark, a low black shape with its masts against the last of the light, standing in toward Saltmere on the evening flood as the coast boat had stood in a month ago. It was too far off to see what flag it flew, or whether it flew one. It had a light on its bow.

Kiva had seen every kind of lamp there was in Saltmere in a month. The white ones on the Long Quay on their fluted posts, and the yellow ones in the low town's windows, and Peverell's oil lamp on the landing, and the three rows of white globes along the Graving's tiers, and the lamps on the Company's punts and the coast boat's bow and the Board's tent and Noa's desk. She knew them as she knew the order of her doors.

The light on the hull's bow was not like any of them.

It was not white. It was not yellow. It was not the colour of anything she could have pointed to on the quay behind her, and it did not flicker, and it did not burn the way a lamp burns. It stood on the hull's bow out on the dark water, small and steady, coming in, as though somebody aboard had looked for a long time for a lamp that was like nothing and had found one, and had lit it, and had pointed the ship at the place where a light had answered.

Noa had seen it too. He stood at the rail with the yellow forms against his chest and did not say anything. Jude did not say anything. Behind them along the Long Quay the lamp-men went on lighting the white lamps one after another, and none of them looked out to sea.

Kiva stood at the rail with her right hand on the iron and her left hanging, and watched it come.

She did not know who was on that hull. She would not know tonight. She thought, watching the light on its bow come steadily in over the dark water toward the sea gate, that somebody on it had dreamed, a long way off, with a fever, of roads and lights and nothing over them and something paying attention. She thought that they had been at a window, perhaps, on the morning of the 23rd, at twenty-two minutes past six by Saltmere's clock, and had seen every lamp they could see go to a point and come back. And that they had got up from the window and found a ship, and come to see who had answered.

She did not think anything else about it. She did not let herself.

She put her right hand inside her shirt and found the disk.

It was cold. It had been cold at the far end of a polished table, and on a bench in a dock with a Glory master at the gate, and on a dock floor with two Glory fields touching across a grey ward and every lamp on the coast gone to a point. It was cold now, with a light on a hull's bow coming in over the water that was like no lamp in Saltmere. It lay against her chest on its cord, a round flat cold thing, and did not tell her anything, and she had not expected it to.

She took her hand out of her shirt and opened her book on the rail.

There was a column in it that nobody had ruled but her, the fourth, on the left, where she wrote within the week after any crossing what the disk had done. She had written in it on Wednesday night, for the two crossings, one line: *Cold. Both. As before.* She had thought that was all she would write in it until the next crossing, fourteen days off at the least.

She wrote in it now, right-handed, badly, leaning on the iron rail at the end of the Mole in the last of the light, with Jude on one side of her and Noa on the other and the hull standing in.

*Thursday the 1st of the fourth month, 2681. Evening. The end of the Mole. A hull standing in from the south, with a light on its bow like no lamp in Saltmere. Wires from three ports over the water: the point, the same minute. The engineer's line vouched.*

*The disk is cold.*

She looked at it. Then she shut the book, and put it in her coat, and stood at the rail and watched the light come in.
