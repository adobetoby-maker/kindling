# Chapter 43 — Kiva: Bearings

Aske had the hand at a quarter to five, by candle, at the north window, with the loft dark behind him and twenty people dressing in it as quietly as twenty people can.

"Warm," he said. "Ten mornings." He held it a while longer than he needed to, palm up on his own palm, with his thumb on the inside of her wrist. Then he wrote it on the card against the window frame and put the card in his coat. "That's the last time I'll say that word about this hand for a while, I think. Remember you heard me say it."

"I heard."

"Then eat." He shut his bag. "Quiller's left bread and cheese on the counter for everybody, and tea in the big can, and he's gone down to the quay himself to watch, the old fool, with a blanket. Eat while you can still hold the bread in that hand."

She ate on the stair, because there was nowhere else to sit. The loft emptied past her in the dark, one at a time, in ones and twos. Old Four went first, solid and silent, in their coats. The Ropewalk's four had not slept in the loft; they lived in Saltmere, in the high town, and would come down from the hill. Twenty-Eight went past her on the stair in a body: Marten, Sedge with her pale hair tied back hard, Bannock filling the stairwell from wall to wall, and Dray last, on her healed leg, with her watch already in her hand. Dray did not look at her. Kiva had not expected her to.

Seven came down together, because that was how they came down.

Perrin had the book. He had taken it from Jude at the stair-head without asking, and Jude had let him, and he carried it under his arm with his hand pressed to his side and his face set, the way he had carried a ladder's foot on the Carry. Anwen had her hair pinned up under her cap and her hands in her sleeves. Jude held his left arm a little away from his body. Pru had her caps fresh from Aske at half past four, ten white fingertips held up in front of her in the candlelight as though she were carrying something on them already.

"Five past five," said Perrin, at the bottom of the stair. He did not need to look at anything to know it. "Basin wall at a quarter to six. Positions at six. Rowan, you're to be on the dock floor by five past, with your fingers on the chalk."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it because I'm the captain and it's the last thing I'll get to say that isn't a declaration." He looked at the four of them in the dark of Quiller's shop, among the coils of rope and the smell of tar. "I'll be on the wall by Polglase's mast. I can't go on the mud. I'll not lift anything. If anybody sees me lift anything, tell Aske, and he'll have me off the wall." He breathed carefully. "That's all. Go on."

Nobody moved for a moment.

"I'd want it said," said Jude, "that you'd be on the mud if you could."

"Everybody knows I would," said Perrin. "That's the worst of it." And he opened Quiller's door onto the street, and the cold came in.

---

The low town was awake.

Kiva had not thought it would be. She came down Quay Street with her four in the dark and turned along the edge of the low town toward the basin, and every house in Ropery Row had a light in it. Doors stood open. In the first doorway a man in his shirtsleeves was carrying a scuttle of coal up a narrow stair, and his wife was behind him with another, and on the step below them a boy of about ten was holding a candle with great seriousness, because that was his job. In the next a woman was lifting a rag rug off a stone floor and folding it over her arm. Further along, a whole family was standing in their own doorway in their coats, looking up the street toward the pump house, not doing anything at all.

*Move the coal up a stair.* Peverell had said it in the Salt Hall to the Harbour Committee's chairman. The low town had heard. It had got up in the dark and done it, as it did every spring when a gale took a pump, without being asked twice.

"They've done this before," said Anwen, very low.

"Every time a pump's gone." Pru was looking at the boy with the candle. "Carrow said. Her mother's been moving the coal up a stair since she was married."

At the end of Ropery Row, where the street came out onto the basin wall, the gratings in the gutter rattled under their boots on the count. Four and four. Kiva walked over the last one and felt it through her sole, and did not stop.

---

At half past five the jars came along the Long Quay from the Mole.

Kiva saw them from the outfall walk, where she had gone to find Mara. They came in the grey half-dark between the white lamps, a long line of people walking one behind another at the same pace, and she did not understand what she was seeing until they were close.

It was the fitters. Peverell's thirty-six, in their aprons, in a line. Each of them was carrying one stone jar against their chest in both arms, the way a person carries a sleeping child, with the straw from the cart still sticking to it and the grey seal of the two hands at its neck. The two sisters of the Order walked at the head of the line with the empty cart, and the Order's flat iron wheels made the only sound on the quay.

"Peverell sent them to fetch their own," said Mara, beside her. She had come out of the pump house door with her watch in her hand and her reading hand in her coat pocket. "At five. Each to the Mole hut, and each to take one jar out of the Master's hands into their own, and carry it back, and not set it down on the town's stone between." She watched them come. "It was the Master's idea. She said a jar that's never heard a beat oughtn't to start hearing one on the way to the cell that needs it. Peverell said if it was to be carried by hand, it would be carried by the hands that were going to put it in."

The line came down the outfall walk past them, thirty-six people with thirty-six jars, and went up the iron stair into the pump house one at a time. Kiva saw the heavy man with the burn scar go by, Hart's mate, with his jar held against his apron as gently as an egg. She saw the girl of nineteen with ink on her apron go by, who had written *there wasn't anything under it but more of itself* on a slate, holding her jar so tightly that her knuckles were white. The girl saw Kiva watching her and looked away, and then looked back, and nodded.

"Hosken," said Mara. "Loveday Hosken. She has case nineteen." She was quiet. "She said to me last night that she'd never held anything worth more than a week's wage in her life, and now she was to hold a thing there wasn't any more of."

"What did you say?"

"I said there's more of her than there is of it, and to remember that if anything went wrong." Mara looked at her watch. "Twenty-four minutes to six."

The last fitter went up the stair. The two sisters turned the empty cart round on the walk and went back along the quay toward the Mole with it, without a word.

Mara put her watch in her pocket and took it out again.

"I've nothing to tell you," she said. "I've been trying to think of something since three o'clock, and there isn't anything. You know what you're doing. You knew it on Monday. I'll be on that landing with this watch, and I'll not see you from it once you're past the second mark, and I'll not know what's happening in the dock until somebody runs and tells me." She looked at the basin, grey and drained and shining, with the dark place ten paces out from the outfalls. "I'd like to know. I'll not ask anybody to run and tell me. I'll know when the Master's ward is up, because Yeo's to whistle, and that's all I need to know, and it's all I'll ask."

"That's the right amount," said Kiva.

"I know it is. That's the trouble with it." Mara looked at her then, with her dry exact face, which had gone a little grey round the mouth since Sunday. "Go on. You're to be on the dock floor by five past. Your captain told the whole loft."

Kiva went. At the corner of the walk she looked back once. Mara was standing at the foot of the iron stair with her watch open in her hand, quite still, looking at the dark place in the mud where the ring would come up.

---

The Proprietor's chair was waiting at the end of the Long Quay, where the quay met the outfall walk, a hundred paces from the pump house stair.

Kiva had to pass it. There was no other way from the outfall walk round the head of the basin to the Graving except along the quay's end, and she had known she would have to pass it since Monday. Her heart began to slow on the second bollard. She kept her left hand in her coat pocket and went on, along the far edge of the quay, by the water, as far from the chair as the stone allowed.

He was sitting in it in his dark coat with a rug over his knees and his left hand flat on the glass on its iron stand beside him. The four men who had carried him stood back by the warehouse doors. Adlam was there, with his hat in his hand. Halden's counsel was there. Kellan stood at the chair's right side in his black and gold, with his mother's watch open in his palm, and he looked at Kiva as she came level and did not say anything.

"Miss Rowan," said the Proprietor.

She stopped. She was thirty feet from him. Her still door wanted to march; she held it.

"I'll not keep you," he said. His voice was as it always was, pleasant and dry, and it carried across the stone without his lifting it. "You've a dock floor at five past, I'm told by everybody on this quay. I'll ask one thing and you may say no, and then I'll be carried to my place at thirteen minutes past and you'll not see me again this morning." He looked at her down the thirty feet, very steadily. "I asked you once at my table, on a Halden mast, for a price. You said no. The Chair has written to me since that it's not to be asked of you, and I've her letter. I'm not asking the Chair." He did not move his hand. "If two of your links fail this morning, and a third is failing, and the slack's going. Will you do the three?"

Kellan, at the chair's side, had gone very still.

Kiva did not answer at once. She had known since Thursday that somebody would ask her. She had thought it would be Yeo, or Coombe, or somebody on the mud with a cask on their trays and the water running. She had not thought it would be him, at a quarter to six, in a chair, with his hand on a glass.

"No," she said.

"Will you tell me why?"

"Because if this morning needs my three, it's not a method. It's me." She kept her voice level, because it was a thing she had decided, and decided things are said levelly. "The three live in one hand. Mine. They're three clean, and nobody can learn them, and nobody can do them after me. If the bank comes out because I did three doors on a sill, then the next bank in the country that steps has to send for me, and the one after that, and if I'm hurt or dead or at home with my aunt, it stays in."

Out at the head of the basin the outfalls went on pouring, four and four.

"That's one engine. That's the thing my aunt stood up in a dock on Sunday and said not to be." She took a breath. "The bank has to come out on things with handles. Wards and Embers and still doors and a word anybody can call. If it can't, then it can't, and we'll have found that out, and written it down."

The Proprietor looked at her for a long time.

"And a fourth," he said. "There's never been a fourth. Your healer's card doesn't have one on it."

"No. It doesn't. That's the other reason." She looked at his hand on the glass. "I don't know what a fourth costs. Nobody does. I'll not spend a thing I can't price on a morning that's meant to be done with things anybody could price."

He laughed then. It was not the short real laugh of his own table. It was quieter, and it did not stay on his face.

"That's a better answer than the one you gave me at my table," he said. "That was a girl saying no to a rich man. This is a catcher saying no to a method." He turned his head away from her, toward the basin. "Thank you. I'll not ask again. Go to your dock, Miss Rowan."

She went. As she passed out of his thirty feet her heart came up a beat, two beats, as though somebody had let go of a rope. Behind her she heard Kellan say something, low, and the Proprietor answer him, and she did not hear what either of them said.

---

At ten to six, from the far side of the basin, she saw the Master of the Order of the Even Hand come along the Long Quay from the Mole.

Clemency Thrale walked as she had walked down the Graving's steps on Sunday, slowly, with her hands folded at her waist and a sister a step behind her on either side. The white lamps were still lit along the quay, pale in the grey, and she walked between them without looking at them. She did not come to the head of the basin, where the pump house was and the Proprietor's chair, and all the noise of the morning. She went along the outer quay, on the far side of the Graving's open mouth, and stopped well back from it, by the gate's moorings, where the black gate lay against the quay's edge with the Company's boats round it: sixty paces from the sill, where she had said she would wait.

She stood there with the harbour at her back. She looked along the quay at the empty mouth of the dock, where the gate had been, and at the mud of the basin beyond it, and across the basin at the pump house.

Kiva, seventy paces off on the basin wall, felt her doors stir. They did not lie down. It was too far. They only lifted their heads, the way dogs lift their heads in a yard when the gate opens, and looked, and lay still again.

"Her hour's begun," said a voice at Kiva's shoulder. It was the sister from the pump hall, the one with her hands in her sleeves, who had paced forty on the quay. "Ten to six. She said she'd be at the mouth when it was wanted and gone by ten to seven. She said if she couldn't keep to it she'd say so first."

"Will she keep to it?"

The sister looked across at the tall grey figure on the outer quay.

"The gate goes back on the flood," she said. "The flood's an hour after the slack. Seven, or a little after." She did not say anything else. Kiva did the sum, and saw that the sister had done it already, and that the Master had done it too, on her own, in a swept hut at the end of the Mole where nothing kept time, and had come anyway.

---

At a quarter to six Yeo had blown one long note on her whistle from the basin wall above the landing, and everybody had gone to where they had been put.

Kiva would remember it afterward as a map. She stood on the basin wall for one minute before she went down, and looked, and said it to herself as Rhea said a slate, so that it would be in her head when she needed it.

At the head of the basin, the pump house. Its six outfalls along its foot, pouring into the hand of water on the mud, four and four. Above them the gallery, and on the gallery Peverell in her apron by the door, where she could see the six pump levers inside and the landing outside, both. Behind her, inside, on the two long galleries, thirty-six fitters standing at thirty-six cases with thirty-six jars at their feet in straw. Below the gallery, on the iron landing over the grating, Hart, on his knees, with his bad hand flat on the bars and his watch on the inside of his wrist. And standing behind him at the landing's rail, with her watch open, Mara.

On the basin wall above the landing, Yeo, with her slate and her whistle. Coombe beside her with her rope. The Chair, in her grey coat with her ledger under her arm, and the Clerk of the Measure beside the Chair, small and grey, with her gold chain. The two Board members.

On the mud ten paces out from the outfalls, round the dark place where the ring would come up, a circle of little stakes that Hart had driven on Tuesday evening by his book, to show where the rim would stand. And a pace west of the stakes, on the Graving side, the first ward line, four of them in a row with their hands at their sides and their doors shut: Anwen, Edda, Hesketh, Sedge. Behind them, Jude and Gower, Embers, unlit. Behind them, twenty-five paces west across the mud at the first stake, Brask and Tolver, the first still plank.

By the outfalls, between the ring's stakes and the pump house wall, Ivey's closing crew in green, five of them on their knees on the mud with their hands on their thighs, and a girl behind Ivey whom Kiva did not know, kneeling at her left hip. Ivey's second.

On the basin wall above the first stake, the Circuit's travelling mast, forty feet of black iron with its eight pens on their wires. Polglase at its desk. Perrin beside the desk with Jude's book under his arm. Orla on her crate beside Perrin, with her stick across her knees.

Back along the wall toward the pump house, above the place where the tear would be open when the ring was lifted off it: Carrow and Dray. They stood a pace apart, not touching, with their hands in their coats. Holloway's other two stood behind them. Dray had her watch out.

On the mud at the first stake, waiting to catch from Brask and Tolver: the catchers' plank. Pru, Treen, Marten, Yarrow. Behind them, the Pascoes, unlit. And twenty-five paces on, at the stone sill of the Graving's open mouth, Bannock and Wace, the second still plank, standing on the granite with the dock floor going away behind them into the shadow of the walls. On the sill beside them, at its north end against the dock wall, Aske, sitting on his bag.

Back along the outer quay, sixty paces from the sill, the Master, waiting for the last plant to be called.

And forty-five paces inside the dock, on Noa's long chalk line across the wet stone: Pettit and Lyle, the third still plank. Rhea Sorn a little behind them with her slate on her belt, and Pike beside Rhea with his half-closed hands at his sides. Noa at his desk under the lane mast against the dock's south wall, ten paces off.

Kiva went down the ladder onto the mud.

She did not stop at the ring's stakes. She walked west, past the first ward line, past Jude, who looked at her and did not say anything, past Brask and Tolver at the first stake, past the catchers' plank, where Pru's capped fingers moved very slightly as she passed, and on across the mud to the sill. She stepped up onto the granite past Bannock and Wace and Aske on his bag, and walked in onto the dock floor. The walls of the Graving went up on either side of her into the tiers, and the tiers were full. She had not looked up at them from the basin. She looked now. The whole of Saltmere that was not on the basin wall was sitting on the Graving's tiers in the grey light, under the iron and glass roof, in silence, with every lamp along every tier lit.

She walked forty-five paces up the dock floor and knelt on Noa's chalk, between Pettit's boots and Lyle's.

"Morning," said Pettit, sleepily.

"Morning."

Lyle did not say anything. She was standing very straight with her chin up. Her left foot, Kiva saw, was exactly where it should be.

Kiva laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the chalk line.

*It keeps time.*

It did. Of course it did. The dock was open to the basin and the basin was full of the town, and the bank was going four and four a hundred paces off at the head of it, and every stone in the Graving was carrying it up into her two fingers. She kept them there. She did not lean. She did not look at anything but the chalk.

---

At six o'clock, on the basin wall, Perrin declared.

Kiva could not hear him from the dock floor. She knew the words, because they had been written on Monday and said on Tuesday and she had them in her book. She heard Noa, at his desk ten paces from her, say them back as he wrote them, in his thin clear reader's voice, because a reader writes what the captain says and says what he writes.

"*Rowan, K. Catcher. Caller. Ember, declared, single, at every carry, as the reserve. A crossing, Ember to still, if a plant fails. Declared by Cade, captain, at six.*"

"*Read,*" said Noa, to himself, and to the pen.

Then, one after another, so that she heard them come along the basin and in at the dock mouth and up the dock floor to her, the planks named their far edges.

She heard Anwen's voice first, from a hundred paces off across the mud, very clear in the cold: "*First line. Pryce has the far edge.*" Then, fainter, Brask's: "*First plank. Tolver has it.*" Then Pru, high and steady: "*Catchers. Finch has the far edge.*" Then Wace, from the sill, gruff: "*Second plank. Mine.*" And then, at her own shoulder, quietly, Pettit: "*Third plank. Lyle's.*"

"*No,*" said Pike.

Everybody on the chalk turned their heads.

He had come up behind Kiva without a sound. He stood a pace behind her left shoulder with his half-closed hands at his sides and his long brown face doing the thing it did, which was very nearly a smile and was not one.

"*Third plank, Lyle's,*" said Pike, to the dock floor. "*And the caller's, mine.*" He looked down at Kiva. "Nobody's named yours. You've a far edge too. You'll be on your knees on this line with your fingers on the stone and a cask coming at you, and you'll not see what's behind you." He did not look away. "You were my second all winter. I'll be yours this morning. Two words, once. That's all."

Kiva looked up at him for a moment.

"Two words," she said. "Once."

"Once," said Pike.

Rhea, a pace behind him, had written something on her slate. Kiva did not see what. She saw Rhea's face, which did not change, and Rhea's hand, which was not quite steady on the chalk, and then she looked back at her own two fingers on the line.

*It keeps time.*

---

The light came up very slowly.

It came grey first, through the iron and glass of the Graving's roof, and lay on the wet stone of the dock floor in a long pale sheet from the open mouth to Noa's chalk and on up the dock to the landward lip. Then it came yellow, a little, along the top of the tiers on the east side, where the sun was coming up behind the high town. The lamps along the tiers went on burning, white and steady, three rows of them, and the lamps along the Long Quay outside the dock went on burning too. Somewhere a gull called and stopped.

Kiva knelt on the chalk with her two fingers on the stone, and waited, and felt the count come up into them, four and four, the town's count, the bank's, the harbour's, a hundred paces off and in every stone between.

She thought, for a moment, of nothing at all. Then she thought of the ferry, with Pru's fingers on her wrist and a voice counting. Then of a creek with a man's boot sticking out from under a fold. Then of the tun on Monday, slopping at its top hoop, and eighteen strangers letting go of a rope's end into the air above an open hand. She thought of Coombe saying *that's what testing is.* She thought that she had never once in her life done anything that was entirely inside her certificate, on her own word, that mattered, and that she might be about to, and that she did not feel anything about it at all except that her knees were cold.

"Five past," said Pettit, behind her, to nobody.

"Six past," said Noa, at his desk.

The count came up into her fingers. Four and four.

Four and four.

Four.

It stopped.

It did not stop as a sound stops. It went out of the stone the way warmth goes out of a cup, all at once and gently, and left her two fingers lying on a chalk line on a cold wet floor with nothing at all under them. Nothing pushing. Nothing keeping time. The stone was still.

She had felt it at the grating on the 9th, and yesterday, with Hart's hand beside hers. She had never felt it alone, a hundred paces from the grating, inside a dock, with nobody to tell her it had come.

"*Quiet,*" said Kiva.

She said it aloud, on the chalk, to Noa and Pettit and Lyle and Rhea and Pike, and to nobody, because she had not been able to help it. Noa looked at his clock.

"*Eight minutes past six,*" he said. "By the mast. The pump house clock's the same, to the second; I set them together at five." He wrote. "*The caller's hand at the third mark. Quiet.*"

And far off, faint, coming in at the dock mouth across a hundred paces of mud and stone, Kiva heard Hart's voice on the landing say the same word, a breath after her.

She got up off her knees.

"Third mark," she said to Pettit and Lyle. "It's good. It's still."

She did not wait for them to answer. She turned and walked down the dock floor toward the mouth, fast, not running, because Coombe had said a caller who runs to her marks arrives at them with her pulse in her fingers. At the sill she knelt again on the granite between Bannock's boots and Wace's and laid the two fingers on the stone, where the iron of the gate's seating went into it.

Nothing. Still.

"Second mark," she said. "It's good."

Aske, on his bag at the north end of the sill, did not say anything. He was looking at her hand.

She went down off the sill onto the mud. Twenty-five paces. The mud took her boots to the ankle and let them go, and took them again. The catchers' plank stood at the first stake, Pru and Treen and Marten and Yarrow, and she went past them and knelt at the stake and laid her two fingers on the wet wood where it went into the mud.

Nothing.

"First mark," she said to Brask and Tolver. "Good."

And then she looked up, east, across the last twenty-five paces of mud to the outfalls, and saw it.

It had begun to come up while she walked. It was coming up now out of the dark place ten paces from the pump house wall, inside Hart's ring of little stakes, all round at once, grey as ash, with the mud sliding off it in long sheets and the white frost forming along its lip. She saw the staves come up out of the mud, long and grey and a little proud, one beside another all the way round, like the staves of a cask. She saw the chime come up, a thick grey rim at the top, frosted. She saw the brown water stand up inside it, held, flat, higher than the mud and higher than the water round its foot, with nothing under it but mud and nothing holding it but the ring.

It rose. It rose a foot, two feet, three. The hand of water round its foot went white where it touched the staves and stiffened. It rose past the height of Anwen's shoulder, in the first ward line a pace from its west rim, so that Anwen was looking up at it.

It stopped.

On the landing, Kiva saw Hart lift his brass level in his left hand and sight across it at the staff. She could not hear him from the first stake. She saw Mara, behind him at the rail, write something on a sheet against the rail with her watch hand.

On the wall, Polglase called it, so that the whole basin heard.

"*Four foot eleven,*" called Polglase. "*Mr. Hart's level. At nine minutes past.*"

The surface of the held water trembled. It trembled in rings from a place a little off the middle, closer together on the side nearest the outfalls, leaning at the pipes. Four times, and stopped. Four times, and stopped.

Kiva walked the last twenty-five paces through the mud to the first ward line and stood at the end of it, at Anwen's left, a pace from the grey staves, with the cold coming off them onto her face and the held water standing level with her eyes.

She lit her Ember.

It came up glad under her breastbone, as it always came, as though it had been waiting by the door. The bar came down her right fist, short and bright, over the knuckles that had peeled on Sunday and were pink and new. She held it low at her side and did not lift it. On the wall above the mast, Polglase's eight pens moved together and wrote one line.

"*Read,*" said Polglase. "*Rowan. Ember. Single.*"

Kiva looked along the first ward line. Anwen, at her right, with her face very white and her hands at her sides. Edda beyond. Hesketh. Sedge at the end, with her pale hair tied back hard and her jaw set. Behind them Jude and Gower with their hands open, unlit, waiting for the word.

She looked up at the wall, where Dray stood above the tear's place with her watch in her hand.

"*Dray,*" said Kiva.

"*One,*" said Dray.

Her voice came down off the wall flat and hard, like a dropped plank, and went across the mud and the held water and the outfalls and in at the open mouth of the Graving to the chalk forty-five paces inside. Kiva heard Pettit, a hundred paces off, take it up under his breath.

"*Two. Three. Four. Rest.*"

"*First line,*" said Kiva. "*Light.*"

Four wards came up together on the mud, low and silver, at the height of a girl's knee, a pace from the grey staves. They came up still, as walls come, and stood. The held water went on leaning at the pipes.

"*One,*" said Dray. "*Two. Three. Four. Rest.*"

"*Keep it,*" said Kiva.

The four walls leaned. They went forward a hand's breadth together toward the staves on *one*, and pressed, and pressed, and came off on the *rest*. On the next *one* they leaned again: four girls from four circuits who had beaten each other on floors all winter, listening to one voice on a wall and not to each other.

On the fourth count, the rings in the held water moved.

They came off the outfall side the way a cloud's shadow comes off a field, and went round until they were closest together on the side toward the ward line. Toward Anwen. Toward Kiva, standing at Anwen's left with her Ember lit. The whole held water, four feet eleven inches of it, level with their eyes, leaned toward them across a pace of frosted mud, and kept leaning, four and four, while Dray counted.

It was looking at them.

"*Far edges,*" said Kiva. Her voice was steady. She was surprised by that.

"*Pryce has it,*" said Anwen.

"*Ready,*" said Jude, behind them, and lit, and Gower lit beside him, two bars on two right fists, bright as pokers.

Kiva watched the rings come round to her one more time. *One. Two. Three. Four.*

On the rest, she said it.

"*Take.*"
