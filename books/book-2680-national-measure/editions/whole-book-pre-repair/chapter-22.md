# Chapter 22 — Kiva: Six of Six

Aske said the price at a quarter to seven on Thursday, at the foot of the gate mast, with his bag between his feet and the sea fog lying on the timber pond again like wet wool.

"The fifth went to a hand below the elbow," he said. "It sulked three nights. It was warm on Sunday at seven, and it's been warm every morning since, and I've seen it every morning." He did not look at Orla on her crate. He looked at Kiva. "I think this one goes to the elbow. I think four nights. And I think that if there's a seventh, whatever it's for and whenever it is, I'll want to have seen this hand warm on more than one morning before I let it go. That's not on the card yet. I'm saying it now so that it's said before and not after."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"That's the price, as near as I can say it." He picked up his bag. "Six of six."

There were more people at the mast than there had been a week ago. The Ropewalk had come again, not the whole school this time, but Carrow and Lyle and their two, and a few others in green. Dray was there on her stick, at the end of the Meridian row. The Clerk of the Measure was not. Noa had his tape threaded and Polglase had his, and Polglase had a book under his arm that Kiva had not seen him carry before. It was thick and black, with a cloth spine, and he put it on his reading desk beside the tape and laid his hand on it.

Perrin stepped up between the two desks.

"*Hand,*" he said. "*To Ember. Rowan, changing on three.*"

She went to the first cross.

It went as the other five had gone. She had stopped expecting it to go any other way, and she did not trust that, and she let it go as it went. The ward came up at her hip. Orla leaned from her crate and struck the iron leg of Noa's desk with her stick, once. The fold, from the far edge, to a penny. The shut. Nobody. The cold came into the hand, at once, a long way up. Then the Ember, glad. Six paces, low, through the fog, onto the second cross.

"One cliff," said Noa. "Twenty-six. Thirty in all."

"*Six,*" said Orla.

The hand was white. Kiva did not hold it up. She knew how far the white went by where the cold stopped, and the cold stopped at the elbow. It stopped in the joint itself. There was an ache there, deep, in the bone, as though the elbow had been knocked on a door frame a long time ago and had only now remembered it.

She put the hand in her coat against her ribs. The disk was cold.

Noa was bent over his tape. So was Polglase. This time Kiva did not go to the desks. She stood on the cross and waited.

"There's a notch," said Noa.

"Six seconds," said Polglase.

He had said it first. Noa looked up at him. The old man had his own dividers out, a small brass pair, and he had measured before Noa had.

"Six," said Noa, after a moment, measuring. "On every pen. Six seconds after the cliff. A week ago, seven."

"I said I'd looked," said Polglase. He opened the black book. "I went back through my book of times. Forty years of hairs on this mast. I'd never looked at them all together, Mr. Bexley. I only wrote down the minute." He turned the book on the desk so that Noa could see. "There's a hair at the springs, most springs, as far back as the book goes. One or two. Since the ninth month there's been a hair at every reading of every candidate at every spring, and some at the neaps." He took his spectacles off. "I didn't notice. I'd written it all down and I didn't notice. I said it was the harbour."

"Is it?" said Noa.

"I've no idea," said Polglase. "I'd like it written that I said so."

Noa wrote. Kiva watched him from the cross. She did not need to see the words. She knew he was writing *six seconds,* and *seven a week ago,* and *eleven in Meridian.* She knew he would not draw a line between them and anything, and that he would leave a space.

At the end of the Meridian row, Dray was looking at the mast. Her face did what it always did. But she had come forward a pace, without her stick, and was standing on the boards on both feet.

---

Orla signed at a quarter past seven, at Noa's desk, with Noa's pen, in the Chair's ledger that he carried in his satchel.

She did not say anything first. She took the ledger and read the line that Ysra Crest had written in the second month, in her level hand: *The fifth and sixth to be seen at the Saltmere gate mast, read by N. Bexley, her sponsor present, one in seven days and not faster. Not certified until the sixth.* Then she read the two lines that Noa had written under it, the fifth and the sixth, with their figures. Then she wrote.

*Rowan, K. The second crossing, hand to Ember. Seen six times on sanctioned masts, read by the office's reader: four at Meridian, two at the Saltmere gate. Certified, on my line, under her healer's card. O. Dane.*

She blotted it with her sleeve. She gave the ledger back to Noa.

"That's six," she said to Kiva. "It's on your certificate. It's in your name. I've only signed it."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. You did six. I sat on a crate." Orla took the green book out of her coat. She had had it since Sunday. She held it out. "Here. Your book. I read it."

Kiva took it.

She knew Orla had read the ferry. She knew Orla had read *I kept my mouth shut,* on the right, small. She waited for Orla to say something about it.

Orla did not. She looked at Kiva for a moment with her crooked face quite still, and then she looked at the mast, and then at her stick.

"It's a good book," she said. "It's got everything in it that ought to be in it. I'd say it's the best I've ever read, and I've read twenty years of candidates' books." She got up off the crate. "Go and eat. Your healer wants that hand at noon."

Kiva went to the edge of the boards and stood by the black water with the book in her right hand and the cold hand in her coat.

It was certified: two crossings now, Ember to still from the summer and hand to Ember from this morning, in her name, on her certificate, on ordinary terms. They were one in seven days, read on a sanctioned mast, declared to a count, with the price said first, and anybody could read the terms and hold her to them.

She had done three doors three times, and it had cost two fingers and an arm for four days, and nobody could hold her to it, because nobody knew what it was. It was on no certificate; the Chair had written that it was not to be asked.

This was different, a thing with a page and figures and a sponsor's name and a reader's. It was small: a hand door folding a load, and an Ember carrying it six paces. Pru could fold; Perrin could carry, in his way; a girl in the Ropewalk could probably do both at once, laid, and never feel the gap.

But it was hers, and it was written down, and she could do it again next week. She found that she was glad of it in a way that she had never been glad of the three.

She opened the book and wrote in the fourth column.

*Thursday the 10th. The sixth crossing, hand to Ember. Certified. Cold. As before.*

And on the left, under it: *A notch at six seconds. Every pen. Mr. Polglase says he has looked now.*

Mr. Adlam came to the loft at half past eleven, as he had promised, with two books under his arm and his hat in his hand.

Seven was there: Perrin, Anwen and Jude at the long table under the south window, which Halden had left empty when it went to its lane. Pru was on the end of a bed with a cord in her lap. Kiva had come up from the gate. Noa had come with Mr. Adlam, because Mr. Adlam had asked him to. He stood by the stove with his satchel and looked uncomfortable.

"I'll show you first," said Mr. Adlam. "Then I'll ask. That's fairest."

He laid the two books on the table. They were bench books. Kiva had seen them in the Works: a bench book was the book that lived beside a bench where cells were made and tuned, and every cell that went across the bench was written in it, with its count. Nobody read them afterward. They went into a press when they were full.

The first was old, in boards gone brown, with a label on the spine in faded ink. *Saltmere Refinery. Bench Two. 2402–2431.* The second was new, in black cloth. *Saltmere Refinery. Bench Two. 2679–.*

"Our refinery here has stepped its banks since it was built," said Mr. Adlam. "Every cell that's gone into a stepped row on that bench has had its count written down when it was salted. *Fell in at so many to the minute.* It's a habit. Nobody needs it once the row's beating." He opened the old book at a page he had marked with a slip. "Here's 2402. A row of twelve, salted late with a pinch off the foundation's stock. *Fell in at forty-eight to the minute.*"

He turned to another slip.

"2431. Forty-eight." He laid the old book aside and opened the new one. "Last month. *Fell in at fifty-seven.*"

Nobody said anything.

"I went to the press at the refinery," said Mr. Adlam. "On Saturday, because I'd an hour. I took out every bench book there is. There are thirty-one of them. I wrote down one count from every year." He took a folded sheet from his coat and laid it on the table. "Forty-eight for a hundred and fifty years, near enough. Forty-nine about 2550. Fifty in 2604. Fifty-two by 2650. Fifty-five in the ninth month of last year. Fifty-seven now." He put his finger on the last figure. "It was slower once. It's quickening. And it's quickening faster now than it ever has."

"The Step," said Noa from the stove. "The beat. Four and four."

"The beat," said Mr. Adlam. "The patent doesn't give a count. It never needed to. A stepped row beats as one. Who cares how fast?" He looked round the table, and he looked, Kiva thought, genuinely astonished, as a man looks who has found that his own front door opens the other way. "I've been with the technical office for sixteen years. I've read the patent more times than any man alive, I should think. I had no idea. Nobody had any idea. Nobody looks at a bench book once the bench is built."

"Is it the cells?" said Perrin. "Getting quicker?"

"It can't be the cells. The cells are new every time; they're salted off the same stock. It isn't the stock; the stock's in a jar." Mr. Adlam sat down on the bench, rather suddenly. "It's whatever the stock keeps time *with.* And I don't know what that is. I thought I did. I thought it was the stock."

Noa had come away from the stove. He stood at the end of the table looking at the folded sheet.

"May I copy that?" he said.

"I brought it for you to copy." Mr. Adlam pushed it across. "I'd like it in your columns, Mr. Bexley, under my name. As before."

Noa took out his notebook. He did not open it at once. Kiva saw him look at it, the black notebook with three columns in it that he had carried since the spring: people, and rifts and spawn, and Saltmere. She saw him think about a fourth, and not draw it. Then he opened it and wrote the figures, in his narrow hand, at the foot of the third.

"The harbour bank," said Kiva. "The Regiment. What's its count?"

Mr. Adlam looked at her.

"I timed it on Saturday," he said. "At the pump house wall. Fifty-seven." He was quiet a moment. "It went into step at twenty past two on the seventeenth of the ninth month. Nobody salted it. Nothing was ever salted into it off any stock. And it's beating at exactly the count that my bench salted a row at last month." He shook his head. "I'd like somebody to tell me I'm wrong about that. Nobody has."

"That's the first thing," said Mr. Adlam. "The second's an offer. I'll make it plainly."

He put his hat on the table, crown down, and laid his hands on either side of it.

"You've two drams of plain ward-trace in a sealed tin. It combed silent on the seventh at your gate. Everybody in Saltmere knows; a clerk of the Board told a clerk of the Company, who told the whole quay." He looked at Jude, who had the book. "I'd like to buy it. Twelve drams of combed ward-trace, clean, at your gate, for the two. Six for one. That's a fair price this winter; it may be a low one by the summer. I'll not go higher, because I'd not want anybody to say Halden had bought plain ash off students at a price they couldn't refuse."

"What's it for?" said Jude.

It was his question. He had asked it of everybody. Mr. Adlam did not seem surprised to be asked it.

"For my comb," he said. "For my seventh reed. I've a reed that's cut to the Step, and every tin I hold it over says *trace* or *full* or worse. I've nothing left that's never heard a beat to hold it over and hear it say nothing. If I can't hear it say nothing, I don't know what it's hearing when it says something." He spread his hands. "I'd use a pinch a month. I'd keep the rest under seal. I'd write down every pinch. That's the truth of it."

Jude did not answer him. He opened the team's book on the table, at a page near the back, and turned it round so that Mr. Adlam could read it, and then he read it aloud anyway.

"*Sealed, medical, W, plain: two drams. Eight-tenths off a husk on the Sag in the fourth month, 2680. One and two-tenths given at the house of the Even Hand on Friday the 27th of the second month, to make good what eased M. Rowan's hand.*" He did not look up. "*For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.* That's under both lines. In my hand. Signed by our captain and our catcher. And in the Order's book, signed by a sister."

"Ah," said Mr. Adlam.

"Twelve drams," said Jude. "We've five held, at the moment. Five, near enough, for four boards. I'd want it said that twelve drams would change what we can do on every one of them." He shut the book. "And it's not ours to sell. It's for a hand."

"Then it's no," said Perrin. "I'm sorry, Mr. Adlam. It's under my name. It's no."

Kiva had not said anything. Mr. Adlam looked at her.

"No," she said. "It was given so that we'd not be asked this. The Master said we'd be offered a great deal. She said if it were ever spent on anything else, she'd want to be told." Kiva held his eyes. "I'd sooner not have to write and tell her."

Mr. Adlam sat for a moment with his hands on either side of his hat.

"I thought it would be no," he said. "I'd have thought rather less of you if it weren't." He took up the hat. "I'll find a pinch somewhere. There's a reference shelf in Meridian. Halloran may take pity on me, though I doubt it." He stood, and gathered up his two books. "Thank you for hearing me out. I'll not ask again. I'll ask somebody else, and I'll tell them you said no, and why, because it's a good answer."

He went to the stair. At the top he stopped.

"I'm told there's a wire for you at the Line's agent on the quay, Miss Rowan," he said. "It came at eleven. I didn't open it. I'm told it's from the seven."

---

The Line's agent in Saltmere was a ship's chandler with a brass plate and a telegraph instrument behind his counter. He gave Kiva the wire across the counter in a brown envelope with the Line's mark on it, and did not look at it while she read it.

*To K. Rowan, bearer, at Saltmere. Heard, at a sitting of the seven, the request of A. Halden to see the plate. The bearer may show it in her own hand, at a table, to whom she chooses, if she chooses. No hand but hers. Not weighed, not tested, not drawn, not copied. Four hands, three fists. For the seven, watch officer, Sag Line.*

She read it twice. Perrin read it over her shoulder.

"Four to three," he said. "That's close."

"It's enough." Kiva folded it. "It says *if she chooses.*"

"Do you?"

She thought about it, standing at a chandler's counter that smelled of tar and new rope, with her cold hand in her coat.

She had never shown it to anybody on purpose but once, in a room at Ferren with a canvas book on an oak table. It was the one thing anybody had trusted her with before they knew what she was. It did not do anything. It did not teach, or speak, or answer, or open a door. It had been warm once, after her first true crossing, and cold ever since, and she wrote that down after every crossing in the fourth column because a page said she must.

And a man who had been alive in 2604 had asked to see it. He had asked properly, through a Line that had voted. Three fists had said no and four hands had said she might choose.

She did not know what he wanted with it. She would not know unless she went.

"Yes," she said. "If he'll have my captain and my sponsor in the room."

"He'll have whoever you bring," said Perrin. "Adlam said so. On the stair, after." He took the wire from her and read it again. "He said, *Tell her the Proprietor expects nothing less.*"

Aske had the hand at noon, when they came back up the loft stair, by the north window.

"Cold to the elbow," he said. He held two fingers in the crook of it for a full minute. "And it aches there. In the joint."

"Yes."

"You'd not have said, if I hadn't asked."

"I'd have said at four."

"At four I'd have known." He wrote. *Cold to the elbow at noon on Thursday. An ache in the joint, reported when asked.* "I'll see it at four, and tonight, and in the morning. I said four nights. I'll be glad to be wrong."

At four it was cold to the elbow still. At ten that night it was cold to a hand below. Aske wrote both down without a word about either.

Kellan Renn had never been in a room with the Proprietor, and on Thursday evening at seven he sat at the man's own table.

It was in the Counting House on the Long Quay. That was Halden's house in Saltmere, and had been since before the pump house was built: a tall narrow building of grey stone with a great many windows, all lit, and a brass plate by the door so old that the letters had worn to shadows. Inside, every room had lamps, and every lamp burned white and steady without a flicker. Kellan had counted nineteen of them between the door and the dining room. He had found himself counting them as Rowan counted things, without meaning to.

Halden License had won its two seeding lanes. It had won them well. Pettit had been pleased in his slow sleepy way, and Yarrow had been loudly pleased, and Hesketh had gone for a walk on the Mole by herself. And at five a man in Halden's livery had come to the loft with a card. *The Proprietor asks the Halden License four to dine at seven.*

So they sat at a long table in a long room with white lamps, the four of them, in their best black and gold. At the head of the table sat a man who had owned the Halden foundation and its pattern since before any of their grandparents had been born.

He did not look it. That was the first thing. Kellan had known that Glory stopped a body aging, as everybody knew, the way one knew that the sea was salt. He had not known what it would look like across a table. The Proprietor looked perhaps fifty-five. His hair was dark, going grey at the temples, cut close. His face was long and lean and pleasant, with deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth. He wore a plain dark coat with no gold on it at all.

His left hand lay on the table beside his plate, on a case.

The case was glass, with a brass frame, about the size of a small box of cigars. Inside it, on a bed of black velvet, there was a cell. It was an old cell, very old, of a pattern that Kellan had seen only in drawings: a squat iron drum the size of a man's fist, with a brass cap and a single brass handle, black with age. The Proprietor's left hand lay flat on the glass over it. It lay there through the soup and through the fish. When he lifted his wine glass, he lifted it with his right. When the meat came, a man in livery cut it for him without being asked, and he ate it with a fork in his right hand.

The hand on the glass did not move once.

"You're looking at my hand, Mr. Renn," said the Proprietor.

Kellan had been. He did not pretend otherwise.

"Yes, sir."

"Everybody does. I'd think the less of a blade who didn't." The Proprietor's voice was pleasant and rather dry, with no particular accent. It was the voice of a man who had talked to a great many people across a great many tables and had stopped performing for any of them. "That's the foundation cell. The first one that was ever stepped, in 2391, on a bench in a shed in a yard that's now under the Meridian tram depot. Everything Halden has ever salted, it salted from that. I keep its count."

"Keep it, sir?"

"Hold it. I've held it since I was a young man." He did not take the hand off the glass. "I eat with it there. I sleep with it there, on a table by the bed. I've not had that hand off this glass for longer than a minute in seventy years. I don't know what would happen if I did." He smiled slightly. "I've never been curious enough to find out. That's a failing in a man of science, Mr. Adlam tells me. He's quite right."

Pettit, beside Kellan, was very still. Yarrow had stopped eating. Hesketh was looking at the cell with her lips pressed together.

Kellan found that he could not look away from it either. He was aware of his own heart, which he was never aware of except in a lane. It was going slowly. It was going more slowly than it should have been, after a good dinner in a warm room. He thought of a pump house hall with six iron pumps going up and down together, and of the bills on the warehouse doors, and he moved his chair a few inches back from the table without knowing why.

"Ah," said the Proprietor. "You've felt it. Move as far as you like, Mr. Renn. Young people fall in with it. I've had clerks faint." He said it quite kindly. "The far end of the table's better. Nobody's ever fainted at the far end."

Kellan did not move further. He sat with his heart going slowly and his hands on the cloth and waited.

The Proprietor asked each of them about their lanes. He asked Pettit about his captaincy and listened to the answer. He asked Yarrow about the Ropewalk's lay and listened to that, and asked two questions that Yarrow could not answer and did not seem to mind. He asked Hesketh about her wall. Then he turned to Kellan.

"You stood for Team Seven last year," he said. "On their book. Mr. Adlam says you still do, for calls in Meridian."

"Yes, sir."

"And you stood against them in the second month, in a Marshal's lane, and wrote them down for your captain on four sheets of our yellow foolscap. And then told them, item by item, what you'd written." The Proprietor turned his wine glass with his right hand. "Mr. Pettit showed me. I thought it was the most honest piece of work I've seen from a blade in some years."

Kellan did not answer. He did not know how.

"I'd like to ask you about Miss Rowan," said the Proprietor.

There it was. Kellan had known since five, when the card came, that it would come to this.

"I'll answer what I can, sir."

"That's a careful answer."

"It's a careful subject." Kellan made himself look at the man and not at the cell. "I'll tell you what's on the record. Anybody could tell you that. She's seated in the Third Division, unclassified, and catcher on Seven. Her Draw read one-six on the new year. On the Hold rig she reads Edge three-one, Ward four-five, Stance six-five. On one door the frame says Flame. On two it gives dashes. She has two crossings certified; the second was this morning, on that mast down the quay. She's read under the rule. One crossing in seven days. Her two fingers don't report." He drew a breath. "That's the sum. I'd expect nothing less of anybody who asked."

"And the three?"

"Three clean," said Kellan. "In all. A yard, the Pit, the Old Yard, last year. It's not on her certificate. The Chair's written that it's not to be asked of her." He found that his voice had gone level in the way that it went level in a lane, when he was carrying a thing carefully. "I know her count for two doors. I learned it on her floor, on her book, and I can't unlearn it. I'll not say it at this table, sir. I'd not say it at any table that wasn't hers."

The Proprietor looked at him for a long moment. His left hand lay on the glass. His face was quite pleasant.

"Would she do it," he said, "if she were asked?"

"It's written that she's not to be."

"By her Chair. I'm not her Chair." The Proprietor set his glass down. "I'm asking you, Mr. Renn, as a man who's stood in a lane with her, whether she would. If somebody asked her plainly, and named a price, and gave her the mast, and her healer, and every condition she liked. Whether she'd do it."

Kellan thought of a girl on a frozen yard with her arm gone white to above the elbow, telling Aske to say the price. He thought of a shed step in the second month and four people in a row with a ledger. *It's not for a floor.*

"No, sir," he said. "I don't think she would."

"Why?"

"Because it lives in one hand. And it cost her two fingers the first time, and an arm for four days the third, and her healer doesn't know it would come back a fourth." Kellan heard himself, and kept going. "And because she's spent the whole winter making something that three people can do instead. She'd say so. She'd say it to your face. I'd expect nothing else."

The table was very quiet. Pettit was looking at his plate. Yarrow had put his fork down.

The Proprietor did not seem offended. He seemed, if anything, pleased, as a man is pleased when a fact turns out to be the shape he had guessed.

"Thank you, Mr. Renn," he said. "That's the clearest account of her I've had from anybody, and I've had a good many." He turned to Pettit. "Mr. Pettit, your four is a credit to my house. I'd like you to know I think so. You stand on Monday, I'm told, at the Bench."

He talked to Pettit about the Bench for the rest of the meal. He did not mention Rowan again.

Kellan walked back along the Long Quay at half past nine with the others. The white lamps stood on their fluted posts without a flicker. Pettit walked beside him and was quiet for a long way.

"He's going to ask her," said Pettit at last.

"Yes."

"For the three. He asked you if she'd do it. He doesn't ask a thing like that over dinner unless he means to ask it again somewhere else." Pettit put his hands in his pockets. "I didn't know he did that. Ask for things. I thought he only owned them."

"He asked properly," said Kellan. "He asked the Line about the plate, and waited for a vote."

"I know he did. I'm not saying he's wicked." Pettit was quiet for a few paces. "He's the best man I ever sat at a table with. I think he's the cleverest. I think he's been right about more things than anybody alive." He sounded troubled, which Kellan had never heard him sound. "I'd not want him to ask me for anything I couldn't give."

They came to Quiller's. The loft's south window was dark; Old Four had gone to bed, by the sound. The north window had a candle in it.

"I'll be a minute," said Kellan.

Pettit looked at him, and at the candle, and nodded, and went up.

---

Kiva was sitting on her bed by the north window with her book on her knee and her left hand inside her coat. Aske was asleep in the bed by the door; Kellan had to step over his boots. Perrin was asleep with Coombe's rope for a pillow. Pru was awake. She looked at Kellan coming down the room, and then she turned over toward the wall, which in Pru was a kind of tact.

Kellan sat on the end of Jude's bed, across the narrow gap from Kiva's. Jude did not wake.

"He asked about you," he said. "At dinner."

"I thought he might."

"I told him what's on the record. The figures. The two crossings. The three clean, and that the Chair's written it's not to be asked." He kept his voice low. "I didn't tell him your count. I said I'd not, at any table but yours."

"Thank you."

"I'm not telling you so that you'll thank me." He looked at the candle. "He asked if you'd do it, if you were asked. Plainly, with a price, and a mast, and your healer, and every condition you liked."

Kiva did not move.

"I said I didn't think you would," said Kellan. "He asked why. I told him. I don't know if I'd any business to." He was quiet a moment. "He was pleased. As if I'd told him something he'd guessed. I don't know what he's going to ask you tomorrow. I don't know if it's the three. I think it is." He turned his head and looked at her. "I'm in his four. I wear his colours. I'll stand in a lane for his house on Thursday and I want to win it. That's one side. The other side is that I told you this anyway."

"I know you did."

"Don't go alone."

"I'm not going alone. Perrin's coming, and Orla." Kiva took her hand out of her coat, and looked at it. It was cold to a hand below the elbow, and the two fingers lay with the others. "Kellan. What's he like?"

Kellan thought about it honestly. He had always done that, and he had always done the sum aloud.

"He's the most courteous man I ever met," he said. "He listens to every word. He doesn't pretend about anything, not even his hand. He's got a cell under it that every Halden bank in the country was salted from, and he's held it for seventy years, and he says he's never been curious enough to let go." He paused. "And when you sit near him, your heart slows down. Mine did. It went to his count. He told me to move my chair."

"His count."

"Whatever that cell's keeping. He said young people fall in with it." Kellan stood up. "Sit at the far end of the table. He said nobody's ever fainted at the far end."

He went down the room, over Aske's boots, to the stair. At the top he turned round.

"He'll ask nicely," he said. "That's what I'd be careful of. Not that he'll ask. That he'll ask so nicely, and be so right about so many things, that you'll want to give him whatever it is just to have been asked by somebody like that." He hesitated. "I did, a bit. At the table. Add that in."

He went down. Kiva sat with the book on her knee and her cold hand in her lap.

Across the room, Pru said, without turning over, "He's nice, Renn. I always thought he was a bit stuck up. He isn't. He's only careful."

"I know."

"I'll not tell anybody he came," said Pru, and went to sleep, or pretended to.
