# Chapter 23 — Kiva: The Proprietor

At seven on Friday morning the hand was cold to the middle of the forearm, and Aske wrote it down, and said, "That's three nights to go, if I'm right."

At nine Kiva was in the keeper's room at the pump house, watching her aunt write a page that could be found wrong.

Mara had asked her on Thursday night, by a note left on the loft stair. *Come at nine. I'd like you to carry a thing to the Clerk with me. M.* So she had come down through the low town in a thin rain, with the gratings rattling on the count, and found them all in the keeper's room with the door shut. Mara was at the table with a clean sheet of the pump house's fault paper and a pen. Hart stood at the window with his book of slacks open in his left hand. Peverell was at the press with the pump house seal, an iron thing like a nutcracker. Yeo was by the door. Rusk was in the corner on a stool with his thumbs in his waistcoat.

"It's a prediction," said Mara, without looking up. "Mr. Hart's and mine. It falls due on the morning of Wednesday the 23rd."

"What happens on the 23rd?"

"A spring tide. The next one. At dawn." Mara wrote a line, and stopped. "If we're right, the basin goes still at a minute that we've written down. The ring stands up at the outfall at the same minute, at a height we've written down. It lies down again when the slack ends. And while it stands, the water inside it keeps the bank's count, and nothing else in the basin keeps anything."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we're wrong, and it's under seal, and everybody can see exactly how." Mara dipped her pen. "That's the point of it."

Kiva understood. She had understood since the fifth month of last year, at a kitchen table, watching her aunt write down what she had been told about a dream and then take it to the Library to have the hour put on it. A page sealed before the thing happened was worth something. A page written after was worth nothing. That was all a seal was for.

Mara wrote. It took her a long time. She stopped at every line and looked at Hart, and Hart looked at his book and said a figure, and she wrote it. Twice they disagreed, quietly, about a minute. Once Peverell said from the press, "Not *the pumps.* The pumps aren't in it. *The basin,*" and Mara crossed out a word and wrote another.

At half past nine she put the pen down and turned the sheet round so that they could all read it.

*Saltmere harbour pump house. The 11th of the third month, 2681, at half past nine in the morning.*

*At the low water of the dawn on Wednesday the 23rd of this month, a spring tide, the slack in the basin will begin at nine minutes past six by the pump house clock, and not before seven past nor after twelve past. It will last not less than eighteen minutes nor more than twenty-two.*

*When it begins, the ring at the outfall will rise from the mud. It will stand at four feet eleven inches on the basin's staff, and not less than four feet ten. It will lie down when the slack ends.*

*While it stands, the water inside it will keep the bank's count, four and four, at fifty-seven to the minute or faster. Nothing else in the basin will keep any count, by the hand on the grating, though the pumps run.*

*We do not know why. We write it so that it can be found wrong.*

*J. Hart, fitter. M. Rowan, Grade Two.*

"Fifty-seven or faster," said Kiva.

"Mr. Adlam timed the bank on Saturday," said Mara. "He came to the door with his watch. He told me what his bench books say." She did not look at Kiva. "I'd not write a slower count than the one we have. If it's quicker by the 23rd, that goes in too."

"*Witnessed,*" said Peverell. She came from the press and took up the pen and wrote at the foot, in her small crabbed hand. *Witnessed, Peverell, keeper, who does not know why either.* She gave the pen to Yeo. Yeo wrote *Yeo, officer* under it, in the brisk upright hand from the letter under the brass weight, and gave the pen back to Mara.

Rusk had not moved from his stool.

"Mr. Rusk," said Mara.

"I'll not sign it." Rusk opened his eyes. "It's not a Works document. It's a fitter's and a reader's. If the Works signed it, the Works would be saying it believed it, and the Works doesn't believe anything that hasn't happened." He looked at the sheet for a long moment. "I'll say I watched it written. You may put that."

Mara wrote it. *Watched written: Rusk, Examiner.* Then she folded the sheet in three, and Peverell put the pump house seal on it, and pressed it, and held it for a slow count of five.

"The Clerk keeps a press," said Mara. "For the Board. Sealed papers, with the hour on them." She stood up and put the folded sheet into an envelope and held it out to Kiva. "I'd like you to carry it. You know how a seal's carried."

Kiva took it in her right hand.

---

The Clerk of the Measure kept her press in a room at the back of the Board's tent, behind a canvas wall, in a black iron box with three locks.

She took the envelope from Kiva without a word, and looked at the seal, and looked at Mara. Then she wrote on the outside of it in a small precise hand. *Received at the Board's press at seven minutes past ten on the 11th of the third month, from M. Rowan, by the hand of K. Rowan. Not opened.* She put it in the box, and turned the three keys, one after another.

"When is it due?" she said.

"The 23rd," said Mara. "At dawn."

"Then it'll be opened on the 23rd at noon, in front of whoever you name and whoever I do." The Clerk folded her hands. "Is it about the harbour bank, Mrs. Rowan?"

"It's about the basin."

"I see." The Clerk looked at her for a long moment, with her shut face. "I'd like you to know that I've had three letters this week from the Harbour Committee, asking whether the Works intends to stop the Regiment during the Measure. I've told them the Board does not intend anything. I've told them the Measure is read on the floor of the Graving and not in the basin." She paused. "I'll tell them now that there's a paper in my press. I'll not tell them what it says."

"Thank you."

"I'm not doing you a favour, Mrs. Rowan. I'm keeping a press." The Clerk turned away. "Good morning."

Outside, on the boards of the timber pond, the rain had stopped. The gate mast stood black against a white sky. Mara walked to the edge of the boards and stood looking at the black water, and Kiva went and stood beside her.

"It's the eleventh," said Kiva.

"I know what day it is."

"Seventy-seven years."

"I read the date in the keeper's book. I read it in the Ferren one in the autumn." Mara had her left hand in her coat pocket. "I'd have chosen any other day to seal a paper, if the slack had let me. It didn't." She was quiet for a moment. "You're going to the Long Quay tonight."

"At eight. With Perrin and Orla."

"To show him the plate."

"If I choose. The seven said I may."

Mara was quiet for some time. A gull came down on the black water of the timber pond and sat there, turning slowly.

"He was alive that night," said Mara. "He was in this town. The refinery on the Long Quay is his house." She did not look at Kiva. "I've read a line in a keeper's book about which lamps stayed lit. I expect you have too."

"Yes."

"Then I'll not say anything else about it." Mara took her hand out of her pocket and laid it, very briefly, on Kiva's arm, on the cold one, above the wrist. "Except that you needn't tell him anything at all. You needn't tell anybody. That's the only thing I know for certain about any of this. It's yours."

She went away across the boards toward the Works' lodging. Kiva stood by the water with the cold arm and watched the gull.

---

"If he asks for the three," said Kiva, "I'll say no."

She said it at six, in the loft, to Perrin and Orla. They were sitting at the long table under the south window, the three of them, with the light going and the stove not yet lit. Orla had come up the stair a step at a time with her stick, and had sat down at the table's end, and had said, *Well,* and nothing else.

"I know you will," said Perrin.

"I'm saying it so that it's said. Before, not after." Kiva had the Line's wire on the table in front of her, flattened. "I don't want either of you to say anything. If he asks. It's mine to say."

"It's under my name," said Perrin.

"The team is. The plate isn't. The three aren't." She looked at him. "I'd like you there. I'd like you to hear it. I don't want you to say it for me."

Perrin looked down at his big hands on the table, and then he went the colour that he went when he was thinking very hard.

"All right," he said. "I'll sit there like a post. I've had practice."

Orla had not said anything. She sat at the end of the table with her stick across her knees and her crooked face quite still.

"I'll come," she said. "I'll sit where I'm put. I'll not open my mouth unless I'm asked a question to my face." She looked at the wire. "I've not been asked a question to my face by anybody in this town but one, and I answered it badly. I'd sooner not do it twice in a week."

Kiva looked at her.

Orla did not explain. She got up, stick first, and stood leaning on it by the window, looking out at the harbour going grey.

"Eight o'clock," she said. "I'll meet you at the door. I'll not climb that stair again tonight."

---

The Counting House stood on the Long Quay between two warehouses, tall and narrow and grey, with every window lit.

Kiva had walked past it every day for eleven days and never looked at it properly. It was the kind of building that one did not look at: plain, old, respectable, with a brass plate by the door worn so smooth that the letters were shadows. But tonight every window was lit white and steady, all the way up, and the light came out onto the wet granite of the quay in squares, and none of the squares flickered.

Orla was at the door, as she had said. Perrin was beside Kiva with Coombe's rope on his shoulder. He had brushed his coat. Kiva had seen him do it, in the loft, at seven, with a clothes brush borrowed from Old Four, very slowly and with great concentration.

A man in Halden's livery opened the door before they knocked.

Inside there were lamps everywhere. Kellan had said nineteen between the door and the dining room. Kiva counted twenty-three between the door and the long room at the top of the stair. She did not mean to count them. She found she had. Whether they hung on brass brackets or stood in glass globes on the newel posts or burned in the great iron ring that was slung from the ceiling of the stairwell, every one of them was white, and not one of them flickered.

And as she climbed the stair, her heart began to slow.

She felt it on the fourth step. She had been a little quick, from the quay and the cold and the knowing where she was going. On the fourth step her heart came down. It came down further on the tenth. By the top of the stair it was going slowly and evenly and heavily, like a pump.

She put her left hand on the banister rail. It was wood, old and polished. The first two fingers lay on it, lightly.

*It keeps time.*

At once, strongly. Four and four. It was not her own pulse. Her own pulse was in it, under it, going along with it, keeping step.

"Rowan," said Orla behind her, on the stair. She had stopped a step below. "You've gone white."

"I'm all right."

"You're not." Orla took her right wrist. She did not ask. She took it as she had taken a hundred wrists on a tear watch, and looked at the clock that stood at the stairhead in a tall case of dark wood, and counted. Kiva watched her lips move.

"Fifty-seven," said Orla.

She said it quite quietly. She did not let go.

"You've never been fifty-seven in your life. You're seventy on a quiet day." Orla looked up the last step at the door of the long room, which was open, with white light coming out of it. "It's him."

The long room ran the whole depth of the house, front to back, with windows at both ends. One end looked over the harbour and the other over the high town. There was a fire at one side. There was a table down the middle, long and dark and polished, with nothing on it but a white cloth at the far end, and two candlesticks, and a glass case with a brass frame.

A man sat at the head of the table with his left hand on the case.

He did not get up.

"Miss Rowan," he said. "Mr. Cade. Mrs. Dane." His voice was pleasant and dry and not loud. "Forgive me for sitting. It's not discourtesy. I can't rise without lifting my hand, and I don't lift it. Mr. Renn will have told you."

"He told me," said Kiva.

"Then he told you to sit at the far end." The Proprietor looked at her across the length of the table. She saw that he had seen her white face, and her hand on the door frame, and Orla's hand on her wrist. "Please do. I'd not have you faint in my house. You'd never forgive me, and I'd never forgive myself."

Kiva did not go to the far end.

She stood in the door and thought about it. The wire said *at a table.* It said *in her own hand.* If she sat at the far end, she would be thirty feet from him, and she would have to lay the plate on a table thirty feet away, and he would be looking at it down the length of a room as one looks at a ship from the shore. She had come to show it to him. She would show it properly or not at all.

She went halfway down the table and sat.

Her heart went slower. She felt it go. Fifty-seven, if Orla had it right; it felt like less. She felt her doors, too, under it. They did not lie down, as they had lain down in a small room with a fire and a woman standing at a window. They did the other thing. The Ember under her breastbone, which was always awake and always glad, came up. It came up as a dog comes to its feet when it hears a band in the street, and it wanted to go. It wanted to go along with the count, to fall in, to march. And the still door under everything, heavy and slow, wanted to beat. She had never in her life felt her still door want to do anything but hold.

She held them both shut. It was like holding two doors shut in a gale. She sat at the table with her hands in her lap and her teeth together and held them, and her heart went on slowly, slowly, in step with something.

"Further, I think," said the Proprietor. He said it quite gently. "One chair. There."

She moved one chair further down. Her heart came up. She felt it come up, a beat, two beats, as though somebody had let go of a rope. The Ember settled. The still door was still.

"Thank you," she said.

"Thank you for not going to the end," said the Proprietor. "I'd have understood it. I'd have been sorry." He looked at Orla and Perrin, standing in the door. "Please sit. Anywhere you like. Mr. Cade, you'll have the rope off your shoulder for a chair, I hope. It's Coombe's, isn't it? I'd know that knot anywhere. She tied one on my yard in 2660 and I've never got it out."

Perrin cleared his throat and took the rope off his shoulder.

The Proprietor was not what she had expected.

She did not know what she had expected. Something tall, perhaps, as she had expected Saltmere to be tall. Something like Thrale: enormous and quiet and very close. He was a man of about fifty-five by his face, lean, with dark hair grey at the temples and deep lines from his nose to the corners of his mouth. He wore a plain dark coat. His right hand lay on the table near a glass of water. His left lay on the glass case, flat, as a man lays his hand on a dog's head, and inside the case, on black velvet, sat an old iron cell with a brass cap.

There was nothing coming off him. She had stood near a Blaze and felt it from the rail like a stove. She had sat near a Glory master in a small room and felt nothing at all, and known that the nothing was enormous. This was different again. There was nothing to feel. There was only a count, slow and steady, in the table and the floor and the banister and the lamps. Everything in the room was keeping it, very quietly, as though it had been asked to and had found that it did not mind.

"You've read my face, I think," said the Proprietor. "You're the first person who's come into this room in a year and looked at me before they looked at the cell. Most people can't take their eyes off it."

"It's what you're holding."

"It is." He looked down at it. "The first that was ever stepped. On a bench in a shed in 2391, by a man whose name nobody remembers, because the foundation sealed the patent under a name that wasn't his. I keep its count. I've kept it since 2611, when the man who kept it before me died with his hand on this glass." He looked up again. "I don't know what would happen if I let go. I've told Mr. Renn that I've never been curious enough to find out. That isn't quite true. I'm very curious. I'm not brave enough."

He said it without any embarrassment at all, as he might have said the time.

"You asked to see the plate," said Kiva.

"I did. I asked the Line, and the Line voted, and I'm told three of the seven would have had me told no." He smiled slightly. "I'd like you to know that I'd have accepted that. I wanted to ask properly. I find I've asked improperly for too much in my life to start again at my age."

"The wire says I may show it, in my own hand, at a table, if I choose. Not weighed. Not tested. Not drawn or copied. No hand but mine."

"Then that's how I'll see it, if you choose."

Kiva put her right hand up to her collar and drew out the cord.

She laid the disk on the table in front of her, on the dark polished wood, with the cord still round her neck and her right hand on the cord. It lay there, dark and smooth, the width of her palm. In the white lamplight she could see the nine rings on it, very faint, going round, one inside another. There was still a thread of grey Kennel ash in three of the grooves.

The Proprietor looked at it.

He did not lean forward. He could not lean far; his hand was on the glass. He sat with his left hand on the cell and his right hand still on the table and looked at the disk across two chairs' distance. He looked at it for a long time. The fire ticked. The lamps did not flicker.

"Is it warm?" he said.

"No."

"It's never warm?"

"It was warm once. After my first true crossing, in the fifth month of last year. It's been cold since." Kiva kept her hand on the cord. "I write it down after every crossing. *Cold.* I wrote it yesterday."

"Near me, it's cold."

"It's cold."

"I thought it might be warm, near me." He said it quite simply. "I've thought so since the summer, when Mr. Adlam came back from the Library and told me what the Reader had said. A plate of nine rings, carried by the first teachers. I thought, if it's what they say, it'll know me. That's vanity." He looked up from the disk to her face. "I'm very glad to have been corrected. It's been a long time since anything corrected me that I couldn't argue with."

"I don't know what it is," said Kiva. "Nobody does. The first book's lost."

"I know. I'd hoped you'd know more than the book. I see you don't." He looked at it again. "Nine rings. I've a cell with one handle and a bench clerk's count. You've a plate with nine rings and a page that says *write down what it does.* I think we're both carrying things we don't understand, Miss Rowan, for people who are dead." He took his right hand off the table. "Put it away. Thank you. I'll not ask again."

She put it away. It went back under her collar, against her skin, cold.

---

"Seventy-seven years tonight," said the Proprietor.

He said it to the window at the harbour end of the room, where the Long Quay's lamps stood in a row along the black water.

"I was twenty-six. I was a bench clerk in this house, on the second floor, with a desk under that window. I'd been here four years and I'd written down nothing in my life but counts. *Fell in at fifty to the minute.*" He did not look round. "On the night of the eleventh of the third month, at about three in the morning, I was at that window. I couldn't sleep. I'd a fever on me. I'd had it since the evening."

Kiva did not move.

"Every lamp on the Long Quay went white," said the Proprietor. "Every one. They were ours. They were stepped, as everything in this house was stepped and still is. They went white, very bright, and stayed lit. And down there, below the wall—" he lifted his right hand an inch toward the low town, "—every lamp in the low town went out. All at once. As if somebody had put a hand over them. And they stayed out for half an hour, and then they came on again of themselves, and nobody ever found a fault."

He turned his head and looked at her.

"I've thought about that for seventy-seven years," he said. "I've thought about it more than I've thought about anything. I've read every fault book in this country that has a line for that night. There aren't many. And what I've come to is this. Whatever happened that night, whatever it was that came, the things that kept time together went white. They stayed lit. And the things that kept their own time, each on its own count, the way the Works likes them, went out."

Kiva sat at the table with her hands in her lap.

She was on a ferry in the middle of a river, with no roof. There were roads, and lights on them, from every side, a great many. Some were bright. Some were dull. Some were going out. And some had gone white.

She had told nobody, not even Mara, with the keeper's book open on the table between them. What she had seen was still a thing without a name, and she could not say whether *white* on a ferry and *white* on the Long Quay seventy-seven years ago were the same word. And here was a man who had stood at a window with a fever on him, and seen lamps go white and lamps go out, and had built his whole life on it, and he was not a fool.

Her jaw ached; she had her teeth together so hard that the ache went up into her ears, and her hands in her lap were hot.

She kept her mouth shut.

"I'd step the country," said the Proprietor, "if they'd let me. Every bank. Every pump house, every lamp. Every box in every tenement. I'd have it all keeping one time, the way this house does, so that whatever comes next time finds it white and not out." He turned his eyes back to the window. "They won't let me. The Works won't, and the Board won't, and the patent says *not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under load,* because it's dangerous. It is dangerous. Your aunt knows what it costs better than I do." He was quiet for a moment. "And now your aunt is going to stop my best machine in this town. I'm told she's sealed a paper this morning. I hope she does it well. I hope she's right. I'd rather be wrong than have the low town flood."

"You think she's wrong."

"I think she may be right about the harbour and wrong about the country. I think I may be wrong about both." He looked at her again. "I'm a hundred and three years old, Miss Rowan. I've been wrong a great many times. I've never yet been wrong about what I saw from that window."

"I'd like to ask you something," said the Proprietor. "I'll ask it plainly, and I'll name a price, and you'll say what you say."

Kiva had known it was coming since Kellan sat on the end of Jude's bed in the dark. She had known it since Mr. Adlam stood on a pump-house walk with his hat in his hand. She found that knowing did not help in the least.

"I'd like you to do the three," he said. "Hand, Ember and still, in sequence, as you did them in the Pit and in the Old Yard. On a mast of mine, at the refinery, beside a stepped bank of twelve cells, with my reader and Mr. Bexley both, and your healer, and your sponsor, and every condition you name." He did not raise his voice. "I want to see whether my banks answer you. Whether the notch on Mr. Bexley's pens is on my needles too. Whether the lamps on my quay do anything. I want to know if what you are and what I hold are answering the same thing."

"It's not on my certificate," said Kiva.

"I know. Your Chair's written that it's not to be asked. I'm not your Chair." He laid his right hand flat on the table. "Forty drams to your team's book, combed clean at your gate. And for you, a seat on my roll whenever you want it, at a Torch's wage, under any healer's card you like, for as long as you like. It's not a bribe. I'd offer it to you if you said no. I'm offering it so that you'll know what I think the thing's worth."

Perrin had not moved. He sat at the end of the table like a post, with Coombe's rope in his lap, and he did not open his mouth. Kiva could see what it cost him. Forty drams. Their book had four and seventy hundredths in it, for four boards.

Orla sat by the fire with her stick across her knees and looked at nothing.

"No," said Kiva.

The Proprietor did not seem surprised.

"May I ask why?"

"Yes." She made herself look at him, and not at the cell, and not at her own hot hands. "Because it lives in one hand. Mine. The first time it cost me two fingers for good. The third time it cost me the arm to above the elbow for four days, and it came back to where it was, and my healer doesn't know that it'd come back a fourth time. Nor do I. It isn't a thing that I can do on a Friday and have back on a Saturday."

"I'd pay for that too."

"I know you would. That's the second reason." Kiva heard her voice go flat, and let it. "If it has a price tonight, it has one next week. And next week somebody will pay more, and somebody else after that. And then it's a thing that's bought, and I'm the only one who can sell it, and every time it's wanted somebody has to turn round and not look at me." She stopped. "I said that to my sponsor once. On a stair. About a method that only lived in my hand. I said I'd sooner it were a thing with handles."

"And this isn't."

"No. It's two gaps and nobody in them but me." She took a breath. "I've spent the whole winter making a thing three people can do instead. It's not done live yet. I'll not show it to you on a mast for a price either. But I've two crossings certified, in my own name, as of yesterday morning. You may read either one on your mast any week you like, one in seven days, with the price said first. That's what I've got that's mine to give."

"A single crossing tells me nothing that Mr. Bexley hasn't told the Board."

"Then I've nothing for you." She held his eyes. "I'm sorry."

The Proprietor sat for a long moment with his left hand on the glass.

"The country, Miss Rowan," he said. Not loudly. "Not me. Not Halden. If what I saw from that window comes again, the country will want to know what it's answering. You may be the only person alive who can show it."

"Then the country can ask." Kiva's hands were burning in her lap. "It can write it on a certificate. It can say who's to read it and what it costs and who pays. The Chair wrote that it's not to be asked of me. If the country wants to unwrite that, it can do it in the Chair's hand, in front of everybody. Not at a dinner table on the Long Quay."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the house, a clock struck the half hour, and the lamps did not flicker.

Then the Proprietor did something she had not expected. He laughed. It was not a loud laugh. It was short and real, and it went across his lean face and stayed there for a moment.

"Thank you," he said. "I'd have asked once in any case. I had to. I'd not have forgiven myself if I hadn't." He looked at her with what seemed to be real pleasure. "I find I'm glad you said no. I didn't expect that. I've been asking people for things for eighty years and I can count on one hand the ones who've said no to my face and told me why."

"Was Kellan one?"

"Mr. Renn told me you'd say exactly that. Very nearly in those words. *She'd say it to your face.*" He turned his eyes to Orla. "Mrs. Dane. You've said nothing."

Orla looked up from the fire.

"I was asked not to," she said. "By her. I've done it." She paused. "She said no for herself. Mark that, in this house. I've not said a word, and nor has her captain."

"I'd seen that," said the Proprietor. "I'd like you to know I'd seen it." He looked at Perrin. "Mr. Cade."

"Under my name as well," said Perrin. His voice cracked a little, and he did not seem to care. "The no. The team's book. I'd like it known."

"It's known," said the Proprietor. "I'll have it written down." He lifted his right hand from the table, a little, in something that was nearly a wave and nearly a blessing. "Go home, Miss Rowan. You're very hot. I can see it from here. I should have sent you to the far end and made you stay there, and I'm sorry I didn't."

---

The fever came on along the Long Quay.

She felt it at the first bollard. Her hands had been hot at the table; now her face was hot, and her neck, and the back of her knees. The cold wind off the harbour went straight through her coat and did not cool anything. By the third bollard she was shaking.

Orla stopped her under a lamp. The lamp burned white and did not flicker.

"Wrist," said Orla, and took it, and looked at her own watch, and counted.

"A hundred and four," she said. "And going up." She did not let go. "It's like the ferry."

"How do you know about the ferry?"

"It's in your book. *A hundred and twelve, by Finch.*" Orla's crooked face was quite close, and quite still. "I read your book every Sunday. I'm not a fool."

She took Kiva's arm on one side, and Perrin took it on the other, and they went along the Long Quay to Quiller's with Orla's stick ringing on the granite. Kiva walked between them. She did not think about anything except keeping her teeth together.

Aske was awake. He was always awake. He had her on the bed by the north window inside a minute, with the candle lit and his watch out and Pru on her knees on the other side.

"A hundred and eight," he said, after a minute. He put the back of his hand against her cheek, as her aunt used to. "Where were you?"

"The Counting House."

"With him."

"Yes."

Aske was quiet for a moment. He took the watch away from her wrist and looked at it in the candlelight, and then he wrote something on her card, standing, against the window frame.

"The ferry was a mile from the house of the Even Hand," he said. He did not look up from the card. "This is an hour from the Counting House. I'll not ask you anything. I'll write down what I've seen." He put the pen away. "*Fever, the second this year. Both near a Glory master, within the day.* That's all I'll write. It's all I know."

He sat on the upturned bucket that he had brought from somewhere and took her wrist again, and counted.

---

She lay on the bed by the north window with the quay lamps in a row along the black water, and she waited for the roof to go.

She knew it would. She had felt it coming in the long room with the fire ticking and her teeth together, as she had felt it on the ferry with her hands on the rail. She lay with her eyes open and her jaw shut and waited. The candle burned. Aske counted. Pru held her other wrist. Somewhere at the far end of the loft, Old Four was asleep and Halden was asleep and Dray was asleep. Kellan was not; she could see his shape at the south window, sitting up.

The fever climbed. A hundred and ten, Aske said, at midnight. A hundred and twelve at one.

The roof did not go.

She lay and waited for it with every part of her that could wait. It did not come. There was the loft's ceiling, dark, with its beams, and the candle's light on it. There was the count, under the floor and the chandlery and the quay, four and four, going on. There was Aske's voice, and Pru's hand, and the fever going through her like a tide coming in over flats.

At about three in the morning, it broke. It broke in a long sweat that soaked the blanket through, as it had on the barge. Aske said, "There," and wrote it, and Pru let go of her wrist for the first time in five hours and sat back on her heels with her face in her hands.

"Nothing," said Kiva.

She had not meant to say it aloud. Her voice was very hoarse.

Aske looked at her over his spectacles in the candlelight.

"Nothing what?"

"Nothing came." She shut her eyes. "That's all. I'm all right."

He did not ask. He wrote, small, on the card. Kiva did not see what. Then he put the card in his bag and sat back on the bucket with his watch in his hand, as though he meant to sit there till morning, which he did.

Pru took her hand away from her face. She looked at Kiva for a long moment in the candlelight.

"I'll not ask either," she said. "I said I wouldn't. On the barge." She went scarlet, even in the dark. "I'd like you to know I want to, though. Very badly. I'm not asking."

"I know you're not."

"Good," said Pru. "Go to sleep."

Kiva did not sleep at once. She lay with her eyes shut and the sweat going cold on her. She thought of a man at a window seventy-seven years ago, with a fever on him, watching lamps go white. She thought that she had sat across a table from him tonight and he had told her that, and she had told him nothing in return. And she thought that she had no idea what she would have told him if she had.

She would write it in the book in the morning. *Fever, after the Counting House. A hundred and twelve, by Aske. Broke at three.* On the left. And on the right, nothing. There was nothing on the right that she could write. Not yet.

She went to sleep at last with her left hand inside her shirt, on the disk, which was cold.
