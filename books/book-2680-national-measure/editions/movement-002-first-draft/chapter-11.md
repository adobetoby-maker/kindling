# Chapter 11 — Kiva: A Stack with Four Legs

Coombe watched the pack drill on Thursday morning from the shed step for the whole of an hour, and in that hour she said one word, four times.

"Again."

Bettony's twelve stamped. Perrin stood on the step below the Marshal with his hands in his pockets and counted aloud, and when Jude's broom handle tapped a junior on the *and* and a file sat down, Perrin said "*Nearest,*" and named the nearer of the two counts that were left, and counted it; and Anwen, behind him, with her eyes on the files' feet, counted the other under her breath, so that when Perrin lost his she could say *yours,* and he could say *mine,* and nobody was left holding nothing. Jude cut a second junior out of time inside four beats. Twice, the third file never found its count at all before Jude's handle was on its shoulder.

The fourth time, Pru was on the north corner of the empty cloth, hauling, and Anwen on the south, so that the captain had nothing in his hands but a number.

"Who hauls?" said Coombe.

It was the first thing that she had said that was not *again.* Perrin turned round on the step.

"Finch, ma'am. And Pryce, when her wall's down."

"Finch isn't on your floor. Your floor's a four." The Marshal tied a knot. "On the twelfth there'll be four of you on that cinder and I'll mark four. On the Longshore there'll be four. Who hauls?"

Perrin looked at Kiva. Kiva looked back, and did not help him, and saw him see that she was not going to.

"Pryce," he said slowly. "When her wall's down. And Talley, when he's cut. He's nothing to do once a thing's fallen but stand over it, and I've seen what happens when he stands over it." He swallowed. "And if Pryce's wall's up and Talley hasn't cut, then nobody hauls, and Rowan waits on her hem with the cloth on the heap, and the bag's slow. I'd sooner have a slow bag than a lost count."

"Would you."

"Yes, ma'am. I've thought about it. I lost one on Monday."

The Marshal looked at him for a moment with her pale eyes, and then she looked at the files of juniors, red-faced, stamping in the frost.

"Better," she said. "You've thought." She coiled her rope. "Now think about what you do on the Longshore when the count your pack finds isn't one that a child can stamp."

She went down the yard and out by the side gate without saying what she meant, and Perrin sat down on the step where she had been and put his head against the doorpost.

"I'd like it known," he said, "that I've never in my life been so tired from standing still."

---

The card came at half past nine on Friday night, by a boy on a bicycle, three words long. *Yard. Now. Finch.*

The van went out of the Graywater hall yard at ten to ten with its rings whining and Ruddock on the tiller, and it had one more person on its side bench than it had carried in the first month and one fewer pair of hands.

Varley sat by the tailboard where Pike used to sit, with a cloth on each shoulder.

He was Imrie's catcher, lent, and Kiva had met him on Tuesday in the mending room for a quarter of an hour, which was as long as anybody had seemed to think that a meeting ought to take. He was a lean man of thirty-five or so with very clean hands and a long patient face, and he talked to his cloths. He did it under his breath, as another man might whistle, and he did it now, folding the corner of the left-hand one over his knee and smoothing it. *There you are. There.*

Pike sat beside him on the bench, in his coat, with his two arms laid along his knees in their linen.

The dressings were thinner than they had been. Ruddock had changed them on Wednesday, and the white went only to a hand above each wrist now, and the fingers showed at the ends of it, grey and stiff and swollen, like the fingers of a glove that has been left out in the rain. He could move them a little. He had shown Kiva on Tuesday. He had shut his right hand, very slowly, a quarter of the way, and opened it again, and then he had put both hands back on his knees and not done it again.

"I read them," he said, under the noise of the rings. "Your two sheets. The pack and the turn. I gave them to Sorn."

"Did she read them?"

"She read them in the mending room with her coat on, standing, as she reads everything. Then she took the pack sheet up the stair with her and didn't give it back." He looked at the floor of the van. "I'd say that's a yes."

Rhea Sorn was on the front bench with her slate on her knee and her hands in her pockets. She did not turn round.

The van stopped on the Brickfields road, where it came down off the levee, a hundred paces short of the tram sheds.

Kiva knew the sheds. Everybody in Meridian knew them: two long brick barns with arched roofs of glass and iron, where the trams went in at night to be swept and greased and have their rings looked at, and behind them, in a squat brick house of its own with a chimney, the bank that drove the whole Brickfields line. *The big tram sheds,* said the Halden patent, in a sentence that she had heard read aloud in a yard in the autumn. *Stepped.* Every cell in that squat house had been salted late with a pinch of counting ash in the year that the line was built, and every one of them beat with all the others, and one man in a glass box drove the lot.

There were three things in the road outside the shed doors.

They were husks, as tall as a man, three thin legs apiece. They stood in a row on the setts with frost going off them in the lamplight, and they faced the shed doors, and they stamped.

*One.* They leaned. *Two.* They came back. *Three.* *Four,* lighter.

Kiva's stomach went cold. She had not expected to see that in the road, on the Brickfields, outside a shed where she had waited for a tram a hundred times. She had thought of it as a thing in a cage, on a cart, brought up from the coast on a boat.

"Gate," said Rhea.

Mace's four went out and brought the wall up across the road, ten paces short of the husks, silver, from the levee's foot to the shed's corner. The three heads did not turn to it. They stood facing the shed doors and stamped.

Rhea tilted her slate to the van's lamp.

"*Before.* Three husks, on end, on the Brickfields road at the tram sheds. They came off a tear in the levee drain at the Gasworks corner, which the Line's closing. They've walked four hundred paces in step, and they've stopped at the sheds, and they face the bank house. Nothing lit inside sixty paces but my gate; the sheds are stepped and the bank house is running, and the night shift's in the glass box, and the Works says it can't stop the line before midnight without leaving three trams in the street." She did not lower the slate. "*A pack: counted aloud by the ward-chief. The blade cuts between his counts and not on them. Cut one in time and it closes.* I had that off a second's sheet, who had it off the Marshal on Monday. *The far edge: the second's. To say. Two words, once.* *Asked of anybody beyond their card: nothing.*" She lowered it. "What's there? Mace."

"A join that I like. And a count that I don't." Mace's voice was flat. "I've been counting since I got down. It's a four, with a light one at the end. I'd say I've got it."

"Nairn."

"Three rungs, one a husk. The near one's a hand nearer the doors than the others; it's the leader, if they've a leader. I'd take the far one first, from the levee side, so the others aren't between me and my way out."

"Pike."

Pike sat on the tailboard. He did not get down. He looked at the road for a long moment, the setts and the gutter and the line of frost going from the husks toward the doors.

"Setts," he said. "They fall to the gutter, east, a hand in four paces. They'll run toward the doors. Varley lays west of the near one with his seam up the fall." He stopped. "Varley lays where he likes. He's the cloth."

"West of the near one," said Varley. "Seam up the fall."

"Then it's three husks, on the ward-chief's count and the catcher's word," said Rhea. "Go."

---

Kiva knelt at Varley's left hem, on the cold setts, a pace and a half down from the corner.

It was not the same as kneeling at Pike's. She had known that it would not be, and it was not, in ways that she had not foreseen. Varley threw the cloth once, and it lay flat at the first cast, but its seam ran a hand off true up the fall, and he knelt and looked at it and left it. He knelt with the gate at his back, as the book said. He put his left knee on the hem, a hand from hers, as Pike had; and then he put his right knee down as well, beside it, so that he was kneeling square on the canvas with both, like a man at prayer, and both his hands were on his thighs.

His cord was in his coat. She saw the bulge of it.

Pike knelt behind them both, off the canvas, on the setts, with his white arms on his knees. He had not been given a place on the slate. He had taken one, at the head of the cloth and a yard back, where he could see the heel of whichever blade was going to cut; and Rhea had looked at him taking it and said nothing.

"*One, and,*" said Mace, from the wall. "*Two, and.*"

He said it at the pitch of a man calling a horse. It went out over the road and the frost and the three grey backs, and Kiva felt every person on that floor fall in with it, as one falls in with a band in the street without meaning to. *Three, and. Four, and.*

Nairn went up the levee side in two long low bounds, dark, on his struts. He landed behind the far husk.

"*Now?*" he said. Not to Varley. To Mace.

"*Two, and—*" said Mace. "*Three—*"

"*And,*" said Varley.

Nairn cut.

It was on the *and.* Kiva saw it go in level at the far husk's knot, a half count after the stamp, before the lean; and the far husk fell. It went down onto the setts in a heap like poured sand, beyond the near husk's legs, a good six paces from Varley's cloth. The other two stopped dead, with their feet up.

"*Dark!*" said Varley. "*One!*"

He did not throw. The heap was not on his cloth. It was on the setts, beyond the near husk, open, and it began, very slowly, to run east down the fall of the road toward the gutter and the shed doors.

"*Rhea,*" said Pike.

He said it from behind them, quite quietly. It was not a call. It was a name.

Rhea Sorn was already moving. She came round the end of Mace's wall on the shed side, walking, as she always walked on a floor, with her blade arm down; and the middle husk, which had stopped dead with its feet up, put them down. It stamped. It was alone. The near husk, beside it, stamped half a beat later, and the two of them were out of step, heavy and late, treading on each other's count, exactly like two files of juniors on a frozen yard.

"*Nearest,*" said Mace. "*The middle. One, and—*"

Rhea's left heel came down flat on the setts.

Pike's head came up. Kiva saw it. She saw his eyes go to the heel, as they had gone to it for eleven years, and saw his mouth open; and then she saw him shut it, with his teeth, as she had once shut hers on a Hobday's corner, because there was a man kneeling square on that cloth whose word it was and not his.

"*—and,*" said Mace.

Rhea cut. The middle husk went down where it stood, in the road, a pace from the cloth's west edge, and lay.

"Mine," said Varley, to nobody. He reached out with the long hook of the cloth's pole and drew the near edge of the canvas over the middle heap, a yard, without getting off his knees, and it went under, and he had it. "*Dark! One!*"

The near husk found its count.

Kiva felt it come. It was through the canvas and the setts under her knee, slow, *pushing,* and her fingers were on the hem and her heart was going like a tram bell and she could not have sworn to a thing. But she saw it as well. The near husk stamped, alone, heavy, and leaned, and came back, and it was not leaning toward the gate. It was leaning toward the shed doors. It took a step toward them, on *one,* and another on *one,* and the frost went off it onto the doors' iron sills and they went white.

"*It's going for the bank,*" said Mace. "*I've lost it. It's not a four. It's a—*"

"Two," said Pike. "It's on a two. It's walking on a two."

It was. Kiva could see it now that she had been told. It stamped and stepped, stamped and stepped, *one* and *two,* toward the shed doors, as a man walks who has stopped marching and is only going somewhere.

"*Mace,*" said Rhea. "Wall. Across the doors. Walk it."

Mace walked it. The silver went along in front of the shed doors like a curtain on a rail, with four wards behind it, and the near husk walked up to it on its two and leaned on it, and stood there, a yard from the iron sills, stamping on the wall.

"*One,*" said Mace. He had it again. "*And. Two. And.*"

Rhea's heel came down.

"*And,*" said Varley.

It fell on the setts in front of the wall. It lay. It was a long way from the cloth, and from Varley's knees, and it began to run down the fall to the gutter, toward the gap under the doors, where the bank house's drain went in.

Kiva watched it run.

She had nothing to do. It was not hers to do anything. She had the far edge of a cloth that had two heaps on it already, and the far edge lay flat on the setts, and there was nothing white anywhere near it; and the third heap was running into a drain twenty paces off, and the only person on that floor whose business it was to stop it was kneeling square on a canvas with both his knees, and he had not moved.

He did not need to. Purvis was at the doors already, with the second cloth, which he had brought off the van on his shoulder without being told because he had nine years of a man who liked to have two; and he threw it over the running heap from the shed side, flat, at the first cast, and put both his boots on its downhill hem.

"*Dark,*" said Purvis, rather breathless. "*One.* I've no catcher."

"*You've a catcher,*" said Varley, without turning his head. "Hold your hem. I'll come to you when these are sealed." He looked down at his own cloth, with two heaps on it under the canvas, and his two knees on the hem. "*Corners.*"

---

It took nineteen minutes from the gate to the last seal, and Kiva said nothing in any of them.

She watched. She watched Varley wait eleven counts on the first heap before he called the corners, and nine on the second; she watched him look at each heap while he waited, as Pike looked, and saw that each of them leaned a hair toward the shed doors and the bank house behind them, and settled; and she saw that he saw it, because his long face did not change, and he called the corners as though it were an ordinary thing. She watched his cord come out of his coat warm. She watched him tie the three turns with his left thumb pinning and his right taking the long end under, the right way round, the way that the book said, because his right thumb was not slow.

On the first bag she watched Pike watch him, and saw the white bound hands on Pike's knees close, both of them, a quarter of the way, on the third turn, and open again.

"Sealed," said Varley. "Fifty-one. Ward, full. This winter's skin."

"Sealed. Forty-eight."

He went to Purvis at the shed doors on his knees, dragging nothing, and knelt on the second cloth's hem, and looked at the third heap for nine counts while Purvis held his boots on the downhill edge, and then he called the corners to two corner-hands who had never in their lives hauled a cloth that their own catcher had not thrown.

"Sealed. Fifty-six." He sat back on his heels. "That's slow. It ran a yard before Purvis had it."

"It ran four," said Purvis.

"Then it's slow and lucky." Varley put his palm on the neck. He held it there a moment longer than he had on the others. "Loud. As loud as the other two. All three."

There was a man in a long coat bent over the levee drain at the Gasworks corner, a quarter of a mile off; Kiva had not seen him come. After a time he lifted his head and called *closed,* faintly, down the road, as a man says that he has shut a gate.

Rhea read the slate in the road with the van's lamp on it.

"*Closed, a quarter to eleven. Lives: none. Works: the shed doors' sills, frosted. Tinned: three bags, at trace, clean. A hundred and fifty-five drams, of a hundred and sixty-two by the book.*" She turned it over. "*Remarks: one. The pack was counted by the ward-chief and cut between his counts, off a method on a second's sheet which the second had from the Charter Board's Marshal on Monday. When it broke, it came back on a count of two and walked for the bank house. I've not seen that. It's the pack's remark, and the method's, and it's a good one.*"

She hung the slate on her belt, and turned, and looked at Kiva for the first time that night.

"Your sheet," she said. "The pack. I've had it on my desk since Wednesday. I'd like it copied for Imrie's van, in your hand, with the Marshal's name on it where you got it from. I'll pay for the paper."

"I've nothing on it about a count of two."

"No. I'll write that myself." Rhea put her hands in her pockets. "It's in my *Remarks* column now. Your column's the *How.* Mine's the *what it did that nobody said it would.* I've found that they go well on the same sheet."

Kiva sat in the van on the way back with her knees against the canvas and Pru asleep against her shoulder, and she watched Pike.

He sat by the tailboard with his white hands on his knees and his eyes on Varley's hands, which were folding the left-hand cloth over and over on Varley's knee, smoothing it, while Varley talked to it under his breath. Pike did not say anything all the way to the hall.

At the hall gate, as Ruddock was backing the van in, he said, to nobody, "He waits eleven."

"He does," said Kiva.

"I wait eight. When it's quiet." Pike looked at his hands. "He waits eleven when it's quiet. I'd not have known that if you hadn't counted mine."

"Is eleven wrong?"

"No," said Pike. He said it slowly, as though it cost something to get out. "No. It's a longer look. On a night like this one, with a bank house leaning at every heap, I'd say eleven's better." He was quiet a moment. "I'd have called it at eight, and been right, and not seen the lean. I'd want that in your column."

"Which column?"

"Not mine," said Pike. "His."

---

The Pit was cold on Saturday morning and full, and this time the Marshal had something on her cart that nobody could see at all.

The cage stood in the middle of the cinder where it had stood on Monday, under its grey ward. There were two stools beside it, eight paces apart, and on each stool stood a lamp-cell of the Wren House's pattern, brass-bound, the size of a loaf; and a Wren House man in a brown apron stood between them with his hands in his sleeves.

The first company was on the rail, all sixteen, and the reserves behind. Selka Dray was at the end, on her two sticks, upright, with Marten beside her. Old Four had Brask's notebook open before anybody had said a word. Kellan stood with Halden in black and gold at the west end, and did not look along the rail, and Kiva did not look at him.

"Rowan," said Coombe, from the steps.

"Ma'am."

"The far gallery. By the mast. Twenty paces from the cage at the least, and you stay there."

Kiva went. She did not ask why. She went round the rail with every head on it turning to watch her go, and stood by the old mast at the east end, by herself, with her hands on the cold oak; and she saw the Marshal watch her all the way there, and nod once when she stopped.

"I've no notion what this would do near her," said Coombe, to the rail at large. "None. She's three doors and declares one, and I've read her tapes. It may be that it would do nothing. I'll not find out on a Saturday morning in a Pit to teach sixteen people a thing they can learn without her." She tied a knot. "That's said once."

She laid her palm on the ward and drew it back.

At first Kiva thought that the cage was empty. Then she saw, on its floor, lying in loose coils like a rope that has been thrown down on a quay, something grey. It was as thick as her wrist and as long as a man, perhaps longer; she could not see where it began. It did not breathe. It did not stamp. One end of it was lifted a hand's breadth off the iron floor of the cage, and turning, very slowly, as the tip of a vine turns in a greenhouse when nobody is looking.

"On the Longshore the crews call that a lodger," said Coombe. "Since it moves in. It's a cord, without a string. It came out of a tear on the flats in the eleventh month by itself and lay on the sand for a week and went nowhere, and the crew that found it thought it was dead. Then a girl came down the flats from the Ropewalk School on a Saturday, with her two doors laid, as they lay them at the Ropewalk, to look at it." She tied a knot. "Light the left cell. Alone."

The Wren House man put his palms over the left lamp-cell and it lit, a steady yellow, alone on its stool.

The lodger's tip did not turn to it.

"Now the right. Two cells, in one case. Lit together. Tuned to agree."

The man went to the right-hand stool. There were two cells in its brass case, Kiva saw now, side by side, and he put a palm over each, and lit them both at once, and turned a screw at the case's foot with his thumb, and the two lights went from a yellow and a slightly paler yellow to one yellow, steady, exactly the same, as two voices on a hymn find the same note.

The lodger's tip turned.

It came out of the cage. It came out over the iron sill, slowly, the whole grey length of it following the tip as a rope follows its end over a gunwale, and it went across the cinder toward the right-hand stool, not hurrying. It went past the left-hand stool and the one lit cell on it without lifting its tip an inch. It reached the right-hand stool and went up the leg.

Somebody on the rail made a small sound.

The tip came to the brass case. It touched the place where the two cells sat side by side, at the seam between them, and stopped there, as a finger stops on a latch.

Then it went in.

Kiva did not see how. She saw the two yellow lights go grey, both together, as though somebody had breathed on a window; and she saw the tip of the cord go into the seam between them as though there were a door there and the door had been left open, and the grey length on the cinder behind it draw up after it, and grow shorter. The two cells went dark.

"Quench," said Coombe.

The Wren House man pulled the case's handle. There was a dry click. The lodger lay on the stool and down its leg and across the cinder, quite still, with its tip in the dark brass seam.

"That girl on the flats had two doors laid," said the Marshal. "She's alive. She's one door now. She was two." She did not raise her voice. "That's a laid door. At the Ropewalk they open two together and twist them to agree, and they win lanes with it, and nobody that I know of has been hurt by it in a lane in forty years. On the Longshore, in the eleventh month, it was an open door. That's what you'll be standing next to at the gate. I'll say it plainly, so that it's been said: I don't know why. I know where it went."

Nobody said anything.

Selka Dray had her sunk grey eyes on the brass case.

"Two doors in one person," she said. "Open together. Agreeing."

"That's a lay, at the Ropewalk. It's a stack, in this city." Coombe did not look at her. "Now I'll show you the part that isn't in anybody's rules."

She lifted the lodger off the stool. She did it with the grey ward, drawn under it like a hand under a sleeping cat, and the whole length of it came up off the cinder and went back into the cage, coil by coil, and lay there with its tip turning.

"Two of you," she said, to two Pit wards in brown who had been standing at the lattice. "A sack."

There was a sandbag on the cinder, ten paces from the cage. The first ward put a hand door under it, a tray of silver, low, as Kiva put hers under a thing for a tap. The second put a still door on it from above, a stance, his boot on the sack's top.

"Both," said Coombe. "Together. You've the sack between you. Agree on it."

They held it. The ward under and the stance on, both on one sandbag, both open at once.

The lodger's tip turned.

It turned toward them. Kiva saw it, from twenty paces, by the old mast; she saw it lift a hand off the floor of the cage and swing, slowly, past the dark cells on their stool, and stop, pointing, at a sandbag on the cinder with two people's doors on it.

"*Off,*" said Coombe. "One of you. Now."

The still-door ward took his boot off the sack.

The lodger's tip hung in the air a moment, pointing. Then it swung away, as a dog's head swings away when the smell it had has gone, and lay down again on the floor of the cage.

---

"Two people," said Kiva.

She had not meant to say it aloud. She was twenty paces off by the mast, and it came out of her quite flatly, and carried, because the Pit was very quiet.

"Two people," said Coombe. "A ward and a stance, on one thing, at once, agreeing. I don't know that it's a stack. I don't know what it is. I've no rule for it and no reading. I know that the lodger went to it." She looked along the rail, slowly, from one end to the other. "Every one of you has done it. You've put two hands on one litter. You've had a ward under a thing and a stance on it and called it a good hold. On a floor with one of those on it, you'll want to know whether you're doing it, and for how long."

"*Mine before yours,*" said Perrin.

He said it under his breath, and Kiva heard it from the other end of the rail, because she had been thinking it.

They had made it in the eleventh month, on the litter over the wall. *They take it, and then you let go. I'd sooner be caught and then let go.* Perrin had turned it on a piece of ground in the Old Yard, against Old Four, with dirt down his neck: her stance under his boots, and then his off. And she had had it from a first-year by a stove on Monday, about a count.

It was the best thing that they had. It had been a hand of four people on the poles of a litter, all of them dark, with nothing open. It had been a still door and a still door on one patch of dirt, like doors, which the Line had a word for and had always called a plank.

It had never been a ward and an Ember on one load.

She had meant it to be. She had lain awake on Thursday night with her book, and the old word that Hollis Vane had read out of a ledger in the autumn, *crossing,* and Pru's fold, and Pike's columns; and she had worked out, at about three in the morning, that the thing she did alone with three doors could be done by three people with one each, and that the place where it would have to go from one of them to the next was the place where *mine* would have to come before *yours.*

"Ma'am," said Kiva. "If it's three people. A ward and an Ember and a stance. Passing one thing. And each of them takes it before the last lets go."

"Then you've a ward and an Ember on one thing, at once, agreeing, for as long as it takes," said Coombe. "And then an Ember and a stance. And I'd not stand near it with a lodger in my Pit." She tied a knot. "Who's thinking of doing that?"

"I am," said Kiva.

The Marshal looked at her across twenty paces of cinder, by the old mast, with her pale eyes.

"Then you'd best find out what your mast says about it," she said. "Before your floor."

---

They found out that afternoon, in the Old Yard, and it was worse than she had thought.

Noa brought the mast. It was not the Circuit's; that went back to the Chair's office on Saturdays and was not to be had. It was the old practice mast from the Low Lane, sixty years old, on a handcart, with its drum and its tape and a brass plate on its foot that said *Not sanctioned* in letters a quarter of an inch high.

"It reads anything that's lit in front of it," said Noa. "It always has. It draws one line, for whoever. The Chair says it can read whatever it likes and enter nothing, which I've always thought was a kind thing to say about a mast." He threaded the tape. "Nothing that it draws goes anywhere but my notebook. I want that said, before anybody lights."

"It's said," said Anwen.

They rigged it as Kiva had drawn it on the back of the tide table at three in the morning, with her aunt's leave.

Gerald hung from the hoist on his pole. A cord ran from his belt across the lane to a ring in the south wall, taut, a yard off the ground; and under the cord, at the wall end, lay a second sandbag with a hat on it, which was the person who was pinned. It was the hill again, in the ninth month: a corner pinning Selka Dray to the ground, and the thing that had had to be done was to take the cord off her and carry it four paces east and plant it, so that the people who had her could pull her out.

She had done that alone, once, with three doors.

"Pru catches," said Kiva. "A ward under the cord, when Jude swings Gerald and it goes slack. One tap. Fold it to a penny. Then it's mine. I take it in my Ember and go four paces east, low. Then it's Perrin's. He's planted on the mark, and he takes it into the ground, and the cord's pinned to the dirt at his feet and not to the sack." She looked at the three of them. "*Mine* before *yours.* At both passes. Pru doesn't shut until I've said *mine.* I don't shut until Perrin has."

"I don't like it," said Pru.

"Nor do I. I'd like to see what the mast says."

They lit. Jude swung Gerald, and the cord went slack for a beat, and Pru's ward came up under it at her right hip, small and silver, and took the tap and rang, and folded from the far edge to a penny in her two palms. She held it out, the way one holds out a coin.

Kiva opened the Ember door.

The bar came down her right fist, long and narrow and dull orange, and she put the end of it to the penny in Pru's palms, as she had planned, and felt the cord's pull come up into the Ember through the touch like the tug of a fish on a line; and Pru had not shut yet, because *mine* had not been said, and for that moment the cord's whole pull lay between them on two doors at once, Pru's silver under and Kiva's orange over, and they agreed. She could feel them agree. It was soft. It was very soft, and very easy, and it cost nothing at all.

She knew that feeling. She had had it three times in the summer, and written it down: *Stacked again. Soft. I knew as I did it.*

"*Mine,*" she said.

"*Yours.*" Pru shut.

Noa made a noise at the mast.

It was not a word. It was the noise that a boy makes when he has put his hand on a stove. Kiva did not look round. She had the cord in her Ember, and Perrin was on his mark four paces east, and she went: one low bound, with the cord's pull riding along her fist, and landed by Perrin's boots with his still door open under them, and put the bar's end to the dirt at his feet.

"*Mine,*" said Perrin.

"*Yours.*" She shut.

The cord went down into the ground at Perrin's boots as though it had been nailed there. The sandbag with the hat on it, at the wall, lay free.

"*Planted,*" said Perrin. "That's— Noa?"

Noa had not moved from the mast. He had the tape in his fingers, and he was looking at it.

"It's one line," he said.

Nobody understood him.

"It draws one line," said Noa. "For whoever's lit. I said. And when Finch had her ward up, it drew a ward line, and when Rowan lit, it drew—" He stopped. "It didn't draw two. It drew one. A ward line and an Ember line on top of each other, agreeing, for a count and a half. One line. It can't tell that there were two of you. There's nothing on this tape that says there were two of you." He looked up, and his face was white. "On a sanctioned tape, I'd have to call that a stack."

The yard was very quiet.

"Whose?" said Anwen.

"I don't know whose. A tape doesn't know whose. It knows that two doors were open on one thing and agreed." Noa held the tape out, at arm's length, as though it might go off. "And there's the same again at the second pass. Ember and stance. One line. A count."

Kiva stood by Perrin's mark with her Ember shut and her left hand cold.

She thought about the Chair's sheet, pasted under Seven's entry outside the ash office, in a level upright hand. *Read from a sanctioned mast by a reader of this office. A stack so read is an incident. One incident at a sanctioned board ends her season.*

If Noa had been at the Circuit's mast instead of the Low Lane's, and the Chair's office had not been shut on a Saturday, and a clerk had written down what he saw, she would be finished. Not for anything that she had done with her doors. She had had one door open the whole time, declared, and it had been the Ember. She would be finished for a thing that two people had done, together, with a cord, in a yard, because two people had agreed on it for a count and a half.

"It isn't you," said Pru. She had come up beside her. Her round face was set. "It's *us.* It isn't yours."

"I know that it isn't."

"I'll say so to anybody."

"I know you will." Kiva looked at the hat on the sandbag by the wall, lying free. "It doesn't matter whose. Coombe said. The lodger doesn't care whose. It went to two Pit wards on a sack who'd never laid a door in their lives."

"Then we don't do *mine* before *yours,*" said Jude.

He had come down from the hoist. He had the team book under his arm, flat, and his white boot.

"Not between a ward and an Ember. Not between the doors." He scratched his jaw. "On the poles, aye. On a mark, under Cade's boots, that's two stances; the Line calls that a plank and it's been a plank for two hundred years and nobody's said it was a stack. But when it's two unlike doors on one thing, then one of you has to let go first. And then there's nobody."

"For how long?" said Perrin.

"I don't know for how long. As long as it takes the next one to open."

"We'll find out," said Kiva.

---

They found out at the second try, and it was too long.

Pru caught the cord on its slack, and folded it, and held the penny out; and this time she shut first, on Kiva's *now,* and Kiva opened the Ember after, on the shut. And between the shut and the open, which was perhaps a count, perhaps a little more, the cord's pull went out of the penny and back into the cord, and the cord went back across the lane to the sandbag with the hat on it, as a stretched spring goes back, and pinned it again.

Gerald swung on his hoist. The penny of silver was gone out of Pru's palms. Kiva had an Ember open on nothing.

"Dropped," said Noa, from the mast. "Two lines, a gap between, nothing across it. Not a stack. That's clean." He looked at his tape. "And the cord's back on the sack. That's a count and a third, the gap. I've measured it."

"That's too long," said Kiva.

"How do you know?"

"Because it's a count and a third." She shut the Ember. "Nobody's a quarter of a count. When I cross, the gap's a quarter. I've been it. I know how long nobody is. I've been nobody in the air over an oak floor and on a hill and in this yard, and it's never once been a whole count. If it were a whole count I'd have hit the ground."

"Then how do we make it a quarter?" said Perrin.

Kiva looked at Pru, and at Perrin on his mark, and at the cord going across the lane to a hat.

"Somebody calls it," she said. "Not *mine* and *yours.* That's two words, and the gap's between them, and it's as long as the time it takes to hear the first and say the second. Somebody calls the gap itself. One word. Pru shuts on the start of it. I open on the end of it. Then it's as long as a word."

"Whose word?"

"Mine." She heard herself say it and did not take it back. "I'm the one who's been in it. I'm the only one on this yard who knows how long a gap is from the inside. And I'm the next door. If I call it, I'm calling my own opening; I'll not be late on my own word."

"And the second pass? You into me?"

"Mine as well. I shut on the start, you open on the end. I'll be in the air."

"You'll be calling while you're nobody."

"Yes," said Kiva. "I'll be nobody with my mouth open. Orla would say that's how I've always been."

Nobody laughed. Pru looked at her for a long moment, and then down at her own hands, at the little finger that came across a cord.

"What's the word?" she said.

Kiva had not thought of one. She found that she had one ready, all the same; it had been lying at the back of her mouth since a Thursday on an oak floor, a month ago, in the air.

"*Nobody,*" she said.

They tried it four more times, before the light went, and it did not work. It was better each time. The gap went from a count and a third to a count, and to two-thirds, and on the last it was a half; and on the last the cord went back across the lane only a hand's width before the Ember caught it, and jerked, and came, and Kiva went four paces east with it and called *nobody* in the air and Perrin opened late, and it went back again, all the way, to the hat.

"Half," said Noa. "Clean. Two gaps, nothing across either." He wound the tape. "It isn't a quarter."

"No," said Kiva. "It isn't yet."

Jude entered it on the shed step, by the lamp. *Saturday the 7th. The Old Yard, on the Low Lane's practice mast, not sanctioned, read by N. Bexley and entered nowhere. A cord passed between three: Finch, Rowan, Cade. Finch, three wards, 0.18. Cade, a stance, 0.04. Rowan, five Embers, off her own thimble. Spent, 0.22. Held, eight and fifty-nine.* He looked up.

"And *went wrong?*" he said. "You'll want that."

"*Mine before yours, between unlike doors, read as one line,*" said Kiva. "*Two people on one thing, agreeing.* Write that the Marshal had a lodger go to two people on a sack, in the Pit, in the morning. Write that the gap's half a count and wants to be a quarter."

"And *why?*"

"Leave it white."

He left it white.

Noa was still at the mast. He had his notebook open on the drum, and he was writing, small, and when he had done he held it out to her without a word. It was a page with three columns ruled on it, people and rifts and Saltmere, in his small square hand, with a great many figures in each. At the bottom, under all three, he had begun a line of his own, and not ruled it.

*Two persons, unlike doors, one load, agreeing: one line on the tape. Q.: is it a stack? Q.: whose? I do not know. I am not going to say.*

"I'll not enter it," he said. "I said I wouldn't. But I'd like it in my book as a question. If you'll let me."

"It's your book," said Kiva.

"It's your tape." Noa went pink. "I'm asking."

She looked at the page. She thought about being asked, and about all the people in that city who had been read off a tape in the last year without anybody asking them first, herself among them; and about a boy who went to the superseded stacks on his own time to look up a word.

"Yes," she said. "Ask it."

---

She crossed on Sunday morning, the 8th, at seven, in the Old Yard, on the Circuit's own mast, with Noa at the drum and Orla on her crate.

It went as the first had gone. *Hand, to Ember. Rowan, changing on three.* The ward at her hip, and Orla's stick on it, once, ringing; the fold, the penny, the shut; nobody, in the hands, cold. The Ember, glad. The bound, six paces, onto a white cross in the frost.

"One cliff," said Noa. "Twenty-six. Thirty in all. A crossing, not a stack."

"*Two,*" said Orla.

The hand was white to the wrist and an inch above it. Kiva held it up to the grey morning and looked at it, and opened and shut it, and the eight fingers went slower than they had gone a week before, the order taking a long way round.

She thought about the yard the afternoon before, and the cord going back across the lane to a hat. She thought about a quarter of a dram going out of her, alone, between two of her own doors, for a gap that nobody else could feel; and about a gap between two people that could be called, and heard, and timed by a boy with a tape, and that cost nothing at all except that somebody had to say the word at the right moment and somebody else had to believe her.

It was a queer exchange. It was ash for trust. She had never before been on the side of it that had the trust.

"You're thinking," said Orla, from the crate.

"I'm working out how long a word is."

"Which word?"

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

Her sponsor looked at her for a long moment, with her crooked face quite still.

"That's two syllables," she said. "In my mouth. In yours it's about one and a half. You swallow the end." She turned the stick between her palms. "Say it shorter."
