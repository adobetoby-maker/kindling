# Chapter 12 — Kiva: A Count Everybody Can Hear

Kellan Renn had written down everything that he knew about Team Seven by half past five on Monday afternoon, on four sheets of the refinery's yellow foolscap, and when he had finished he sat on the bench in the Halden locker room and read it over and found that he did not like himself very much.

It was a good list. That was the trouble.

He had not had to think about any of it. It had come out of the end of his pencil as fast as he could write, in the order that it happened on a floor, as though it had been waiting behind his teeth since the Regional to be asked for. *The catcher tests ground before she stands on it. Always. In the open, a full count before she uses it. Brask had a boy stand on her marks. Since the sixteenth of last month she tests under Cade instead: he plants, she opens under his heels, she says "mine," he says "yours," and steps off. Three counts. In those three counts Cade can't move.* He had drawn a line under that one without meaning to.

*On a litter over a wall they take before they let go. "Mine," then "yours." Two to the poles for half a count.*

*Cade calls "off" early. He calls it when it's wanted, she'd say. I'd say early.*

*Pryce walks a wall. Forty paces. On Wednesday she turned one round a moving thing; I wasn't there, I had it from Hesketh, who had it from a junior. She calls her own turn now.*

*Talley cuts heavy, from the shoulder, and stays over what he's cut. He's learning not to. He's not learned it.*

*Last Monday, at the Pit, Cade counted a pack aloud from the steps. One-and-two-and. He's to count on any floor where a thing keeps time, and he's to haul nothing while he counts. He minds.*

And at the bottom, by itself, because he had written it last and then sat looking at it for some time:

*They say everything aloud. It's their whole method. They say what they'll do and whose it is and when it changes hands, and they say it so that anybody can hear it.*

He had taught them some of that. He had stood in the Old Yard in the spring with his ears white and asked a catcher *now?* over his shoulder for the first time in his life, because he had been made to see that a blade who cuts on his own word is a blade who cuts on the wrong one. He had been proud of it. He was still proud of it.

And he had written it down on yellow paper, in a locker room, for Pettit.

"You've been an hour," said Pettit, from the door.

Pettit was a heavy fair boy with a still door and a slow, sure, rather sleepy way of standing that made people underestimate him, which he liked. He had been Halden License's captain since the Regional. He had never once asked Kellan what Seven would do, in all the weeks that Kellan had cut for him; and Kellan had been grateful, and had also noticed it, and wondered what it meant.

"It's done," said Kellan.

"You needn't."

"I know I needn't. Birch said. You said, at the Regional. *Nothing owed.*" Kellan squared the four sheets on his knee. "That's why I've done it."

Pettit came in and sat on the bench opposite and did not reach for the sheets.

"Tell me why," he said. "Before I read it. I'd like to know what I'm being given."

Kellan had worked that out on the tram, too, as he worked everything out, and he had not cared for the sum.

"I stood up at a kitchen table a fortnight ago," he said, "and told four people and a first-year that I wanted to know what I am. I said it to their faces. I said I wanted to stand in the best four in the country and find out whether I'm the weak corner." He looked at the yellow paper. "Tomorrow there's a lane. The Marshal's put us against them. If I go out on that yard and cut as a blade cuts, and don't use what I know, then I'm not standing for Halden. I'm visiting. I'll have kept a piece of myself back for them, and it'll be the best piece, and I'll never know whether I'm the weak corner because I'll never have stood in your four with the whole of me."

"And if you use it, and they lose?"

"Then they'll have lost to a four that read them," said Kellan. "Which is what'll happen at Saltmere, on the first board that matters, to a four from the coast that's never heard of the Old Yard. Brask read them in a fortnight with a notebook. I've had a year." He held the sheets out. "Better me than a stranger. That's the other half. I'd not have said it first, since it sounds noble, and it isn't. The first half's the true one."

Pettit took the sheets. He read them slowly, all four, with his lips not moving, the way that a man reads who intends to remember; and when he came to the last line he read it twice.

"*They say everything aloud,*" he said.

"Yes."

"And you'll be listening."

"I'll be listening," said Kellan. "I taught Rowan's four half their words. I'd know them in my sleep."

Pettit folded the sheets and put them in his jersey.

"Yarrow'll want them," he said. "And Hesketh. I'll not have them read in the yard. Not where Birch can see; he'd think we'd put you up to it." He got up. "Renn. I'll say a thing, since you've said yours. We didn't ask you because we thought you'd say no. And then we'd have had to know that you'd said no."

"I'd have said yes."

"I know that now," said Pettit, and went out.

Kellan sat in the locker room for some time after he had gone. He had his cuff in his lap. He was counting the eyelets on it, and he made himself stop at nine.

It was not a betrayal. He had been over it on the tram from both ends, as a sum, and it was not. Seven knew what he knew; they had been there for every word of it. He had given away nothing that was not already on their own sheets, in Rowan's own hand, in seven columns. All that he had done was to put it in the shape that a four uses when it wants to beat somebody.

That was what was hard. Not that it was theirs. That it was so easy to write down.

---

Healer Aske had the hand at seven on Monday, under the lamp at the Wren House, and he held the wrist for a minute by his watch and said, "Cold to the wrist. Not past. Slower to shut than last week." He wrote it on the card. "*Monday the 9th. After the second crossing. Cold to the wrist at seven.* I'll see it at noon."

At noon it was cold to the knuckle. At four, Kiva went back up the Wren House stair on her own, and he took the wrist again, and nodded.

"Warm," he said. "Four o'clock. Last week it was noon." He looked at her over his spectacles. "That's four hours. I said I'd say so each time."

"You've said."

"Then I'll write it." He did. *Warm at four. A sulk of four hours longer than the first.* "I don't know yet what it's sulking about, and I'd like you to know that I've looked. It isn't the two fingers; they're as they were. It's the rest of the hand, and it's slow to come back from wherever it goes when the ward shuts." He capped his pen. "The card stands. Sunday."

She went down the hill to the Old Yard with the hand in her coat and the four hours in her head. Somebody on the Pit's rail had said on Saturday that a thing which got worse a little each time was a thing that had a far end, and she had not liked it then and liked it less now.

The Marshal had the Old Yard to herself when Kiva got there, with a ball of chalk and a hurdle.

---

The lane on Tuesday was not the lane that Kiva knew.

It had the same two rows of black posts, and the same scraped strip of dirt between them, forty paces from the sheds to the gap in the bottom wall; and it had the same crate a pace north of where it ought to be, with Orla on it. But Coombe had been at it since seven with a Wren House man and a handcart, and when the first company came down the yard at two and stood along the north row, they found that it had been made into something else.

There were two hurdles. Each was a sheep hurdle of woven hazel set upright in the dirt, chest high, across the whole width of the lane; one stood six paces on the sheds' side of the middle and one six paces on the gap's side, so that the middle twelve paces of the lane were a pen with a hurdle at either end of it.

The litter lay in the middle of the pen. It was Gerald, with his hat on.

And on the dirt inside the pen, and on either side of it, there were squares chalked white, a stride across, five on each side, scattered as though somebody had thrown them.

"Bog," said Coombe. "That's what a chalk square is. A still door doesn't plant in bog. If a foot that's planted is in a square, that's a down, and the planted one kneels. Any other door may cross it. A blade may stand in it all day." She tied a knot. "Circuit rules otherwise. Three for the litter over your own line. One for a down. If the glass runs out, a point to the half where it's lying. And a litter that goes over a hurdle goes over on a pass, two to two, top to top; it's not thrown, it's not dragged, and if it touches the hurdle's top it's dropped and it's anybody's. Three carries. I've a glass." She held it up. "Twenty-Eight and Old Four have had theirs this morning. It was five to four, and I'll not tell you which way."

"Old Four," said Selka Dray, from the north row, on her sticks. "She'll not tell you, so I shall. Brask's notebook."

"Seven, Halden," said Coombe. "Declare."

Perrin stood on the shed line with his cuff in his fist.

"T7. Captain, Cade, carry. Still. Catcher, Rowan. One door, declared: the still. Hold, Pryce. Hand. Kill, Talley." He said it straight through, without stopping, and Kiva heard that it had been said in a mirror that morning a good many times.

"Halden License," said Pettit, at the gap. "Captain, Pettit, carry. Still. Catcher, Yarrow. Hand. Hold, Hesketh. Hand. Blade, Renn. Ember."

Kellan stood at the north end of the black-and-gold line with his own cuff laced. He looked down the lane. He looked at Perrin, and at Anwen, and at Jude's white boot, and then at Kiva, quite steadily, for about a count; and then he looked at the chalk squares inside the pen, and Kiva saw his lips move once, as though he were adding something up.

"*Go,*" said Coombe, and turned the glass.

---

They did what they had done against Old Four, because it had been the best carry that they had ever made, and because nobody had told them not to.

Perrin went up the north side of the lane at a walk, and Kiva went with him, and Anwen went up the south with her hands low; and at the near hurdle Perrin went over in one heave, with his hands on the hazel, and Kiva after him, and they were in the pen. Jude came last, slowly, on his bad leg, and stood at the hurdle where he could see.

Kiva looked at the dirt.

There were three chalk squares on the north side of the litter, between it and the posts, and two on the south. She looked for the nearest ground that was not chalk and was not anybody's, and it was two paces north of the litter's head, between two squares.

"There," she said. "North of the head. Between the chalk."

Perrin went to it and planted. The lane boomed. He stood on it like a post going in.

Kiva opened her still door under his boots.

She had done it fifty times since the sixteenth of the first month. She knew how it went. She felt the dirt under his heels come up into her door, packed and dry, and solid to a depth that she could not find the end of; and no chalk; and no room underneath.

"*Mine,*" she said.

"*Yours,*" said Perrin, and stepped off, forward, toward the litter's poles.

And Kellan touched him.

Kiva did not see Kellan come. Nobody did. He was at the gap end with his cuff laced and his edge unlit, and then he was not; and then there were two long low bounds along the south row of posts, dark, and a line of blue-white along his fist, and he was in the pen, behind Perrin, a pace off, at exactly the moment that Perrin's weight came off his planted heel and went forward onto his toe.

It was a light touch. It was a tap on a door, between the shoulder blades.

"Down," said Coombe. "Cade. Kneel."

Perrin knelt. He knelt with his hands a foot from the poles and his mouth open.

Pettit was in the pen. He had come over Halden's hurdle without anybody seeing him either, because everybody had been looking at Kellan; and he did not go to the litter's head, where Kiva was planted on Perrin's ground with her door open and nothing to lean against. He went to its foot. He took the foot poles and turned the litter round on its head like a gate on a hinge, half a circle, and Yarrow had the head poles before it had stopped, and they were carrying.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen.

Her wall came up across the pen, silver, low, between the litter and Halden's hurdle. It was a good wall. It came up fast and true and it stood.

Hesketh's came against it from the other side, and the two walls met in the middle of the pen with a noise like a shut drawer and leaned on each other; and while they leaned, Yarrow and Pettit carried Gerald round the south end of both of them, through the gap between Anwen's wall and the south row of posts, which was exactly a litter's width, because Hesketh had pushed it there.

At the hurdle they went over two to two. Yarrow put the head poles on the hazel's top and said nothing at all, and Pettit, behind him, put the foot poles on, and the two men on the far side, who were only Hesketh, coming off her wall, and Kellan, coming off his touch, took the poles from beneath, and the litter went over the hurdle as smoothly as a tray going over a counter.

Nobody had said *mine.* Nobody had said *yours.* Kiva had been listening for it with every hair on her head, and it had not been said.

They carried Gerald down the last six paces and over the gap line with his hat on.

"Three," said Coombe. "And one. Halden, four. Seven, nothing. Set."

---

Perrin got up off his knees as though he were a great deal older.

"I'd like it known," he said, "that I didn't see him."

"You weren't meant to," said Jude. He had come over the near hurdle, slowly, and stood beside them in the pen. "He went on your *yours.* The moment you went off your heel. I saw that much from the wall. He was in the air before you'd said it. He was going on the word."

"He knew it was coming," said Anwen.

"Of course he knew it was coming," said Kiva.

She was very angry. She was surprised to find that she was, and she put the anger down on the dirt beside her, carefully, and looked at it, because it was not useful and she would want to know afterward what it had been.

It was not at Kellan. That was what surprised her. He had done exactly what she would have done, if she had had a year of somebody's words; she would have listened for the one that meant a man's weight was going to move, and gone on it. She was angry at the word. She had said *mine* fifty times since the sixteenth of last month, aloud, in front of everybody, and been pleased with how well it worked; and every time she had said it she had been telling the whole yard that in three counts the carry would step off his heel.

"It's Brask," she said. "It's Brask with a boy on my marks, over again. Only it's not my test he's read. It's the word after it."

"We don't say it, then," said Perrin.

"We have to say it. It's how you know I've tested."

"Then I'll stand on the mark and you test and you don't say it, and I'll guess."

"And step off a count early onto ground I've not finished with." She shook her head. "No."

"Then *I'll* hold the mark," said Jude.

They looked at him.

"I'm the Kill. I'm wanted when there's a thing to cut. In a lane there's nothing to cut but people, and Renn's the one I'd want, and he's not near the litter till he's touched somebody." Jude scratched his jaw. "So I plant, and Rowan tests under me, and Cade goes to the poles from the start without waiting for anybody. And when I step off, I'm the one he touches. I'd sooner it was me than the carry."

It was a good notion. Kiva could see nothing wrong with it, which was what worried her.

"Yes," said Perrin. "Talley holds. I'm on the poles. Pryce, a wall south, early, before Hesketh's up, so they can't go round the end." He looked at the hurdle at their own end, six paces behind them, the hazel chest high. "And when we get to that, it's *mine* and *yours.* It has to be. It's a pass."

"*Go,*" said Coombe.

---

Jude planted two paces north of the litter's head. Kiva opened her door under him.

"*Mine,*" she said, quite low.

"*Yours,*" said Jude, and did not step off. He stood. He stood on the mark like a man waiting for a tram, with his long arms loose and his head on one side, and his eyes on the south row of posts, where a blue-white line had gone along last time; and Kiva planted on his ground, at his heel, with her door open, and leaned her weight into the dirt, and the yard took her.

Perrin had the head poles. He was lifting.

Pettit came over Halden's hurdle and set his feet in front of Perrin and opened his door, and leaned.

It came down through Perrin and through the litter's poles and through Kiva's shoulder where it touched Perrin's back, and it went into the ground under her, and stopped. It was like having a wardrobe pushed against one's back by a patient man. She did not move. Perrin did not move. Pettit leaned, with his slow sleepy face and his whole weight, and nothing moved at all.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen, and her wall came up south, early, across the pen's end, before Hesketh's had begun.

Kellan was not on the south side.

Kiva looked for him and could not find him, and that was worse than seeing him. He was not on the north row. He was not in the pen. Then she saw him, at Halden's hurdle, standing quite still on the far side of the hazel, dark, with his cuff laced and his hand down. He was not coming. He was watching.

He was watching Jude.

"He's waiting," said Jude, without moving his head. "For me to come off."

"Then don't come off."

"I'm not."

Perrin and Yarrow had the litter between them, pole to pole, and nobody was carrying it anywhere. Yarrow had come round Hesketh's wall and taken the foot poles from the other side, and he and Perrin were standing in the middle of the pen with Gerald between them, each pulling, like two men with a sack of meal in a doorway.

"Glass," said Coombe, from the crate. "Half."

It was going to be the glass, then. The litter was in the middle of the pen, a hair to Seven's side, Kiva thought, and if the sand ran out with it there, that was a point. That was something.

Then Pettit stopped leaning.

He did it all at once. He took his door off Perrin as one takes a hand off a door, and Perrin, who had been leaning back against it with the whole of his weight for half a minute, went forward a step before he could stop himself, onto the litter's poles, toward Yarrow. The litter went with him a yard. A yard toward Halden's hurdle.

Kiva had her door under her own heels and her weight in the ground and nothing in front of her to stop. She stayed where she was, planted, and the litter went away from her.

"*Perrin—*"

"I've got it." He had. He set his feet and dragged it back, and Yarrow let him, a foot, two feet; and then Yarrow and Pettit together took the foot poles, and Hesketh had come off her wall and had the north side, and it was three on the litter to one, and they lifted, and carried.

"*Jude!*" said Perrin.

Jude came off the mark.

He had to. There was nobody else. He went across the pen at his limping run to the litter's south side to take a pole, and Kiva saw his weight go off his planted heel and onto his toe.

Kellan came over the hurdle.

It was one bound. He did not touch Jude. He went past Jude, and past the litter, and touched Anwen, who had let her wall down a moment before to run for a pole herself, between the shoulders, as lightly as a man taps a barometer.

"Down," said Coombe. "Pryce."

And at Halden's hurdle there was nobody to take the litter on the far side, because Kellan was in the pen; and so Pettit and Yarrow did not go over. They turned. They put the litter down, quite gently, in the middle of Halden's half of the pen, a yard short of their own hurdle, and stood over it.

The last of the sand ran out.

"Halden's half," said Coombe. "One. And one for the down. Halden, six. Seven, nothing." She set the glass on the crate. "Set."

---

"He didn't go for me," said Jude.

He was sitting on the hurdle, at their own end, with his white boot out in front of him. Nobody had told him to sit. He had sat because it was the first place he came to.

"He waited for you to come off the mark, and he knew you'd have to, and then he went past you. He went for Pryce." He scratched his jaw, slowly. "He knew she'd drop her wall to run. She always drops her wall to run. I've seen her do it all year."

"So have I," said Anwen. She was standing at the hurdle with her arms crossed and her face quite white. "I didn't know I did it."

"He knew."

"Of course he knew. He's stood next to me on a floor every week since the fifth month." She looked down the lane at the black-and-gold line, at the gap. "He's got us written down. All of us. I'd want it said that that's what's happening."

"It's said," said Kiva.

She was looking at the crate. Orla sat on it with her stick between her boots and her face as still as a shut door; and the Marshal stood beside it with her rope in her hand, and she was not looking at Seven at all. She was looking at Kellan. She had been looking at Kellan, Kiva thought, since the glass was first turned.

Perrin had not said anything.

He was standing in the middle of the pen with his hands hanging, where he had been when the sand ran out; and he was looking at the litter, lying a yard from Halden's hurdle with its hat on. He had gone red when the first carry went. He had not gone red this time. He had gone a queer flat color, as though somebody had wiped him with a cloth.

"I've lost it," he said.

"It's two carries," said Jude.

"I've lost the lane. We're six behind with one to go, and there's three for the litter and one a down, and we'd need them all, and Renn's got every word we've got." Perrin turned round. "That's mine. I want that said. I'm the captain, and I put Talley on the mark, and I put Pryce south, and every time I've opened my mouth this afternoon I've told him what I was going to do next."

Kiva opened her own mouth, and shut it.

It was not hers to say. She had wanted to, very badly; she had wanted to tell him that it was not his, that it was all of theirs, that it was the four's and the words' and a year of Kellan standing next to them. It was all true. It was also what one said to a person who had just taken a thing on himself, to make him put it down again, and she had seen in the spring what it did to Anwen to have her things taken off her before she had finished carrying them.

So she waited.

"You're wrong about one part," said Coombe.

She had come up the lane. Nobody had seen her come; she was simply at the near hurdle, with her rope, and the pale eyes were on Perrin.

"It isn't yours. It's your four's, and it was your four's before you were captain of it. But a captain doesn't stand in a pen and tell me whose it is." She tied a knot. "A captain says what's next. Say what's next."

Perrin stared at her.

"I don't know what's next," he said.

"Then find out. You've two minutes before I turn this glass, and four people who want to be told." The Marshal turned her back on him and walked down to the crate. "I'll want it said aloud, Cade, since that's how you do things in this yard. I'll be listening. So will he."

---

They stood in the pen in a ring, the four of them, with their heads close, and Pru came over the hurdle from the shed step to stand behind Kiva, since she was the reserve and nobody had told her not to.

"He's keeping our count," said Perrin.

He said it slowly. He had his eyes shut.

"That's what it is. It's the pack. He's not looking at us. He's listening. *Mine. Yours.* He goes on *yours.* When Talley's planted, he waits for Talley to come off, since he knows Talley will have to. When Pryce's wall's up, he waits for her to drop it and run, since she always does." He opened his eyes. "We're a pack. We've a count among ourselves. And he's cutting on it."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then he's cutting in time," said Jude.

"He's cutting in time, and it works on us, since we're not a pack of husks. We fall over." Perrin looked round at them. "So we break our own count. We do what Talley did to the near one in the Pit. We go on the *and.*"

"We can't go on the *and* if we've told him where the *one* is," said Anwen.

"Then we don't tell him." Perrin put his big hand on the litter's near pole, where it lay in the middle of the pen. "No words on a pass. None. When I'm ready to give, I tap the pole. Twice. On the second tap, you take. You don't say *mine;* you take. And I let go when I feel you've got it, and I don't say *yours.* It's the same as before. It's only that nobody can hear it."

"He'll see you tap," said Kiva.

"He'll see the first one. Then he has to guess how long till the second. And it won't be the same each time." Perrin swallowed. "I'll count it in my head. A different count each time. Two. Four. Three. Nobody knows it but me, and whoever's taking watches my hand and not my mouth."

"That's Pike," said Kiva.

She said it before she had thought. They all looked at her.

"The heel. He watches the blade's heel, not her face. He calls *now* off her heel and she doesn't know he does it. It's a count that nobody says." She looked at Perrin's hand on the pole. "It's yours. It's only that a catcher's been doing it for twenty-six years without telling anybody."

"Then that's the pass," said Perrin. "And the mark. Rowan tests under Talley, and says nothing. Talley watches Rowan's left hand. When it's flat on her knee, she's tested and it's clean. When it's in her coat, it isn't." He looked at Kiva. "Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Talley doesn't come off the mark. Not for anything. If I'm short a pole, I'm short a pole." He went on, faster now. "Pryce. You run with your wall up. Walk it. Walk it the way you turned Talley and me on the sheet, round the end, and don't drop it till you've got the pole in your hand. He's waiting for you to drop it."

"I can't walk it and carry."

"Then you walk it and don't carry, and I'm short a pole, and I drag it. I've dragged Gerald before." He took his hand off the litter. "And Talley."

"Aye."

"When he comes, he'll come for whoever's passing. He's been going for whoever's about to move. You don't go to the litter. You go to him. You watch him and nothing else, and when he comes over that hurdle you're between him and the pass."

"That's not a Kill's job."

"In a lane it's the only job a Kill's got," said Perrin. "You said so yourself in the first carry, and I didn't listen."

He stood up straight. He looked, Kiva thought, about as happy as a man who has been told that the boat is leaving and he is to row it.

"That's what's next," he said. He said it to the crate, loudly. "Ma'am."

"I heard," said Coombe, and turned the glass.

---

Kellan heard it too. Kiva watched him hear it, at the gap end, with his cuff laced. She watched him take in the tap and the hand on the knee and the Kill who was to stand between him and the pass, every word of it, as he had taken in every word all afternoon; and she saw his lips move once, adding.

It did not matter. That was what Perrin had seen, and what she had not seen until he said it. It did not matter that Kellan had heard what they would do. He had not heard when.

Jude planted two paces north of the litter's head. Kiva opened her door under him, and felt the dirt come up clean, and put her left hand flat on her knee, and said nothing.

Jude did not move his head. He had his eyes on her hand. He saw it go flat, and his shoulders went down an inch, and that was all.

Perrin had the head poles. Pettit came over Halden's hurdle and leaned on him, and it went down through Perrin and through Kiva and into the ground, and stopped, as it had stopped before. Pettit leaned. Nobody moved.

Anwen's wall came up south. She did not drop it. She walked it, round the end of the pen, slowly, from its far edge, as she had walked it round two boys and a sheet, and Hesketh's wall came against it and could not get round the end of it, because the end was coming round to meet her.

Yarrow came for the foot poles. Anwen's wall was between him and them. He went round the north side, past the chalk, and got a hand on the foot, and Perrin let him, and they stood again, pole to pole, pulling.

"Glass," said Coombe. "Half."

Pettit stopped leaning, all at once, as he had before.

Perrin went forward a step onto the poles, as he had before. And this time he did not stop himself. He went with it. He let the litter go a yard toward Yarrow, and another, so that Yarrow, who had been pulling against a still door with his whole weight, found that he was pulling against nothing and sat down in the dirt with Gerald's foot poles in his lap.

Perrin tapped the head pole.

It was a small sound, knuckles on ash wood. Kiva heard it. Kellan heard it; she saw his head come round at the gap.

Nobody took the pole. Anwen was walking her wall. Jude was on the mark. Kiva was planted at Jude's heel with her door open. There was nobody to take it, and Perrin had known there would be nobody, and he took the head poles in both hands and dragged.

He dragged Gerald backward across the pen, away from Yarrow, on the litter's foot, with the foot poles scraping the dirt, toward Seven's hurdle. He went through a chalk square, which did not matter, because he was not planted; he was dragging, like a carter with a broken cart, with his ribs telling him things and his face scarlet.

He got to the hurdle.

"*Pryce,*" said Kiva. It was not a call. It was a name.

Anwen let her wall go. She did it a pace from the hurdle, not before, with her hand already going for the near pole; and she went over the hazel in one scramble and stood on the far side with her hands up, under the poles' ends.

Perrin lifted the head poles onto the hurdle's top. He tapped. Once.

Kellan came over Halden's hurdle.

Kiva saw him go. She was planted, with her door open, and she could not have moved if the lane had caught fire, and she saw him come: one bound into the pen, dark, and a second already gathering, along the north row of posts toward the place where Perrin stood at the hurdle with his knuckles on the pole and Anwen waiting under it. He was going for the pass. He had heard the first tap and he had seen Perrin's hand and he had done the sum, as he did every sum, and he was going for the second tap before it came.

Jude was between them.

He had come off the mark. He had come off it a count earlier, on nothing, without being told; he had seen Kellan's weight shift at the far hurdle the way that Pike saw a heel, and he had gone, limping, across the pen, on his bad leg. He did not go to the litter. He did not go to the hurdle. He went to a place on the dirt where a blade in the air would come down if it was going to the hurdle by the north row, and he stood there, and lit.

Kiva had never seen his kill lit in a lane. It came down out of his shoulder the way it had come down on the husk in the Pit, heavy and straight; and Kellan came down out of the air into it.

"Down," said Coombe. "Renn."

Kellan knelt in the dirt, with his edge out, a foot from Jude's boots. He looked up at Jude. His face was quite still, and very white, and something was happening at the corner of his mouth that Kiva could not read.

Perrin tapped.

Anwen took the poles from under. She did not say *mine.* Perrin felt her take them and let go, and did not say *yours,* and the litter came down off the hazel into Anwen's arms and Jude's, who had come back for it, and onto the dirt on Seven's side of the hurdle.

"*Carry,*" said Perrin.

They carried. It was six paces to the hurdle and six more to the shed line, and they had five of the twelve when Hesketh's wall came up across the lane in front of them, silver, from row to row; Hesketh had come round the end of her own pen at a run with her hands up, and Pettit was behind her, and Yarrow had got up out of the dirt.

They pushed. Anwen put her own wall against Hesketh's and walked it, from the far end, and Hesketh's bent and gave a foot.

The sand ran out.

"Seven's half," said Coombe. "One. And one for the down. Seven, two." She set the glass on the crate. "Halden, six. Seven, two. That's the lane."

---

They sat on the shed step afterward, in a row, the four of them and Pru, with the frost coming back onto the yard as the light went. Nobody had anything to say.

Coombe stood in front of them. She did not sit. She had her rope in her hand and a great many knots in it.

"You lost," she said.

"Yes, ma'am," said Perrin.

"Six to two. You lost because your best habits were the first things that a good four took off you. Your test, your *mine,* your wall that drops when it runs. You've built every good thing you have this year on saying it aloud, and you said it in front of a man who'd written it down." She tied a knot. "That's not a fault in saying it aloud. On a floor with a thing that has no ears, it's the best habit you've got, and I'll want every word of it on the twelfth. In a lane, against a four that's done its reading, it's a count everybody can hear."

Nobody answered.

"In the last carry you broke your own count. Cade's tap. Talley off the mark on the blade's weight and not on a word. Pryce walking her wall and not dropping it till the pole was in her hand." The Marshal looked along the row. "It got you a down and a point and the litter over your hurdle. It was the only carry this afternoon that Halden didn't read before you'd made it. Do it again. Not here. On a floor of your own choosing, in front of somebody who's had your words for a year." She coiled the rope. "And then do it to a coast four that's never heard of you, at a gate, and see if it holds."

She went up the yard and out by the side gate.

Kellan came down the lane a quarter of an hour later, alone, in his own coat, with his cuff in his pocket.

He stood in front of the shed step where the Marshal had stood. His ears were white.

"I wrote you down," he said. "Yesterday. Four sheets. I gave them to Pettit. I'd sooner you heard it from me."

"We'd guessed," said Anwen.

"I know you had. I'd still sooner you heard it." He took a breath, and let it out. "I'll tell you what was on them. All of it. You'll meet a four at Saltmere that has it written. Better you know what a page of you looks like, and better it's me who wrote it."

"Why?" said Perrin.

"Because I said at your table that I wanted to know what I am, and I found out this afternoon that I'm a man who can write you down in an hour," said Kellan. "And I'd sooner be that man where you can see him."

Kiva had the Works paper out. There were three sheets left. She took one and ruled it in seven, on her knee, while he talked, and he talked for a long time, standing in the cold, in order, as things happen on a floor. *The test in the open. Mine, then yours, then three counts with the carry on his heel. The wall that drops when it runs.* He did not leave anything out. He did not make any of it kinder than it was.

When he had done, she read it back to him, and he nodded.

"That's what Brask has," he said. "And more. Brask doesn't know about the wall."

"He will," said Anwen.

"He will." Kellan looked at Jude. "You came off the mark on my weight. Not on a word. Who told you to do that?"

"Nobody," said Jude. "I watched your feet. Rowan's catcher does it. It's on a sheet."

Kellan was quiet for a moment.

"I didn't see you come," he said. "I'd the tap counted. I'd have had the pass on the second, I'm nearly sure, I'd the count in my head, and I came down into you." Something moved at the corner of his mouth again, and this time Kiva could read it. It was not hurt. It was the look that he had had in the spring, at a kitchen table, the first time that a catcher said *now* to him and it was right. "I'd like to try it again. The tap. When there's a floor. I'd like to know whether I can read it, now that I know it's there."

"So would we," said Perrin.

"Then I'll come." Kellan put his hands in his pockets. "On a day that isn't a Tuesday or a Thursday. And I'll not write it down."

"Write it down," said Kiva. "We'll want to know what you read."

He looked at her. He looked at the sheet on her knee, ruled in seven, with his own words in the first four columns.

"I'd expect nothing else," said Kellan. He said it to the sheet.

When he had gone up the yard, Jude opened the book on his knee and wrote by the last of the light.

*Tuesday the 10th. The Old Yard, the Marshal's lane, against Halden License. Three carries. Lost, six to two. Pryce, two walls and a walk, 0.19. Cade, three stances and a drag, 0.15. Talley, one down, 0.12. Rowan, stances, off her own thimble. Spent, 0.46.* He did the sum twice. *Held, seven and eighty-three.* He looked up. "And the crossing on Sunday. I've not read that out. Three-tenths. It was eight and twenty-nine before this."

"That adds," said Anwen.

"It adds." Jude shut the book. "I'd like it said that I've never once enjoyed it adding."

Kiva wrote, under *Went wrong,* in the last of the light: *Our count was everybody's. He kept it.* And under *Second try,* because it was true and because she would want to read it on the twelfth: *A tap, not a word. A hand on a knee. A Kill who watches the blade's feet. Ours, and nobody else's.*

Then she sat with the pencil over the *Why* column for some time, with the frost coming down on the yard, and found that for once she had something to put in it.

*Why: a thing with no ears can't hear us say it. A man can. We'll want both.*
