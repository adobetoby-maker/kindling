# Chapter 15 — Kiva: Something Quiet

The carrier put them down at the foot of Cowl Edge at a quarter to three on Thursday afternoon, with their sheets and their poles and a sack of bread, and turned his cart round in the lane without getting off it.

"I'll be here Saturday at noon," he said. "If you're not here Saturday at noon, I'll be here Saturday at four. If you're not here at four, I'll tell the Line." He looked at the five of them standing in the mud with their gear round their boots. "I've carried a deal of things up this road. I've never carried a team that paid me to go and find something."

"We're licensed," said Perrin.

"I don't doubt it. I've a nephew in the Fourth." The carrier clicked to his horse. "He'd give his ears for it."

The Edge stood up over them in the grey afternoon like the side of a house.

Kiva had never seen it. She had heard of it all her life, as one hears of the sea: a long gritstone scarp on the north side of the Sag, sixty feet high in places, black, with a fringe of dead bracken at its foot and heather on its top. Below it, to the south, the whole country fell away into the great shallow bowl of the Sag itself, where the Line kept its oldest watch. From the lane she could see the bowl's far rim, twelve miles off, blue, with the road to the coast going round it like a thread round a cup.

The Line's hut stood at the Edge's foot, where a path went up through a cleft in the rock. It was built of the same black stone, with a turf roof and one window, and there was smoke coming out of its chimney.

Hollis Vane was standing in its door.

"Cade," he said. "You've come under your own name."

"Yes, sir."

"Then I'll say what I am, once, so that it's said." The Home Line's foreman was a square brown man with a weathered face and a slate under his arm, and he said it in his outdoor voice, as he said everything. "I'm the Line's hand on this ground. I'm a witness and I've a scale. I'll weigh what you bag and comb it and write it on the Board's card. I'll not say a word on your floor. If you're hurt, I'll carry you down. That's all I am." He held out his hand. "The card's four-tenths, to the Board, before you go up."

Jude counted it out of the team's tin into Vane's palm. It was the first ash that Kiva had ever seen a team pay to be allowed to go and do something dangerous, and she watched it go.

"Four-tenths," said Vane, and wrote it. "Come in. I've a fire and a map, and you'll want both."

---

The map was on the hut's table, weighted at its corners with four stones, and it was the Line's own: the Edge and the moor above it, and the gills that came down off the moor into the bowl, drawn in brown ink by somebody who had walked every yard of them.

"The tear was here," said Vane. He put his finger on a place on the moor's top, a mile north of the hut, where three gills began. "On the Saturday, at four. I'd six on the Sag and two up here; we had it shut by eleven. Four came off it first." He moved the finger. "Three went down this gill. Together. Slow. I've had a lad on the Edge with a glass every day since, and they've gone a little way each day, down, and stopped, and gone on. Yesterday they were here." He tapped a place where the gill came out of the moor's edge into the head of a narrow wooded valley. "This morning they were here."

His finger had moved half a mile.

"What's there?" said Perrin.

"Garside's mill." Vane did not look up. "A fulling mill, on the beck. Old. Garside and his wife and two children and a man. It has a wheel."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"A wheel," said Kiva.

"An overshot wheel, eighteen feet. It turns all day and all night, since he has cloth in the stocks for a merchant at the Sag and he's late with it. I sent the lad down this morning to tell him to stop it. He said he'd stop it when the Line paid for the cloth." Vane's voice did not change. "He's not wrong. The Line can't."

"And the fourth?" said Jude.

Vane's finger went the other way. North, up the moor, away from the gills and the valley and the mill, a long way, two miles, to a place where there was a small brown mark in the ink and a word that Kiva could not read upside down.

"*Kiln,*" said Vane. "An old limekiln on the tops. Nobody's burned lime there in a hundred years. It went up there on the Sunday, alone, slow, and it's sat in the kiln's mouth since. It hasn't moved. The lad's been to look twice. It doesn't come out."

"It's gone away from everything," said Kiva.

"It's gone as far from everything as there is on this ground," said Vane. "There's no house within three miles of that kiln. No lamp, no wheel, no water but a spring. There's sheep. It's not touched a sheep." He looked at her for the first time. "I've been on this moor since the Line first gave me a slate, and I've never seen a thing come off a tear and go *up.* They come down. They go to houses. They go to lamps and wheels and drains and banks. That one went up and sat down."

Kiva found that she had put her left hand flat on the map, on the brown ink of the moor, and she took it off again.

"The three first," said Perrin.

He said it at once, before anybody else could. He was standing at the table's end, with the license in his coat and the coil of tarred rope over his shoulder like a bandolier, which he had begun to carry without explaining why.

"The three are going to the mill. The mill has a wheel and four people and a man. If we go up for the quiet one first and walk past three husks on their way to a wheel, and they're at the mill tomorrow night, that's under my name." He looked round at them. "So it's the three, at first light. And if we're still on our feet, the kiln."

"At first light," said Vane, and wrote it on his slate. "I'll say nothing. I'll be behind you with a scale."

---

Kiva lay awake for a long time that night on the hut's plank bench, with Pru asleep against the wall beside her and the fire going down.

She was thinking about a wheel.

She had stood on a pavement on the Brickfields road in the tenth month with her palm on a flagstone and felt a push that came, and then a long nothing, and then came again. It had been a pump, two hundred paces off, driving the water under the street, and the drum that her aunt had filled had fallen in with it. She had stood on the setts outside the tram sheds a fortnight ago and watched a pack walk for a bank house on a count of two.

Everything in this country that came off a tear went toward something that kept time. A string had gone to a drain. A pack had gone to a bank. A cell joined to a bank by nothing but water had fallen into step with it. Three husks were walking down a gill toward an eighteen-foot wheel that turned all night.

And one had gone the other way.

She did not know what that meant. She was very careful, lying on the plank with her eyes open in the dark, not to decide what it meant. She had seen her aunt not decide things for a whole autumn, at a kitchen table, with a pencil, and she had learned how it was done. One wrote down what had happened, and left the square at the end white, and waited.

She only knew what she wanted it to mean. She wanted it so much that it frightened her.

"You're awake," said Jude, from the other bench.

"Yes."

"So am I. I'm thinking about what it's for." His voice was very low in the dark. "I've asked it three times. I'll stop asking it after tomorrow. I'll know."

---

They went down the gill in the grey before sunrise, with the frost on the bracken and their breath going up, and they found the husks' track before they had gone a quarter of a mile.

It was a line of white. Each print was three small round marks, set wide, like the feet of a milking stool, frosted into the dead grass so hard that they crunched under a boot. There were three lines of them, side by side, and every fourth print in each line was a little deeper than the others.

"Stamp," said Perrin, crouching. "That's the one. Every fourth step, they've stamped." He counted along the line with his finger. "One, two, three, *four.* That's light. They're stamping light on four. Like the Pit."

"Then they've still their own count," said Anwen.

"Up here. They had their own count up here." Perrin stood up and looked down the gill, where it went into trees. "We'll see what they've got at the bottom."

They went down in the order that they had settled at the hut, by Perrin's word, and nobody argued. Anwen went first, because a wall wanted to be in front. Then Perrin, with nothing in his hands. Then Kiva, with a cloth on each shoulder, Seven's and Pru's own, and her left hand in her coat. Then Jude on his bad leg, which had outlasted Coombe's Thursday by a fortnight and was better; and Pru last, with Mrs. Pryce's grey double rolled on her back, and the cords and the spare poles and the comb in a bag. Vane came after all of them, a long way back, with his scale on a strap; Kiva did not hear him once.

The gill went down steeply through birches and then through oaks, with the beck loud beside it, white over black stones. The light came up grey. At the bottom of the trees the gill opened out into a small steep valley with the beck going down the middle of it in a cut of its own, and fields either side, and walls. At the foot of the valley, a quarter of a mile off, there was a stone building with a slate roof and a chimney smoking, and against its end, black and wet and turning, a wheel.

Kiva heard it before she could see it properly. It was a deep slow sound under the noise of the water, a creak and a heavy wet slap, over and over, as the buckets came round and emptied.

*Creak. Slap.* A long nothing. *Creak. Slap.*

She stopped on the track. She put her left hand flat on the top stone of the wall beside her, light, with the first two fingers on the cold grit and the three held off it.

*Pushing. And not.* It was very slow. *Pushing.*

"Something's keeping time," she said. "Through the wall. I can't say what time."

"The wheel," said Perrin.

"I'd think so. I can't weigh it."

He stood on the track with his eyes shut, listening, his lips moving. Kiva watched him do it, and saw the moment when he had it.

"*Creak,*" he said. "*And.* It's a two. A slow two. The creak's the one and the slap's the and." He opened his eyes. "Where are they?"

They were in the mill yard.

Kiva saw them from the bottom wall of the last field, where the track came down to the beck. They were standing on the cobbles between the mill's end and the wheel-race, in a row, three of them, tall and grey, with the frost going off them in the grey light and the cobbles white round their feet. They faced the wheel.

They were stamping.

*Creak.* They stamped, all three. *Slap.* They leaned, all three, toward the wheel. *Creak.* They stamped.

It was not their count. It was not the count of four, with a light stamp at the end, that they had walked down the moor on. It was the wheel's. They had come down a gill for two days and stood in a mill yard in front of an eighteen-foot wheel that turned all night, and they had stopped keeping their own time and begun keeping its.

There was a light in the mill house's window. Kiva saw a child's face at it, small and round, and then a woman's hand pulling the child away.

"Under my name," said Perrin, very quietly, to nobody.

He went down to the yard gate, and the others went after him, and he stood at the gate and looked at the yard as Rhea Sorn looked at a street.

"Rowan."

"The yard falls to the race," said Kiva. She had her still door open under her right heel, on the track, by its corners. "East. A hand in four. It's steep. Anything that runs, runs into the race."

"Pryce."

"I'll want a gate between them and the house. And I'll want to turn them, if I can, so that their backs are to the race. It's not a skin; it's a pack. But I'd not have a heap run into that water."

"Talley."

"Three knots. They're close together. I'll cut the near one first, the one by the house. On your *and.*"

"On the wheel's *and,*" said Perrin. "It's theirs now. I'll count it." He took a breath that Kiva heard. "Finch, you're on the rail with the cords and you don't light. Witness."

"Behind you," said Vane, from somewhere up the field.

"*Go,*" said Perrin.

---

Anwen went through the gate first and brought her wall up between the three husks and the mill house door, silver, waist high, across the cobbles. The husks did not turn to it. They faced the wheel and stamped.

Kiva went to her ground. She had chosen it from the field. There was nowhere to stand below the husks; the lip of the race was a yard past their feet. So she had taken a place on the cobbles three paces west of them, on the uphill side, between them and the house. A rung that fell would run east, down the fall, away from her and toward the race, and she would have to throw east after it and have her cloth over it before it got to the lip. She tested her ground, clean, and planted, and stood with her cloth on her shoulder.

"*Creak,*" said Perrin, at the gate. "*And. Creak. And.*"

Jude went in.

He walked toward the near husk on his bad leg with his head on one side, into the breath, with the frost going up his boots. He stood a pace from it. He lifted his arm.

"*Creak—*"

The wheel creaked. The husks stamped.

"*—and,*" said Perrin.

Jude cut.

It was a good cut. It came down on the slap, after the stamp, before the lean, out of time with the husks and in time with nothing; and the near husk fell. It went down onto the cobbles in a heap like poured sand, and Kiva's cloth went over it at the first cast, and she was on her knees on the hem.

"*Dark! One!*"

The other two stopped dead, with their feet up.

*Creak,* said the wheel.

They stamped.

They had not lost it. Kiva felt it go through the canvas and the cobbles, slow, *pushing;* and she saw the two of them come down on the creak together, as though nothing had happened, as though the near one had never been. They had not had to find their count. It was turning in front of them, eighteen feet high, wet and black, and it gave it back to them on every stroke.

"*They've got it,*" said Perrin. His voice cracked. "They didn't lose it. They've— It's the wheel's, they can't lose it, it's *there—*"

Jude cut the second on the next slap.

The knot closed. It closed from the bottom up, as water closes behind an oar, and the third husk leaned in on it, and the two of them stood together, one broad thing with six legs, and stepped onto Jude.

He went back. He went back on the bad leg and it gave, and he went down on one knee on the cobbles in the breath of two husks; and the frost went over his shoulder and down his arm, and he made a sound through his teeth.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen.

Her wall came off the house door and swung across the yard between Jude and the two husks, as she had swung it in the Pit, from the far end; and it stood with the two of them leaning on it, stamping on the creak; and Jude was on the other side of it, on his knee, with his left arm white to the elbow.

"*Talley,*" said Perrin.

"*It's a breath. It's not taken. I can feel my hand.*" Jude got up. He got up badly, and stood. "I can't cut them. Not with that turning. It gives it back to them every stroke."

Kiva was on her knees on the hem with a sealed Handful under her canvas and nobody to haul the corners, and she was looking at the wheel.

It was not hard to see. It had been in front of her since the field. It was eighteen feet of black wet oak, turning on its axle at the end of the mill, and above it, where the race came along the hillside from the beck, there was a wooden channel on stilts that carried the water out over the top of the wheel and let it fall into the buckets; and at the head of the channel, twenty paces up the bank, there was a hatch. A square board of oak in a frame, with an iron bar through a ring at its top, to let the water into the channel or shut it out.

She had seen a bar like it before. She had seen two boys put their whole weight on one, in the dark, under a hill, while a string stood in a drain.

"Stop the wheel," she said.

Perrin turned his head.

"Stop the wheel. They're keeping its time. Take its time away from them." She heard her voice go up and brought it down. "There's a hatch. At the head of the race, up the bank. Shut the hatch and the water stops, and the wheel runs down, and they'll have to find their own count again. And when they're finding it, they've three counts. Like the Pit."

"The miller said he'd not stop it."

"The miller's in the house with his children behind a window," said Kiva. "It's not his to say. It's yours. It says so on your paper."

Perrin looked at the hatch, twenty paces up the bank. He looked at Anwen at the end of her wall, with two husks leaning on it and her heels going back an inch in the cobbles. He looked at Jude with his white arm.

"*Two to the bar,*" he said. "Finch. With me."

"I can't light."

"You can pull. It's a bar. It wants weight, not a door." He was already going. "Pryce. The count's yours. I'm off it. *Yours.*"

There was no gap. Kiva heard it. Anwen had been counting under her breath behind him since the gate, off the husks' feet, as she had counted behind him on a frozen yard with twelve children stamping; and she said it at once, without looking round, with her eyes on the husks and her hands on her wall.

"*Mine. Creak. And. Creak.*"

Perrin went up the bank at a run with Pru behind him, and Kiva heard his boots slip on the frozen turf and catch. He got to the hatch. He put both hands on the bar, and Pru put hers beside them, and they leaned.

The bar did not move.

"It's frozen," said Pru. "It's frozen in its ring."

Perrin planted.

Kiva felt it from forty paces, through the cobbles, through her own planted heel: a still door going down into a hillside like a post going into soft ground, wide, all of it. He had his feet set either side of the hatch frame and his whole weight on the bar, and his still door open under him so that the hill took him and gave him back; and he pulled.

The ice in the ring cracked like a shot.

The hatch went down. It went down into its frame with a groan of wet oak, and the water that had been pouring out along the channel above the wheel stopped pouring, and went back into the beck over the hatch's top in a white sheet, and the channel above the wheel ran thin, and thinner, and was a trickle.

The wheel went on turning.

It had eighteen feet of wet oak and a full set of buckets on it, and it went on turning on its own weight, slower, the creak longer, the slap later, and slower.

*Creak.* The two husks stamped.

*Slap.* They leaned.

*Creak.* It was late. They stamped on the place where it ought to have been, and it came after them, a long beat late; and they stamped again on the creak, and they were late now, and the wheel was slower still.

"*They're losing it,*" said Anwen. "Rowan. They're losing it."

"*Count them,*" said Kiva. "*Not the wheel. Them.*"

The wheel stopped.

It stopped with a bucket at the top, dripping, and a long groan from its axle, and stood. And the yard was suddenly very quiet: only the beck, going over the hatch in its white sheet, and the two husks on the cobbles, with their feet up, quite still, not knowing which foot was meant to be down.

*One,* said Kiva's lips, without her meaning them to. *Two.*

They stamped.

It was not together. The near one stamped, heavy, and the far one a half beat after, as two files of juniors had stamped on a frozen yard when one file sat down. They were not keeping the wheel's count. They were not keeping any count. They were each trying to find one, and finding different ones, and treading on each other's heels.

"*Nearest,*" said Anwen. "*The near one. One. And—*"

"*Talley,*" said Kiva.

Jude was round the end of the wall. He had come round it while she spoke, on his bad leg, with his white arm hanging and his good one up, and he was a pace from the near husk in its breath.

"*—and,*" said Anwen.

He cut.

The near husk fell. It went down in a heap on the cobbles, a yard from the race's edge, and began to run.

It ran east, down the fall of the yard, as Kiva had said that anything would; and it ran fast, open and loose, toward the stone lip of the race, a yard off, and the black water below it.

Kiva's second cloth was on her shoulder. She threw.

It was not a good cast. She threw it from her knees, with her right arm, past Anwen's wall and Jude's legs, at a heap that was moving, and it went out crooked and came down across the heap's leading edge and the race's lip together, half on the cobbles and half hanging over the water.

"*Dark! One!*"

Some of it went in. She saw it go: a grey tongue of ash running off the cobbles under the cloth's edge and over the lip and down into the race, where the water was still moving, slow, back toward the beck; and it went into the water like flour into a bowl and was gone.

The rest lay under the canvas, on the stone, on the lip.

The last husk was alone.

It stood on the cobbles by itself, with nothing to keep time with, no pack and no wheel; and Kiva felt it through the canvas under her hand as she had felt the pack on the road go to a count of two. It was searching. It stamped, alone, and leaned, and turned on its three thin legs toward the mill house, where there was a light in the window and a child's face that had come back to it.

"*Mine,*" said Anwen.

She walked her wall. She walked it from the far end, round, in front of the husk, between it and the house, so that it walked into the silver and leaned there; and she kept walking, and it followed the wall round as a cow follows a fence, until its back was to the house and its face was to the race and the stopped black wheel.

"*Stopped,*" said Anwen.

"*Two,*" said Kiva. She could feel it now. It had found something. "It's on a two. It's walking on a two."

"*One,*" said Anwen. "*And. Two—*"

Jude cut it on the *and.* It fell where it stood, on the uphill side, away from the race, onto the cobbles of the yard, and lay there, and did not run, because the fall of the yard was toward the race and it had come down with its back to the house and its heap behind it, uphill; and it leaned a hair toward the wheel, and settled.

There was no third cloth.

Kiva got up off the hem of the second, where it hung over the race's lip, and went across the cobbles to the last heap, and knelt beside it, and put her right hand flat on the stone next to it, and had nothing to throw.

"*Finch!*"

Pru came down the bank at a run with Mrs. Pryce's grey double off her back, and threw it over the heap from three paces, flat, at the first cast.

"*Dark! One!*" said Pru. "I'm not lighting. I'm only saying it."

---

It took the best part of an hour to seal the three bags and get Jude's arm into the mill house kitchen, and in that hour Kiva found out what a moving pack had cost them.

The first bag was clean. Anwen and Jude hauled the corners, Jude with one hand, and Pru tied, three turns, the little finger across, and sealed at forty-seven.

The second, on the race's lip, was not. When they drew the corners in, the canvas that had hung over the water came up wet and grey and frozen stiff where the husk's breath had gone over it as it fell, and the neck would not come up straight. Pru tied it at sixty-three, with her teeth set, and said that she would have it written that it was the cloth and not her.

"It's the cloth," said Vane. He had come down into the yard at last with his scale. "That sheet's frosted to the weave. It'll not draw again." He held up the stiff grey corner of it to the light. "I'd not throw that on anything. I'd not lie on it."

It was Pru's sheet, the second that Kiva had carried down. The one with her own name on the pole.

She looked at it. She did not say anything for a moment, and then she said, quite steadily, "It's a sheet. It was always going to be a sheet."

The third bag was clean. Pru tied it on the grey double at fifty-one.

Vane combed them on the mill's doorstep with the comb from Pru's bag. He held it over each neck in turn, with his head on one side, and Kiva stood beside him with her hands in her coat and listened.

On each the third reed sang, clear and thin.

On each the seventh sang under it. It was very faint, a high thread of a note, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass in another room. But it sang.

"*Ward,*" said Vane. "*Full. Trace.*" He said it three times. "Clean, the three. They'll count. I'll weigh them at the hut."

Kiva had known that they would sing. She had known it since the field, since the moment she saw three husks in a yard keeping a wheel's time. She had known it at the hut, at the map, when Vane said *Garside's mill,* and she had known it on the tram in the first month, when Kellan said that it had all had the hair since the thaw. She did not mind it. It was what the pack was. They had come for the pack because it was going to hurt somebody, and they had killed it, all three, and there was a child at a mill house window with a woman's hand on her shoulder who was going to go to bed that night in a house that nothing was standing outside.

They had killed well. She knew it, and she let herself know it, standing on that doorstep with the frost on her boots. A pack that kept a wheel's time had been broken by stopping the wheel, and a count had gone from the captain to the Hold without a gap, and a wall had walked a thing round so that it fell uphill. Every one of those things had been a word on a sheet in a kitchen a fortnight ago, and none of them had been hers.

The miller came out of his door while Vane was writing. He was a big grey man in a leather apron with his sleeves rolled, and he looked at the stopped wheel and the hatch shut at the head of his race and the white sheet of water going over it back into the beck, and then he looked at Perrin.

"You shut my hatch," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"I've cloth in the stocks for a merchant at the Sag. I told the Line's lad that I'd not stop the wheel unless somebody paid for the cloth."

"I know you did," said Perrin. He had gone red; he did not stop. "They were keeping its time. We couldn't break them while it turned. I shut it. Under my name." He took the license out of his coat, folded, and held it out, as though the miller might want to read it. "That's my name. If you want to send to anybody about the cloth, you'll send to me. T7. Cade."

The miller looked at the paper and did not take it. He looked at the bag on his doorstep, and at Jude, sitting on his own kitchen bench through the open door with his white arm in Mrs. Garside's lap and a bowl of warm water, and at the child's face at the window.

"I'll open my hatch when you've gone," he said. "I'll not send to anybody." He went back in. At the door he stopped. "You might have knocked."

"I'd no time to knock."

"No," said the miller. "I dare say you hadn't."

---

They went up onto the moor at one o'clock, the five of them and Vane, with two sheets and a frosted one rolled on Pru's back because she would not leave it, and Jude with his left arm strapped across his chest by Mrs. Garside in a linen sling.

"I can cut with the right," he said. "It's the right I cut with."

"You'll cut if you're asked," said Perrin.

"I know I will. I'm telling you that I can."

The moor was not like the gill. Kiva had never been anywhere like it. Once they were up over the lip of the Edge, by the cleft behind the hut, it went away in front of them in every direction, brown and grey and flat and enormous, under a sky that was the same color all the way round, with no tree on it and no wall and no house. The wind came across it from the north without anything to stop it, and went through her coat as though she had not got one on. There were patches of old snow in the hollows. There were sheep, far off, that lifted their heads and watched them go by and put them down again.

There was nothing that kept time.

She tested it. She did it every half mile, without stopping, without saying so: a hand flat on a stone, or on the frozen peat at the side of the track, light, the two fingers on it and the three held off. Nothing. Nothing. A slow push once, which she thought was her own heart, because she had been climbing, and Pru, who had seen her do it, came up beside her and took her wrist without being asked and said *eighty-eight,* and it was.

Then nothing again.

The kiln was where Vane's finger had put it on the map. It was a low round tower of rough stone on the side of a slight rise, a little taller than a man, with a black arched mouth at its foot facing south, toward the Sag, and a heap of old white spoil beside it that the snow had got into. There was a spring below it, in a cut in the peat, with a stone trough.

The thing was sitting in the kiln's mouth.

Kiva saw it from three hundred paces, a grey shape in the black arch, low; and then from a hundred, and it was a husk, sitting down. She had never seen a husk sit. It had folded its three thin legs under it like a cow in a field, and its knot was low, nearly at the ground, and the grey column of it went up into the arch's dark and was lost there. There was no frost round it. That was the first thing that she noticed, and the thing that she remembered afterward: the peat in front of the kiln's mouth was not white. Its breath, if it had one, was not going anywhere.

It did not move as they came. It did not turn whatever it had to turn toward them.

They stopped at fifty paces. Nobody said anything. The wind went across the moor and moved the dead grass round the spoil heap, and that was the only sound.

"Rowan," said Perrin. He said it very quietly, as though the thing might hear.

Kiva went forward alone.

Nobody told her not to. She went to twenty paces, and knelt on the peat, and put her left hand flat on a stone that stuck up out of the ground there, grey and lichened, with the first two fingers on it and the other three held off, so light that she could hardly have said that she was touching it at all.

She waited.

*Nothing.*

She waited through a slow thirty. She did not count it; she had given that up. She knelt with two dead fingers on a stone on a moor and listened with them as she had listened on a rail and a flagstone and a surface plate and a wharf, and there was nothing. There was a stone. There was the cold, which her thumb told her about. There was not the least push, not now, not after a long nothing, not at all.

She held out her right wrist behind her without looking round, and somebody took it. It was Pru; she knew the fingers.

"Seventy," said Pru, after a moment. "Seventy-two. You're quite calm."

"Then it's not me," said Kiva. "And it's nothing. There's nothing here keeping time. Not the stone. Not that."

She had never in her life put her fingers on anything, since the autumn, and had them say nothing, and believed them.

She got up and went back to the others.

"It's quiet," she said.

They all looked at the kiln.

"Quiet," said Jude. He was standing a little apart, with his strapped arm across his chest and his good one hanging. "Then I'll ask it, since I said I would, and since it's the last time."

"Ask," said Perrin.

"It's sat there five days. It's not touched a sheep. It went up here away from every wheel and lamp and drain on this ground, and it's sat down." Jude did not take his eyes off the black arch. "If I cut it, and it's plain, it's for a hand. I'll be glad. If I cut it, and it isn't, it's for the book, a share of it, and it's sat here five days harming nothing, and I've killed it for a reading." He scratched his jaw with his good hand. "I'm not saying don't. I'm asking what it's for. I'd like to hear somebody say it before I go up there."

Nobody answered him for a moment. The wind went across the moor.

"It's on Line ground," said Vane. He had come up behind them, with his scale. He said it quite gently, for him, in his outdoor voice. "It came off a tear. It's sat five days. It'll sit till the thaw, and then it'll come down, since they always come down; and when it comes down it'll go to the Sag, where there's two hundred houses and a pump. I've seen it. I'd not say that it's harming nothing. I'd say it's waiting."

"You don't know that," said Jude.

"No. I've watched every thaw on this ground bring them down. I've never seen one go up." Vane looked at the kiln. "I'd take it."

"It's for a hand," said Kiva.

She heard herself say it, and heard how much she wanted it to be true, and said the rest because it was also true.

"If it's plain, it's for a hand. That's why we came up. And if it isn't, it's still a loose thing on Line ground over a valley with a mill in it, and Vane's right, and we'd take it anyway; we'd just not have walked two miles into a wind for it." She looked at Jude. "I can't tell you which before you cut. I've two fingers that say nothing's keeping time, and I've never had them say that before, and I don't know what it means. I'd like it to mean plain. I'd like it so much that I'm not to be trusted about it."

Jude looked at her for a long time.

"That's an answer," he said. "I'd want it said that it's an answer."

He went up to the kiln.

He went alone, slowly, with his head on one side, on his bad leg, with his strapped arm across his chest; and the others came after him to ten paces and stopped. Kiva went to her ground. She tested it, under her heel, by its corners: peat, frozen, solid, and under the peat, a long way down, rock. No room. It fell south, a little, toward the Sag. She planted, a pace south of the kiln's mouth and two to the side, and took the cloth off her shoulder.

Perrin did not count. There was nothing to count.

Jude stood in front of the kiln's black mouth, a pace from the grey thing sitting in it. It did not move. He looked down at it for a moment, and Kiva could not see his face.

Then he lit, and cut.

It came down out of his right shoulder, heavy and straight, into the low knot where the thing sat; and the thing did not stand, and did not lean, and did not close. It came apart. It went down onto the floor of the kiln's mouth in a heap, softly, like a sack let down by a careful man, and lay there.

It did not run. The kiln's floor was flat stone. It did not lean.

"*Dark! One!*"

Kiva's cloth went over it, flat, at the first cast. She knelt on the hem, at the kiln's mouth, and looked at the heap under the canvas.

It did not lean anywhere. It lay perfectly flat. It did not lean to the north, or the south, or to the Sag, or to anything; and she waited eleven, as a catcher waits when it is quiet, and it did not stir.

"*Corners,*" she said.

Anwen and Perrin hauled. The neck came up between them, straight. Pru knelt at it and tied, three turns, the little finger across, and sealed at fifty-two, and did not say anything at all.

Vane came forward with the comb.

He knelt on the peat at the kiln's mouth, beside the bag, and held the comb over its neck, with his head on one side, as he had held it on the mill's doorstep. The six of them stood round him in the wind on the top of the moor, and nobody breathed.

The third reed sang. It was clear and thin and very cold, and it went away across the moor in the wind.

Kiva waited for the seventh.

It sang.

It was faint. It was as faint as any that she had ever heard, a thread of a note so high and thin that she thought for a moment that she had made it up, out of wanting. But Vane's head did not move, and Pru's face did not move, and Jude had shut his eyes. It sang under the third, a long way down, like a wet finger round a glass in another house.

"*Ward,*" said Vane. "*Full.*" He held the comb a moment longer, as though it might change its mind. "*Trace.*"

He lowered it.

"Clean," he said. "It'll count. It's not plain."

Nobody said anything.

Kiva knelt on the peat at the kiln's mouth with her hand on a sealed bag that had come off a thing that had gone up instead of down, and sat, and harmed nothing, and kept no time that two fingers could find. It had sung the seventh reed exactly as everything else had sung it since the thaw, in every yard and wharf and road and Pit in the country.

She had not known how much she had wanted it until she had it in front of her, sealed, and it was not.

It was not that she was surprised. She had told Jude that she was not to be trusted, and she had meant it, and she had been right. It was that she had let herself want a thing on a moor, for an hour, with her whole body, for the first time since a hill in the autumn. The wanting had nowhere to go, and went down through her into the frozen peat like a still door with nothing under it.

"Then it's for the book," said Jude.

He said it quietly, to the bag. He had not opened his eyes.

"A share of it. Whatever it weighs." He opened them. "I'll write it. I'll write that it sat five days and went up instead of down and kept no time that Rowan could find, and sang. And I'll write that I asked what it was for before I cut it, and was told, and it was a true answer and it was the wrong one."

"It wasn't wrong," said Kiva. "It was the only one there was."

"I know. I'll write that too."

Vane had his slate out. He wrote on it, kneeling, in the wind.

"I'll say a thing," he said, without looking up, "since I said I'd say nothing on your floor, and your floor's done. That was a good hunt. I've walked a deal of fours over this ground under a company's officer, and I've never once walked one that went down a gill in the dark and broke a pack at a wheel by shutting a man's hatch, and walked two miles into a north wind after, and killed a thing in a kiln's mouth clean, and asked first what it was for." He finished writing. "It's not plain. Nothing is. I'd have told you so at the hut if you'd asked me. You'd not have believed me, and you'd have been right not to."

They weighed the bags at the hut that night, on Vane's scale, by the fire, with the wind going over the turf roof.

*The first: thirteen drams and eight-tenths, Grade First. The second, on the race's lip: twelve and six-tenths, Grade Second; some of it in the water, and the cloth frosted. The third: fourteen and seven-tenths, Grade First. The fourth, at the kiln: eighteen and one-tenth, Grade First.* Vane wrote each one on the Board's card as he read it off. *All four: ward, full; trace on the seventh. Clean. Fifty-nine drams and two-tenths.*

"Half to the city," he said. "Twenty-nine and six. An eighth to the casualty reserve: seven and four. An eighth to the Line, for its ground and its closing and its hand: seven and four. An eighth to the Board, on the card: seven and four." He looked up. "And an eighth to the four. Seven and four-tenths. To T7, under its captain."

Jude wrote it in the team book by the firelight, on his knee, with his good hand, and read it out so that everybody heard.

"*Thursday the 19th to Friday the 20th. Cowl Edge and the moor above it, on the Line's ground, under the Board's hunt card, our own entry, under Cade. The card, 0.40. A pack of three husks at Garside's mill, broken by shutting the mill's hatch; three bags. A husk alone, at the old kiln on the tops; one bag. Fifty-nine and two-tenths, clean, after the comb; shared half, and an eighth four times. To the book, 7.40. Pryce, a wall and a walk and a count, 0.31. Talley, five cuts, 0.18. Cade, a stance at the hatch and a stance on the way down, 0.12. Rowan, stances, off her own thimble. Finch, nothing lit; four cords tied, a sheet frosted. A cord frosted and thrown away, 0.06.*" He did it twice in the margin, by the fire. "*Held, eight and ninety-three. Sealed, eight-tenths.* And a sheet to buy."

"Eight and ninety-three," said Anwen.

"Up six and a third, on the hunt. Less a sheet." Jude shut the book. "I'd like it said that I've never been up on a hunt before. In the book, I mean. I'd like it said that it's a strange thing to be up and to feel worse."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Kiva sat on the plank bench with her back against the hut's black stone wall and her left hand flat on her knee, and she looked at the fourth bag, the kiln bag, standing on the hearth with the others, with Vane's tag on its neck. *Ward, full. Trace.*

There was eight-tenths of a dram in a tin under wax in the Low Lane's quench box that had come off a husk on the Sag in the fourth month, before any of this, and it had never had the hair. It was the last of it, as far as anybody knew, between Meridian and the sea. It was worth more than every bag on that hearth together, and it would not ease a hand that had been taken, only one that had been starved, and when it was gone there would be no more of it.

There had been a moor, and a thing that went up, and two fingers that for the first time in their lives had said *nothing* and been believed.

And it had sung.

"There's one place," said Vane.

He was at the table, writing up the card by the lamp. He did not look round.

"Between here and the coast. On the road. I'd not have said it if you'd brought plain off that moor; it'd have been none of my business. You didn't." He blotted the card. "There's a house above the ferry that's never stepped anything in its life. It's never had a lamp-cell in it, nor a pump, nor a clock. It's had plain ash in its cellar since before the patent. It's the only plain ash on the road south that I know of, and I've walked that road every year that I've carried a slate."

Kiva did not move.

"The Even Hand," she said.

"The Even Hand." Vane put down his pen. "Your aunt had a letter from them in the summer. I heard of it at the watch. They asked you to go as a guest, for a year, one door, every other shut. You said no. Your aunt left it on the latch."

"I know what she left it."

"Then you'll know that the latch is on your side of the door, not theirs." He turned round on the bench and looked at her, at last, across the hut, with the fire between them. "I'm not telling you to go. I'm the Line's hand; I've a scale. I'm telling you where the plain is, since you came two miles into a wind for some and went home with an eighth of a share. What you do about it's yours."

Kiva looked at the kiln bag on the hearth.

She thought about a year, one door, every other shut, and a house with no clock in it, and a sister of that Order who had sat beside her in a reading room in the summer and watched her lips move as she read a dead woman's fever, and had it written down.

She thought about a tin with eight-tenths in it, and Pike's grey bare hands on his knees, and the next person who would be hurt.

"It isn't for a year," she said. "If I go. I'd want that said before anybody writes to anybody."

"Then say it," said Vane, "to whoever you write to," and turned back to his card.
