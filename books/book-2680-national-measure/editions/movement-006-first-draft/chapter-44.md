# Chapter 44 — Kiva: Three in a Row

The four walls went forward on *one* and did not come off on the rest.

They went in under the grey staves at the foot, where the mud had slid away from them, low and flat, the way a spade goes in under a sod. Anwen's went in first, because she was nearest, and Edda's beside it, and Hesketh's, and Sedge's at the end, four silver edges sliding under the chime of a cask as wide as a cart is long, side by side, until there was a floor of ward under the whole west half of it.

And it came to them.

Kiva saw it come. The held water leaned at the line, four feet eleven inches of brown water standing in the air with nothing under it, and the ring leaned after its water. It came off its foot. It came off the mud where it had stood since the autumn the way a cask is tipped off a cart onto a sledge, except that it did not tip. It slid. It slid a pace west, upright, with its held water level, off the dark place in the mud and onto the four walls, and stood on them.

The weight went into four girls' arms.

Kiva was at Anwen's left, a pace off, with her Ember lit at her side. She saw it go into Anwen. She saw Anwen's shoulders go down an inch and her elbows lock and her face go white to the mouth, and she saw the same thing happen to Edda beside her, and to Hesketh, and to Sedge, one after another along the line as the weight found them, like a wave going along a row of posts. Nobody made a sound. The cold came off the staves onto Kiva's face. Frost began to form along the silver edges of the walls where the grey sat on them.

"*Two,*" said Dray, on the wall. "*Three. Four.*"

The ring stood on the ward line and its held water trembled, four times, leaning at the four of them.

"*Rest,*" said Dray.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

She said it as she had learned to say it on the Graving floor on Monday, from her stomach, long, a word that belonged to nobody's ear in particular. Four walls opened. They opened together, on the end of the word, into the air, as eighteen strangers had let go of a rope's end above an open hand. And for a quarter of a count the cask stood on nothing.

It hung a hand's breadth above the frosted mud with nothing under it at all, grey and staved, with its brown water standing in it, belonging to nobody. Kiva had seen a sleeve hang like that at Mallow Creek. It had been the length of a room. This was the height of a man and as wide as a cart, and full, and it hung there in the grey morning in front of twenty-four people on a wall and the whole of Saltmere behind them, and did not fall.

Then Jude and Gower had it.

They came in under the chime on either side, north and south, with their bars lit on their right fists and laid flat, and the foot of the cask came down onto the two bars and stayed. Kiva saw the weight go into them as it had gone into the wards. Gower's knees bent and straightened. Jude's face did not change at all. He had his left arm held away from his body as he had held it all week, and his right fist under the chime, and his eyes on the held water.

"*Carrying,*" said Jude.

"*One,*" said Dray.

They walked.

They walked west across the mud toward the first stake, slow, on Dray's count, a pace on each beat and a pace on the rest, with the cask riding between them on their two bars a hand above the mud. Kiva walked behind them with her Ember lit and low. She watched the held water. It leaned at Jude and Gower as it had leaned at the wards, four and four, and as long as it leaned at them, they were the thing it wanted, and it rode.

Behind her she heard the mud make a sound she had not heard it make before.

She did not turn round. It was not hers. She heard Yeo's whistle, two short notes, and Yeo's voice from the wall, flat and brisk: "*Wall. On my slate. Cut what comes.*" And she heard Ivey's crew go across the mud to the place where the cask had stood, fast, five of them and a second, the way a crew goes to a thing it has been waiting six months to close.

She heard, too, a voice she knew from the Lane, very clear, on the wall: "*Carrow. Edge. Declared. Single. Read me.*"

And Polglase's, from his desk under the travelling mast: "*Read.*"

At fifteen paces Jude said, low, "It's on my knuckles."

Kiva looked. The grey had begun to come round the bar on Jude's fist the way it had come round hers at Mallow, a thin cold wrap at the knuckles, folding to the nearest thing. Gower's was the same. They were carrying a thing that wanted to fold onto whoever held it, and they had held it fifteen paces, and it was beginning.

"Ten more," said Kiva. "Keep it."

"*Two,*" said Dray. "*Three. Four. Rest.*"

They kept it. At twenty paces the wrap was round Jude's knuckles to the first joint. At twenty-three it was round Gower's to the wrist, and Gower had begun to breathe through his teeth. At twenty-five the stake came up under the cask's west rim. Brask and Tolver stood on either side of it, square, with their hands open and their feet apart on the mud and their doors already open, waiting, the way still doors wait, like furniture somebody has carried in and set down.

"*First plank,*" said Kiva. "*Far edge.*"

"*Tolver has it,*" said Brask.

"*Rest,*" said Dray.

"*Nobody.*"

Jude and Gower opened their fists. The bars went out from under the chime and the grey wrap came off their knuckles with them, peeling away into the air. The cask hung over the stake on nothing for a quarter of a count, and then the two still doors were under it, coming up out of the mud the way a post goes into wet ground, slow and very sure. The cask came down onto them. It stood on the mud by the first stake as though it had grown there, upright and full, with its held water trembling four and four.

"*Planted,*" said Brask, quite politely.

"*Ten past,*" called Polglase from the wall.

Kiva let her breath out. She had not known she was holding it.

Jude was looking at his right hand. The skin across the knuckles was white where the wrap had been, and he turned it over and back and flexed the fingers.

"It's all right," he said. "It's cold. It's not taken." He looked at the cask standing by the stake, a pace from him, with its water over his head. "I'd want it said I was frightened."

"Everybody was," said Gower, who was a blade from Old Four and had never said three words to Jude in his life. "Come on. We've the sill to get to."

They went off across the mud toward the dock mouth, the two of them, on the north side of the route, wide of the catchers' plank, to wait for the third pass. Kiva watched them go for a moment. Then she turned round and looked back at the place where the cask had stood.

---

It was open.

Where the ring had come up out of the mud there was a seam, long and grey, as long as a cart, with its lips frosted white, lying in the mud ten paces from the outfalls and breathing. Kiva had seen tears on the Longshore and in the Pit and at Mallow Creek, and she had seen them close under a crew's hands. She had never seen one that had had a thing standing on it for six months. It breathed slowly, on no count she knew, and every breath sent up threads.

They came up out of the seam the way cress comes up on a wet cloth, except all at once, in the time it took to breathe: grey and thin and reaching. They reached for the nearest thing that kept time. Kiva saw the first of them bend toward the outfalls, a yard away, pouring into the hand of water on the count; and she saw Ivey's crew on their knees on the mud round the seam, five of them with their hands out flat and the white light coming up under their palms; and she saw the threads bend away from the outfalls and toward the crew, because the crew was nearer, and kept its own time.

"*Wall,*" said Yeo. "*Cut.*"

Carrow and Dray came down off the basin wall onto the mud.

They did not come down together. Carrow came first, light and fast, with her edge lit on her right fist, and stopped two paces from the seam on its north side. Dray came after her on her healed leg, steadily, and stopped beside her, a pace off, with her own edge lit and her watch still in her left hand. Kiva saw Carrow look at her.

"Your count," said Carrow. "I'll strike on it."

Dray did not answer. She looked at the threads coming up out of the seam, and at Carrow, and for one breath Kiva thought she would say no, because Dray did not strike on anybody's word but her own and never had.

"*One,*" said Dray. "*Two. Three. Four. Rest.*"

They struck on the rest. Both of them. Carrow's edge and Dray's went through the nearest threads together in one stroke, from two sides, and the threads went grey and fell back into the seam like cut hair. On the next rest they struck again. On the third they had found each other: Dray counting, Carrow striking on the count, Dray striking with her, two bars going through the grey in the same instant from two places a pace apart, so close together that from the first stake they looked like one bright line.

"*Polglase,*" said Dray, between strokes, not taking her eyes off the seam. "*What does it say.*"

Polglase, at his desk under the travelling mast, had his eyes on his pens.

"*One line,*" he called. "*Two people. Rowan's rule doesn't touch it: a stack's one person's. It's a lay. Between two.*" He wrote. "*It's not an incident under any rule I've got.*"

Dray struck again on the rest. Her face did not change.

"*That's fairer,*" she said.

Ivey's crew closed the tear at fourteen minutes past six. Kiva was not there to see it. She heard about it afterward, from Pru, who had seen it from the first stake: how Ivey had gone in on her knees to the seam's north lip with the white light under her hands, and her second, a girl of sixteen called Tamsin whom Ivey had taken on the Saturday after the Hunt, had knelt at Ivey's left hip with her own hands in her lap and her eyes on the far end of the seam. How the seam had reached for Ivey with its last threads, and the girl had said *far edge,* two words, once, and Ivey had turned her right shoulder and the threads had gone past it into Carrow's and Dray's line and been cut. How Ivey had shut it from the north end to the south as a woman runs a seam shut with her thumb, and it had lain still, and was mud.

"She'll keep her," said Pru. "Ivey. That girl. She said so on the mud. *I'll keep you.*" She went scarlet. "I'd have liked somebody to say that to me."

That was afterward.

---

At eleven minutes past six Kiva was at the catchers' plank by the first stake with her Ember lit, and the second pass was hers.

Pru had the far side of the cask, on her knees on the mud at its south-west quarter. Treen had the north-west. Marten and Yarrow had the two east quarters, with their backs to the outfalls. Four catchers' wards came up at their hips at the four quarters of the cask's foot, low and small and flat, silver, each the width of a doormat. They were the smallest wards on the basin. They would not have stopped a child. They were in the right places.

Behind them, the Pascoes stood with their hands open, unlit. They were both tall and fair, brother and sister, from Holloway, and they had not said ten words between them since Monday, and they were looking at the held water in the cask the way people look at the sea when they have only ever seen a river.

"*Catchers,*" said Kiva. "*Far edge.*"

"*Finch has it,*" said Pru.

"*One,*" said Dray, from the wall above the seam, without missing a stroke.

The four trays leaned on the count. The held water came round to them.

"*Rest.*"

"*Nobody.*"

Brask and Tolver shut. The cask came off their stillness and hung on nothing, and the four catchers' trays were under its foot at the four quarters before it had finished hanging. Kiva saw Pru's capped fingers whiten on the silver. She saw Treen's quick dark face set. They held it. Four small trays held a cask as tall as a man, full of water, at the four quarters of its foot, the way four people hold up the corners of a table.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva, on the next rest.

The trays opened. The cask hung. The Pascoes' bars came in under the chime on the north and south sides together, one bright and one brighter, and the cask came down onto them.

"*Carrying,*" said the elder Pascoe. His voice was higher than she had expected.

They walked.

Kiva walked behind them. Twenty-five paces of mud to the sill. Dray's voice came across the basin from the seam behind her, *one, two, three, four, rest,* and the held water leaned at the Pascoes, and the grey began to come round their knuckles at ten paces, as it had come round Jude's at fifteen. Ahead of them the sill of the Graving's mouth stood up out of the mud, a long step of grey granite with the gate's iron seating set into it, and on the granite stood Bannock and Wace with their doors open, waiting.

At twenty paces the mud changed.

Kiva felt it change under her own boots before she saw anything. It had been soft and even all the way from the stake, taking the boot to the ankle and letting it go. Here, in the last five paces before the sill, it was softer. It was where the hand of water that lay in the dock mouth at low tide drained out over the sill into the basin, and it had been draining there twice a day for three hundred years, and the mud under it was not mud but slurry.

The younger Pascoe's right boot went in to the knee.

She did not fall. She went down on that leg, a foot lower than her brother, all at once, and her bar went down with her fist, and the north side of the cask went down a foot with it.

The held water slopped.

It did not spill. It was held; it could not spill. But it rose up the south side of the cask as water rises up the side of a pail when the pail is tipped, a long brown lip of it, higher than the chime. And at the top it went white. It went over the far lip in a sheet, the side away from the Pascoes, toward the sill, a white sheet of frosting water coming off the chime like a wave coming off a wall, toward the two still doors waiting on the granite.

"*Far edge,*" said Pru.

Two words, once, from behind Kiva, high and sharp. Kiva did not know when Pru had come up behind her. She had come.

Wace stepped back. He was a catcher before he was a still door, and he had been taught that word since he was twelve, and he shut his door and stepped back off the granite onto the stone of the dock floor behind him without thinking, and the sheet went past the place where he had been standing.

Bannock did not step back.

He was a still door and nothing else. His door was open into the granite, and a still door open into stone is a post in the ground, and posts do not step. He stood there, enormous and calm, with his hands open at his sides and his door down through his boots into the sill, and the white sheet came over the chime and down onto his thighs and went down the front of him to his boots.

He made a sound. It was not loud. It was the sound a big man makes when he puts his hand on a stove he did not know was lit. And his door went out. Kiva felt it go, through the granite, the way she had felt Brask's go in: a post pulled out of wet ground. He sat down on the sill where he stood, with his legs white from the hip to the boot and frost going over them like salt, and did not get up.

The Pascoes were at the sill's foot with the cask on their bars, the younger one still down to the knee in slurry, and nothing to plant it on but Wace, alone, a pace back on the dock floor, opening his door again into the stone, alone.

One still door could not plant a cask that size. They had found that out on Monday with the tun. A plank was two.

"It's on my wrist," said the elder Pascoe, very quietly, as though he were telling her the time. "It's past my wrist."

Kiva looked at his fist. The grey had come round his bar and his knuckles and his hand to the wrist and was going up his forearm, cold and thin and folding, and it was doing the same on his sister's, and they had carried it twenty-five paces through mud and a slurry and a stumble, and they were done.

She did not decide it. She had decided it on Monday, at a trestle, in a room full of pumps. She had decided it in a ferry's fever and on a bench in a dock. She had decided it on Thursday morning, at a window, when she told her healer what the week's crossing was for.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

The Pascoes opened their fists. The cask hung in the air over the slurry at the sill's foot, on nothing, grey and staved and full, with the frost on its south side where the sheet had gone over, and it belonged to nobody.

She took it.

She put her right fist under the chime with the bar lit along it, at the north side, where the elder Pascoe's had been, and it came down onto her bar. It came down onto her the way a wall comes down onto a man who has put his shoulder under it to hold it up: all of it, at once, everything a Barrel weighs and everything the town's count weighs inside it, onto one girl's right fist. Her knees went. They went to the mud and she let them go, because she could not have stopped them, and she knelt in the slurry at the foot of the sill with the cask riding on her bar a foot above her head and the held water leaning at her, four and four, and the grey beginning at once to come round her knuckles.

It was heavier than anything she had ever carried in any door. It was heavier than she had known an Ember could hold. It was heavier than the sleeve at Mallow Creek by so much that she understood, kneeling there, that she had never really known what a Barrel was. She had read the word in the Board's books for a year. This was what it meant.

She could hold it for a count. Perhaps two.

"*Wace,*" she said.

"*Ready,*" said Wace, behind her on the stone, with his door open.

She shut her Ember.

There is a moment in a crossing when nothing is open. Kiva had been through it six times on sanctioned masts and once in the Pit with a field direction and three times with three doors, and she knew it as she knew the stair at home in the dark. It was a quarter of a count, perhaps a little more, with every door she had shut and the ash in her going nowhere, and in it she was nobody's either. She was not a catcher or an Ember or a still door. She was a girl kneeling in mud with her hands empty.

The cask hung over her on nothing.

Then her still door opened.

It opened down through her knees into the slurry and through the slurry into the granite at the sill's foot, and it was slow and heavy and very sure, as it always was, the way a post goes into wet ground. She felt it find the stone. She felt Wace's beside it, a pace off, already there. And the cask came down onto the two of them together, onto her stillness and his, and stood. It stood on the sill of the Graving's open mouth, upright and full, with the held water trembling in it four and four over her head, and did not fold, because there was nothing in a still door for it to fold onto. There was only ground.

"*Planted,*" said Wace.

Kiva knelt in the slurry with her still door open and her hands on her knees and the cask standing on her.

Ten paces inside the dock, at his desk under the lane mast, Noa looked at his pens.

"*Cliff,*" he said. His thin reader's voice carried up the dock and out of the mouth to the basin wall as clearly as Dray's. "*Ember shut. Gap. Still open. A crossing, single, declared at six, by the captain's voice.*" He wrote. "*Eleven minutes past six and forty seconds. Read.*" He wrote again, and looked at the tape a moment longer. "*The notch at five.*"

On the basin wall, under the travelling mast, Perrin had his hand pressed hard to his side. Beside him Orla sat on her crate with her stick across her knees, and she did not move at all. The Chair, further along the wall, had opened her ledger against her arm and was writing.

Kiva did not see any of them. She was looking at her own left hand, lying on her knee in the slurry.

It was warm. It had been warm for ten mornings, and it was warm now. Nothing had happened to it yet. Aske had said the seventh went past the elbow and sulked five nights, and that it would begin within the hour, at the knuckle, and come up. It had not begun. She had crossed, on a sanctioned mast, read by the office's reader, declared by her captain's voice, inside her certificate, on her own word, with her sponsor sitting on a crate on the wall thirty paces away. It was the first thing she had ever done that mattered that was entirely inside the lines somebody else had written for her. She found that she did not feel anything about it at all, except that the cask was very heavy and her knees were wet.

"Rowan," said a voice at her shoulder.

It was Aske. He had come along the sill from its north end, where Bannock had sat down, with his bag in his hand. He did not touch her. He looked at her left hand on her knee.

"Warm," said Kiva. "Still."

"It'll not be warm by seven." He looked along the sill at Bannock, sitting on the granite with his white legs out in front of him and Wace's coat already over them. "He's burned. Hip to boot. Not taken. He'll stand by Friday." He looked back at her. "You'll be holding that till the third pass catches it from you."

"Yes."

"Then I'll be here." He set his bag down on the granite beside her and stood over it, an old man in a black coat on the sill of a dock with a cask of held water standing on a girl's stillness beside him. "That's all. I'm here."

---

The first ward line came down the steps at the dock mouth's south side at twelve minutes past.

They had run the whole length of the basin wall from the outfalls, along the top, the four of them, while the second pass walked across the mud below them. Anwen came down the steps first with her cap gone and her hair coming down at one side. Edda came behind her, small and stern, and then Hesketh, and Sedge last, breathing hard. They went past the cask on the dock floor side, inside the mouth, and stood in their line on the granite on the far side of it from Kiva, between the cask and the dock. Four walls came up at their knees, low and silver.

Behind them, on the dock floor, Jude and Gower were waiting with their bars unlit and their knuckles white.

"*First line,*" said Kiva, from the slurry. "*Far edge.*"

"*Pryce has it,*" said Anwen.

"*One,*" said Dray, from a long way off, on the basin, by the seam.

The four walls leaned. The held water came round from Kiva to them across the cask's width, the way it had come round on Tuesday on the landing and an hour ago at the ring. Kiva felt it go. She had been the thing it wanted for a minute and a half, kneeling under it with her stillness in the granite, and she felt it stop wanting her and turn, and it was like having a hand taken off the back of her neck.

"*Rest,*" said Dray.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

She shut her still door. Wace shut his. The cask came up off the sill on nothing for a quarter of a count, and Anwen's line took it on four walls laid flat, at the foot, on the dock side, and held.

Kiva was dark.

She knelt in the slurry with every door shut and her hands empty and her knees soaked through, and she was nobody's. The cask was on Anwen's line, a pace off across the granite. In a moment it would be on Jude's bar and Gower's, and they would walk it in, forty-five paces up the dock floor, the long pass, the one Kiva had said on Monday was nearly too long, to Noa's chalk line, where Pettit and Lyle were waiting with their doors open to plant it.

"*Nobody,*" she said, on the next rest, from her knees.

Anwen's line opened. Jude and Gower took it.

"*Carrying,*" said Jude.

They turned and began to walk it in. Kiva watched them go up the dock floor between the walls of the Graving, two figures with a grey cask riding between them on two bright bars, with the tiers rising on either side full of silent people and the lamps along every tier burning white, and forty-five paces of wet stone ahead of them to the chalk.

At the chalk, forty-five paces off, Maud Lyle's voice came down the dock.

"*Kiva.*"

It was not loud. It was very clear, and very steady, the voice of a girl who had decided to say a thing and was saying it.

"*I can't,*" said Lyle. "*I'm saying it before.*"

Kiva looked up the dock.

Lyle was standing on the chalk beside Pettit, very straight, with her chin up and her hands at her sides. Her left foot had gone back. It had gone back a yard, not an inch, and the rest of her had not followed it, so that she stood on the chalk line with one foot behind her like a girl about to run and holding herself from running by her whole will. Her face was white as paper. Her still door was open; Kiva could see its weight in the way she stood. But it was going back with her foot, into the stone behind the chalk and not under it, and it would not stand where a cask could come down on it.

"I'd stand if I could," said Lyle, down the dock, quite steadily. "It went into me there. At the ankle. It's coming and I can't keep my foot." She did not look away. "I'm sorry. I'm saying it now so it's not after."

Pettit, beside her, had not moved. He was a still door, square and sleepy, and he stood on the chalk with his door open, alone.

One still door could not plant it. A plank was two.

Jude and Gower had stopped. They stood on the dock floor six paces in from the sill with the cask riding between them on their bars, and they had heard her, and they were looking back at Kiva over their shoulders.

"Keep walking," said Kiva. "Walk it in. Slow. As far as you can."

"It's on my knuckles already," said Jude.

"I know. Walk it."

They walked.

She knew the sum. Everybody on the dock floor knew it. Two Embers, with a count, could carry it fifty paces before it folded on them, maybe. It had been on their knuckles at fifteen on the first pass. It was on them now at six. They would get thirty paces up the dock, perhaps thirty-five, and then they would have to say *nobody* or have it fold on them, and there would be nobody to say it to but Pettit alone on the chalk ten paces further on, and one still door could not hold it, and it would come down on the dock floor between them, held water and all. Inside the dock. Inside the mouth. Where the Master was not yet standing, because the Master would not come down to the sill until the plant was called, and the plant could not be called, because there was no plank.

The cask would spill on a dock floor open to the basin, with the slack going, and the held water would run down the floor to the mouth and out into the basin, and the count would go back into the harbour's water, and the bank would not come out.

Kiva knelt on the sill with every door shut.

She had made one crossing. It was the week's. It was on her certificate, and on her healer's card, one in seven days, and she had made it eleven minutes ago, and it was spent.

She looked at Aske.

He was standing over his bag on the granite a pace from her, an old man in a black coat. He had heard Lyle. He was looking at Kiva's left hand, lying warm on her knee. He did not look at her face. He did not say anything for the space of one breath.

"Ten," said Kiva. "You said. After a door, ten counts dark before any other. Count me ten."

Aske looked at her then.

"*One,*" said Aske.

He said it as he said a pulse, against his watch, flat and exact. He did not raise his voice.

"*The hand goes.* Two. *Not up the arm. The hand. To the wrist.* Three. *For weeks. Three of them, if I'm guessing well, and I've guessed well at everything else about that arm.* Four. *Then it comes back as it was, the two fingers as they are, nothing more.* Five. *And I'll not sign one in seven after. It'll be one in fourteen, for the year at the least, and maybe after.* Six. *I don't know what else. Nobody does. It's never been done.* Seven. *That's twice I've said it, Monday and now. I'll not be said to have said it once.* Eight. *Say you heard.*"

"Heard," said Kiva.

"*Nine,*" said Aske.

She looked up at the basin wall.

Perrin was there, under the travelling mast, beside Polglase's desk, thirty paces off across the mud, with Jude's book under his arm and his hand pressed to his side. He had heard Lyle; the whole basin had heard Lyle. He was looking at Kiva. She could not see his face at that distance. She could see that he was not moving.

He was the brake. He had kept it for Seven since the autumn. He called *off* when the thing in front of them was going wrong, and everybody stopped, and nobody argued with him, because that was what the brake was for. He could call it now. He could call *off* from the wall, and she would have to stay dark on the sill with her hands empty, and the cask would come down on the dock floor when Jude and Gower could not hold it any longer, and the bank would not come out, and it would be his word that stopped it and not hers, and her hand would be warm in the morning.

She waited for him to call it.

"*Ten,*" said Aske.

"*Rowan,*" said Perrin.

His voice came across the basin and in at the mouth and up the dock floor, as it had come across the Graving on the Carry from the foot of a ladder, saying *set.* It was not loud. It did not need to be.

"*Ember, and a crossing to still. The second in the hour. Against her card, with the price said first. Declared by Cade, captain.*" He stopped. Then, because he was Perrin and could not help it: "*I'd like it known it's mine as well.*"

"*Read,*" said Noa, from the dock floor, writing.

Kiva lit her Ember.

It came up under her breastbone as it always came, glad, as though it had been waiting by the door, and it did not know anything about cards. The bar came down her right fist. She got up off her knees out of the slurry onto the granite, and past Aske, who did not move, and down off the sill onto the dock floor, and ran.

---

She ran forty paces up the dock floor of the Graving between the walls, with the tiers going up on either side of her full of people who made no sound at all, and the lamps burning white along every tier, and her own boots loud on the wet stone, and ahead of her two figures and a grey cask, going slower and slower.

Coombe had said that a caller who runs arrives at her mark with her pulse in her fingers. It did not matter now. There was no mark to test. The mark was tested; she had tested it at eight minutes past with her two fingers on the chalk, and said *good,* and it was still good. She needed only to get there.

She caught them at thirty-four paces.

Jude was walking with his teeth shut and his right fist under the chime, and the grey had gone round his hand to the wrist and up his forearm under the sleeve. Gower was the same on the other side, and walking with his eyes shut, which Kiva had never seen anybody do while carrying. The held water leaned at them, four and four. It wanted them. It was folding onto them as fast as it could and they were walking it in anyway, a pace on each beat and a pace on the rest, because Dray's voice was still coming up the dock faintly from the basin, and they were keeping it.

"*Jude,*" said Kiva.

He opened his eyes. He looked at her bar, lit, beside his.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva.

They opened their fists. She did not see them do it. She was already under the chime on the north side with her bar laid flat, and the cask came off their two bars and hung on nothing for the space of a quarter of a count, and came down onto hers.

It came down onto her as it had come down at the sill.

It did not put her on her knees this time. She had known it would come, and she had set her feet on the stone before it came, and she took it standing, with her right fist under the chime and her arm bent and her whole back taking the weight. She could not lift it higher. She could not have lifted it an inch. She could carry it, perhaps, for a count or two, the way you can carry a thing too heavy for you as far as the next table if the table is near enough.

The table was eleven paces off. Pettit stood on the chalk with his door open and his hands at his sides, alone, watching her come, and Lyle a yard behind him with her face white.

Kiva walked.

She walked eleven paces up the dock floor with a Barrel on her fist. She did not know afterward how. She walked on Dray's count, which she could still hear, faint, from the basin, one and two and three and four, and on the rest. The grey came round her knuckles at the second pace and round her hand at the fifth, cold, a thin wrap folding to the nearest thing, and the nearest thing was her. At the eighth pace it was at her wrist. At the tenth it was past it.

At the eleventh she was on the chalk, beside Pettit's boots.

"*Pettit,*" she said.

"*Ready,*" said Pettit.

She shut her Ember.

Nothing was open. The cask hung over the chalk line on nothing, and so did she, a girl standing on a wet floor with her right fist empty and the frost still on it, and in the quarter of a count between the one door and the other she felt her left hand go.

It did not go cold. It had gone cold six times, after six crossings, from the knuckle up, slowly, over hours, and she knew that cold as she knew the order of her doors. This was not that. It went the way a lamp goes when somebody turns the key the whole way down. It was there, at the end of her arm, warm, the hand she had held out to Aske at a quarter to five; and then the light went out of it. From the tips of the fingers to the wrist, all at once, it was gone. There was a weight at the end of her arm, a little heavier than a hand, and nothing in it.

Her still door opened.

It went down through her boots into the chalk and the stone of the Graving's floor under it, slow and heavy and sure. Pettit's was there already, a pace off. The cask came down onto the two of them and stood.

"*Planted,*" said Pettit.

He said it quite politely, as Brask had, as Trewin had at Mallow Creek. Then he looked at her.

"Rowan," he said. "Your hand."

"I know."

Ten paces off, under the lane mast, Noa had his eyes on his pens.

"*Cliff,*" he said. His voice had gone higher than she had ever heard it. "*Ember shut. Gap. Still open. A crossing, single. The second inside the hour, declared by the captain's voice, the price said first.*" He wrote. "*Fourteen minutes past six and fifty seconds. Read.*"

He did not say anything about the notch. Kiva did not ask.

---

On the outer quay, high above the dock mouth, the Master of the Order of the Even Hand came down the steps.

Kiva did not see her come down. She felt it. She was standing on the chalk with her still door open into the stone and a cask standing on it, forty-five paces from the sill, and her doors felt the Master come down the steps one at a time, the way a dog in a yard feels a stranger come in at the gate long before it lifts its head. When the Master reached the sill, Kiva felt her stop there, and stand, and turn to face the basin with her back to the dock.

Then the ward came up.

Kiva saw it, because everybody saw it. It came up across the whole mouth of the Graving from wall to wall, from the granite of the sill to a man's height and then higher, grey, not silver, the grey of a winter sea under cloud. It was not a wall as Anwen's was a wall. It did not stand in front of anything. It hung across the dock mouth the way a held breath hangs in a chest. Through it, faintly, Kiva could see the basin beyond the mouth, and the mud, and the seam that Ivey's crew had nearly shut, and the pump house at the head of it all with its six outfalls pouring into the hand of water. She could not hear the outfalls any more. She could not hear Dray.

On the basin wall, Yeo's whistle went, three short notes.

It was for Mara. Kiva knew it was for Mara. It said the Master's ward was up and the sink was shut and the bank was the Works'. She did not look round at it.

Her still door wanted to lie down.

She felt it begin the moment the ward came up. It was not like Thrale on the quay on Saturday, at forty paces, when her doors had lain down like dogs and she had let them. It was a slow heavy pull, down and away, the way a tide pulls at a post, and her still door was holding a Barrel. If it lay down, the cask would have nothing under it but Pettit, and one still door could not hold it, and it would come down on the dock floor, held water and all.

"It wants to lie down," said Pettit, very calmly, a pace off. "My door. Does yours?"

"Yes."

"Forty-five paces," said Pettit. "The sister paced forty."

"She paced it on the quay. In the town." Kiva did not take her eyes off the held water. "Here she's got a ward up and the whole harbour on the other side of it." She set her feet. "Hold it. It's only wanting."

"It's wanting a good deal," said Pettit, but he held it.

Rhea Sorn came up beside her on the chalk.

She came from behind, where she had been standing with Pike since six, and she stopped at Kiva's right shoulder with her slate on her belt and her edge unlit. She did not look at Kiva's left hand. She looked at the cask standing on Kiva's still door, a pace from her face, with the held water trembling in it, four and four, and the grey staves with the frost along them, and the chime.

"It's to be cut," said Rhea. "On my slate. Out of time. In the rest." She did not raise her voice. "I can't hear Dray through that ward. Nobody in here can." She took her slate off her belt. "You've your door in the stone under it. You'll feel its count. Give it to me."

Kiva had never read a count through her still door in her life. She had felt the town's count through bollards and gratings and bed rails, with her two fingers, which could not tell her what time. But on the Counting House's stair, and on the Long Quay yesterday, thirty feet from a man with his hand on a glass, her still door had wanted to beat. It had known a count then, well enough to want to march to it. She had spent all her strength holding it shut. She had never once thought to ask it what it knew.

She asked it now.

It was there. It came up out of the stone under the cask into her still door and up through her boots, slow and heavy, four and four, as the town's count had come up through the iron of the bedstead in the loft every night for three weeks. The cask's count. The bank's. The harbour's. It had always been there, in the stone, under every still door she had ever opened in Saltmere. She had never listened.

"*One,*" said Kiva. "*Two. Three. Four.*"

Rhea lifted her hand.

"*Jude. Gower,*" said Rhea, without looking round. "*Edges. North and south. On the word.*"

Kiva heard them light behind her.

"*One,*" said Kiva. "*Two. Three. Four.*" She felt the rest come up out of the stone. "*Now.*"

"*Cut,*" said Rhea.

Two bars went into the cask from two sides at once, in the rest, through the grey staves above the foot. Kiva felt them go in. She felt the cask's count stop in the stone under her still door the way a heart stops under a hand, between one beat and the next, not on a beat. The staves went grey. They went soft. They went grey all the way round, from the cuts outward, the way frost goes over a field, and slumped.

And the held water came over the chime.

It came over all of it at once, on every side, four feet eleven inches of held water with nothing holding it any more, coming down the outside of the slumping staves in a white sheet, frosting as it came, onto the dock floor. It came toward Kiva's knees.

"*Far edge,*" said Pike.

Two words, once, from behind her left shoulder, where nobody had been watching but him.

Kiva shut her still door and stepped back. She stepped back and to her left, the way she had stepped off a line at Mallow Creek on Pru's word, and the white sheet came down onto the chalk where her boots had been and went past her on the right, a hand from her leg, and on down the dock floor. Pettit did not step back. He was a still door, and posts do not step, and the sheet went over his boots to the ankle and frosted them like salt, and he looked down at them and said "*Oh,*" very mildly, and shut.

The cask fell in on itself. There was no sound. It went down into the white water spreading over the dock floor as a sandcastle goes down when the tide reaches it, and was grey, and then was nothing but a long low drift of grey under the moving water, and the water ran on down the dock floor's slope toward the mouth.

It ran forty-five paces down the Graving, white and then brown and then grey, spreading from wall to wall, carrying the drift with it. Kiva watched it go. She watched it reach the sill, where Aske stood over his bag and Anwen's line stood shut on the granite and Bannock sat with Wace's coat over his legs. She watched it reach the Master's ward.

It stopped.

It came up against the grey across the dock mouth and lay there, against it, the way water lies against a gate, and did not go through. It spread back from the ward across the floor of the dock, a shallow still pool from wall to wall, grey with ash, and was still. Nothing in it trembled. Nothing in it kept any time at all.

The Master stood on the sill with her back to the dock and her hands folded at her waist and the held water of the Barrel lying against her ward at her heels, and did not move.

"*Sixteen minutes past,*" said Noa, at his desk.

Kiva sat down on the dock floor.

She did not decide to. Her knees decided, as they had decided on a bench on Sunday. She sat on the wet stone a pace from the chalk with the grey water lying shallow round her, and Pike sat down beside her without being asked, with his half-closed hands on his knees.

She lifted her left arm with her right hand and laid it in her lap.

The hand lay there at the end of it. It was her hand. It had the two dead fingers that had been dead since the autumn, and the scar across the heel from a can's lip when she was eleven, and the ink on the side of the first finger from writing left-handed all week. She looked at it for a long time. It did not tell her anything. It did not say *something's pushing.* It did not say *keeps time,* or *nothing.* It did not say it was cold. It lay in her lap like a thing she had been asked to hold for somebody.

"Gone?" said Pike.

"Gone."

He did not say anything to that. He sat beside her on the wet floor of the Graving, an old catcher with his hands closed halfway, and looked down the dock to the mouth, where the Master stood with her back to them and her ward across the opening and the flood not yet come.

"You were my second all winter," said Pike after a while. "I've been yours for an hour." His long brown face did the thing it did, very nearly. "It's a hard job. I'd not known."

"Two words, once."

"Two words, once," said Pike. "That's the easy part."
