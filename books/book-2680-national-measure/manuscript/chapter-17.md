# Chapter 17 — Kiva: Thirty-Six Cells

Jude had been at the rail since Sunday morning, and on Monday afternoon he was still there.

The coast boat had come out of the river mouth at first light on Sunday, past a bar of yellow sand with the surf going white along it, and turned south. Since then there had been nothing on the left hand but cliffs, and nothing on the right hand but the sea. Jude had stood and looked at it through a whole day and a night and half of another day. He had eaten standing. He had slept, as far as Kiva could tell, sitting on a coil of hawser with his good arm along the rail and his chin on it.

"You'll wear it out," said Perrin.

"I'd want to see it do that."

The sea was grey-green near the boat and grey far off, and between the two there was a line where the light lay on it like a blade. It moved all the time. It did not move as a river moved, in one direction, going somewhere. It lifted and let itself down, everywhere at once, as though the whole of it were breathing in its sleep.

Kiva stood beside him. She had her left hand in her coat, against her ribs, where the disk was. She had been doing that since the barge, and she did not altogether know why. The disk was cold. It was always cold.

"It doesn't keep time," Jude said.

"What doesn't?"

"The sea. I've been counting the big ones. Seven, then four, then eleven, then nine." He did not turn his head. "I thought it would. I thought it would come in and go out like a clock. It doesn't. It's every count at once."

"There's a tide."

"The bosun says there's a tide. I've not seen it. I've seen this." He scratched his jaw with the hand that was out of the sling. The other arm lay across his chest, strapped, with Mrs. Garside's knot still in the linen at his shoulder. "I'd want it said that it's bigger than I thought. I knew it would be big. It's bigger than that."

Pru came along the deck with Aske behind her.

The healer had a way of arriving that Kiva had come to know on the barge. He did not say what he was there for. He stood, and waited, and presently she found that she had taken her left hand out of her coat and given it to him.

He held the wrist for a minute by his watch. The boat went up and came down. He did not seem to notice.

"Warm," he said. "As it was on Wednesday. Your fever's gone off clean, by the look of it." He let her have the hand back. "I'll not have a crossing the morning after a coast boat. I'll say that now, before anybody has it in his diary."

"Nobody's said a day."

"Your sponsor will. She's been on the transit since Friday with the officers, and she'll have had three days to think of one." He put the watch away. "I'll want two nights in a bed that doesn't move. Then I'll look at it, and then I'll say."

"Thursday," said Kiva.

He looked at her over his spectacles.

"Thursday at the soonest," he said. "That's what I'll write. You may tell her I said *soonest.* She'll know what it means; she's had a knee for twenty years."

Perrin had come round the deckhouse with the coil of tarred rope on his shoulder. He looked at the cliffs. He did not have a shed door, and he had not had one since the Sag, but he had found that a cliff would do.

"Fourteen," he said.

"It's the thirtieth," said Jude, without turning.

"I know it's the thirtieth. I'm saying what it comes to."

---

Saltmere came round the last headland at a little after four.

Kiva had not known what she expected. She had seen it on a map in the watch room at Ferren, as a name with a harbour drawn beside it like a bite out of a biscuit. She had heard it said all her life in the same voice as the National Measure, which was the voice for a place where things were decided. She had expected something tall.

It was low. That was the first thing.

The cliffs fell away on the left hand into a long ridge of grey roofs, climbing a hill. Below the ridge, between the hill and the sea, there was a wide flat country of slate roofs and chimneys, a whole town of them, lying lower than she would have believed a town could lie. And between that town and the sea there was a wall.

It was a great curved wall of grey stone, a mile of it, which came out from the foot of the hill and bent round in an arm, so that the sea broke white along its outside while the water within it lay flat and brown, crowded with masts. Where the arm came nearest to closing, there was a gap, and over the gap there was an arch of stone with something hanging in it, black, like a portcullis in a picture of a castle.

"The Mole," said the bosun, at her elbow, as he had been saying things all the way down the coast. "That's the sea gate. It comes down in a gale. It's not been down since the winter before last." He pointed with his chin at the low town. "That's under the sea, that is. Half of it. At high water on a spring you could stand on the Mole and look down the chimneys."

"And it doesn't flood?"

"The pumps," said the bosun. He said it as the shopkeepers in Coper Street said *the Works,* as a thing that was there the way the hill was there. "Up at the head of the basin. You'll hear them."

She could see the pump house. It stood where the brown water came to an end against the land, at the head of an inner basin: a long stone building with a row of tall chimneys along its roof, like the pipes of an organ. There was no smoke from the chimneys. There was a faint shiver in the air above them.

The boat came in under the sea gate. Kiva looked up as they passed beneath it. It was iron, black with tar and green at its lower edge, hanging on chains as thick as her waist. Its bottom edge was ten feet above the water. The sea went under it into the harbour as though there were nothing there at all.

Then they were inside. The water flattened. The wind dropped. And a sound that she had not been able to hear for the sea came up through the boat's planking, into her boots, and into her knees.

It was very low. It was not a noise so much as a pressure, coming and going. It came four times and stopped, and came four times and stopped.

*Four and four.*

She had heard it before, in a book. She had read it in a patent, on a reading-room table, through the eyes of a boy who had laughed aloud at how simple it was. She had never stood on it.

---

They came alongside the Long Quay at five, in the last of the light.

The quay was granite, wide enough for two carts, with iron bollards at every twenty paces and a row of warehouses behind. Their doors were painted green, which was the Saltmere Company's colour. The lamps along the quay were already lit. They were tall iron lamps, each on a fluted post, and each burned with a steady pale light that did not flicker at all.

There were bills pasted on the warehouse walls. There was one on every door, the same bill, in black and green.

*THE REGIMENT.*

*THIRTY-SIX CELLS IN STEP SINCE TWENTY PAST TWO ON THE 17TH OF THE NINTH MONTH.*

*NOT A STROKE DROPPED.*

*SALTMERE WELCOMES THE NATIONAL MEASURE.*

Somebody had drawn on one of them, in chalk, a row of thirty-six little soldiers in a line with their arms swinging. Somebody else had added a drum.

"They're proud of it," said Anwen.

She had come up from below with the team's box, and she stood on the quay with it at her feet and read the bill twice, as she read a card before she signed it.

"I'd be proud of it," said Perrin. "Six months and not a stroke. My dad's crane drops a stroke if you look at it."

"Your dad's crane isn't keeping a town out of the sea."

"No. It's keeping a load off my dad." Perrin shifted the rope on his shoulder. "I'm only saying I'd be proud."

Kiva had gone to the nearest bollard.

She did not decide to. She found that she was standing beside it with her left hand out of her coat, and she looked round the quay once. There were porters, and a cart, and a man selling something hot from a barrow, and nobody looking at a girl beside a bollard. She laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron, lightly, at the top, where it was worn smooth by ropes.

*Something's pushing.*

That was all they ever said at first. Then, because she kept them there and did not lean, they said the other thing.

*It keeps time.*

It came up through the iron from the granite, and through the granite from whatever lay under the quay, which was water and mud and the sea. It was not her own pulse. Her own pulse was quick from the stair and the gangway, and this was slow. It came, and came, and came, and came, and stopped; and came four times again.

She took her fingers off.

Pru was beside her. She had not been asked to come. She had Kiva's right wrist in her hand already, and her eyes on the nearest lamp, because there was no clock.

"Eighty," said Pru. "You walked up the gangway. That's all."

"Then it's not me."

"What isn't?"

"It's the bank." Kiva put the hand back in her coat. "It's in the water. It's in the quay. It's in this whole town." She heard her own voice, and made it level. "It's not mine, and it's not anybody's in particular. It's their count. They've a right to it."

Pru looked at her for a moment, as she looked at a cord before she tied it.

"Well, I'm not standing on it all night," she said, and went red, and went to help Anwen with the box.

---

The Meridian Collegiate Circuit had taken the sail loft over Quiller's chandlery, two doors along from the Company's office. It was one long room under the roof, with a window at each end and twenty iron beds in two rows. It smelled of tar and new rope and the paraffin in the stove. Halden License had come down by the station transit three days before and had the beds by the south window. Old Four had come by the road and the coast boat a day ahead of Seven and had the beds by the stove, and had been warm ever since, and said so.

Selka Dray was sitting on a bed by the north window with her left leg straight out in front of her.

She had one stick, not two. It lay on the blanket beside her. Kiva saw that, and saw that Dray saw her see it.

"Rowan," said Dray.

"Dray."

That was all. Dray's face did what it always did, which was nothing. She looked past Kiva at the four of them coming in with their sheets and poles, and at Aske, whom she knew, and at Pru, whom she knew less well. Then she looked back at the window, where the lamps of the Long Quay stood in a row along the dark water, burning without a flicker.

Kellan was at the south end, in the black and gold. He was sitting on a bed with a pair of Halden boots in his lap and a rag in his hand. He looked up when Seven came in. He did not get up.

"You came round the Sag," he said.

"We did."

"Four days. And a ferry. And a barge. And a boat." He put the boot down. "The transit was a day and a half. I'd like it on the record that I offered to pay half your fares from my own wage, and Cade said no in a letter of eleven lines."

"Twelve," said Perrin. "I signed it."

Kellan nearly laughed. It went across his face and did not stay.

"I heard about the fever," he said to Kiva.

"Who from?"

"Talley wrote it on the outside of a parcel to my mother. *Rowan had a fever on the ferry, broke Saturday, tell Renn.* My mother wired it on." He looked at her. "You look all right."

"I am all right."

He nodded, and went back to the boot, and did not ask her anything else. She was grateful for that. She had found, since the ferry, that she was grateful to anybody who did not ask her anything.

They had six beds at the north end, next to Dray's. Aske looked at them, and at the stove, which was at the other end of the room, and at the north window, which did not fit. He put his bag on the bed nearest the door, where he would have to be climbed over by anybody who went out in the night.

Kiva took the bed by the window.

She lay on it in the dark, after the lamps in the loft were out, with her coat over the blanket. The quay lamps lay along the water in a row. Under the floor, and under the chandlery, and under the quay, the town's count went on, four and four, so low that she could not tell whether she was hearing it or feeling it in the bed's iron frame.

She had kept her mouth shut on a ferry. She had kept it shut on a barge. She found that she was very tired of keeping it shut, and that she would go on doing it, and that both of those were true.

She went to sleep counting, and did not know whose count it was.

---

The Charter Board's gate was in the old timber pond, at the landward end of the Graving.

Kiva had not known what the Graving was until she stood beside it. It was a dry dock. It was a great stone trench cut into the shore, three hundred paces long and as deep as a church, with stone steps going down its sides in tiers like the seats of a theatre, so that a whole town could sit on its walls and look down at whatever was done on its floor. At its seaward end there was a gate, a floating thing of black timber and iron, holding the harbour out. At its landward end there was nothing but a stone lip and a drop. Somebody had roofed the tiers with iron and glass on iron pillars, so that it was half a dock and half a hall.

"That's where the boards are," said Anwen. "The Bench and the Lane. On the floor of it."

They stood at the lip and looked down. The floor of the Graving was eighty feet below them, flat stone, wet in places, with men chalking lines on it. It looked very small and very far away.

"I'd pay money to know how they get the litter up," said Perrin.

"They don't. You carry it out at the bottom." Jude was looking at the gate at the far end. "They shut that, and pump it dry, and it stays dry. The bosun said. The whole sea on one side of a door and you on the other."

The timber pond lay beside it, behind a brick wall: an acre of black water, roped off into squares, where the Company had once kept its baulks of timber floating. Half of it had been boarded over now. On the boards there were tables and a tent and a long queue of people in the colours of six circuits. And in the middle of the boards there stood a mast.

It was taller than the Circuit's travelling mast. It was taller than the masts on the Low Lane. It stood forty feet up, of black iron, with its rings and its drum at the top and a gallery round it halfway, and from the drum hung eight pens on eight wires. There was a stand at its foot, a reading desk on legs with a sloping top. A narrow young man in a grey coat was standing at it, threading tape.

"Noa," said Pru.

He looked round. He had his pencil behind his ear. He raised it, as a man raises a hat, and went back to his tape.

The queue moved slowly. At its head there was a long table with four clerks behind it in the Board's grey, and on the table there was a row of combs. Each four came to the table in turn, and put down its book, and put down every tin and thimble and jar it had brought, and stood back. A clerk opened every one. A clerk held a comb over every one. Another clerk wrote down what the comb said.

Anwen had the team's box. She had carried it from the loft without being asked, and she stood with it on her hip and watched the table.

"Every dram," she said.

"Every dram," said Jude. He had the book under his good arm. "It's the rule. It was the rule at the Regional, it's the rule here. There's nothing in our box that isn't in the book."

"I know there isn't."

"Then what's the matter?"

Anwen did not answer at once. She looked at the box on her hip, and Kiva knew what she was looking at, because she was looking at it as well. Under the thimbles and the book's tin, wrapped in a cloth, there was a smaller tin with two seals on its lid. One seal was Seven's. The other was grey wax, with a mark on it like two hands laid flat side by side.

"They'll comb it," said Anwen.

"They comb everything."

"I know. I want it said before they do." She looked at Kiva. "Whose word is it? Yours or the captain's?"

Kiva thought about it.

It was under Perrin's name. It had been signed under Perrin's name in a courtyard on a wet morning, and she had signed beneath him. But the clerk at that table did not know what was in the tin. He would open it and comb it, and the comb would say what it said, and the people in the queue behind them would hear. Then everybody would know what Seven had, and nobody would know why.

"Mine," she said. "If the captain will let me."

Perrin looked at her. He did not ask why. He said, "Go on, then."

They came to the table. Jude put down the book. Anwen put down the box, and opened it, and took out the thimbles one at a time, and the book's tin, and the stoppered jar of Kiva's own half dram. Last of all she took out the small tin and set it on the table by itself.

Kiva put her right hand flat beside it.

"This is sealed medical," she said. "Ward-trace. Two drams. It's plain."

The clerk looked up. He was an old man with a grey moustache, and he had been combing since seven, and his face said so.

"Plain," he said.

"It's written in our book, and in the book of the Order of the Even Hand. Eight-tenths was off a husk on the Sag in the fourth month of last year. A dram and two-tenths was given us by the Order on Friday the 27th, to make good a hand. It's bound." She kept her voice level, as she would have kept it on a hem. "It's for a hand. It's not for a floor, or a bank, or a crossing, or a sale. Please write that beside whatever the comb says."

The clerk looked at the tin, at the grey seal with its two flat hands. He looked at her.

"I'll write what the comb says," he said. "You may write what you like beside it."

He broke neither seal. He lifted the lid on its hinge, a crack, as far as the wax would let it go without parting, and held the comb over the gap.

The third reed sang. It sang full and clear, like a spoon on a glass.

The seventh did not sing at all.

The clerk held the comb there for a long time. He tilted his head, as a man does who is listening for a clock in another room. Then he lowered the comb, and shut the lid down on its wax, and wrote.

*T7. Sealed medical. W, full. I, silent.*

He looked up again.

"I've combed four hundred tins since seven o'clock," he said. "That's the first that's been silent on the seventh." He turned the ledger round. "Write your line."

Kiva wrote it with her right hand, under his. *For a hand. Bound in the Order's book and ours. Not for sale. K. Rowan, for T7.* Perrin took the pen from her and wrote *P. Cade, captain* under it without being asked.

When she turned round, the queue behind them had gone quiet. She saw faces in Saltmere green and in the brown and buff of a circuit that she did not know, and they were all looking at the small tin on the table. Nobody said anything. A girl at the back said something to the boy beside her, low, and the boy turned and said it to the next.

"Well," said Jude, quite quietly. "That's done."

"That's said," said Anwen. "I'd sooner it was said."

---

The four behind them wore brown and buff.

They were from the Tarn, Jude said, reading the badge on the nearest sleeve. It was a hill circuit, inland and north, a long way from anywhere. The four were big and slow-moving and very polite, and they had brought their box up the queue on a hand barrow. The girl who unpacked it was perhaps fifteen, a reserve, with her hair in two plaits. She laid out their thimbles and then, last, a stone jar with a cork in it. She looked very proud of it.

"Plain," she said to the clerk. "Ward. Half a dram. It was my grandfather's. He kept the chandlery at Tarnfoot for fifty years and he never sold a pinch of it."

The clerk held the comb over the jar.

The third reed sang thin. It sang as a spoon sings on a glass that has a crack in it.

The seventh was silent.

Kiva heard Pru draw in her breath beside her. She heard it because she had drawn her own. It was the same thin note that had gone along a counter in Meridian a week ago, in front of a woman in black with red hands.

The clerk tipped the jar toward the light. He held it out to the girl, so that she could look into it herself, and Kiva saw her look, and saw the black streaks in the grey, fine and dull.

"I don't—" said the girl.

"It's cut," said the clerk. He said it quite kindly. "It's old enough. The seventh's right. But it's been stretched to make the weight, with soot by the look of it, and there's half of it here, perhaps less." He wrote. "It's not your doing, miss. I'll write that."

"He'd not have," said the girl. Her chin had begun to shake. "He'd never."

"Very likely not. It may have been done before it came to him. It may have been done last week by somebody who wanted a shilling." The clerk shut the jar. "That's the third I've combed this morning. It's everywhere, this winter. Folk have a little plain, and it's worth ten times what it was, and they stretch it, or somebody does. It's not a crime that I know of. It's a pity."

The girl took the jar back. She held it in both hands. Her captain, who was a broad slow boy with a face like a gatepost, put his hand on her shoulder and left it there.

Pru had gone white instead of red. That was unusual in her. She stood looking at the stone jar with her mouth shut tight, and Kiva knew what she was seeing, because she was seeing it too: a counter, and a jar, and a woman saying *he was a good carter.*

"It's the same," Pru said, under her breath. "It's the same thing."

"It isn't anybody," said Kiva. "That's the trouble. It's just people."

"I'd sooner it were somebody," said Pru. "You could do something about somebody."

---

Orla Dane was sitting on a fish crate at the foot of the mast.

Kiva did not know where she had found a fish crate on a boarded-over timber pond. She supposed that Orla could find a crate anywhere, as some people could always find a chair in a full room. She was in her old coat with her stick across her knees, and she was watching the mast and not the queue. She did not look round when Seven came across the boards to her.

"You're late," she said.

"We came round the Sag."

"I know how you came. I've had a letter from your healer every day since the ferry, in a hand like a hen's foot." She looked at Kiva then, once, up and down, as she looked at a candidate who had come off a floor. "You had a fever."

"It broke on Saturday."

"So he says." The crooked face did not change. "He also says *Thursday at the soonest,* with a line under *soonest.* I've been on this crate for an hour deciding whether to argue with him, and I've decided I won't." She tapped the stick on the boards. "Thursday at seven. Here. On that. If he says yes in the morning."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"I didn't ask you." But something in Orla's shoulders came down an inch. "Sit, if you're stopping. They're reading the host's first four, and you'll want to see it."

At the mast, Noa had finished threading. He stood at the reading desk with his tape running and a pencil in his hand. Beside him stood an old man in Saltmere green, very upright, with white hair cut close to his skull and a pair of steel spectacles. He had a reading desk of his own, a yard from Noa's, with a tape of his own.

"Who's that?" said Perrin.

"Their reader," said Orla. "Polglase. He's read on that mast for forty years. They asked for him to stand beside ours, because it's our rule that's reading their people." She said it flatly. "I'd have asked the same."

Kiva knew about the rule. Everybody from Meridian knew about it. It had gone south in the Chair's own satchel, under seal, and the Board had broken the seal at its sitting and adopted it for the Measure: *any candidate with more than one door to be read from a sanctioned mast by a reader of the Meridian office.*

A clerk in grey stood up at the foot of the mast with a list.

"The Ropewalk School, Saltmere, first four," he said. "Carrow. Lyle. Under the rule."

Two girls came out of the crowd at the rail.

The first was tall and very straight, with black hair tied back hard and a face that had been in the wind all its life. She was perhaps eighteen. She wore Saltmere green with a rope worked on the sleeve in yellow thread, and she walked onto the boards under the mast as though she had been walking onto boards all her life and had never yet found one that she did not own. The second was shorter and broader, with fair hair cut to her ears and a square, careful face. She walked half a pace behind the first, and a little to one side, the way that Anwen walked behind a wall.

"Carrow," said Orla. "Juniper Carrow. Captain. The best blade on the coast, they tell me, and the papers tell me twice. The other's Lyle."

The rule had been written for Kiva. At the Regional she had been read on every floor from a sanctioned mast, as a condition on her certificate, because she had three doors and nobody else had. Now a girl with a rope on her sleeve had walked out under a mast of the same kind, in her own town.

"Which doors?" said Kiva.

"Watch."

The clerk read out the declarations. *Carrow: edge, and stance, laid. Lyle: ward, and stance, laid.* He read them in a perfectly ordinary voice, as he would have read *catcher* or *carry.*

Kiva felt Perrin go still beside her.

"Laid," he said. "That's—"

"That's what they call it," said Orla. "Hush."

After the Regional, Selka Dray had asked in pencil, from an infirmary bed with her leg in a board, that Kiva's condition stand for anybody with more than one door. She had said that it worked. She had said it so that nobody need ever again stand in a lane against somebody with more than one door and not know what was being done. It had not been written for anybody else. But it said *any candidate,* and the Board had read it as it was written.

Carrow went to the white cross on the boards under the mast. She did not do anything that Kiva could see for a moment. She stood with her weight even and her hands down. Then she opened.

Kiva had watched a great many people open a door. She had watched Kellan's edge come up on his fist, quick and hot, and Perrin's still door go into a floor like a post going into wet ground. She had felt her own doors open and shut, one at a time, with the gap between them like the cold drop between two stairs.

This was not like that. Carrow's edge came up on her right hand, a bar of heat the length of her forearm, bright as a poker. And at the same instant, not after and not before, her feet went into the boards. It was her still door. Kiva felt it go down through the planking into the black water of the pond beneath, as a still door goes into a floor. And the two of them did not fight. They did not lean on each other. They went round each other like two strands of a rope going round, and pulled tight, and held.

Carrow stood planted under the mast with a lit edge on her fist and her feet in the floor, and she was smiling a little. It was the easiest thing that Kiva had ever seen anybody do.

At the reading desk, the pen dropped.

It dropped once, at the instant she opened, and drew one line. It did not draw two lines, close together, as a plank would draw them. It did not draw a line and a gap and a line, as a crossing would. It drew one line, firm and single, as though there were one door open on the mast and not two.

Noa looked at it. Polglase looked at his own, which said the same thing.

"One line," said Noa, aloud, as he always did. "Two doors declared. One line on the tape."

"*Laid,*" said Polglase. He said it as a man says *amen.*

Carrow shut. Both doors went together, as they had come, and she stepped off the cross. Lyle went onto it. Her wall came up at her hip, low and silver, the width of a door. Her feet went into the boards under it in the same breath. The pen drew one line.

"One line," said Noa. "Two doors declared."

The clerk with the list came to Noa's desk. He held out his hand for the tape. Noa did not give it to him. He picked up his pencil and wrote in the ledger on the desk's ledge, in his narrow upright hand, and Kiva knew what he was writing before she could have read it. She knew it because she had read the rule herself, and the rule had not been written for anybody but her, and it said what it said.

*Carrow, J., Ropewalk. Two doors declared, edge and stance. One line, so read: a stack. Under the rule, an incident.*

*Lyle, M., Ropewalk. Two doors declared, ward and stance. One line, so read: a stack. Under the rule, an incident.*

*N. Bexley, reader to the Placement Chair's office, Meridian.*

He signed it. Then he turned the ledger round on the ledge so that Polglase could see it. The old man read it with his lips shut. He did not argue. He wrote in his own ledger, underneath his own reading, in a hand as upright as Noa's.

*Laid. As taught. The column is the Saltmere circuit's, and it is not an incident in this town. W. Polglase.*

Then the noise came.

---

It came from the rail first, from the green coats, as a long low sound like a wave going back off shingle. It went along the queue. It went up the boards to the tent where the Board's officers stood. A man in Saltmere green with gold on his collar walked out of the tent and across to the mast, quite fast, and began to speak to the clerk in a voice that Kiva could not hear but could see.

Carrow had come off the boards. She stood a little way off with her arms folded and Lyle beside her, and she was not smiling now. She was looking at Noa. Then, because Noa had his head down over his tape, she looked along the rail at the people who had come with him. Her eyes came to Seven, and to Orla on her crate, and to Kiva.

She came across.

"You're Rowan," she said.

"Yes."

"I've heard about you." Her voice was low and rather pleasant, with the coast in it, a long flat way of saying things. "You're the one with three. The one they wrote it for."

"It was written for me," said Kiva. "Yes."

"And now it's written for us." Carrow unfolded her arms. "I've been laying since I was eleven. My mother laid. Her mother laid, on this harbour, when the spawn came over that Mole in wagonloads. We've a column for it. It's the oldest column we've got." She looked at Kiva without any heat at all. "Your reader's just called it a stack. On our own mast. In front of our own town."

"He wrote what the tape said and what the rule says. He'd have written it if it were me."

"I don't doubt it. I've heard he's very honest." Carrow tilted her head. "Is it a stack?"

Kiva thought about it, because she had been asked.

It had looked like one, to her. Two unlike doors open at once and agreeing: that was what Meridian meant by a stack, and that was what she had been taught since she was thirteen to call a miss. In her own body it had been soft and cheap and open to anything near. In a pit in the second month she had watched a lodger lift its tip and turn toward two people's doors on one thing, agreeing.

But it had not looked like a miss. It had looked like the easiest thing in the world.

"I don't know," she said. "In Meridian it would be. I've only ever seen one done by accident, and it went wrong. I've never seen it done on purpose by somebody who knew how." She held Carrow's eyes. "It didn't look like an accident."

Carrow looked at her for a moment longer.

"Well," she said. "That's more than your reader said." She turned to go, and turned back. "We've drawn you. Tomorrow at nine, in the Ropewalk. The first seeding lane. I thought I'd say so, because you're polite."

"I know," said Perrin. He had the draw in his pocket; it had gone up on the tent at noon. "We'll be there."

"I know you will," said Carrow. "I've heard you're all very honest."

She went back across the boards to Lyle. The man in green with gold on his collar was still talking to the clerk. Other people in green had come over to listen.

---

"It's not an incident," said Selka Dray.

She had come up behind them on her stick. Kiva had not heard her. The stick made no sound on the boards; Dray had wrapped its ferrule in leather. She stood with her weight on her right leg and the left one very slightly bent, and she looked at the mast and not at any of them.

"It is on the tape," said Anwen.

"It's on our tape, under our rule, in our column. In their column it's a lay, and the Board will say so by Tuesday." Dray's voice was perfectly flat. "I wrote that rule to read every door in a lane. I didn't write it to stop anybody. If their doors look like a stack on a tape, that's their tape. They can read it to anybody they like."

"They'll hate us for it," said Perrin.

"They do already. They'll hate us for a fortnight." Dray turned her head. Her sunk grey eyes came round to Kiva and stopped there. "They'd hate us more if it were only you. It was only you. Now it's everybody. That's fairer."

Kiva found that she agreed.

She had not expected to. She had stood under that rule since the summer, the only person in Meridian who had to be read, and she had hated it, and she had kept it. She had kept it because it was fair, and because it was the price of being let stand at all. And now it read everybody who opened two doors. Carrow had stood under the same mast and been read and called the same thing.

It was fairer. It was also going to make a whole town dislike everybody from Meridian for a fortnight, and that was hers too. It had been written for her.

"Yes," she said. "It is."

Dray looked at her. Her face did nothing. But she nodded, once, very slightly, as a referee nods at a call that was right.

"I'm walking," she said. "Without this, by the Lane." She lifted the stick an inch. "I'll be in a lane with you. On a declared count. With a needle on me." She paused. "I've not changed my mind about you."

"I didn't think you had."

"Good," said Dray. "I'd not want it thought."

She went away across the boards toward the tent, slowly, on her stick, and the people in green made room for her without knowing who she was.

---

The draw was pinned to the tent's front pole by evening, under a lamp, with the Board's seal on it. Seven's three seeding lanes ran down it in a column: *Wednesday the 2nd, nine o'clock, the Ropewalk: the Ropewalk School, first four. Saturday the 5th, two o'clock, the Ropewalk: the Ropewalk School, second four. Saturday the 12th, nine o'clock, the Graving: the Tarn circuit, first four.*

Under it there was a second sheet, newer, in a clerk's hand.

*The entries of the reader to the Meridian Placement Chair's office, made under the rule this afternoon, stand on the tape. Whether they stand as incidents for candidates of the host circuit is before the Clerk of the Measure, who will rule when every candidate under the rule has been read on the same mast in the presence of both readers. Seeding lanes go ahead in the meantime under protest. By order.*

"*Under protest,*" said Jude. "Whose?"

"Everybody's, by the look of it," said Anwen.

They walked back along the Long Quay in the dark. The lamps burned on their fluted posts without a flicker, and the bills were pale on the green doors. *NOT A STROKE DROPPED.* Somebody had chalked under one of them, since the morning, in a large round hand: *GAPPERS GO HOME.*

"What's a gapper?" said Perrin.

"Us," said Pru. "It's us. It's what we do between doors." She went scarlet. "A girl at the queue said it. I asked her what it meant and she told me, and then she said she was sorry, and then she said it again."

Perrin looked at the chalk for some time.

"I've been called worse," he said. "I've been called a Stonehand to my face by a boy from the Hill. That was worse. This is only true."

At Quiller's door, Kiva stopped.

The chandlery was shut, with its shutters up. The loft's window was lit above it, and she could hear Old Four by the stove arguing about something. Out on the dark water of the harbour, a boat was coming in under the sea gate with a lantern on its bow. And under her boots, through the granite, through the mud and the water under the granite, the town's count went on. It was steady and slow and very patient, and it had not dropped a stroke since the ninth month.

She had come two hundred miles. She had come to stand in lanes, and to be read on a mast, and to make two crossings on a sponsor's line. She had not come to tell anybody that their machine was dangerous. That was other people's work, and she was glad of it.

But she was the reason that a rule had read a girl with a rope on her sleeve and called her a stack. Tomorrow she would stand in a lane against that girl, in that girl's own school, in front of that girl's own town. Whatever happened, she would not be able to say that it had nothing to do with her.

"Rowan," said Perrin, from the stair. "Thirteen."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it." He held the door. "Come in. Talley's reading us the book, and he says it's short."
