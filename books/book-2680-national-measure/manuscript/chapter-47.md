# Chapter 47 — Kiva: Its Own Count

Aske wrote the fourteen at seven o'clock on Thursday morning, at the north window, with the hand lying in his own like a thing he had been lent.

He had pricked it again first, the thumb and the heel and the place on the inside of the wrist, and watched her face each time. He had pricked an inch above the wrist and she had said *there,* and he had nodded. Then he had laid the hand back on her knee and taken out the card and his pen, and written, standing, against the window frame, slowly, so that she could read every word over his arm.

*Thursday the 24th of the third month, 2681. The left hand, as Wednesday: gone to the wrist, not cold. Feeling begins an inch above. The arm aches to the shoulder; less than last night. Three weeks, I think, from the 23rd. Then as before.*

*From this day: one crossing in fourteen days, at the most. For the year, at the least. The hand seen the next morning.*

*The three doors are not on this card. They were never on it.*

*I said the price on Monday and on Wednesday, before. I said it right.*

He capped his pen and turned the card round, though she had read it.

"That's the fourteen," he said. "It's written. It'll not come off in a hurry." He put the card in his coat. "I'd like you to know that I'd have written it after a second crossing whatever the second was for. A bank or a lane or a stray dog in a drain. It's not a punishment. It's what the arm told me on Wednesday, and I've written what it told me."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it because some fool at a sitting will say the girl was made to pay for saving a harbour, and I'd like there to be a card that says otherwise." He picked up his bag. "Eat. And use the right hand for everything, and don't try to make the left one do anything by looking at it. It'll come back when it comes back. Looking makes it slower. I've no evidence for that whatever, but I've believed it for thirty years."

Hart was on the landing at nine, kneeling at the grating with his bad hand on the bars, though there was no slack at nine and no ring and nothing in the basin but the flood going out under the sea gate.

Kiva went down the ladder. She did it one-handed, with her right hand on the rungs and her left arm held against her coat, and he heard her coming and did not look round.

"It doesn't ache," he said, when she was on the landing. "That's what I'm doing. Six pumps on six counts, ten feet from my hand, and it doesn't ache. It ached near the bank for twenty years and near the Regiment for six months, every hour but the slack. It aches near a drum and a stepped yard and a man in a chair with his hand on a glass. And it doesn't ache near this." He looked at it on the bars, the fingers half curled. "I've been here since six trying to make it tell me why."

"Has it?"

"No." He took it off the grating and sat back on his heels. "It never tells me why. It tells me *ache* and *quiet.* I had to find out what they meant by sitting here twice a day for six months and writing them down next to the sea gate's table." He looked at her left hand, hanging at the end of her arm against her coat. "Aske's card says three weeks."

"Three weeks."

"Lay it on," said Hart. "Next to mine. I'd like to see what it does."

She knelt. She picked up her left hand by the wrist with her right and laid it on the grating's wet bars, palm down, with the two dead fingers flat, beside his. Then she took her right hand away and left it there.

Nothing. The iron was under it, she supposed. She could see it was. She could not feel it at all.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing," said Hart. He looked at the two hands on the bars side by side, his ruined one and her gone one, for a long time. "When you came, you asked me what your two fingers could tell you. I said that was the wrong question. Ask them the same question at the same hour every day, yes or no, and write it down, and let the book do the telling."

"I've done it every day since the 13th."

"I know. You wrote *yes* every day. Then yesterday you wrote *no answer.*" He did not look at her. "Your aunt told me. She tells me what's in your book, sometimes, when she's tired, and then she's angry with herself for it." He was quiet. "You'll be wanting to stop. For three weeks. Because there's no answer, and it seems foolish to lay a dead hand on a rail at a quarter to seven and write down that it's dead."

Kiva did not say anything.

"Don't stop," said Hart. "That's all I came down here at six to work out, and I've worked it out." He looked at his own hand on the iron. "I told you I asked mine the wrong question for ten years. I didn't tell you what it said, the first of those years, when I'd only just come to this coast and didn't know what to ask at all. It said nothing. Not ache, not quiet. I'd lay it on the grating every morning because the grating was here, and it was a lump of meat on the end of my arm, and I'd write *nothing* in a book, and I never knew why I bothered."

He turned the hand over. "Then one morning it ached, at the pumps, and nowhere else. And I'd a year of *nothing* written down at the same hour to set it against. That's how I knew it was new. That's how I knew it was the pumps and not the weather."

He put the hand back in his jacket.

"That's what a ruined hand can be asked," he said. "The same thing every day. Whether it answers or not. So that when it does answer, you've something to set it against." He got up off his heels with his good hand on the rail. "Yours'll answer in three weeks, if Aske's right, and he's right about that arm more than anybody I've known about anything. Write *no answer* for three weeks. Then you'll know it's come back by the morning it says *yes.* Not by looking at it."

Kiva picked up her left hand off the grating with her right and put it back against her coat.

"You'd not have told me that a fortnight ago," she said.

"I'd not have known to. I've only known it since this morning. Since it didn't ache." Hart looked at the six outfalls pouring into the basin, every one on its own count. "I've sat here twenty years with a hand that aches near a count. There's no count here now. Not one. Six, and none of them the town's. I don't know what my hand's for any more." He was quiet a moment. "So I'll ask it. Tomorrow at six. And the morning after. And I'll write down what it says."

Orla found her on the timber pond boards at a quarter past ten, sitting on the end of a crate by Noa's empty desk with her book on her knee, writing right-handed.

"Crest's struck my line," said Orla.

She said it standing, with her stick, the way she said everything, roughly and briefly. She did not sit down.

Kiva looked up.

"Yesterday at four," said Orla. "On the first crossing. *A crossing in the field, inside the certificate, on the candidate's own word.* You can read it at her lodging if you want, she'll show you. The certificate's in your name. Your conditions stand." She looked at the book on Kiva's knee. "You'll still write your book to somebody weekly. It'll be to the Chair now, not me. You'll still declare at every board. All of it stands. Only my name's not under it."

Kiva did not say anything for a moment.

"You asked her," she said. "On Sunday. I didn't know."

"You weren't meant to know. It was mine to ask." Orla looked at the mast standing over the empty desk, black against a white sky. "I asked it on Sunday so it'd be asked before. I'd not have it said that I let go of you after you'd done something, as if I were taking my hand off a thing that had been proved. I let go of you before. It was proved after." She put both hands on her stick. "That's all. I'm not your sponsor. I'm a woman with a bad knee on a crate. You'll see me at home, I expect, in the shop, being rude to your aunt."

"I expect so."

"Good." Orla turned to go, and stopped, and did not turn back. "You waited for the price. Both times. On the sill, with Aske counting you ten." Her voice did not change. "Nobody taught you that on the Line." She went off along the boards toward the gate with her stick, and did not look round.

The Company's yard was behind the Long Quay, a cobbled square with green vans along one side and sheds along the other, and at two on Thursday afternoon Yeo had her closing crews in it in a half circle on the cobbles, eighteen of them in green, and a sack of wet sand on a board in the middle.

"You said yes on Thursday," said Yeo to Kiva. "A week ago. To come to the yard and show my crews your columns, not a Lane night. It's not a Lane night." She looked at Kiva's left hand. "If you'd rather not, with that, I'll understand it."

"I'd rather," said Kiva.

Jude was there. He had come at one to sign his card, and had signed it on the tailboard of a van, and was standing at the back of the half circle in his Meridian coat with the card in his pocket and his knuckles still white across the backs. Ivey was at the front, broad and square, with her second beside her: Tamsin, sixteen, with a long plait and a face that went pink when anybody looked at it.

Kiva did not show them the columns. Ivey had copied the columns twice and read them to the crew a week ago. She showed them the word.

She had them stand in threes round the sack: a ward, an Ember, and a still door, three people from three different vans who had never worked together. She had them light, single, declared to Yeo, one door each. She had the ward lift the sack on its board an inch off the cobbles. Then she said *nobody,* from her stomach, long, the way she had said it on the Graving floor, and the ward let go into the air, and the Ember's bar came in under the board, late, and the sack tipped and slopped wet sand over somebody's boots.

"Again," said Kiva.

They did it again. They did it for an hour. She had them let go of a rope's end into the air above an open hand, as Coombe had, and they laughed at it and then stopped laughing. She stood in the middle of the yard with her right hand in her coat and her left hanging. She watched three Company hands who had never stood together pass a sack of wet sand from a ward to an Ember to a still door on her word, and drop it, and pass it again, and not drop it.

At three she stopped calling.

"Ivey," she said. "You call it."

Ivey looked at her.

"I can't," said Ivey. "I've not been inside both gaps. I've one door. I've never crossed in my life."

"I know. I've been thinking about that since Monday." Kiva looked at the three round the sack, waiting. "I thought the caller had to have been inside both gaps, to feel when the load's nobody's. That's what's on my sheet. It's how I started." She was quiet a moment. "On Wednesday Dray called the count from the wall. She's one door, and she's never crossed, and she called every pass, and every plank on the mud kept it. I only said the word. She gave it its time." She looked at Ivey. "You've watched me say it for an hour. Watch their hands, not the sack. When the ward's let go, it's nobody's. Say it before that, on the rest, and they'll let go on the word."

Ivey looked at the three round the sack for a long time.

"Tamsin," she said. "Count me."

Tamsin went pink to the hair. "*One,*" she said. "*Two. Three. Four. Rest.*"

"*Nobody,*" said Ivey.

She said it too short, the first time, a catcher's two words, and the ward let go half a count late and the sack slopped. The second time she said it longer. The third time she said it from her stomach, as Kiva had, and the ward opened on the end of it and the sack hung over the cobbles on nothing for a quarter of a count, and the Ember had it, and it did not slop at all.

The crew did not cheer. They were the Company's, and the Company does not cheer in a yard. But Kiva saw three of them look at each other.

"Again," said Ivey, and called it again, to a different three.

Kiva stood in the yard and watched her for a while. Then she took her book out and wrote, right-handed, standing.

*Thursday the 24th. The Company yard. Ivey called the pass, three strangers, a sack. Tamsin counted.* She looked at it. Then under it: *My sheet's wrong on the first line of the limits. Write it again.*

Jude had come round the half circle and was reading over her shoulder, as he always did, without asking.

"You've been wrong on a sheet," he said.

"Since the second month."

"Good," said Jude. "I'm glad." He scratched his jaw with his white-knuckled right hand. "It's the first thing you've written that anybody in this yard could have written instead."

The Charter Board sat in the Salt Hall at two on Friday afternoon, and every seat was taken, and the gallery was full to the rail.

Kiva sat on the third bench with Seven. Perrin at the end, Anwen, Jude, Pru, herself, with her left hand in her lap under her right. Behind them, the Ropewalk. Behind them, Twenty-Eight, with Bannock sitting very carefully on the end of the bench with his legs out straight in front of him in bandages, because Aske had said Friday and it was Friday. At the front, at the long table under the windows, the Clerk sat with the two Board members, and the Chair at the end with her ledger, and the Harbour Committee's chairman on his own bench with his gold watch in his hand.

Halden's counsel sat at the end of the first bench with a folder. The Proprietor was not there. Kellan sat behind counsel in his black and gold with a sheet of figures on his knee, and Adlam beside him.

The Clerk read the order. She read it without a paper, in her even voice, and it took eleven minutes, and the Salt Hall did not make a sound for any of them.

She read that the Charter Board had had the evidence of the Measure, the sittings of the 18th and the 20th, and the record of the 23rd at the Saltmere harbour pump house. It ordered that every municipal bank in the country, of whatever kind, be put on its own count: pump house, lamp office, drum, tenement box and tram shed. Each cell was to keep its own, no two alike in any house, each pump on its own cells, and each to be kept there. No municipal bank was to be stepped from this day, nor joined by water or by iron to any bank that was. Any that had fallen into step was to be taken out of it by the Board's own officers as plain ash could be found for it.

Every jar and tin of plain ash in the country, in any hand, was to be declared to the Board within the year. Not taken; declared, and counted, so that the Board would know what there was. What there was would be allotted to banks first and hands with them, and no bank before a hand.

She read that the Board would raise a travelling commission to do it, under its Hunt Marshal, Dinah Coombe, with readers from the Works of every circuit that would lend them and officers from every company that would, to go to every municipal bank on the Board's roll within two years and read it, and put it on its own count, and write down in seven columns what was got wrong first.

Then she stopped, and looked at the second bench, where Kellan was sitting.

"The Board will hear the price," she said. "It asked Mr. Renn for it on Wednesday night, because he's done every other sum in this hall, and because the Board would like a sum it can't be accused of having done for itself. Mr. Renn."

Kellan stood up.

He read it from his sheet, standing, in his thin careful voice, without any expression at all, exactly as he had read the comb's figures a week ago.

"A bank on its own count loses light," he said. "A stepped bank of thirty-six cells drives one load on one beat. Thirty-six cells on thirty-six counts drive it unevenly, and some of every minute they're working against each other. The Ferren bank's lost a ninth of its light since the autumn, by the Works' figures. The Saltmere bank's lost the same since Wednesday, by the keeper's. Call it a tenth across the country, at the least. A tenth of the light in every municipal lamp from here to the hills."

He turned the sheet.

"It costs money. Every bank on its own count wants a reader a quarter, to see it's still on its own, and fitters who can keep thirty-six counts and not one. The keeper here says her fitters will curse it every winter for the rest of their lives, and she's right; it's a third again in wages, by her book, and every pump house on the roll will find the same. That's the towns' to pay. The Board's order says so."

He turned it again.

"And it costs the Halden foundation its municipal licences." He did not look at counsel's back. "The foundation's stepped a hundred and twelve municipal works under licence since 2391. Tram sheds and lamp offices, mostly, and a few drums. Every one of them comes out of step under this order, and every licence fee with it. I've the foundation's own index for the figure. It's the foundation's oldest income." He was quiet a moment. "That's not the foundation's oldest property. Its oldest property was a cell under a glass on the Long Quay, and it went to its own count on Wednesday at twenty-two minutes past six, and I was there. That's not in the Board's order. It's not anybody's order. It's only so."

He sat down.

Halden's counsel stood up at the end of the first bench.

"The foundation will not contest the order," he said. His voice was neat and dry, and it was, Kiva thought, very tired. "I'm instructed to say so. I'm instructed to say that the foundation's yards, refineries and lift yards will remain stepped, as they are the foundation's own and not the country's, and that the Board's order does not reach them, and that the foundation does not ask it to."

He looked at the Clerk. "And I'm instructed by the Proprietor to say one thing in his own words, which I've written down so that I'll not improve it." He read from a slip. "*I am not certain I was wrong. I believed for seventy-six years that the lamps that keep time together are the ones that go white instead of out. On Wednesday they did neither. I do not know what they did. Nor does the Board. I would ask the Board not to say in its order that I was wrong, because it doesn't know that either.*" He folded the slip. "That's all, ma'am."

The Clerk looked at him.

"The Board doesn't say he was wrong," she said. "The Board says it doesn't know, and that a country that doesn't know shouldn't be one engine while it finds out. That's the Works' reading. It's the only one in this hall that isn't a reading of what's coming. It's a reading of what to do in the meantime." She folded her hands. "Write that the Board heard Mr. Halden, and that it doesn't say he was wrong, and that it doesn't say he was right, and that it orders the banks on their own counts anyway, because a fitter's rule is all it's got."

The heavy Board member pushed his spectacles up onto his forehead.

"It's a slower country," he said, to nobody in particular, quite mildly. "And a darker one, by a tenth. And dearer."

"Yes," said the Clerk. "That's the price. It was said first. The Board rises."

On the Harbour Committee's bench the chairman had opened his gold watch and was looking at it. Then he shut it and put it in his pocket, and Kiva saw that he had not looked at the time at all. He had only wanted to hold something.

On Saturday morning at ten, Kiva went along the Long Quay to the Counting House, because the Proprietor had asked her to, by a card in his own hand that came to the loft on Friday night. *If you'd care to. Not for anything. A. H.*

Every window was lit, as it always was. The lamps on the brass brackets in the hall were lit, white, and the lamps in the glass globes on the newel posts, and the great iron ring slung from the stairwell's ceiling. Kiva went up the stair with her left hand in her coat and her right on nothing, and on the fourth step she waited for her heart to slow, and it did not.

It went on as it always went. Her Ember lay quiet under her breastbone. Her still door did not want to march. She went up the stair to the long room at the top, and the lamps did not flicker, and nothing in the house kept a count that her doors could feel at all.

He was at the head of the long table, in his chair, with a rug over his knees.

The glass case stood where it had always stood, at his left hand, on the polished wood, with its brass frame. His left hand was not on it. It lay on the table beside the case, palm down, on the wood. On the glass of the case, in a faint fog that the clerks had not cleaned off and he had told them not to, there was the print of a hand, five fingers and a palm. Under the print, inside the case, an old small cell turned its light, yellow, slow, three and a pause and two, uneven, like nothing she had ever seen in a Halden house.

"Miss Rowan," said the Proprietor. "Sit where you like."

She did not go to the far chair. She stopped halfway down the table, with the window over the harbour behind her and the white cloth and the two candlesticks still between them, and sat. Her heart did not change.

"That's nearer," said the Proprietor.

"There's nothing to sit far from."

"No." He looked down at his left hand on the table. "No, there isn't. I find people in the house keep coming nearer, and don't know why, and stand about at my elbow with papers looking uncomfortable." He moved the hand an inch on the wood. "It closes now. Halfway. Aske came on Thursday and said it would close the rest of the way in a month, and that the heart was a perfectly ordinary heart for a man of fifty-five and he'd not speak for a man of a hundred and three." Something moved across his lean face. "I liked him."

"Everybody likes him, after."

"Yes. I'd imagine that's the case." He looked at the glass case beside him, at the print on it and the slow uneven light under it. "It's its own now. The first. I put my hand back on it on Wednesday night, when they'd carried me home, to see. It didn't come back to me. It went on doing that." He watched the light go round, three and a pause and two. "I've decided to leave it on the table where I can see it. The foundation would like me to put it in the vault. I've said no."

Kiva waited.

"I asked you here for nothing," said the Proprietor, "and I'll keep to that. I'll not ask you anything. I'd only like to say one thing to you, because you're the only person who's sat at this table who saw what I saw from that window, and you'll understand it, and nobody else will." He looked at her. "On Wednesday at twenty-two minutes past six, every lamp I could see from the outfall walk went to a point. Not white. Not out. A point." He was quiet. "I've waited seventy-seven years to see the lamps do it again. White or out. I've arranged my whole house and half the country so that when they did, they'd go white. And they did neither. They did a thing I hadn't got a word for."

He looked at her for a long moment, steadily, down the table.

She knew what he was not asking. She had heard him read Annick in a Board's evidence; she had seen his eyes come round to her at this table on Sunday when she said there was a thing she had no word for. He had read that line in 2610. He was not asking.

"I don't know what they did either," said Kiva.

"No." The Proprietor smiled then. It was not his short real laugh. It was smaller, and it stayed. "I've believed one thing for seventy-six years, Miss Rowan. I believe it still. Some go white and some go out, and I'd still sooner they went white. I'm not certain I was wrong. I'm only certain I let go." He looked at his hand on the wood. "That's a different thing to be certain of. I'd not have thought, at a hundred and three, that I'd find a new thing to be certain of."

They sat for a while. The fire ticked. The cell in the glass case went round, three and a pause and two.

"I'll not keep you," said the Proprietor at last. "You've a healer who'll want to know where you've been, and friends with cards on a board, and Rhea Sorn has a night on her van on Monday, I'm told, and a card that ends on Wednesday." He did not move his hand. "Go out the way you came. Touch the banister if you like. It'll not say anything to you now."

She touched the banister going down. It did not say anything. It was a banister.

Kellan had the second white card in his hand on the Mole on Saturday afternoon, sitting on the warm stone with his back against the wall and his boots out toward the sea.

He did not wait for her to ask. He held it out to her across the stone as he had held out the first, without comment.

*Mr. Renn. The foundation no longer claims the Step. It will want a man to count what it has left, and what it does with it, and to say so under his own name. To me, and to nobody between. Not counsel's. Mine. From the autumn, at the Meridian yard, at a Fire's wage. You may say no. You've said it to this house twice, and it was right both times. A. Halden.*

Kiva gave it back.

"You said no twice," she said.

"I said no at his table, and I said no to counsel, on a card with a line on the back that said *given through the foundation's counsel, as the foundation's.*" Kellan turned the card over. There was nothing on the back. "This one hasn't got a back."

"And you'll say yes."

"I've done the sum." He put the card in his shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket over it. "The foundation's got a hundred and twelve works to give up, and its yards to keep, and a claim it's let go of. And a ledger that's been kept for two hundred and ninety years by men who wrote down what the house wanted to hear. Somebody's going to count it now. Either it's somebody who's never stood in that house, and doesn't know where the figures hide, or it's somebody who has."

He looked at the sea. "My mother's a grader. She says the worst thing you can do to a number is let it mean what somebody needs it to. I've been doing figures against that house since the autumn from the outside. I'd like to try doing them from the inside, where it's harder, for a man who's asked for it."

"He'll not be there forever."

"No. He's a hundred and three, and his heart's an ordinary heart for a man of fifty-five, and Aske won't speak for it." Kellan was quiet. "That's in the sum too. If he goes, I'll have a year of figures under my own name that the next one can't unwrite. That's worth a year." He looked at her sideways, with his narrow careful face. "Halden License doesn't come into it. I've told Pettit. I'll cut for them on the roll till the autumn, if they're called, and then I'm the house's clerk and not its blade."

"And Seven."

"Seven's going four ways," said Kellan. "I was only ever on its book as a blade who's told where he's wanted. I was told where I was wanted, and went. Every time."

Perrin was on the end of the Mole on Sunday morning with his hand pressed to his side and a letter in his other hand, looking at the sea gate.

"I've written to Rusk," he said, when she sat down beside him. "Yes. The trunk crew at the Brickfields, from the autumn, when my card's up." He did not give her the letter. "*Cade, P., carry.* Not *the winner of the Carry.* I asked him to leave that off. He wrote back from the Works' lodging an hour later on the same sheet: *I've never heard of the Carry.* He's a liar. He was on the tiers." Perrin looked at the letter. "It's the nicest thing anybody's said to me since the ladder."

"Why the trunk crew?"

He was quiet for a while.

"Gower," he said. "Old Four's blade. He envied me. All week. Since the Carry. He said so, in the loft, to my face, the way I said it to yours in the autumn about the three doors. *I'd give my right arm for a board with only my name on it.*" Perrin looked at the sea gate. "And I found out what it's like. It's worse. It's much worse being envied than envying. When I envied you, I'd something to want. When Gower envied me, I'd only a thing he wanted, and I'd won it on one Tuesday on a ladder, and he'd every right to think it was luck." He folded the letter. "I'd rather be the new man on the trunk crew who lifts things than the boy who won the Carry for the rest of my life. I'd rather have to show them."

"You'll show them."

"I know. That's the good part." He looked at her left hand, lying in her lap. "You were declared on Wednesday by my voice. Twice. The second one against your card." He did not look away. "I've thought about it every hour since. I was the brake. I could have called *off.* Everybody on the wall was waiting for me to."

"I was waiting for you to."

"I know you were. I could see you from the wall, kneeling on that sill with Aske counting." Perrin's face went the colour it went when he was thinking. "I didn't call it because it was yours. Not mine. It's the captain's to declare, and it's the captain's to stop, and I've stopped Seven a dozen times since the autumn when it was going wrong. It wasn't going wrong. It was going the way you'd said on Monday it might, with the price said first, and you'd heard it." He breathed carefully. "If I'd called *off,* your hand would be warm, and the bank would be in, and it'd have been my word. I'd have kept it for you. I'd have been very proud of keeping it for you."

"And you'd have been wrong."

"And I'd have been wrong." He put the letter in his coat. "I'd like it known that I was nearly wrong, and wasn't." He looked at the sea.

The Company's runner came to Quiller's door at a quarter past eleven on Monday night with the call for Rhea Sorn's last night on the van, and Kiva went with dark hands, because that was all she had and it was all a second ever had.

It was a cellar in the Tanyard, below Ropery Row, where the water had stood to the shin on Wednesday morning for seven minutes and gone out again. Something had come up in it on Monday evening, the runner said. A householder had gone down for coal at ten and seen a grey thing standing in the corner where the water had lain longest. He had come up again very fast and shut the trap and sent for the Company. The Company had sent for Graywater, because Graywater's van was in Saltmere on the Board's warrant and the Company's crews were all on the Longshore at the night low.

Graywater's van stood at the head of the Tanyard with its lamps lit. Rhea was at its tailboard with her slate. Doran Mace was there with the ward line, and Nairn, and Ruddock and Tibb. And Pike sat on the tailboard with his hands on his knees, half closed, the way they had been since the dressings came off.

"A string," said Rhea, when Kiva came up with Pru beside her. "Three husks on a cord. Handful at most. In a cellar ten feet by twelve, with a coal heap in one corner and a copper in another, and one trap to get in by." She looked at Kiva's left hand. "Pike's catcher tonight. His hands close halfway and he can carry a voice and a corner. You're his second, as the card says. Dark hands." She did not look away. "That's what you've got. It's the last night on the card. I'll not ask you for more than's on it."

"It's enough."

"I know it is," said Rhea. "That's why I wrote it that way in the autumn." She took her slate off her belt. "Pike."

Pike got down off the tailboard.

They went down the trap into the cellar one at a time by the ladder, the ward line first, then Nairn with his blade, then Pike, then Kiva, one-handed, with Pru below her on the ladder's foot with a hand ready that she did not need. The cellar was low and black and smelled of wet coal and the tannery. Mace's lamp, set on the copper, showed the floor: stone, still dark with Wednesday's water at the low end, and in the corner where it had lain longest, three grey shapes standing on a thread of grey, shoulder to shoulder, very still, like three men waiting for a door to open.

"They're not keeping a count," said Pike, very low, looking at them.

Kiva looked. They were not. A pack kept a count among itself; Coombe had taught them that in the second month, and they had met one at a mill in the hills keeping a wheel's time, and every pack on the coast since had moved on the town's count, four and four. These were standing still. Nothing about them moved on any beat at all.

"There's no count left in this cellar to keep," said Kiva. "Since Wednesday. There's nothing under the floor."

"Then they'll not move on one." Pike looked at them a while longer. "Good. That's one thing less."

They did it as Pike had done a hundred strings in twenty-six years, on wharves and in drains and in a coal yard below the Hythe in the first month, where his hands had been taken. Mace's line put a wall along the cord's near side. Nairn cut the cord at the middle on Rhea's word, from the ladder's foot, where she stood with her slate. The three husks went soft and grey on the stone. And Pike went to the far end of the string on his knees, to the corner, and laid his half-closed hands flat on the cellar floor beside the last husk, and began to call the hem in, low, in the old words, as Kiva had heard him call it on a night in the first month before a fold took both his wrists.

Kiva knelt at his other side, at the near end, with her right hand flat on the cold stone and her left in her lap, and her mouth shut. A second sets nothing. She calls nothing. She is not to open her mouth until the bag is sealed.

She watched his hands. She watched them on the stone, closed halfway, the right thumb slow as it had been since the autumn before last, in his own writing. She watched the hem come in under them, grey, a thin sheet of the dead string's skin folding in from the near end toward him, the way a second watches a master fold a cloth.

And she watched the far end. Pike could not; his face was turned to his hands. Nobody else in the cellar was watching it; Mace's line was watching the near side and Nairn was watching the cut and Rhea was watching Pike. Kiva watched the far end of the hem, by the coal heap, where it lay against the wall.

It lifted. A hand's breadth, no more, the way a page lifts in a draught. It lifted toward the nearest thing, and the nearest thing was Pike's left wrist, a foot from the coal heap, where his hand lay on the stone.

"*Far edge,*" said Kiva.

Two words, once.

Pike took his left hand off the stone and laid it down again a foot nearer his knee, without looking, as a man moves his hand off a table when somebody says *hot.* The far end of the hem came down on the stone where his wrist had been, and lay there, and was nothing but grey skin on a cellar floor.

He did not look at her. He went on calling the hem in, low, in the old words, and the hem came in to his hands, and he folded it once from the far edge and once again, and it was a bag.

"*Sealed,*" said Pike.

Then he looked at her.

"You'd your mouth shut the whole time," he said. "Till the far end. Then two words." His square grey face did the thing it did. It went the whole way, this time. Kiva had never seen it go the whole way. It was a smile. "That's a second. I'd not had a better."

"You were mine on Wednesday."

"I was. It's harder from the other side." He picked up the bag with his half-closed hands, carefully, against his chest. "I watched yours. You watched mine." He held the bag out to Rhea at the ladder's foot. "Twenty-six years, I've been doing it, with nobody at my far edge but the corner-hand."

Rhea took the bag. She looked at it, and at Pike, and at Kiva kneeling on the cellar floor with her right hand on the stone and her left in her lap. Then she wrote on her slate, standing at the ladder's foot by the light of Mace's lamp on the copper, slowly, every column.

Kiva read it upside down, as she had read it every night on the van since the first month.

*Before: a string of three in a cellar in the Tanyard, Saltmere, after Wednesday's water. Not keeping any count. Closed: at a quarter to one, on the cut and the fold. Lives: none. Works: the near side walled, the cord cut at the middle, the hem called in by the catcher. The far edge called once by the second, before the bag was sealed. Tinned: one bag, Handful, to be combed. Remarks: none.*

Under it, she wrote one more line, below the columns, where the slate had no column. Kiva could not read it from where she knelt. Rhea did not show it to her. She hung the slate on her belt.

"That's the card done," said Rhea. "Six nights. The first month to the third, two a month, dark hands, Finch with her for the cord, Seven first when Seven was called, and once I went short for it on a coast night and it cost me half a rung." She looked down at Kiva. "Wednesday's the 30th. The card says we look again, and either of us may say no." She turned to the ladder. "I'll be at the van at noon. Come and look."

They went up the ladder into the Tanyard. At the gate, two days later, the bag combed red, like everything else that had been killed that year; but Kiva did not know that yet, and at a quarter to one on Tuesday morning in a lane below Ropery Row, with the van's lamps lit and Pike sitting on the tailboard holding his half-closed hands out to the warmth of the lamp, she did not care.
