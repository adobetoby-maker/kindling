# Chapter 31 — Kiva: Nobody

Sixty paces along the east bank of Mallow Creek is a long way in mud.

Kiva ran it. She ran it with Pru beside her and the mud taking her boots to the ankle at every stride, and the whole time she was running she was looking at the grey fold lying on the west mud across the channel, flat, still, with nothing under it that moved. It lay there as a sheet lies over furniture in a shut house. At one edge of it, the edge nearest the thread of water, a man's boot stuck out. It was a thigh boot, black, with mud on it to the knee.

She stopped on the east bank opposite it, at the channel's edge, with the thread of slack water between her and the fold. She stopped because the rule said stop.

*A person down on the mud is the officer's crew's. The fours stand off.*

The officer's reserve was under the fold.

"*Ivey,*" said Yeo, on the spur.

Her voice did not rise. It went up the creek, flat and brisk, to the place sixty paces upstream where Ivey and the closing crew were kneeling in the mud round Seven's tear with their hands out and the white light under them. Kiva saw Ivey's head come up. She saw her look down the creek, and see, and get up off her knees.

She would be a minute. A minute through mud, and then she would be at the fold, and she would do what the book said. She would go to the near edge. She would put her ward under it and lift.

The fold's far end lay on top of the near edge, where it had come down across Penhale's back.

"Ma'am," said Kiva.

Yeo did not look at her. Yeo was looking at the far post of the lower stow, standing whole in the channel's bend, with the red-haired boy of the Tarn standing beside it in the punt and the boatman holding the punt against it with his pole. The boy's edge was unlit. He was looking up at the spur with his face like paper.

"*Cut it,*" said Yeo. "*The far post. Now.*"

"*No,*" said Pru.

She said it before Kiva could. She said it loud, from the bank, scarlet to the hair, to an officer of the Saltmere Company who had not asked her anything.

Yeo turned her head.

"If you cut the post, the skin folds," said Pru. Her voice shook and kept going. "From its far edge. Toward the nearest line. We saw it at Gannet Creek, a cut post's skin, it folds onto whatever's nearest. Ma'am. They're the nearest. They're under it."

Yeo looked at her for one breath.

"*Hold,*" she said to the punt. "*Don't cut.*" The red-haired boy took his fist away from the post as if it were hot.

Then there was nothing anybody could do. Kiva stood on the bank and knew it. The book said lift, and lifting brought the far end over the lifter. The post could not be cut, because the skin would fold tighter. Nobody could go onto the mud but the officer's crew, and the officer's crew was Ivey, sixty paces off in the mud, coming, and Ivey would lift.

"Ma'am."

It was Perrin.

He had come along the spur from Seven's half. He had not run; he could not run. He had walked as fast as a man walks with a cracked rib, with his hand pressed to his side and his face grey, and he stood at Yeo's shoulder now on the stone.

"There's a way," he said. "It's three people. Two of them are on that bank."

Yeo looked at him.

"Finch catches the fold on her ward," said Perrin. "Rowan carries it, with her Ember. It's declared; I declared it at eight. The third plants it. On this bank, away from them. Nobody holds it long enough for it to fold on them." He breathed in, as far as he could. "It's been done on a yard. Twice before Coombe. Once on Sunday with him." He pointed. "He's the third."

Trewin was on the east bank below the spur, with the punt's line in his two hands. He had not let go of it. He was looking at the fold across the channel with his gatepost face gone quite white, at the place where a plait of brown hair had come loose from under the grey and lay on the mud.

"I'll plant," said Trewin. He did not look away from the plait. "Ma'am. If you say."

Yeo looked at the fold. She looked at Ivey, forty paces off now, coming down the west mud as fast as anybody can come through mud. She looked at Kiva and Pru on the bank. It was three seconds, perhaps. Kiva watched her face through all three of them, and saw an officer of thirty years on the coast decide a thing that was not in any book she had ever read.

"It's mine," said Yeo. "On my word. Do it."

Kiva went down off the bank into the channel. Pru came after her.

The thread of slack water was a yard wide and a hand deep. They went across it in one stride and up onto the west mud, and Kiva went to the fold. Not to the near edge. To the far end, the closed end of the sleeve, where it lay heaped over the near edge and over whatever was under the near edge, grey and frosting, with the cold coming off it in a breath that she could feel on her face.

She knelt beside it. Pru knelt beside her.

"It's ten paces long," said Pru. Her voice was very small. "I can't fold ten paces. My ward's the width of a doormat. I fold a rung. I fold a cord."

"Don't fold it," said Kiva.

Pru looked at her.

"Don't lift it," said Kiva. She heard her own voice and made it level. "It folds to the nearest thing. That's all it ever does. On a wharf it folded to a catcher. At Gannet Creek it folded to a man on a line. Up the creek just now it folded to my line and I came off it. It wants to fold. Let it come to you." She pointed at the mud beside the fold's heaped end, a pace off. "Put your ward there. Low. At your hip, the way you catch at a can. Don't put it under anything. Be the nearest thing. And when it comes over onto your tray, finish it. It's already folding. You're only finishing the fold."

Pru looked at the place on the mud. She looked at her own two hands.

"And then nobody," she said.

"And then nobody. I'll say it. You let go on the word."

Pru's ward came up at her hip.

It came up low and small and flat, silver, a tray the width of a doormat, a pace from the heaped grey end of the sleeve. It was the smallest ward Kiva had ever seen lit on a beach. It would not have stopped a child. It was the nearest thing.

Kiva lit.

She lit nothing but her Ember. Declared, single, off her own thimble, by her captain's voice at eight that morning on a causeway. It came up glad under her breastbone, as it always came, as though it had been waiting by the door, and the bar came down her right fist, short and bright, and she knelt on the mud with it at her side and did not lift it.

On the east bank, six paces off across the thread, Trewin had let go of the punt's line. He had come down off the bank into the channel. He stood on the east mud with his boots square and his hands open at his sides and his eyes on Kiva's mouth.

"*Ready,*" he said. His voice was very deep, and quite steady.

The fold moved.

It did it slowly. The heaped closed end of the sleeve, lying across the near edge and across Penhale's back, lifted. It lifted off whatever it lay on, a hand, two hands, grey and streaming, frosting the air. It lifted the way it had lifted off the mud when Seven's near post was cut, in a long grey lip, and it bent as a page turns.

It turned toward Pru.

It came off the man under it and turned in the air and came down onto Pru's tray, the whole heavy grey end of it, as a wet sail comes down onto a deck. Kiva saw Pru's arms take it. She saw them go down an inch under the weight and come up again. She saw the cold go into Pru's hands at once, the fingers whitening at the tips.

"*Fold,*" said Kiva.

Pru folded.

She did it as she had done it at the Bench on Monday, and at the gate mast, and on a yard in the second month with Coombe on the step: from the far edge. She tapped the grey once with her right hand and brought the tray in on itself, the far edge first, and the grey came in with it. It came in heavy and slow. It was not a cord. It was not a rung. It was ten paces of live skin folding into itself on a doormat of silver, and it came in, and in, to the size of a loaf, a grey lump on Pru's tray that pulled at it like an anchor and steamed.

The rest of the sleeve went with it. Kiva saw it go. All of it, the whole grey length of it that had swung round on its post over the west mud, came up off the west mud after its end as a blanket comes up after the corner you lift it by. It came up off the near edge. It came up off the fold over Bridie. It hung in the air from the post in the bend to Pru's tray, a long grey curve, streaming.

Under it, on the mud, there was a man on his knees.

Kiva did not look.

"*Nobody,*" she said.

Short, with the swallow.

Pru let go.

For a quarter of a count, the grey lump hung over the mud a foot above Pru's empty tray, with the long curve of the sleeve going away from it to the post, and nobody had it. It did not fold. There was nobody for it to fold to. It hung in nothing, belonging to nobody, as a coin hangs at the top of a toss.

Kiva took it.

She put her right fist under it, with the bar lit along it, and it came down onto the bar and the Ember took the weight of it. It was heavy. It was heavier than anything she had ever carried in any door, heavier than the cord on the yard, heavier than she had known an Ember could hold. It dragged at her fist toward the post in the bend, where the sleeve was still hung by half its mouth. It pulled like a dog on a lead.

She went.

She went low, six paces east, across the thread of slack water, with the grey lump riding along her fist and the whole long sleeve coming after it through the air. She did not bound high. She could not. The weight would not let her; it held her down like a hand on her shoulder. She went across the channel a foot above the mud, with the struts running out of her calves, and halfway across she felt the lump begin to unfold.

It was opening on her fist. She felt it go round her knuckles, cold, a grey wrap, the way it had gone down onto Pru's tray. It was folding to the nearest thing. The nearest thing was her hand.

*Nobody.*

She had not said it yet. She was not down yet. She was a foot off the east mud with the grey wrapping round her fist and Trewin's boots in front of her, square, planted on nothing yet, waiting, and she said it.

"*Nobody.*"

From the air. Before she landed. She always did.

She let go.

Trewin opened on the end of the word.

He did not wait to see her land. Kiva saw that he did not wait. She saw his face, white, the face of a man stepping off a wall into the dark, and she felt his still door come up out of the east mud under the grey lump in the same breath as her own hand came empty. It came up slow and heavy and very sure, as a post goes into wet ground. The lump came down onto it. It did not hang. It did not have time to hang. It was planted, into the mud of the east side of Mallow Creek, six paces from anybody, with the long grey sleeve stretched taut from Trewin's boots across the channel to the far post in the bend.

It writhed once. Kiva saw it. It tried to fold, the way it had tried on Pru's tray and on her fist. It tried to fold toward Trewin.

He did not move. He stood on it with his still door open into the mud, and it could not fold onto the ground, and it lay still.

Kiva landed on the east mud and went to her knees.

"*Planted,*" said Trewin.

His voice was quite polite.

"*Cut,*" said Yeo.

The red-haired boy in the punt lit his edge and cut the far post, and the top of it came off and went down into the slack water where his purse was, and nobody hauled it. And the stow died.

It died all at once, as Seven's had. The far post went grey and soft and slumped. The long grey sleeve that stretched across the channel from the post to Trewin's boots went grey along its whole length, and thin, and then it was a film of ash lying across the mud and the thread of water, thin as a cobweb. The lump at Trewin's feet went grey with it. It lay on the mud under his boots, and he stood on it, and it was ash.

Trewin shut. He stood for a moment longer, quite still. Then he looked down at his boots.

They were white to the ankle. Frost lay on them like salt.

Kiva was on her knees on the east mud. She looked at her right hand. The knuckles were white, and the backs of the first two fingers, where the grey had gone round them. They did not hurt yet. She knew they would.

She made herself look across the channel.

The west mud was bare now where the fold had been. The sleeve had come up off it and gone across, and there was nothing between the sky and the two people it had been lying on.

Penhale was on his knees. He was bent forward over the girl, with his back to the sky. His ward was still up. It was up over both of them, broad and low and grey, a roof, the way he had held it when he leaned in under the near edge to take her arm. But it was not lit any more. It was grey as the ash on the mud. It was the shape of a ward, standing on nothing, like frost on a window in the shape of a fern.

His back was white. His smock that had been black once was white from the collar to the waist, and his red beard was white, and his hair. He did not move.

Under him, under the grey shape of his ward, on the mud at the edge of the thread of water, Bridie Hebden lay on her side with her face turned up into an inch of air. Her eyes were open. Her plaits had come down. One of her arms was out from under him, the left, and his hand was round it at the wrist, holding it.

"*Down,*" said Yeo, on the spur. "*Everybody. Off the spur. Now.*"

They got Bridie out first.

Afterward Kiva could not have said who did what. She knew she went back across the thread of water on her knees and her hands, because her knees were wet to the thigh afterward and her right hand was screaming. She knew Pru was there, with her own hands held out in front of her as if they belonged to somebody else, the tips of the fingers white. She knew Anwen came down off the bank and Jude after her, and Ivey came the last ten paces through the west mud with her face set, and the red-haired boy jumped out of the punt into water to his waist and waded.

She knew that somebody had to take Penhale's hand off the girl's wrist, and that it was Ivey. Ivey knelt in the mud and took the white fingers one by one and opened them, very gently, as one opens the hand of a sleeping child to take something out of it. They opened. They did not close again.

Anwen and Jude drew Bridie out sideways from under him, along the mud, by her shoulders. She came out stiff and cold and wet. Her legs below the knee, where the sleeve's edge had lain across them, were white through her stockings. Her face was white. Her lips were moving.

"Fathom," she was saying. "One fathom. Two fathom." Her teeth were going so hard that the words came out in pieces. "He was telling me. Under it. He was counting. To keep me still." Her eyes went past Kiva to the man on his knees. "He stopped at three."

Kiva put her left hand on the girl's shoulder. It was the only hand she had that did not hurt. The two fingers lay on the wet brown and buff of the coat and told her nothing.

"You're out," she said. "You're on the mud. You're out."

"Is he—"

"Ivey's with him," said Kiva.

Ivey had turned him over.

She had turned him over onto his back on the mud, quite gently, with her own coat under his head, and she was kneeling beside him with both her hands flat on his chest. They were a catcher's hands, broad and square and dark with the work, and the white light that had been under them at the tear was not under them now. She was not reading anything. She was only holding them there.

His face was white. His eyes were half open. His mouth was a little open in the red-white beard, as if he had been going to say something cheerful and had stopped, as Bridie said, at three.

Ivey took her hands away.

"*Ma'am,*" she said, up the bank, to Yeo. She did not raise her voice either. It was a Company voice. "He's gone."

---

The flood came in at nine minutes to one.

Kiva saw it come. She was on her knees on the west mud beside Bridie, with Pru holding the girl's head out of the thread and Anwen chafing her white legs through the stockings with both hands, and she happened to look at the marsh grass on the west bank. It stirred. It stirred very slightly, all together, inland. And the thread of water in the channel's bottom, which had been lying in its bed like a ribbon since twelve minutes past twelve, began, very slowly, to run the other way.

Seven minutes, she thought. The stow had turned at a quarter to one, or a minute before. The water had turned at nine minutes to.

Seven minutes. And her fingers had said nothing, the whole time, and the grass had stood up straight.

She did not think anything else about it. There was not room. She put it somewhere, the way she had put Mace's *down a hand* and Penhale's count somewhere, and left it there.

"*The flood,*" said Yeo, from the bank. "*Out of the channel. All of you. Now.*"

They carried them out.

They carried Bridie up the west bank first, on the punt's floorboards, which the boatman had pulled up and passed across. Anwen and Pru and the red-haired boy carried her, with Jude at the head holding the boards level with his good arm. She was talking all the way up the bank, in pieces, about fathoms. At the top of the west bank there was nothing but marsh grass, and they laid her down in it and Anwen took off her own coat and put it over her.

They carried Penhale across the channel to the east side.

The water was coming in now. It was coming up the thread from the sea, a hand deep, two, brown and quick, and it was at their shins by the time they had him on the boards. Ivey took the head. Trewin took the foot, in his white boots, with his face set. Jude went back down into the water to take a side, and the boatman the other, and Kiva took a corner with her left hand, because her right would not close.

They went up the east bank with him. It was steep. It was mud. Halfway up, Jude's bad foot went from under him and the board tipped, and Kiva felt the weight come onto her left arm all at once, and her two fingers said *something's pushing,* and she held.

At the top of the bank, at the edge of the spur, Perrin was waiting.

He was not supposed to lift anything heavier than a cup. Aske had written it on a card. He knelt at the edge of the spur and put both his big hands under the head of the boards as they came up over the lip, and took the weight, and lifted. Kiva saw his face as he did it. It did not change. Only his breath went short and stayed short.

Nobody told him to stop.

They laid Davy Penhale on the stone of the spur, on the punt's floorboards, with his white face to the sky and the wind blowing his white beard sideways. Yeo took off her coat. She knelt and laid it over him, from his chest to his boots, and then she put out her hand and closed his eyes, the left and then the right, with her thumb. She did it as if she had done it before.

She stayed there on her knees with her hand on the coat for a long time.

---

Coombe came at ten past one.

She came along the spur from the causeway at a walk that was nearly a run, in her grey Board coat, with the rope in her hands. Kiva watched her come all the way. She came past the place where Seven's stow had been, a film of grey on the mud under brown water now, and past the place where the Company crew had been kneeling at the upper tear, and past the punt drawn up on the stone. She stopped where Yeo was kneeling.

She looked down at the coat on the boards. She looked at the red-white beard blowing out from under it.

She did not tie a knot. Her hands were quite still on the rope.

"Yeo," she said.

"Dinah." Yeo did not look up. "He went in by the book. To the near edge. He got his ward under it and lifted, and he had her arm." Her voice was perfectly flat. "And then the far end came round. It's a sleeve. It's got two ends. The book's for a sheet."

Coombe did not say anything.

"The girl was on it," said Yeo. "On the skin. Freeing a line. She asked me, from the bank, was it ground. It was lying down. I said what the book says." She took her hand off the coat. "I said it."

"I know," said Coombe.

"It was still on a post. The far post. It turned on it." Yeo looked up at last, at the creek, where the flood was coming in over the mud. "Before the water turned, Dinah. I had my watch on it. Seven minutes before."

Coombe looked at the creek too.

Then she turned her head and looked across the spur, at Kiva. Kiva was sitting on the stone with her back against the flag pole and her right hand held in her left on her knee, because it had begun to hurt now, and Pru was beside her with her own hands held out in front of her, the fingertips white and beginning to blister. Coombe looked at Trewin, standing a little way off by himself with his frosted boots on the stone and his hat in his hands. She looked at the film of grey ash across the channel, stretched from the east mud where Trewin had planted to the stump of the far post in the bend, going under now in the brown flood.

She looked at it for a long time.

"Who called it?" she said.

"Rowan," said Yeo. "On my word. Finch caught. Rowan carried. The Tarn's captain planted." She was quiet a moment. "It held. It took the skin off the both of them in the time it takes to say it. And he was dead under it already."

Coombe came across the spur to Kiva.

She did not crouch. She stood over her, with the rope in her hands, and the pale eyes looked down at Kiva's white knuckles and at Pru's white fingertips.

"It held," said Coombe.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Write it." The Marshal's voice did not change at all. "In your seven columns, with every hand in it by name. Who caught. Who carried. Who planted. Who called it. Whose word." She was quiet. "And his. In the first column. Before."

"Yes, ma'am."

Coombe looked at her a moment longer. Then she went back across the spur to where Yeo was kneeling, and she knelt down too, on the stone, on the other side of the boards, in her grey Board coat, with the rope across her knees. The two of them knelt there with Davy Penhale between them and did not say anything to each other at all.

---

The Hunt went on.

Kiva had not thought that it would. She had thought, without thinking it, that when a man died the board would stop, the way the board stops in a lane when the glass is turned over and the marshal lifts his hand. It did not. From the spur she could see the other beats on the Longshore, the flags on their poles standing up out of the marsh at the heads of the creeks, a quarter of a mile apart all the way to the sea. On every one of them there were people, small and dark against the mud, working the flood. A long way off on Brine Reach somebody was hauling a line. Nearer, on the next creek south, a Company punt was going out across the brown water toward a post.

"Mallow Creek's closed," said Yeo.

She had got up. She stood on the spur with her notebook open in her hand, and her face was quite composed, and her voice was flat and brisk, the officer's voice that she had used at nine.

"The lower tear's under the flood," she said. "It'll wait for the bottom of the night's ebb. Ivey's crew will close it near one in the morning, if it's still there, with a guard on it till then." She looked at the creek. "The upper tear's closed. Seven's. They had it shut at twenty to one." She wrote. "Seven's two bags are up the east bank, shut; Talley and my boatman dragged them up on their lines when the flood came. The Tarn's first the same. The Tarn's second is in the creek under the far post, open, and nobody hauled it. That's the creek's." She wrote again. "The net's tin is in the punt."

She wrote for a long time. Kiva watched her. She wrote standing, fast, in the brisk upright hand, and once she stopped and looked at what she had written, and did not cross it out, and went on.

Then she read it aloud. Kiva did not know if she meant to. She read it the way Rhea read a slate, to the four who had stood on the beat, and the Tarn's four, and the Company crew, and the Marshal kneeling on the stone.

"*Closed, Mallow Creek, at one, on the flood. The upper stow, Seven's, cut in the slack, two posts, clean; the near post shed a rung on a surge before, held in part on a wall laid on the water. The lower stow, the Tarn's, cut on one post in the slack. The far post stood. The stow turned on it at a quarter to one, in the slack, with the Tarn's catcher on the skin by the officer's word, and the skin came over her.*"

She stopped. She went on.

"*Lives: one. D. Penhale, catcher, the Saltmere Company, reserve to the officer, went to the near edge by the book and was taken under the far end. The girl lived under his ward.*" Her voice did not change. "*Hurt: B. Hebden, catcher, the Tarn, both legs below the knee, a burn of cold. P. Finch, catcher, Team Seven, the fingers of both hands at the tips. K. Rowan, catcher, Team Seven, the right hand across the knuckles. Trewin, captain, the Tarn, both feet to the ankle.*"

She turned the page.

"*The skin was taken off them, on the officer's word, by a pass in three. A ward to an Ember to a still door. Called. Nobody holding it twice.*" She looked up. "*That's entered for whoever reads it. Remarks: none, to any four. The remark's the book's.*" She shut the notebook. "And mine. I said it was ground."

Nobody said anything. The flood came in over the mud of Mallow Creek, brown and quick, and covered the place where Bridie had lain, and the place where Penhale had knelt, and the long grey film of the dead stow that stretched across the channel from Trewin's planted end to the stump in the bend.

Jude was standing at the edge of the spur, looking at it.

He had his left arm against his chest and his good hand open at his side. He was looking at the water coming in. Kiva had seen him look at the sea from a boat's rail, and from a causeway at two in the morning, and from the end of the Long Quay last night with his hand on a bollard. He was not looking at it that way now.

"I'd want it said," said Jude, to nobody, very quietly, "that I've seen it come in now. Over somebody."

Nobody answered him. Yeo, behind him, put her notebook in her coat and stood for a moment with her hand still on it. She looked at the back of his head.

---

Rhea Sorn went to the Company yard at four, when Gannet Creek was closed and her slate was written and her own four bags were at the gate being combed red.

She had heard it on the causeway at half past one, from a Board clerk running with a paper, and she had not let herself go to Mallow Creek. It was not her beat. Her beat was Gannet Creek, and Old Four and a Westerley four on it, and a kiddle of five posts working the ebb. Her two fours had cut it in the slack on her word, and the Company's crew had closed it at the low, and she had written it on the slate, every line. She had not let her hand shake while she wrote it. *Lives: none.* She had written that at one o'clock, at the very minute, as it turned out, when they were laying him on the spur at Mallow.

The yard was behind the Long Quay, a cobbled square with green vans along one side and the Company's sheds along the other. There was a green van in the middle of it with its doors open and nobody near it. Inside, on a stretcher, under a Company coat, there was a long shape.

Yeo was sitting on the tailboard of the next van with her hands in her lap.

Rhea went and sat down beside her. She did not say anything for a while. Neither did Yeo.

Across the yard, by the shed doors, Pike was sitting on an upturned crate. He had come on Graywater's van to the Longshore at seven to sit on the hard at Gannet Creek and watch, because he could not stay away from a creek, and he had come here after. His hands lay on his knees, closed halfway. He was looking at the open van.

"He had Pike's place," said Rhea at last. "On the coast night. The Monday of last week. At the purses."

"I know. He told me." Yeo did not turn her head. "He said your old man nearly smiled at him once. He was very proud of it."

"Pike nearly smiles at everybody."

"Davy didn't know that." Yeo looked at her hands. "He'd been reserve on four Hunts. He'd never gone onto the mud. He said it was dull, and he was paid for it, and his mother liked it."

Rhea had nothing to say to that.

"I told the girl it was ground," said Yeo. "It was lying on the mud like a hem, and it was on a post, and I didn't look at the post. I looked at the skin." Her voice did not change. "Coombe's page is at the printer. *A hem that lies down under a thing on its feet is not ground.* I heard her say it at Hollins three seasons gone, over a lock-keeper. I thought it was a good line. I thought, when it's printed, I'll teach it."

"So did I," said Rhea. "When it's printed."

"Who had his far edge?"

Rhea turned her head.

"Davy's," said Yeo. "When he went to the near edge and lifted. Who was watching the far end of the sleeve for him?"

"Nobody," said Rhea.

"Nobody. The corner-hand had it, by the book. The corner-hand was the Tarn's boy, hauling a line with his eyes on the line, as the book tells him to." Yeo was quiet for a moment. "On the Company's crews we give the far edge to the corner-hand. We've done it since before my mother. We don't keep seconds. A second's an inland thing. A Graywater thing."

"It is," said Rhea. "It was. Since the first month."

"Before that?"

"Before that I'd a catcher who watched my heel, and nobody at the far edge at all, for eleven years. And I'd have told you we didn't need one." Rhea looked across the yard at Pike on his crate. "Then I had a second. A girl of seventeen with two dead fingers. She called the far edge in my van this winter, and on Gannet Creek in the dark, and the line came off it. And on the Tuesday of last week, at dawn, I went short, by my own card, and for one count at dawn there was nobody at the far edge but a man with half a fist doing two things at once." She was quiet. "Half a rung went into Gannet Creek. That's all it cost me."

Yeo did not say anything.

"Her second called it today," said Rhea. "Up your creek. From three paces. I heard that on the causeway too."

"Finch." Yeo nodded. "She called it at a quarter past twelve, on Seven's stow, and Rowan came off her line, and the fold lay down where she'd been. I wrote it in my book. I said I'd not seen anybody watching for it." She looked at the open van. "Half an hour later I'd a man under a fold sixty paces down the same creek, and nobody watching for it, because the book doesn't watch for it. I wrote that too."

They sat a long time.

Pike got up off his crate. He did it slowly, and stood for a moment, and then he walked across the yard to the open van. He did not get into it. He stood at its tailboard with his half-closed hands at his sides and looked in at the long shape under the coat. He stood there for as long as it would take to count three fathoms of line, hand over hand. Then he came back across the yard.

He stopped in front of the two officers on the tailboard.

"He counted it in fathoms," said Pike. "On a drawstring. I'd never heard it before the creek. *One fathom. Two fathom.*" His voice was thick. "I'd my hands half shut on a sack, and I couldn't call it for her, and he called it. He did my place, that night. He did it well." He looked at his hands. "I'd not have had him do it if I could have done it myself."

"I know," said Rhea.

"He had nobody at his far edge," said Pike. "That's all. That's what I came over to say." He looked at Yeo, and his face did the thing it did, which was very nearly a smile, and was not one. "I'm sorry for your man, officer."

"Thank you," said Yeo.

Pike went away across the yard to Graywater's van and sat down on its tailboard by himself.

Rhea took her slate off her belt. She did not know why. It was written; Gannet Creek was closed and entered and there was nothing to add to it. She held it on her knee, slate blue, with her six columns ruled down it. *Before. Closed. Lives. Works. Tinned. Remarks.*

"The girl's columns," said Yeo. "Rowan's. Seven columns, they say. On the far edge, and the second, and what Pike does that he never says."

"Ten sheets," said Rhea. "Pike's craft. She wrote them this winter, in the van, after the coal wharf." She did not look up from the slate. "And a sheet on the pass. The three. She wrote that for Coombe, in the second month."

"I'd want a copy," said Yeo. "For my crew. Ivey first. Before the Lane."

"It's not mine to give. It's the girl's."

"Then I'll ask the girl."

Rhea looked at the slate on her knee for a long time. She had ruled it herself, every line of it, every night for eleven years, and she had written *Lives: none* on it more times than she could count, and twice she had not. She thought of a card in a kitchen in the autumn, slate blue, read aloud, signed on the back by four children. *Then we look again, and either of us may say no.* She thought of the thirtieth, a fortnight off.

She had spent the spring's advice already. She had spent it at Gannet Creek at one in the morning on a mud hard. She did not have another.

"Ask her tonight," she said. "Before she sleeps. She'll want to have said it to somebody before she sleeps." She hung the slate back on her belt. "That's not advice. That's a thing I know about her."

"Is there a difference?"

"Not much," said Rhea Sorn, and sat on the tailboard beside the other officer until the light began to go.
