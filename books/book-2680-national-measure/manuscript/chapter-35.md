# Chapter 35 — Kiva: One Door Each

Kellan was at the draft board at eight, in the black and gold, reading his own card with his hands in his pockets.

Kiva saw him from across the boards and went over. She had meant to find him at the loft and had missed him; he had gone down the stair at six, Pru said, before anybody else was dressed, with his boots in his hand so as not to wake the room. Now he stood alone in front of the deal board with the green baize along its top, and there were nineteen cards on it, and he was reading the one with the Halden mark at its head as though it were a sum that would not come out.

He heard her and did not turn.

"You've heard," he said.

"Heard what?"

"Then you haven't." He took one hand out of his pocket and touched the card's corner, as Jude had touched his yesterday, and put the hand back. "It doesn't matter. It's mine. What do you want, at eight in the morning, with your hand in a bandage and that face on?"

"To tell you something before you hear it in a hall tonight," said Kiva. "So that you've had it first, from me."

He turned round then.

She told him on the boards by the draft board, with the timber pond lying black beside them and the gate mast standing over Noa's empty desk. She did not tell him all of it. She told him that she dreamed with a fever, and had since she was thirteen. It was the thing in Annick's notebook and the Ferren keeper's book and the keeper's book at the pump house here, on the same night in 2604. Her aunt had written it down the same night last spring, in her words, and had it sealed before she read anybody else's. And she meant to stand up at the Salt Hall at seven and ask the Board to open the page.

Kellan listened with his face quite still. When she had done, he did not say anything for a while. He looked at the gate mast.

"That's not a sum," he said at last.

"No."

"I was trying to make it one. While you talked. I can't." He took his hands out of his pockets. "I'll tell you what I can do with it. The house will want it. He'll want it more than he wanted the three, because the three was a thing you could do and this is a thing you've seen. He's been looking for somebody who saw what he saw for seventy-seven years." Kellan's voice was flat, as it went when a sum was bad. "He'll be glad. That's the part that frightens me. He'll be so glad."

"I know."

"Do you? He sat at that table and told you about a window. I've heard him tell it four times at dinner. Every time he gets to *white* his voice goes quiet." Kellan stopped. "I'm not telling you not to. I'd not. I'm telling you what my house will do with it, because nobody else in that hall will know."

"That's why I told you first," said Kiva.

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and glanced back at the card on the baize behind him, with the Halden mark at its head.

"I've a thing of my own tonight," he said. "In that hall. It's not as big as yours. It's big enough." He did not explain. "I'll see you on the floor at eleven. Halden's at nine. I'll be on the tiers for Carrow, with the rest of Meridian, wanting you to lose and wanting you to win." He nearly smiled. "I'd expect nothing else of myself."

He went away toward the Graving's steps with his hands back in his pockets.

Jude was at the board when Kiva turned round, with the book under his good arm, reading his own card again for the fourth time.

He did not mind her reading it over his shoulder. He moved a little, to let her. It was a plain white card in the Company's green, pinned at the top with one brass pin, in Yeo's brisk upright hand.

*The Saltmere Company. Talley, J., edge, Meridian, Team Seven. To the closing crew under Officer Yeo, on the Longshore and the boats, at a Company hand's wage, housed, from the first of the fourth month. Six months, and then we look again. A card is not a seat. Yeo.*

Under it, smaller, in the same hand, there was one more line.

*You asked me on the Ropewalk steps what it was for. Come and find out.*

"She was at the board on Wednesday night," said Jude. "Behind us. She didn't say anything." He looked at the line at the foot. "I knew she would. I said so at the window. I said I'd stay if she asked." He scratched his jaw with his right hand. "She's asked. I'd thought I'd feel something. I feel as though I've been handed a parcel and I don't know what's in it, and it's heavy, and it's mine."

"You don't have to answer today."

"I know. It says six months, and then we look again." Jude almost smiled. "That's Rhea's card. She's copied Rhea's card. She'll have had it off Rowan's columns, through Ivey." He touched the corner of it again. "I'll answer after Wednesday. I want to see the bank come out first. If it comes out." He looked at the sea, past the end of the timber pond and the Graving's gate, grey and breathing. "If I'm to spend six months carrying ash up a bank, I'd like to know first that a bank can be carried down."

Perrin's two cards were in the bottom corner, where they had been yesterday. He was standing three paces off, pretending to read the Lane board.

One card had the Halden mark. *Halden Lift Yards, the Long Quay. The winner of the Carry, Meridian, to the lift yard, carry, at a carry's wage, on the Proprietor's roll, from his healer's leave.* The other was on the Works' grey paper, in a small square hand. *The Municipal Works, Meridian. Cade, P., carry, to the trunk crew at the Brickfields, from the autumn, when his card is up. Rusk.*

"Two," said a voice behind them. It was Gower, from Old Four, with a pasty in his hand and nothing unkind in his face. "Two cards, Cade. For carrying a dummy up a ladder. I carried mine up a stair and nobody's carded me for anything."

"You got face," said Perrin. "Ten seconds."

"I got face. I'm not saying I didn't." Gower bit his pasty. "I'm saying two. Hesketh's been at the board since seven counting other people's. Half the loft's been." He went away, eating.

Perrin stood looking at the two cards with his hand pressed to his side.

"I wanted it," he said. Kiva did not think he was saying it to her. "On the boat. Something with only my name on it. I said so to Pryce on the Ropewalk steps." He reached out with his free hand and took the Halden card down off its pin. "That one hasn't got my name on it. It's got *the winner of the Carry.* They've carded a Tuesday."

He took a pencil out of his coat and wrote on the back of it, against the baize, with his hand pressed to his side, in his round careful hand. *No, with thanks. It was mine, the ladder, but it was one Tuesday. P. Cade.* Then he pinned the card back up, face to the board, so that only the back showed.

"And the other?"

"It says *Cade.*" He did not touch the grey card. "It says *when his card is up.* Rusk read the Chair's ledger and Aske's card both before he wrote it. That's a man who's counted the ribs." He looked at it for a moment longer. "I'll leave it up. I'll answer at home."

They went down the Graving's steps together, slowly, with Perrin a step at a time. Halfway down he stopped and breathed.

"Gower thinks I'm pleased," he said.

"Aren't you?"

"I'm pleased about the grey one." He went on down. "I'd thought being envied would feel like the top of the ladder. It feels like Gower eating a pasty and counting." He was quiet for three steps. "Give me the foot of the ladder, and you saying *set.*"

---

The Graving was full at a quarter to eleven, and the whole of it was green.

Kiva had thought it was full a week ago, on the Saturday, against the Tarn's first four. She had been wrong. That Saturday there had been room on the tiers to put a hand down beside you. Now the green came down the steps to the floor's edge and stood there, ten deep on the bottom tier, and the boys who sold pasties had given up selling and were sitting on their baskets. The brown and buff of the Tarn sat in its knot in the middle. Meridian's colours were above it, all four of the first company, and Kellan among them in his black and gold. The iron and glass roof went over all of it, and the grey light came down on the white chalk of the pen eighty feet below as it comes down into a church.

At the seaward end the floating gate held the harbour out, and Kiva could hear the sea breathing against its timber. Under that, through the stone of the dock floor, there was the town's count.

The Ropewalk's first four came out of the side door in their shirtsleeves, and the Graving stood up.

It did not cheer. It stood, all of it, tier after tier, as a church stands. Carrow walked first, with the rope on her sleeve. Lyle walked half a pace behind and to one side, as she always had, but there was nothing at her hip now, and her hands hung at her sides. Tolver came after with his cheerful face. Treen came last, small and dark, with her sleeves rolled and her cord at her belt.

They stood behind their hurdle and looked at the Commodore lying in the middle of the pen. Then Carrow looked down the pen at Seven.

She looked at Perrin outside the pen, behind Seven's hurdle, with his hand on his side. She looked at Jude's left arm, and Pru's ten caps, two of them fresh, and the linen on Kiva's right hand. She looked at them each for exactly as long as it took to see what was there, and then she looked at Kiva and inclined her head, as a fact about the weather.

"*Declarations,*" said the marshal.

"The Ropewalk," said Carrow. Her voice went up the stone walls, low and flat and pleasant, with the coast in it. "*Carrow, edge. Declared. Single.*"

The Graving made a sound. It was very small. It was the sound of four thousand people drawing in their breath and not letting it out.

"*Lyle, stance. Single. Tolver, stance. Treen, ward.*"

At the mast on the north side of the pen, Noa wrote. Polglase wrote. Kiva saw Noa look up at Carrow from his desk, once, with his narrow face quite still, and then down again.

"*Team Seven,*" said Perrin. "*Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Finch, ward, catcher. Rowan, stance, declared, by her captain's voice. Cade, captain, standing out on his healer's card.*"

"*One door each,*" said the marshal, to nobody in particular, and then looked as though he wished he had not. He turned his glass. "*Ready.*"

They had made the plan at the long table at half past nine, and it was Thursday's plan, because Thursday's plan had worked.

Kiva would plant on the litter's head and sit there, as she had sat on it against the Tarn, so that nobody could carry what nobody could lift. Anwen's wall would keep their still doors off the foot. Jude would take whoever came near, with his edge on his right fist. Pru would call hands. And at the glass, the point was Seven's, every carry, and whatever Jude cut, and in the third carry, if it was close, they would try the hurdle with their elbows.

Carrow had been on the tiers on Thursday afternoon. Kiva had seen her, two rows below Rhea, with Treen beside her, watching.

The bell went.

Kiva went for the litter. She got there first. She went down astride the head poles with her forearms along the canvas and opened her stance, and her feet went into the Graving's floor, down through the granite, and held. Anwen's wall came up on the south side of the foot. Jude went north. Pru stood behind the litter's middle with her ward at her hip, low, and her eyes on hands.

Lyle walked down the pen and stopped two paces from Anwen's wall, and planted.

She did not have a wall. She had nothing at her hip and nothing on her hands. She stood with her feet in the stone and her arms at her sides, and then she bent her knees, and put her weight down into the floor, and leaned her shoulder against Anwen's wall.

Kiva saw Anwen feel it. She saw Anwen's heels go into the stone, right, and her wall bow, a finger, two fingers. Lyle was leaning from the floor. She was leaning as Anwen had taught her to lean on a bare board at eight o'clock yesterday morning, with her weight going down and coming back up through her heels and her back and her shoulder, and it was moving Anwen's wall.

"*She's got it,*" said Anwen, between her teeth. "*I gave it her.*"

Carrow had not moved from the Ropewalk's end of the pen.

She stood a pace behind Treen with her edge lit on her right fist, bright as a poker, and her feet on the stone like anybody's, and she was not looking at the litter at all. She was looking at nothing. Treen was looking at Jude.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

Carrow walked. It was not a run. She walked down the south side of the pen, past the chalk squares, toward the litter, with her edge lit and her feet coming up off the stone at every step. Kiva felt it go past her through the floor, loud, as Lyle had said it would be loud. It was one door, walking.

Jude went to meet her. That was the plan. Whoever came near, Jude took them.

"*Hands, north,*" said Pru.

Jude checked. Tolver had come up on the north side, very fast for a big boy, with his long arms out and his still door ready, reaching for the litter's head poles where they stuck out past Kiva's forearms. Jude turned to take him. It was the right thing to do. Pru had called it, and it was right.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

Carrow was a pace from the litter. Her edge came down on the word, flat, quite lightly, on Kiva's shoulder.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "Seven."

Kiva knelt. She had to shut and let go to kneel, and the moment she let go of the poles Tolver had them. He did not lift. He held the head, and Lyle came off Anwen's wall, and the two still doors took the Commodore by the poles together and walked him back down the pen as two men carry a sleeping friend upstairs. Carrow walked beside them with her edge lit. Jude went for Tolver's back and Treen said *hands* and Carrow's edge came round between them, and Jude stopped, because the alternative was to walk into it.

At the Ropewalk's hurdle Carrow shut and vaulted the hazel, and turned, and took the head poles from under on the far side. Treen came over after her and took the foot.

"*Three,*" said the marshal. "*And a down. Four to the Ropewalk.*"

The Graving did not make the warm sound. It let its breath out. It was a long sound, like a wave going back off shingle, and it went round the tiers and came back, and then the green began to talk all at once.

Kiva knelt on the stone in the middle of the pen with her hands on her knees and counted ten, as a downed fighter counts, and did not look at anybody.

She had seen it. She had seen it from her knees, a pace off, with her shoulder still warm where the edge had touched. Carrow had not looked at the litter. She had not looked at Jude. She had walked down the pen with her eyes on nothing and her edge lit, and she had brought it down when Treen spoke and not a hair before. Treen had been watching Jude's hands. When Jude's hands went north, Treen said *set,* and Carrow came down on the nearest thing she could reach, which had been Kiva.

She was not reading the lane. She was being read for.

"She's taking Treen's count," said Kiva, at the hurdle, in the pause.

Perrin was outside the pen with his hands on the hazel, not leaning. Anwen was white and breathing hard, with her forearms shaking in her sleeves. Jude had his edge shut and his right fist opening and closing at his side.

"She's never done that," said Kiva. "Not in the Ropewalk, not at the Hunt. Carrow reads everything herself. That's what she is." She heard her voice go flat, and let it. "Now she doesn't read anything. She walks, and Treen reads, and Treen says *set,* and she brings it down. It's what I did to you on the ladder. *Set.* Off your heel."

"She's had it off you," said Anwen. "On the Inner Lock. Half the town was on the lip."

"She's had it off me," said Kiva. "And it works. She's not got two doors to lay, so she's got two people instead. Treen's eyes and her edge."

"So we take Treen's eyes," said Jude.

"How?" said Perrin.

Kiva had been asking herself that on her knees for a count of ten. She looked at Pru.

Pru had her hands up in front of her face in their caps. She was looking at them, at the linen on the tips of her fingers, two of them fresh, and then at Kiva's right hand in its bandage, and then at Jude's left arm hanging.

"Treen watches hands," said Pru slowly. "Like me. She's a hand door. She watches hands because she is one." She looked up. "That's what you said to me. On the steps. After the Tarn."

"Yes."

"So she called *set* off Talley's hands going north. And she'll call *now* off whoever's got their hands on a pole." Pru turned her capped fingers outward. "We haven't got hands. Not on the poles. Not you and not me. We carried it over on our elbows yesterday."

Kiva looked at her.

"If we carry it on our arms," said Pru, "there's nothing for her to read. No fingers. No hands. Only elbows." She went scarlet, all the way up. "She'll have to read something else, and she's never had to. And by the time she's found it, we're at the hurdle."

Perrin made a sound that was not quite a laugh. He took his hands off the hazel.

"No sitting on it," he said. "Not against a blade who's being read for. We carry. On your arms. Finch and Rowan on the poles, elbows, all the way down the pen, and Pryce and Talley off them." He looked at Anwen. "Lyle's leaning on you with your own lean."

"I know she is."

"Then don't lean back. Walk." Perrin's face was the colour it went when he was thinking very hard. "Walk your wall from the far end, round her, the way you'd walk it round a post. She's planted. She can lean, but she can't follow."

"*Rowan,*" said the marshal. "*Your declaration for the second carry.*"

"*Ember,*" said Perrin, before Kiva could open her mouth. "*Rowan, Ember, declared, single, by her captain's voice.*" He did not look at her. "You'll not need your stance if you're not sitting on it. You'll need your legs."

"*A change between carries,*" said the marshal, "*declared to the box, with nothing open in the pause. It's on her card.*"

At the mast, Noa wrote.

The second carry was the best four minutes of lane Kiva had ever stood in, and she could not afterward remember more than half of it.

She remembered the bell. She remembered going for the litter's head with her Ember lit under her breastbone, glad, as it always came, and the low bound it gave her down the stone. She remembered putting her forearms under the head poles, both of them, the left sleeve and the right bandage, crossed at the wrists as one holds out one's arms for a coat. She remembered the weight coming down into the crooks of her elbows, and Pru beside her at the foot with her forearms crooked and her capped fingers spread up and out like the fingers of a girl drying her nails.

She remembered that her hands were in the air, and so were Pru's, and that there was nothing on the poles but cloth and bone.

And she remembered Treen.

Treen was at the Ropewalk's end with her ward low at her hip and her eyes going, quick and dark, from Kiva's arms to Pru's, from Pru's to Jude's fist, and back. Kiva saw her look for hands and not find them. She saw Treen's mouth open on a word and shut again. Carrow was walking down the south side of the pen with her edge lit, waiting to be told, and Treen did not tell her.

"*Walk,*" said Kiva. "*West. Along the north.*"

They walked. They walked the Commodore down the north side of the pen, close against the posts, on their arms, as two washerwomen walk a basket between them. Behind them Anwen had stopped leaning. Lyle was planted two paces off her wall with her shoulder against it, leaning from the floor, and Anwen walked the far end of her wall round. She did not push the near end. She walked the far end in a quarter circle, slowly, as Mace had taught her on a van's line at home, and the near end came after it. Lyle leaned on a wall that was no longer where she was leaning. She leaned on air, from the floor, with her feet planted and all her weight coming up through her heels, and there was nothing there.

She did not fall. She was too good to fall. But she had to shut her stance to keep her feet, and for a count she stood in the middle of the pen with nothing open at all, and Jude was there.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "The Ropewalk."

Lyle knelt. Kiva saw her face as she went down, square and careful, filing a thing away to be thought about later. Then she saw Lyle look across the pen at Anwen and, for the first time Kiva had ever seen, nearly smile.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

It was late. Kiva heard that it was late. Treen had found something to read at last: Jude's feet, coming back from Lyle, wide and slow on the bad side. She said *set* off his feet, and Carrow brought her edge down where Jude was going to be. Jude was not there. He had stopped. He had stopped because he had heard Pru say, quite quietly, *Talley, stay,* and he stayed, a pace short, and Carrow's edge came down on stone.

"*Late,*" said Kiva, under her breath. "*She's late.*"

They were at the hurdle. Anwen had come round the end of her own wall at a run and gone over the hazel, and Jude after her, and the two of them turned on the far side with their good hands up. Kiva and Pru lifted together, on their forearms, which was not a thing anybody had ever done in a lane, and laid the Commodore on the top of the hazel like a coat across a gate. Anwen took the head from under in her two hands. Jude took the foot in his one. It went over. Two to two. Top to top.

"*Over,*" said the marshal. "*Three to Seven. A down. Four to Seven. Four to the Ropewalk.*"

The Graving made a sound that Kiva had not heard it make all week. It was not the warm sound and it was not the long sound of breath going out. It was somebody high up, a long way off, shouting *elbows!* in a voice that cracked, and then a great many people laughing, and some of them were in green.

They lost the third carry by the glass, and it was Kiva's fault, and Pru's, and nobody's.

The Ropewalk had learned. Kiva watched Treen learn it in the pause, at the far hurdle, with Carrow bent beside her and the two of them talking very fast and low. When the bell went, Treen did not look for hands. She looked at feet. She had watched Jude's feet in the second carry and found she could read them, and now she was reading everybody's: Anwen's heels, Pru's quick small steps, Kiva's own boots coming down after the Ember's bound.

She had taken that off Kiva too. Kiva watched feet. Everybody on the Circuit knew it. Pike had said it on a tailboard in front of half Graywater. *You watch where people put their feet.*

It was a strange thing to see your own habit in somebody else's eyes, being used against you.

Seven went for the litter on their arms again. The Ropewalk did not let them get it moving. Tolver planted at the head before Kiva got there and sat on it, as Kiva had sat on it on Thursday and again at the start of today, with his long arms flat along the canvas and his feet in the stone. Lyle planted at the foot. Carrow walked.

"*Feet,*" said Kiva. "*She's on feet. Talley, don't move them—*"

"*Hands,*" said Pru, at the same instant, from the other side of the litter. "*Tolver's hands, he's—*"

Jude stopped between the two words.

He had been told two things at once, by two voices, about two places. He stood a pace from the litter's middle with his edge lit and his weight on neither foot, and he did not know whose word to take, and for a whole count he took neither.

"*Set,*" said Treen.

Carrow came down. She did not touch Jude. She did not need to. She put her edge across the gap between Jude and the litter's head, lit, a bar of heat the length of her forearm, and held it there, and Jude could not reach Tolver without walking into it. And while he stood there, and Kiva stood there, and Pru stood there with her hands up, Lyle leaned.

She did not lean on anybody. She leaned on the litter. She was planted at the foot with her shoulder against the canvas, and she put her weight down into the floor and let it come back up through her heels, from the floor, as Anwen had taught her; and the Commodore went east under her shoulder a hand's breadth, and another, with a scrape of canvas on stone, until its middle lay across the chalk.

"*Glass,*" said the marshal. He came down off his box and looked, and walked round it twice, as the marshal had walked round it a week ago against the Tarn's first four. "*In the Ropewalk's half. By a hand.*" He went back up. "*A point to the Ropewalk. Five to the Ropewalk. Four to Seven.*"

The Graving let out its breath.

Carrow came down the pen afterward, as she had come down it in the Ropewalk, at a walk.

She stopped on the far side of Seven's hurdle and looked at them over it. She was breathing hard. There was sweat in her hairline, and her quick blunt face had a look on it that Kiva had not seen there before, which was the look of somebody who has just done a thing they hate and done it well.

"*Elbows,*" she said.

"Finch's," said Kiva.

"Finch's." Carrow looked at Pru. "She took Treen's eyes off her. I'd not have thought of it in a year. I'd not have had to. I've always had my own eyes." She was quiet a moment. "I had to read your feet in the third. Treen did. She's had that off you."

"I know. I saw."

"And I had that off you as well." Carrow lifted her right hand, with the edge dark on it. "The word. *Set.* I heard it twenty-two times on the lip of the Inner Lock on Tuesday, and on Wednesday night I asked Treen to say it to me while I walked." Her face did not change. "It's the worst thing I've ever done. I've never in my life lit a door on anybody's word but mine. I feel like a carthorse with somebody else's hand on the reins."

"It gets easier," said Kiva. "That's not the same as better."

Carrow looked at her for a long moment.

"No," she said. "It isn't." She glanced at Lyle, who had got up off her knee and was standing by the Ropewalk's hurdle with Treen, quite still, looking at Anwen. "Your wall taught mine to lean, and then walked away from her. She'll not forget that. Nor shall I." She turned to go, and turned back, as she always did. "There's a thing they're saying on the quay. That you'll not cross this week. Dray's four had it from your healer, in the loft."

"It's so."

"Why?"

Kiva thought about whether to say. She found she did not mind saying it to Carrow, here, over a hurdle, with the whole town talking about elbows.

"It's spoken for," she said. "For Wednesday, if anybody asks."

"The basin." Carrow's face did not change at all. "They're going to stop our bank."

"They're going to try."

"I know they are. Half the Ropewalk's fitters' children, and my mother says the low town will be wet for eleven minutes and she's moving the coal up a stair." Carrow looked past Kiva, past the Graving's gate, at the harbour, which could not be seen from the dock floor. "If they try, I want to be on the wall. Not on the tiers. Not in the Mercury. On the wall." She looked back. "That's not a thing I'd say to anybody from Meridian. I'm saying it to you because you kept a crossing for it."

She went away up the pen, at a walk, with one door and nothing open.

---

Kiva went to find Hart at half past two, when the basin was still.

She did not go to the pump house to ask him anything at first. She went because it was the slack, and the Board's sitting was at seven, and she had never in her life stood up in a hall full of strangers and asked to be heard about a thing that was not a door, and she wanted to stand somewhere quiet first. The pump house's outfall walk was the only quiet place she knew in Saltmere. For twenty minutes twice a day, if a ruined hand was right, it was the only quiet place in the town.

He was on the iron landing over the grating, as he always was, with his book of slacks open in his left hand and his watch on the inside of his wrist. His bad hand was not in his jacket. It was out, resting on the landing's rail, with the fingers half curled.

"Quiet," he said, without looking round. "Since nineteen past. It's the neap. It came when the sea gate said it would, and twenty-three minutes behind it, like a dog." He turned the watch. "On Wednesday it'll not."

"I know. Mara says."

"Your aunt says what I tell her. I tell her what the hand says." He looked round at last. "You've something on your face. You've had it since the shop door, by the look of it."

She stood on the outfall walk below him with her bound hand against her coat.

"The Board sits at seven," she said. "On the Chair's three sheets. Mara's going to stand up and say what she'll do on Wednesday. The Harbour Committee's going to stand up and say why she can't. And Halden's counsel's going to stand up and say whose ash it is." She looked at his hand on the rail. "Nobody in that hall will say what the hand says. They'll say *a fitter's hand,* and they'll say it the way Polglase says *it's the harbour.*"

Hart did not move.

"I'd like you to say it," said Kiva. "Under your own name. The held slack. The dog. That on Wednesday it'll not walk, and nobody can say why, and you've written it every spring since the ninth month." She made herself go on. "I'm asking a thing of a ruined hand. I know I am. I don't know what you're let ask it."

He looked at her for a long time from the landing, with the brown water still and flat below him and the six outfalls pouring into it on the count, and the count not going anywhere.

"It's let be asked one thing," he said at last. "*Quiet,* or *not.* That's all it does. It doesn't know why, or what, or how long. It knows *quiet.*" He turned his bad hand over on the rail, palm up, as Lyle had held her hand at her hip with nothing in it. "It was the best hand at the Bench in 2660. Your sponsor will tell you. It could read a cell through a wall. Now it reads one thing, and aches near everything else, and a girl from Meridian with two dead fingers has come down a walk to ask it to stand up in a hall." He was quiet a moment. "Nobody's asked anything of it in twenty years, but *is it quiet yet, Jess.*"

"I'm asking."

"I heard you." He shut his book of slacks. "Your sponsor will be in that hall."

"Yes."

"Then so will I." He put the book in his jacket. "I'll say what the hand says, and I'll say it's ruined, before anybody else does. And then I'll sit down." He looked at her. "You've not come here to ask me that. That's the second thing. What's the first?"

She had not known it showed. She stood on the walk and found that it did, and that she did not mind it showing to him.

"I'm going to stand up as well," she said. "After Mara. About a thing that isn't a door."

Hart did not ask what. She had thought he might. He looked at her face for a long time, and at her hands, and then he turned his watch on his wrist, once, and looked at the water.

"Ache," he said quietly. "Thirty-nine past. That's twenty minutes. It's going out again." He did not look round. "Same hour every day, Rowan. Never on purpose. You'll have done it already, today."

"Not yet. A quarter to seven."

"Then you'll be in a hall at a quarter to seven, standing up." He nearly smiled. "Lay them on the rail of the Salt Hall's gallery. It's iron. It'll say *yes.* Everything in this town says *yes.* Write it down anyway."

She went back along the walk toward the low town. Behind her, on the landing, she heard him open his book again and write, in his slow left hand, and she did not look back to see what.
