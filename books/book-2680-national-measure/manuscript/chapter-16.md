# Chapter 16 — Kiva: The Even Hand

The fourth crossing went on Sunday the 22nd, at seven, in the Old Yard, with a thaw coming up the hill and the frost going to water under the posts.

*Hand, to Ember. Rowan, changing on three.* Orla's stick on the ward, once. The fold. The shut. Nobody, in the hands, and the cold coming into them as it came now, at once and a long way up. The Ember. Six paces, onto a white cross that was half washed out.

"One cliff," said Noa. "Twenty-six. Thirty in all."

"*Four,*" said Orla.

The hand was white to the middle of the forearm. Kiva did not hold it up. She put it inside her coat against her ribs, where the disk was, cold, and stood on the cross while the thaw dripped off the shed roof; and she knew, before Aske had ever seen it, that it would not be warm in the morning.

It was not warm in the morning. It was not warm at noon, or at four, or at nine on Monday night, when she went up the Wren House stair with her aunt behind her and Aske held the wrist for two minutes by his watch and wrote, and did not say anything until he had written.

"*Cold to the middle of the forearm at seven on Monday. To the wrist at nine at night.*" He capped his pen. "I said I'd say so before and not after. I'm saying it. The card's held. There's no fifth until I've seen this hand warm on a morning, and there's no mast on a road in any case."

"I know there isn't."

"I know you know. I'm writing it so that your sponsor can't say that I didn't." He looked at her over his spectacles. "Noon. Four o'clock. A night. Two nights, now, by the look of it. I don't know what it's sulking about. I don't know whether it'll sulk three nights at Saltmere after the fifth, or a week after the sixth." He was quiet for a moment. "I'll say a thing that isn't on any card. I'm coming south with you. By the road, since that's how you're going."

"The Circuit's paying for you to go on the transit with the officers."

"The Circuit's paying for me to go to Saltmere. It didn't say how." Aske put the pen away. "I'll not have that hand looked at by a ferryman."

On Tuesday at seven it was cold to the knuckle. On Wednesday at seven, in the dark, by a lamp in the Wren House yard with the carrier's cart standing at the gate and the horse blowing, Aske held it for a minute by his watch and said, "Warm."

He wrote it on the card, standing against the wall. *Warm at seven on Wednesday. A sulk of two nights. Four of six, hand to Ember.* Then he got up onto the cart beside Pru, with his bag on his knees, as though he had been doing it all his life.

---

Jude had read the month out on Monday night, standing by the dresser at the Rowan kitchen table, from the first line to the last, because Perrin had asked him to and because, he said, a book that went south ought to go south with everybody having heard what was in it.

It took a quarter of an hour. Nobody interrupted.

"*Carried, the first of the second month: held, nine and sixty-four. Sealed, medical, W, plain: eight-tenths.*

"*Hand to Ember, Rowan, four crossings, on the Circuit's mast, read by N. Bexley: the 1st, 0.31; the 8th, 0.30; the 15th, 0.31; the 22nd, 0.30. One and twenty-two.*

"*The Pit, the 2nd, the Marshal's pack: 0.28. The Old Yard, the 4th, the sheet and the turn: 0.24. The 7th, a cord between three, unsanctioned: 0.22. The Marshal's lane, the 10th, against Halden License, lost six to two: 0.46. The 11th, the handed crossing: 0.15. The Marshal's floor, the 12th, four heads at a dram: 4.0, and our doors, 0.44; licensed, one remark. The 13th, the handed crossing before the Marshal: 0.18. The 16th, the tap against Renn: 0.15.*

"*Cowl Edge, the 19th and 20th, our own entry: the Board's card, 0.40; our doors, 0.61; a cord, 0.06. Fifty-nine and two-tenths, clean, after the comb; an eighth to the book: 7.40, credit.*

"*A purse sheet and no pole, to replace Finch's, frosted at Garside's mill: 2.40, today, at the chandler's on the Hythe.*" He looked up. "*Held, six and twenty-three. Spent this month, ten and eighty-one. Credited, seven and forty. Nine and sixty-four, less ten and eighty-one, and seven and forty. Six and twenty-three.*"

"That adds," said Anwen.

"It adds. I've done it four times." Jude did not close the book. "*Spoken for: two crossings at the Saltmere gate, if the hand lets them; six-tenths, near enough. Uncommitted, five and sixty-three.* That's every door that four people light on four boards in front of a Board, and every cord and every lamp. It's not a great deal. I'd want everybody to know that it's not a great deal."

"And sealed?" said Perrin.

"Eight-tenths. Not opened. Not made up." Jude scratched his jaw. "There's one more line. It's for tomorrow. I've left room."

The line for tomorrow came at eleven on Tuesday morning, across the ash office counter, in a stone jar the size of a fist with a brown card round its neck.

A woman in widow's black had brought it up from the Brickfields on the tram. Her name was Mrs. Ottery. Her husband had been a carter to the wardens of the Levee end for thirty years and had died in the twelfth month, and she had found the jar on the top shelf of his tool cupboard with his winter boots, and somebody at the chapel had told her that there were students at the Circuit who would give anything for plain.

*Handful, plain,* said the card round its neck. *Tinned the 3rd of the eleventh month, 2669. Winter stock.*

"Half a dram," said Mrs. Ottery. "He always said it was half a dram. I'd take two and a half for it. That's what the chapel said it was worth now. I'd not ask more than it's worth."

Anwen had the team's tin and the book. Pru had the comb. Kiva stood at the end of the counter with her left hand in her coat, and she looked at the jar, and at the woman's hands on it, which were red and cracked and held it as one holds a thing that belonged to somebody.

"May we comb it?" said Pru.

"I'd want you to."

Pru held the comb over the jar's open mouth. The third reed sang, thin and faint.

The seventh did not.

There was a moment in the ash office when nobody breathed. Kiva felt it go along the counter like a draught: the clerk, and Anwen, and the woman in black, and two juniors who had come in for a thimble and stopped in the door. It was the first time that any of them had heard a comb held over a jar since the thaw and the seventh reed stay still.

Then Pru tipped the jar, very slightly, toward the window, and looked into it.

She did not say anything. She held it out to Kiva, without a word, and Kiva looked.

The ash in the jar was grey, as ward-trace is grey. But it was not all grey. There was black in it, fine and dull, in streaks, like pepper stirred into flour and not stirred enough; and where the black was, it lay heavier, and did not shine at all.

"Soot," said Pru, quite quietly.

"It's plain," said Mrs. Ottery. "It didn't sing. You heard it."

"It didn't sing, Mrs. Ottery, because there's half as much in it to sing as there ought to be." Pru said it gently, for her, and went scarlet. "Soot doesn't sing. Soot doesn't do anything. It's been stretched. Somebody's put soot in it to make it weigh half a dram. There's a quarter of a dram here, perhaps less. I'd not put it on a hand. It might do nothing. It might do worse."

The woman stood at the counter with her red hands on the jar.

"He'd not have done that," she said.

Nobody answered her. The clerk looked at the ledger. Kiva thought about a can of civic stock in the Works' store, in the autumn, that somebody had found cut with soot and put down and nobody had taken up, and about a carter who kept his winter boots on the top shelf of a cupboard, and about thirty years of hard winters on the Levee end; and she did not say any of it, because it was not hers to say to a woman in black at a counter.

"We'll not buy it," said Anwen. "I'm sorry. I'd want it said that we're sorry."

"I'll not sell it, then." Mrs. Ottery put the lid back on with great care, and put the jar in her bag. "I'll not sell it to anybody. I'll put it back on his shelf." She went to the door. At the door she turned round. "He was a good carter," she said, and went out.

Jude wrote it in the book that night, on the line that he had left room for.

*Tuesday the 24th. The ash office. Offered: half a dram of "plain" ward-trace, winter stock of 2669, for 2.5. Combed by Finch: the seventh did not sing; cut with soot to about half. Refused. Nothing spent.*

He looked at it for some time after he had written it.

"I'd want it said that I've never been so sorry not to spend anything," he said.

---

Kiva wrote her card on Monday night, after the book was read, at the end of the kitchen table, on a plain one from the shop. It took her five tries to make it fit.

*We are coming by the road, five of us and our healer, and will be at your gate on Thursday the 26th before dark, if the carrier keeps time.*

*We have come off a moor with nothing plain. We have eight-tenths, sealed, for a hand, and no way to make it up.*

*It is not for a year. I would want that said first.*

*K. Rowan, catcher, T7.*

She showed it to Perrin, since it was under his name as much as hers, and he read it twice and said that he would like it known that he had never written to an Order in his life and did not mean to begin, and signed under her.

She showed it to her aunt.

Mara read it at the far end of the table, under the lamp, with the tide table pushed aside. She read it as she read a fault report, without saying anything, and then she put it down on the oilcloth and laid her left hand flat on it.

"You'll want my letter with it," she said.

"Your letter?"

"I had six questions from that house in the summer. I said that I'd answer two of them. I said it to you, in this room, and I've not done it." Her aunt did not look up. "I'll not have you go through that door on a latch that I left open and never closed. I'll write tonight. It'll go with yours by the Line runner in the morning."

"What are the questions?"

"Four of them are yours," said Mara. "If you ever want them. The other two are mine. I'll not tell you what I say." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "I've found that I'm a woman with a sealed page in a press at the Library. I'd not have you think that I'd lost the habit."

---

Mara Rowan wrote her letter at half past eleven on Monday night, at the kitchen table, alone, with the lamp turned down and her niece asleep in the next room.

It was the second letter that she had written that night. The first had gone into an envelope already, sealed and addressed, and lay on the dresser beside the brass weight with the Works' stamp on its corner. It was to Officer Yeo of the Saltmere Company, and it was four lines long, and it had taken her an hour.

*You asked twice for the Works' reader for the spring. I have read your fitter's six minutes against your harbour's table every night for six weeks and cannot make them agree, and I cannot do it from here. I will come in the first week of the third month, by the Works' transit, with Examiner Rusk. I will not promise to read your bank. M. Rowan, Grade Two.*

She had not told Kiva. She would tell her on the cart, perhaps, on Wednesday morning, or she would write it to Saltmere, where it would arrive after the girl had already had a week of standing in a strange country on one door. She had not decided. She had found, since the autumn, that she was a woman who decided a great many things in the middle of the night at a kitchen table and then did not tell anybody until it was too late for them to argue, and she did not altogether like it in herself, and had not stopped.

She had been afraid of it for six weeks. She was still afraid of it. She had one hand that read, and thirty-six cells on a harbour that did not lie still, and every night she had laid that hand flat on the oilcloth and felt the grain of the wood through it and thought, *that's what I'll lose.* She had sat across this table from a girl who had lost two fingers on a hill in the dark for a method that nobody else could do, and who had spent the whole of this month making it into a method that three people could, and Mara had watched her do it.

She had not been brave about it. She was going anyway. She thought that perhaps that was all that brave had ever meant.

The second letter was harder, and shorter.

She took the Order's letter out of the dresser drawer, where it had lain since the summer under the license. It was on thick grey paper, and it had six questions on it in a small upright hand, numbered, and a line at the bottom that she had read perhaps forty times: *We ask nothing of the girl. These are for whoever will answer them. The door is on the latch. C.T., for the house.*

She wrote the numbers of two of the questions at the top of a clean sheet. She wrote an answer under each.

They were not long. The first took three lines and the second took one. She did not read them over when she had done, because she knew what they said, and because she had found that if she read a true thing over she began to make it tidier, and she did not want to make these tidy.

Under them she wrote: *The other four are my niece's to answer, if she ever chooses, and not mine. She is coming to your door on Thursday for a hand and not for a year. I would ask you to take her at her word. She means it. M. Rowan.*

She sealed it. She put it beside Kiva's card on the dresser, and beside Yeo's letter, and stood for a moment looking at the three of them under the brass weight in the lamplight: a card in a square hand, and two envelopes in her own.

Then she turned the lamp out and went to bed, and did not sleep for some time.

---

The road went round the Sag.

It went round it for the whole of Wednesday and the first half of Thursday, on the carrier's cart, at the pace of a tired horse, with the great shallow bowl of the Sag always on their left hand, brown and grey and enormous, and the black line of Cowl Edge going away behind them on its far side until it was a thread and then nothing. It was raining by Wednesday noon, a thin cold rain off the hills, and the five of them and Aske sat under a tarpaulin on the cart's bed among their sheets and poles and the team's tin and the warrant's papers, and nobody said very much.

Perrin had the coil of tarred rope over his shoulder. He had not taken it off since the moor.

"Twenty," he said, at Wednesday's dusk, at the inn where the carrier stopped. He had no shed door to chalk it on. He said it to the window. "Twenty days. It's the twenty-fifth."

"Nineteen," said Jude. "Tomorrow's nineteen."

"I know what tomorrow is. I was saying what today is."

They came down off the Sag's south rim on Thursday a little after two, and the rain stopped, and the country opened out below them into a long green valley with a river in the bottom of it, broad and brown and slow, going away south toward a haze that Kiva did not know the name of; and on the far side of the river the road went on, south, and on the near side it came down to a long stone slip with a flat ferry-boat tied to it and a bell on a post.

Above the slip, on a bluff over the river, there was a house.

It was long and low and built of grey stone, with a slate roof and a great many chimneys, and a wall round it, and inside the wall the tops of bare trees; and it stood on its bluff with its back to the Sag and its face to the river and the south, quite plain, like a farm. There was smoke going up from three of its chimneys. There were no lights.

"That's it," said the carrier. "The ladies' house. I'll put you down at their gate, and I'll be at the slip at eight in the morning for the first ferry. I'll not come in. I never do. I've nothing against them," he added, "only it's very quiet."

---

A woman opened the gate before they had knocked.

She was perhaps forty, in a plain grey dress and a grey wool cap, with her hands folded at her waist; and Kiva knew her. She knew her at once, from across a reading room in the Library in the summer, a table and a green notebook and a box that had been disputed for seventy years. The sister had sat at the end of that table, with a pencil, and had written that the reader's lips moved, counting, from the third line to the last.

The sister knew her too. She looked at Kiva for a moment with a perfectly calm face, and inclined her head an inch.

"I sat by you in the summer," she said.

"I know."

"Come in. All of you. Your healer as well." She stood aside. "The Master has your card, and Mrs. Rowan's letter. She'll see you after supper. We eat at dusk."

"What time's dusk?" said Jude.

"When it's dark," said the sister.

She took them across a courtyard paved with flags and into the house by a low door, and along a stone passage with doors on either side, all shut, and into a long room with a fire at one end and a table down the middle and candles in iron holders on the walls, not yet lit. It was very clean and very plain. There was nothing on the walls at all.

Kiva stopped in the doorway.

She did not know at first what she had stopped for. Then she did. She was listening for a thing, as one listens for a clock in a strange house, and there was no clock. There was no tick anywhere. There was the fire, and the rain dripping off the eaves outside, and nothing else; and under the fire and the rain there was not the faint low hum that came up through the floor of every house in Meridian from the box in the cellar, and through every pavement from the drum under the street.

She put her left hand flat on the door's stone jamb, light, with the first two fingers on it.

*Nothing.*

She held it there. Pru came up beside her, without being asked, and took her right wrist, and looked at the window, since there was no clock to look at, and counted under her breath.

"Sixty-eight," said Pru. "You're quite calm."

"Then it's nothing," said Kiva. "There's nothing in this house keeping time."

She had had her fingers say *nothing* once before in her life, three days ago, on a stone on a moor, by a kiln, and the thing in the kiln had sung on the seventh reed. She took her hand off the jamb, and put it in her coat, and found the disk there. It was cold. She had known that it would be.

---

They ate at dusk, at the long table, with eleven sisters in grey who did not speak during the meal and did not seem to mind that their guests did; and after supper the sister from the gate came and stood at Kiva's elbow and said, "The Master."

Kiva went. She did not ask whether the others might come. Perrin got up as she did, without asking either, because it was under his name; and the sister looked at him and nodded, and the two of them went after her along the stone passage to a door at its end.

The room behind it was small, with a fire and two chairs and a window over the river, black now, with one light on the far bank where the ferryman lived. There was a woman standing at the window with her back to them.

Kiva felt her before she saw her face.

She had been near a Blaze for a month. She had stood on a cinder floor while a Hunt Marshal drew back a ward over a cage with one hand, as one draws a curtain, and she had felt it from the rail like a stove from a doorway. This was not like that. There was nothing to feel from a doorway. The air in the small room was quite still and quite ordinary, and the fire burned in the grate, and the woman at the window was only a tall thin woman in grey with her hands folded at her waist.

Kiva's doors lay down.

All three of them. She felt it happen, and could not have stopped it, and did not want to. The Ember under her breastbone, which was always awake, which had come gladly to every call since she was thirteen, went still. The hand door, close, under the skin of her palms, went still. The still door, heavy and slow under everything, which she had thought could not go any stiller, went still in a way that she had no word for, as a pond goes still when the wind drops. They did not shut. They had not been asked to shut. They lay down where they were, as dogs lie down in a room when somebody comes into it whom they do not know and do not need to watch.

She had stood in a frame in the Measure Hall in the spring and felt it go through her like a hand through a drawer, calling every door it found. This called nothing. It did not look. It was simply there, enormous, very close, in a small room with a fire; and it was the quietest thing that she had ever been near in her life, and the largest, and she could not have said why she knew that it was both.

The woman turned round.

She might have been fifty. Her hair was quite white and cut short under a plain cap, and her face was long and pale and very calm, with deep lines at the eyes; and her eyes were grey, and they came to rest on Kiva and stayed there, without searching, as a hand rests on a table.

"Miss Rowan," said Clemency Thrale. "Mr. Cade. Sit down. I'll stand, if you'll let me. I've been sitting with your aunt's letter for two days and I find I've no more use for chairs."

Kiva sat. Her knees had decided, as Anwen's had at a kitchen table.

"You came for a hand," said the Master.

"Yes."

"You wrote that it was not for a year. You wrote it in the third line, and you said that you'd want it said first, and you didn't say it first; you put the hand first." The grey eyes did not move. "I'd like to know why."

Kiva had not expected that. She thought about it, honestly, since she had been asked, in a room where she could not have done anything else.

"Because the hand's why we're here," she said. "The year's why we might not be. I'd sooner you knew what we wanted before you knew what we didn't."

"That's a fair order." Thrale's hands stayed folded at her waist. "I'll say what we are, once, and then I'll say what I'll do, and then you may say no to either. That's the rule of this house. It's the only rule we have that isn't about doors." She looked at the window, at the one light on the far bank. "We keep one door each, for life, and every other shut. We keep no clock and no cell and no pump. We've stepped nothing; we never have. There's plain ash in our cellar that was tinned before the Halden patent was sealed, and some that was tinned before there was a Halden, and it's never been near a thing that kept time. There are forty-one jars of it. There have been forty-one jars since I was made Master, and before that there were forty-four." She paused. "I can hold a great deal, Miss Rowan, and for a very long time. I can't make a jar more than it is. Nobody can. When you go down to the ferry in the morning, there'll be forty."

Kiva did not say anything.

"We don't sell," said the Master. "We've never sold. We've been offered a great deal this winter, by people I'd not have expected to write to us, and we've sold nothing, and we won't. But we've always made good a hand." She looked back at Kiva. "Your aunt's letter says that in the tenth month your team's tin of plain eased her reading hand. One dram and two-tenths, out of two, for a hand that was starved between the cells of a bank on a hill, so that she might read again."

"Yes."

"Then your tin eased a hand. We'll put back what it gave. One dram and two-tenths of plain ward-trace, from our cellar into your tin, so that your tin's what it was before it did a kindness." Thrale's voice did not change. "Not for your team. Not for a floor or a crossing or a bag or a gate. Not for a bank. Not to be sold, whatever you're offered for it; and you will be offered a great deal. For a hand. Whoever's hand is hurt next, in your four or out of it, if it's a hand that plain will ease. It'll be written so in our book and in yours, in your hand and your captain's, and if it's ever spent on anything else, I'd want to be told."

Perrin had gone very red.

"That's binding," he said. "Ma'am. On us. On the tin."

"It's binding. That's why I'm saying it to you, and not only to her. It's under your name, I'm told." The grey eyes went to him for the first time. "You may say no. I'd think none the worse. A tin that's bound is a tin you can't use when you'd most like to, and I'd not pretend otherwise."

Perrin looked at Kiva. Kiva looked back, and did not help him, as she had not helped him on a frozen yard; and she saw him see that she was not going to, and saw him decide.

"Yes," he said. "Under my name. For a hand."

"Miss Rowan?"

"Yes," said Kiva. "For a hand."

"Then it's done." Thrale did not move. "The sister will bring it to you in the morning, in your own tin, under our seal and yours, and you'll both sign. Tonight you'll sleep under this roof, and you'll keep our rule while you do, which is no door lit and no clock wound, and you'll find that it's very easy, since there's nothing here that wants either." She paused. "That's all that's asked. A night. Not a year."

Kiva sat in the chair with her doors lying down inside her like sleeping dogs, and found that she could not think of a single thing to say.

"Your aunt answered two of my six questions," said the Master. "She answered them shortly, and I think truly. She wrote that the other four were yours, if you ever chose." The grey eyes rested on her, quite still. "I'll not ask them. Not tonight, and not by letter, and not if you come back. If you ever want them, the latch is on your side of the door. It always was."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I've only said what's so." For the first time something moved in that long pale face, at the corner of the mouth, and went again. "I'll say one thing more, since you've a long road and I've a short memory for what I've decided not to say. Your sister at the gate wrote, in the summer, that you counted when you read. I've thought about it since. I've not decided what I think. I'd not have you go south believing that I'd decided."

"Nor have I," said Kiva.

"No," said Clemency Thrale. "I didn't suppose that you had."

She turned back to the window.

"I've not been past that gate in eleven years," she said, to the glass. "The Master keeps the house. When she goes out, the house has no Master, and it's never been without one long. I've gone out twice in my life." She was quiet a moment. "I'd like to see the sea again before I'm very much older. I don't expect to. Good night, Miss Rowan. Mr. Cade."

---

Kiva slept that night in a narrow white room off the stone passage, under a grey blanket, with Pru in the other bed and the rain on the window; and she slept as she had not slept since she was small, without waking once, without dreaming of anything at all.

In the morning the sister brought the tin.

It was Seven's own tin, which Anwen had carried down from Meridian in the team's box with the wax still on it, and the sister had taken it from Anwen at the door last night and had it all night in the house. It had a second seal now beside the first, grey wax, with a mark on it like two hands laid flat side by side; and inside it, the sister said, was what had been put back.

She held a comb over it, in the courtyard, in the grey light, before anybody signed. She did it without being asked.

The third reed sang.

The seventh did not.

It did not sing at all. It was not that it sang faint. It was still, a thin bar of metal in a row of thin bars of metal, with the third ringing beside it like a spoon on a glass; and Kiva stood on the wet flags with her hands at her sides and listened to it not sing, and it was the first time since the thaw that she had heard a comb held over ash and heard nothing on the seventh but nothing.

Aske had come out behind them with his bag. He stood beside her and listened as well, and he did not say anything, and she saw that his eyes had gone bright behind his spectacles, and that he was not going to remark on it.

"*Sealed, medical, W, plain,*" said Jude, at the table by the door, writing in the team book. "*Eight-tenths, off a husk on the Sag in the fourth month, 2680. One and two-tenths, given at the house of the Even Hand on Friday the 27th of the second month, to make good what eased M. Rowan's hand. Two drams.*" He stopped writing. "*For a hand. Not for a bank, a floor, a crossing or a sale.* I've put that under both lines. I'd want it under both."

"Both," said Perrin, and signed, under his name, and gave Kiva the pen.

She signed. *K. Rowan, catcher, T7. For a hand.*

The sister signed the Order's book, at the same table, with the same pen, and blotted it, and shut it; and then she looked at Kiva across the table, quite calmly, for a long moment, as she had looked at her across a reading room.

"Your lips didn't move," she said. "Last night, at supper. I watched."

"There was nothing to read."

"No," said the sister. "I know."

---

The ferry went at eight.

It was a flat broad boat with a rail round it, worked by a cable and a windlass, and it took the carrier's cart and his horse and the six of them and their sheets and poles and the team's box, with room over; and the ferryman wound the windlass, slowly, and the bank with the slip and the grey house on its bluff went away behind them into the morning, and the brown water went by under the boat's flat bottom with a sound like somebody shushing a child.

Kiva was hot by the middle of the river.

She did not notice it at first. She was standing at the rail with her hands on it, looking south, down the long valley toward the haze where the river went, and the coast; and she thought that it was the sun, which had come out over the Sag behind them and was warm on her back for the first time in weeks. Then she found that her hands on the rail were hot, both of them, the eight fingers that reported and the two that did not, and that the wind off the water, which was cold, was not cooling them.

She took them off the rail. She put the left one inside her coat against her ribs, where the disk was, and the disk was cold, and her ribs were not.

"Rowan," said Pru, beside her. "You've gone red."

"It's the sun."

"It's not the sun. You've gone red from the collar." Pru took her wrist without asking. She looked at the far bank, since there was no clock, and counted under her breath; and Kiva saw her face change.

"A hundred and twelve," said Pru. "You're not frightened. You're not *doing* anything." She did not let go. "Aske."

Aske came along the rail. He took the other wrist and held it for a minute by his watch, with his face quite still, and then he put the back of his hand against her cheek, as her aunt used to when she was small.

"Fever," he said. "Sit down. Here, on the box. Talley. Your coat."

She sat down on the team's box, against the rail, in Jude's coat. The far bank came nearer, very slowly, on the cable. She heard Perrin ask whether it was the hand, and Aske say that he did not think it was the hand; and Anwen say something that she could not hear; and the windlass creak, round and round, slow, and the water shush under the flat bottom of the boat.

Then the roof went.

---

It was not a roof. It was not the sky over a river valley in the second month, grey and white with the sun coming through it. It was nothing.

She had known it would be. She knew it as she knew the order of her own doors. It came as it had come when she was thirteen and on Classification Day, all at once and from every side, and there was nothing over her at all; and the nothing was not empty, and it was not dark. It was enormous. It was paying attention.

There were roads. There were lights on them, going along them, a great many, unlike, from every side, small and far off and moving; and she was above them, as she always was, looking down.

She was not so high as she had been.

She did not know how she knew it. There was nothing to measure it by. But the lights on the roads were nearer than they had been, by a little; she could see that some were bright and some were dull and some were going out, and that some had gone white; and she had not been near enough to see that, before, she thought. She did not know what it meant. She did not want to know what it meant. She was lower, and the nothing over her was paying attention, and she could not have said to what.

She kept her mouth shut.

She found that she was doing it. She had a hand on her wrist and a voice somewhere counting, and something in her that had learned in a van, at a hem, with a cloth under her hands and two words owed and nothing else, had shut her teeth. She did not say anything. She did not say what she saw. She held it behind her teeth, as she had held *Hobday's proud* behind her teeth on a road in the frost, because there was nobody on that boat whose word it was, and it was not her place to say it.

And then there was a bank, and a boat bumping against a slip, and a horse being led off up a ramp, and the sky was a sky again, grey and white, with the sun coming through it.

---

She was on the river by noon, on a long low barge going south with the current, under a canvas awning, on a straw mattress that Aske had bought off the bargeman for a shilling. The fever stayed all that day and all that night, and broke on Saturday at about four in the morning, in a long sweat that soaked the mattress through.

Aske counted it. He sat beside her on an upturned bucket with his watch in his hand and took her wrist every hour, and wrote what he found on her card in pencil, and did not ask her anything at all.

At about two on Saturday morning, when it was worst, he said, without looking up from his watch, "Have you had one of these before?"

"Yes," said Kiva.

He wrote it.

"I'll not ask what," he said. "I'll count."

Pru held her other wrist through most of the night, on her knees on the barge's boards, and did not ask her anything either. Once, when the fever was high and Kiva's teeth were shut so hard that her jaw ached, Pru said very quietly, "You can say it, if you want. Whatever it is. I'll not tell anybody." And Kiva shook her head, once, on the wet straw; and Pru said, "All right," and went on counting, and did not ask again.

By Saturday noon she could sit up. By Saturday evening she could eat. The barge went on down the river, slow, between green banks that she did not know, with willows on them and cattle standing in the shallows and here and there a village with a church; and the river got wider, and browner, and slower, and the air began to smell of something that she had never smelled before and could not put a name to, flat and cold and clean, coming up from the south on the wind.

"Salt," said Jude.

He was sitting at the barge's bow with his strapped arm across his knees, looking south. He had been sitting there since noon.

"It's salt. The bargeman says. It comes up the river on a south wind, thirty miles." He did not turn round. "I'd want it said that I've never smelled it."

She wrote her book that evening, under the awning, by the bargeman's lantern, sitting up on the dried straw with Jude's coat round her shoulders.

It went to Orla on Sundays. It would go to Orla at Saltmere, at the gate, on a crate, on the first Sunday of the third month. It had lain on the kitchen table every night for Mara for a spring and a summer and an autumn and a winter, and it had never once had a page torn out of it, and it had never once had in it what she had seen when there was no roof.

She wrote on the left, where the things went that she had seen.

*Friday the 27th. The ferry below the house of the Even Hand. Fever, from the middle of the river. A hundred and twelve, by Finch. Aske counted it all night. Broke Saturday at four. I can sit up.*

*Cold, the disk. Before and after. I looked.*

On the right, where the reasons went, she wrote nothing for a long time.

Then she wrote one line, small.

*I kept my mouth shut. I'd learned how.*

She shut the book, and laid it on the straw beside her, under her hand. The barge went on down the river in the dark, with the lantern swinging at its bow and the salt coming up on the wind, and somewhere ahead of it, three days off, a gate that would open on Tuesday; and in the team's box under the awning, in a tin with two seals on it, there were two drams of plain ward-trace that were for a hand and for nothing else.

Perrin was asleep against the box, with a coil of tarred rope over his shoulder.

Before he slept, he had looked at the bank going by in the dark for a long time, and said, to nobody, "Sixteen."

It was the twenty-eighth. Nobody had told him to count it. He had counted it anyway.
