# Chapter 41 — Kiva: A Full Bucket

At a quarter to seven on Monday morning Kiva laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the iron latch of the loft's north window, because it was the nearest thing and she could not yet stand up for long enough to reach anything else. She meant to keep the morning's quarter to seven from now on; Sunday evening's had been Pru's, written for her.

*It keeps time.*

It came up through the latch from the frame, and through the frame from the wall, and through the wall from the chandlery and the quay and the black water under the quay. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster. It was the same answer it had given every day since Hart had taught her to ask, and she found she was glad of it, the way a person is glad to find the stair where she left it in the dark.

She wrote it in her book with her left hand, badly. *Monday the 21st. A quarter to seven. The north window latch. Yes.*

Her body felt as though somebody had wrung it out and hung it over a rail. The blanket under her had dried stiff in the night. Her mouth tasted of copper and her knees did not belong to her yet. That was the fever's bill, and she knew it as well as she knew Aske's handwriting: a day like this one after every fever, with the world a little too bright and too far away, and then it would be over and she would be hungry.

At the south end of the loft Kellan was sitting on the edge of his bed in his shirt with the white card in his hand.

He had been sitting like that at midnight. She did not think he had moved much since. Halden License's beds were round him in a row, and Pettit was asleep on his back with his mouth open, and Yarrow had one arm over his face. Kellan was the only one awake at that end. He was holding the card by its edges as he held a ruled sheet he did not want to smudge.

She got up. It took two tries. She went down the loft between the beds with one hand on the iron bedsteads, and Old Four's stove ticked as she passed it, and Selka Dray, who was lying with her eyes open, watched her go and said nothing.

Kellan looked up when she sat down on Yarrow's trunk opposite him.

"You're a bad colour," he said.

"I'm told."

"Talley said a hundred and four." He turned the card over once in his fingers and back. "Aske said it to the stove, at about one, as though the stove had asked. Then he went to sleep sitting up."

"It broke at eleven." She looked at the card. It was thick and white, the size of a man's palm, with a small embossed mark in the corner that she had seen on the Counting House's door and on a glass case on a polished table. "You don't have to tell me."

"I know I don't." He did not move for a moment. Then he held it out to her, the way he had once held out a sheet of figures across a table at home, without comment, so that she could read it for herself.

The hand was large and upright and old.

*Mr. Renn. On Wednesday at the dawn slack, if Mrs. Rowan and the keeper will have it, I mean to hold the harbour's pumps on my own pattern while the cells are dark, so that the low town is not wet for longer than it must be. I should like a clerk beside my chair who will tell me the time and the count aloud, and will not tell me what I'd like to hear. This is not the foundation's request. It is mine. If you say no, say it to the man who brings this, and I shall think none the worse of you. A. Halden.*

Kiva read it twice and gave it back.

"He's offered the pumps," she said.

"He wrote to the keeper last night. A man went along the quay at ten with the letter and came back for this one at eleven." Kellan laid the card face down on his knee. "Six pumps. He'd hold them from the time the cells are quenched to the time the new cans are salted. Mrs. Rowan's sheet says that's six minutes, if nothing goes wrong. Six pumps for six minutes at fifty-seven strokes a minute is two thousand and fifty-two strokes." He looked at the back of the card. "He wants somebody to count them."

"Will you?"

"I've been sitting here working out whether I'd be counting for him or for the low town," said Kellan. "It comes out the same number both ways. That's what I can't get past." He was quiet. "He's a hundred and three. He's had his hand on that glass since 2611. That's seventy years, near enough, and something over twenty-five thousand days, and on every one of them he's had somebody beside him to tell him what he wanted to hear. Counsel. The clerks. His own house." He turned the card over again, face up. "He's asked for one morning of somebody who won't. I think that's the most honest sum anybody in that house has written in my lifetime."

"His pattern's the Step," said Kiva.

"I know it is. So will your aunt. She'll have said so before the letter was finished being read." Kellan put the card in his shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket over it. "That's her sum. Mine's the one on the card." He looked at her properly then, with his narrow careful face. "I'll go. I'll stand where he puts me. I'll tell him the count, and the time, and if he does something stupid I'll tell him that too, in front of his counsel." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's the first job I've ever been offered that's exactly what I'm for."

Behind her, Dray had turned her head on her pillow.

"Renn," she said, quite flatly, from three beds off. "If you're going down there, take a watch that isn't his."

Kellan looked past Kiva at her.

"I've my mother's," he said. "It loses four seconds a day and I know which four."

Dray looked at the ceiling again. "Good," she said.

---

Aske had the hand at a quarter past seven, at the north window, with the light coming in grey off the harbour and his watch open on the sill.

He held it for a long time, palm down and then palm up, and pressed the backs of the fingers, and the heel, and the place on the inside of the wrist where the cold had always begun.

"Warm," he said. "Eight mornings." He wrote it. Then he put two fingers on the side of her neck instead, under the jaw, and counted against his watch, and wrote that too. "Ninety. You're a day behind yourself and you'll be a day behind yourself till tomorrow. Eat whatever Quiller puts in front of you and then eat it again."

"I'm going to put my name down this morning," said Kiva. "At the Board's tent. For Wednesday."

He did not look up from the card.

"I know you are. You kept the crossing for it. You told me at this window on Thursday." He capped his pen. "I said the price twice last week, for the seventh. You've heard it. Past the elbow, five nights, and I'll not be surprised by six. I'll not say it a third time, because nothing's moved it."

"No."

"Here's the thing I haven't said." He took his spectacles off and folded them, which he did not often do, and held them in his hand. "Nobody's asked me, and I'd rather say it now than at six on Wednesday with the water running. If you make your crossing, and something goes wrong after it, and you make another in the same hour, against my card."

He did not raise his voice. "I don't know what it costs. Nobody does; it's never been done. I'll tell you what I think. I think the hand goes. Not cold up the arm, the way it's gone after every one of these. Gone. The hand itself, to the wrist, the way your two fingers went and didn't come back, except I think the rest of it comes back, in weeks and not in nights. Three, if I'm guessing well. And then I think your arm's told me something about itself it can't untell, and I'll not sign one in seven after that. A fortnight between, for the rest of the year at the least."

Kiva sat very still on the edge of the bed.

"That's a guess," said Aske. "It's an honest one. It's the price as near as I can say it. I'll say it again on Wednesday, at the time, if it comes to that, because I'll not have you do it on a guess you heard two days before with a fever in you." He held up one finger. "And a rule, which isn't a price. If you've crossed, and shut, and you mean to light anything again after, you're dark for ten counts first. Nothing open. I'll not have a crossing's gap and a fresh lighting run together on a reader's tape where nobody can tell one from the other, least of all your arm." He put his spectacles back on. "If it doesn't come to that, I'll have said it twice for nothing, and you know what I think about that."

"Twice for nothing's cheaper," said Kiva.

"Than once too late." He wrote one more line on the card, and turned it, because she had asked nobody to keep anything from her any more. *Price of a second in the hour said, Monday, before. A guess. The hand to the wrist; weeks; then one in fourteen.* "There. It's written before it's wanted. That's all a card's for."

---

The Board's tent at the timber pond had its flaps tied back at nine, and there was a table across its mouth with the warrant on it, and a queue.

It was not a long queue. That was the first thing Kiva noticed, coming along the boards between Anwen and Jude with her legs not yet quite her own. On Saturday night ninety-six people had stood in rows on the Graving's floor while the Measure was read. This morning there were perhaps forty in the queue, and they were not standing as they had stood on the floor, in fours, by their colours. They were standing as people stand outside a door they have not been through before.

The warrant was a single sheet in the Clerk's own hand. It lay under a brass weight. Kiva read it over Anwen's shoulder while they waited.

*A Barrel response at the harbour pump house, Saltmere, at the dawn slack on Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, 2681, on the keeper's leave. Under the Saltmere Company's officers. Any four on the national license roll may stand in it, by name, on the Board's warrant and the Board's ash, from Monday. Those who stand will stand where the officer puts them and will close nothing. No four is obliged. A four that does not stand loses nothing by it, and the Board will write that it did not, so that nobody may say otherwise.*

Under it there were already names. The Ropewalk School, first four, in Carrow's round firm hand: *Carrow. Lyle. Tolver. Treen.* Old Four, under Brask's: four names. Halden License, under Pettit's slow careful one: *Pettit. Yarrow. Hesketh. Renn, by the Proprietor's leave, at the pump house; not on the mud.* Holloway's second four had signed at eight, the clerk said, before anybody else was up. All four of them.

"Holloway," said Jude, reading it.

"The lamp-man's town," said Anwen.

Westerley's first four had come and gone. They had not signed. Their captain had written a line under the warrant instead, in pencil, and initialled it: *Westerley first: not standing. We've a keeper at home who'd want us there, and we'll go to him.* Holloway's first four had sent their captain at eight with a note that said only *Not standing. With respect.* The Board's clerk at the table had copied both lines into the ledger, and under each had written, in a small neat hand: *Loses nothing by it.*

Nobody in the queue looked at those lines for long. Kiva saw that too, and was glad.

When Seven came to the table, Perrin signed first, because he was the captain. He wrote *Cade, P., captain,* and then stopped with the pen over the paper and looked at it for a moment.

"I can't stand on the mud," he said to the clerk at the table. "My healer's card. No plant, no lift. Till the 30th."

"Then write what you'll do," said the clerk, not unkindly.

Perrin wrote: *To declare for the four, and keep the brake. Not on the mud.* He handed the pen to Anwen. She signed without saying anything, and so did Jude, who steadied the sheet with his left hand while he wrote *Talley,* because Aske had said Monday, and it was Monday. Pru went scarlet at the table, for no reason anybody could see, and wrote *Finch* very small.

Kiva signed last.

She wrote *Rowan, K., catcher,* and then, because it was true and because she had put a thing on a table under her own name once already this week and found that she could, she wrote under it: *Caller, if the officer will have me. One crossing kept, as certified.*

The clerk read it and blotted it.

"The officer's at the pump house at half past ten," he said. "With the keeper and the Works' reader and the Marshal. Your captain's asked for. So are you."

Behind them in the queue somebody had come along the boards without a stick.

Selka Dray signed under Twenty-Eight's name, after Marten and Shapter and Bannock. She wrote her own name, standing, with her weight on the healed leg, and she did not write anything after it, because Dray never had. Then she turned round and found Kiva still beside the table.

"Wednesday," said Dray.

"Yes."

"I'm on my feet." Dray's face was a shut door, as it always was. "That's all I'm here to say. I've said it."

She went away along the boards toward the loft. Carrow, who had been watching from the tent pole with her arms folded, watched her go, and then looked at Kiva, and lifted one hand very slightly, palm out, the way Brask had once lifted a hand to say that a call had been fair.

---

They did not meet in the keeper's room. There were too many of them for it. Peverell had set a trestle table on the floor of the pump hall instead, between the third pump and the fourth, under the two long galleries where the thirty-six cells stood in their tall glass cases with their lights turning together.

It was the loudest room Kiva had ever sat down in. The six iron pumps went up and down on either side of the table, slow and enormous, each one as tall as a cart standing on end, and the whole floor shook with them, four and four. Every voice at the table had to be lifted a little to be heard over the stroke. It was the strangest place in the world, Kiva thought, to make a plan for stopping a thing: in the middle of it, with your feet on it.

Yeo sat at the head, because it was her warrant. Mara sat at her right with her reading hand flat on a sheaf of fault paper. Peverell sat at her left in her apron with her spectacles on their cord. Hart stood behind the keeper's chair with his good hand on its back and his bad one in his jacket. Rusk had a stool at the far end, with his thumbs in his waistcoat and his eyes half shut. Coombe sat on a crate with her rope in her lap. Rhea Sorn stood by the fifth pump in her slate blue with her own slate on her belt, because the officer had asked Graywater for an officer and Graywater had sent her. Noa had his ledger.

And beside Noa, very upright on a borrowed stool, sat a sister of the Order of the Even Hand in plain grey, with her hands in her sleeves: the one who had stood at the Order's gate in the second month and looked at Kiva as though she were a parcel that had come to the wrong house.

The captains sat along one side. Carrow, Pettit, Brask, Marten, a tall freckled girl from Holloway's second four called Bolitho, who looked at the pumps as though she had never seen one so big, and Perrin, at the end, with his hand pressed to his side. Kiva sat beside Perrin. She had been asked for.

"I'll say what it is first," said Yeo, "and then the keeper will say what her house will do, and then the Works' reader will say what the bank needs, and then Mr. Hart will tell us when the water's still. And then we'll find out what we've got wrong." She looked round the table with her flat brisk face. "There'll be something. There always is. I'd rather find it on Monday."

"Say it so a child could say it back to you," said Coombe, from her crate. "Before you say anything else. If you can't, you don't know it yet."

Yeo looked at Mara.

Mara did not look at anybody. She looked at her own hand on the fault paper, as she looked at a fault report before she read it across a counter.

"It's a bucket with no bottom," she said, "that holds water anyway, because the water's keeping the town's time." Her dry voice went across the table under the pumps' stroke without having to be lifted. "If it spills in the harbour, the time goes back into the harbour, and the bank can't come out. If we cut it where it stands, there's a Barrel of open ash in the water the pumps draw from, and that can cook a bank. So we pick it up full, and we carry it out of the harbour without spilling it, and we empty it in the sink." She lifted her eyes. "The sink's the Graving. Then we take the bank out, while the basin's still."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The pumps went up and down.

"I could say that back," said Perrin. "What I'd like to know is what the bucket weighs."

"So would I," said Brask. He was the big mild captain of Old Four, who read things the way other people read the labels on jars, and he had been looking up at the galleries while Mara spoke, at the cells. "It's not a bucket, though. I've watched it twice from the wall. It's a cask. It's got staves up the outside, at the springs, grey ones, and a chime round the top, and no head and no bottom." He scratched his cheek. "The water's its head. And the head's keeping time."

Hart, behind the keeper's chair, looked at Brask for a long moment.

"That's better than anything in my book," he said. "Write it down, somebody."

Noa wrote it down.

Then they went round the table, and it took an hour and a half, and Kiva would remember afterward that almost all of it was clocks.

Hart had the first. He had his book of slacks open on the table under his good hand, and his watch on the inside of his wrist, and he spoke as he spoke to fitters, plainly, one figure at a time. The spring dawn slack had held its hour for a fortnight while the sea gate's table walked. It would come on Wednesday at nine minutes past six by the pump house clock, if the sealed page was right, and it would last about twenty minutes. Inside that twenty minutes the basin would be still, every part of it, except the one thing standing in it. When the slack ended, his hand would ache, and the flood would come in under the sea gate, and the thing would lie down into the mud if it was still there to lie down.

"And give the count back," said Mara. "That's the winter. Six cells went back in, on the second day, at the end of the slack. I've had it wrong for four months. It wasn't the water moving. It was that, lying down, and giving back what it held." She did not look at Peverell's fitters' names on her fault paper. "If it lies down in the basin while my thirty-six are filling, it's the winter again."

"So it comes off first," said Yeo.

"So it comes off first."

Peverell had the second clock. Her pumps would run on the bank as they always ran, until the thing was out of the basin and in the Graving and the Graving was shut. Then, on Mrs. Rowan's word, the thirty-six cells would be quenched together, and the pumps would lose their drive. The old cans would be drawn. The fresh cans would be set. Each would be salted with a jar of plain and given a card with its own count, not two alike in the house. Then she would start each pump herself, one at a time, on its own six cells.

"Six," said Rusk, from the end of the table, without opening his eyes.

Peverell turned her head.

"You said eight in the Salt Hall, Mrs. Peverell," said Rusk. "On Friday. *Each on its own eight cells.* I wrote it in my book because it was wrong. Thirty-six cells over six pumps is six. Eight's the Ferren figure. Twenty-four cells, three pumps."

Peverell looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment. Then she took her pencil out of her apron and drew a line through something on her own sheet and wrote above it.

"I read it off the Ferren sheet," she said. "I said it in front of the Board. I'll say it was wrong in front of the Board on Wednesday, in seven columns." She looked at Rusk. "Thank you, Mr. Rusk. You might have opened your eyes."

"I'd not have heard it better," said Rusk.

It was a small thing. Kiva found that it was the thing she liked best in the whole morning. A keeper had read a number off a sheet that had been written for a different house, and said it in a hall, and a man who did not believe anything that had not happened had written it down because it was wrong, and on Monday it was six. It was exactly what Mara had meant by *what was got wrong first.*

Then the keeper took a letter out of her apron and laid it on the table.

"From the Long Quay," she said. "Last night at ten. It's to me, because it's my house, and to Mrs. Rowan, because it's her method. I'll read it, because the officer should hear it and so should the captains."

She read it. It was the Proprietor's offer, as Kellan had said it in the loft: the six pumps held on his own pattern from the quench to the salt, so that the low town was wet for minutes and not for a quarter of an hour. He would be carried to the outfall walk. He would keep his hand where he always kept it. He would let go on Mrs. Rowan's word, and not before, and not after.

"His pattern's the Step," said Mara.

She said it at once, as Kiva had known she would.

"It is," said Peverell. "He says so, three lines down. *Mrs. Rowan will say that my pattern is the Step, and she will be right.*" Peverell turned the letter round on the table so that Mara could read the line. "He says that while the cells are dark and the old cans coming out, there's nothing in the house for his count to get into. He says that when you say *salt*, he'll let go, and the pumps will stand for as long as it takes your thirty-six to fill and count, and then I'll start them on their own. Four or five minutes standing, he reckons, and not eleven. And not fourteen."

"Fourteen," said Yeo.

"The carry's three minutes, if it goes," said Peverell. "Maybe more. My pumps run through the carry. Then the quench, the drawing, the setting. If I'd no help, from the quench to the last pump started is near fourteen minutes. That's two foot in Ropery Row on a spring, Mrs. Yeo, not one. That's the cellars to the stair-head." She did not look at anybody. "Penhale's mother's in Ropery Row."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The pumps went up and down.

"Then he'll have to let go," said Mara, "when I say. Not a stroke after." She put her reading hand flat on the letter. "I'll take it. On that condition, written, in his hand and mine. If he holds one stroke into the salt, the cans hear it and the cells go back in, and it's all for nothing, and the low town's dry for nothing."

"The Works would like to see it done," said Rusk, "before it's done."

Mara turned her head.

"I don't believe anything that hasn't happened," said Rusk mildly. "You know that. Ask him to come and hold one pump for one minute tomorrow, with its cells lit under it, and let go of it on your word, in front of the keeper and me. If he does it, I'll believe he can do it on Wednesday. If he won't come, I'll believe that too."

"He'll come," said Peverell. "He said he'd come Tuesday if it was wanted. It's in the letter. Line nine."

"Then it's wanted," said Rusk, and shut his eyes again.

The sister of the Order had not moved while they talked about the pumps. When they turned to the Graving, she took her hands out of her sleeves.

"The Master will stand across the mouth," she said. Her voice was low and very clear, the way Thrale's was, and it did not need lifting either. "She's said so. When the thing's inside and the gate can't yet be floated back, she'll hold a ward across the dock mouth, and nothing that keeps time will come out past her, and nothing will go in. She can't hold it and move. She can't hold it long. She can be in this town an hour at a time." She looked along the table. "And she'll lay down every door within forty paces of her, without meaning to, as she always has. You'll want to know that before you put anybody's door near her."

"Forty," said Coombe.

"On the quay, on Saturday, I paced it myself after." The sister turned her eyes to Kiva for the first time. "Miss Rowan's doors lay down at forty. In our house they lay down at less. The Master says that in our house she'd nothing to hold against."

"And here she'll have the whole harbour," said Kiva.

The sister looked at her, and did not say yes or no.

"Then the plant's past forty," said Yeo. She drew it on the table with her finger, in the dust from the pumps, and they all leaned in to see. The basin, with the pump house at its head. The ring, ten paces out from the outfalls. The Graving lying along the west side, with its mouth opening onto the basin at the south-west corner, fifty paces from the ring across the mud.

"The gate's floated out tomorrow on the noon high water. It can't go back in till there's a flood under it to float it, an hour after the slack, maybe more. The Master waits back on the outer quay till the last plant's called, well clear of the sill. Then she holds the mouth till the gate's back. The thing goes in past where she'll stand, and down the dock floor, and it's planted forty-five paces in, where her stillness can't reach whoever's planting it. And it's cut there, on an officer's slate." She looked up at Rhea. "Sorn, it's yours, the cut. If you'll have it."

"I'll have it," said Rhea.

"Fifty paces across the mud," said Brask. "And forty-five in. Carrying a cask full of water with no bottom. Who carries it?"

They all looked at Kiva.

She had known they would. She had thought about it since a ferry and a fever and a creek with a man's boot sticking out from under a fold, and she had known since Thursday what she would say, and she found now that saying it at a table in the middle of six iron pumps was not the same as knowing it on a bench.

"Nobody carries it far," she said. "That's the whole of it. Nobody holds it long enough for it to fold on them." She put her left hand on the table, flat, the way Anwen put her hands on a card. "It's the pass. Catch, carry, plant. Three people with unlike doors, and somebody to call the pass who's been inside both gaps. We did it on a yard in the second month. We did it at Mallow Creek with a sleeve ten paces long, and it held. This is bigger. So it's not three people. It's three planks: like doors on one load, not two still doors on a patch of ground. Wards together for the catch. Embers together for the carry. Still doors together for the plant."

She looked at Yeo's lines in the dust. "And it's not one pass. A carry can't go the whole way with a thing like that on its fists. On one pair of fists it'll start to unfold by twenty paces. On two, with a count, by fifty, maybe. So it's three passes, and none of them longer than fifty. Off the water to a mark on the mud. From the mud to the sill. From the sill to the plant, past the Master's forty."

"Three in a row," said Coombe.

"Three in a row. I call them. I test every mark first, with my fingers, before anything's planted on it. If my fingers say a mark keeps time, it's not a mark." Kiva kept her voice level. "And I carry an Ember lit, declared, at every carry, behind the plank, as the reserve. If a plant fails, and the load's on nobody, I take it on my Ember, and I cross to still, and I plant it myself. Ember to still. It's on my certificate. One crossing. I've kept it for this."

"And if two fail?" said Rhea.

Kiva looked at her across the pump hall.

"Then I've a healer who said the price this morning," she said, "before anybody asked him. And I'll hear it again before I do anything about it."

Rhea held her eyes for a moment. Then she nodded, once, and wrote something on her slate.

"Students close nothing," said Coombe. "Say who closes the tear."

"Mine," said Yeo. "When the thing's lifted off it, there'll be whatever it's been standing on, open in the mud. Ivey's crew closes it. Students on the basin wall to cut whatever comes up out of it, on my slate, and not one stroke without my word." She looked at Carrow. "That's the wall, Miss Carrow. You asked for it."

"I did," said Carrow.

"And who has your far edge?" said Coombe.

She said it to the whole table. She had said it on a causeway at the Hunt to every catcher in the draw, and the printed page with Penhale's name at its head was in every officer's pocket in Saltmere now; Kiva could see the corner of Yeo's copy at the top of her coat.

There was a short silence.

"Every plank names it before the pass," said Kiva. "Out loud. Who's watching the far side of the load while the rest of them watch the near side. I'll not call a pass till I've heard it." She found she was looking at Rhea again. "A second's place. The far edge is the second's."

"Then it's written," said Yeo, and Noa wrote it.

They went on. They went through the planks name by name, who had which door and who had rehearsed with whom, and Kiva saw, as the names went down on Noa's sheet, that most of them had never stood in a pass in their lives and that some of them had never stood beside each other on any floor. The Ropewalk's four had beaten Seven twice. Old Four had protested Seven's Carry. Twenty-Eight's blade had beaten Seven's catcher in a lane on Saturday, two hours before the Measure was read. Halden License had beaten nearly everybody, and was standing without its blade. And they were going to put their hands under one thing together at nine minutes past six on Wednesday and let go of it on a word.

"It can't be done by strangers," said Perrin. He said it quietly, to the table, with his hand pressed to his side. "It's on Rowan's sheet, under the limits."

"Then they'd better stop being strangers," said Coombe. "By Wednesday." She stood up off her crate with her rope in her hands. "The Graving floor at two. All of them. Bring whatever's nearest to a cask full of water that you can lift with three wards. I'll bring the rope."

---

What was nearest to a cask full of water, it turned out, was a cask full of water.

The Company's cooper sent it down from the yard behind the Long Quay on a dray: a brewer's tun with eight iron hoops, as wide across as a big man is tall, standing on end with no head in it. They lowered it down the Graving's ramp on rollers and stood it on the stone of the dock floor at the seaward end, on the chalk where the lane pen had been, and the Company's men filled it from the fire main with a canvas hose until the water stood in it level with the top hoop. Then they nailed a board across its bottom from the outside, because a tun with no head and no bottom would not have held a cupful.

"It's not right," said Brask, looking at it. "It's got a bottom."

"It's got weight," said Coombe. "That's what it's got. It'll not fold to the nearest thing, and it'll not keep time, and it'll not frost your hands. It'll be heavy and it'll slop. That's all I'm asking of it."

They began at a quarter past two, with the tiers empty above them and the iron and glass roof letting the grey light down on the wet stone, and for the first hour it went very badly.

It went badly in a way Kiva had not foreseen, and it took her most of that hour to see why.

The catch was not the trouble. Four wards came up together under the tun's foot, Anwen's and Edda's and Hesketh's and Shapter's, silver and low, and lifted it an inch off the stone, and held. That was good. The carry was not the trouble either. Jude and Gower stood behind the wards with their edges lit on their right fists, and when the word came they took the weight on the bars and walked it, slow and steady, ten paces down the floor, with the water slopping at the top hoop. And the plant was not the trouble. Brask and Tolver were still doors, and they stood square on the chalk with their hands open, and when the word came their doors went down into the stone and the tun came down onto them and stood.

The trouble was the word.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva, the first time, short, with the swallow, the way she had said it at Mallow Creek.

Anwen's wards opened. Edda's opened. Hesketh's opened, half a count late. And Shapter's did not open at all. It held on under the tun's foot, alone, a quarter count and then a half, and the tun tipped on it, and slopped a bucket of the Company's water over Shapter's boots and Jude's, and Gower swore.

"Again," said Coombe, from her step on the bottom tier.

They did it again. This time Shapter opened on the word and Hesketh held. The third time, Hesketh and Shapter both opened and Edda held, because she had been watching the other two and not listening.

On the next pass it was the carry. Treen and Pru and Marten and Yarrow, the catchers' plank, caught the tun from Brask and Tolver's plant, and Kiva called the pass to the Pascoes, who were brother and sister from Holloway, both Embers, both tall and fair and both very silent. And Treen held. Treen held on under the tun a full count after the word, with her ward low at her hip and her quick dark face set, and the Pascoes' edges were under it as well, and for a full count the tun belonged to two planks at once. It did not slop. It went perfectly steady. Treen looked up, pleased.

"That's a stack," said Kiva. "Between people. It's two planks holding one thing, agreeing. It's the thing we can't do."

"It held," said Treen.

"It held because it's a tun with a board nailed under it." Kiva heard her own voice and made it plainer. "The thing in the basin goes to the nearest thing. If two of you have it at once, it goes to whichever's nearer, and that's the one it folds on. That's Penhale. That's the whole of Coombe's page." She did not look at Coombe. "The pass only works because for a quarter of a count nobody has it at all. There's nobody for it to go to."

Treen did not answer. But she had gone, Kiva saw, a little pale under the wind-brown.

"She's laid," said Carrow, from behind the plank, quietly. She had come down off the wall's place where Coombe had put her and stood with her arms folded, watching. "She's not laid now, she's one door. But she was laid for four years, and so was I, and so were Lyle and Tolver. That's what laid is. You hold till the other agrees. You never let go of your half till the other half has it." She looked at Kiva. "You're asking us to let go first. Into nothing."

"Yes."

"Every one of us has been taught since we were twelve that that's how a load gets dropped."

"I know." Kiva had not known, until Carrow said it. She found that she did now, all at once, and that it explained the whole of the last hour. It was not that they were slow. It was that their hands were good, and had been taught well, and would not do the one thing she was asking them to do. "It's how a load gets dropped, if there's nobody waiting. There's always somebody waiting. That's what the call's for."

Coombe got up off her step.

She came across the wet stone with her rope in her hands and her grey Board coat dark at the hem, and stopped in front of Treen, and held out the rope's end.

"Hold that," she said.

Treen held it.

"Now let go of it," said Coombe, "into the air. Not into my hand. Not down. Out." She held her own hand a foot below the rope's end, open, waiting. "I'll have it. You'll not see me take it. You'll have let go before I do."

Treen let go. The rope's end dropped half a hand through the air and landed in Coombe's palm, and Coombe closed her fingers on it.

"Again," said Coombe.

They did it for twenty minutes, all of them, wards and Embers and still doors alike, in pairs across the dock floor in the grey light, letting go of a rope's end into the air above an open hand and not looking to see if it was caught. Kiva did it with Pru, and then with Hesketh, and then with the elder Pascoe, who let go of it very carefully as though it might break. It was absurd. It was the most useful twenty minutes of the day. By the end of it, when Kiva called *nobody* over the tun, every ward on the plank opened on the end of the word, together, and the tun hung on nothing for the space of a quarter of a count before the Embers had it, and it did not slop at all.

"There," said Coombe. "That's strangers stopping."

At half past four they ran it end to end three times, on the dry floor, with the tun, from one chalk mark to the next and the next and the next. On the third run Kiva found that she was not saying *nobody* the way she had said it at Mallow, short, with the swallow, to two people who had rehearsed it with her on a yard. She was saying it louder, and longer, and from her stomach, so that a girl from Holloway twelve paces off with her back half turned would hear it at the same instant as Anwen at her elbow. It was a different word when eighteen people had to hear it at once. It had to be a word that belonged to nobody's ear in particular.

She stood by the tun at the end of the third run, with the water slopping quietly against its top hoop and the grey light coming down through the glass, and understood that she had been calling the pass for four months as though it were hers.

It had never been hers. It had been Jude's name for it, and Pru's fold, and Perrin's plant, and the Line's old word.

She wrote in her book that night, on the right, left-handed, under the day's line: *The call's not mine.* Then, after a while, under that: *Somebody else will have to call it. Teach it so they can.*

At five Coombe took her rope and went up the steps.

"Tomorrow at the slack," she said over her shoulder, "you'll all stand on the basin wall and watch the real one come up, and not touch it, and not light a door, and not say a word. Then you'll come back here and find out what this tun was lying to you about." She stopped on the third tier and looked down at them, eighteen strangers and a cask, on the wet floor of the Graving. "It'll be lying about something. It's a tun."
