# Chapter 45 — Kiva: Let Go

Mara Rowan had her watch open in her left hand and her reading hand in her coat pocket when Yeo's whistle went three short notes, and she did not look up from the second hand.

"Fifteen minutes past," she said, to Hart, on his knees at the grating below her. "And forty seconds."

"Quiet," said Hart. His bad hand lay flat on the bars. "Still quiet."

She had not seen the dock. She had not seen anything of it after the cask went past the first stake and the second pass took it on toward the Graving's mouth, a grey shape riding across the mud between two fair heads. She had heard Noa's thin voice say *cliff* from the far side of the basin, eleven minutes past and forty seconds, and *read,* and she had kept her watch hand still. She had heard a girl's voice say *I can't* a long way off, and then a long quiet, and then Perrin Cade's voice from the wall, declaring, and saying that it was his as well. She had heard *cliff* again. She had kept her watch hand still. She had told her niece at half past five that she would not ask anybody to run and tell her anything, and she had not.

Now the whistle said the Master's ward was up and the sink was shut, and the rest of it was hers.

"*Quench,*" said Mara.

She did not raise her voice. Peverell was in the pump house doorway above the landing, where she could see the landing and the six pump levers inside both, and Peverell said it after her, inside, in her small dry keeper's voice: "*Quench.*" And thirty-six fitters on two galleries pulled thirty-six red handles together.

Mara heard it through the wall. It was a sound like a long breath let out. The thirty-six lights that had turned together four and four for six months went out in their tall glass cases all at once, and the pump house was dark.

The pumps did not stop.

She had known they would not. She had read the letter on the trestle on Monday and watched it done on Tuesday on one pump with its cells lit. She still felt it in her back teeth. Six iron pumps that had lost their drive in the same breath ought to have stopped on the stroke, and the outfalls below her ought to have stopped pouring. They missed one stroke. All six. Then they went on, up and down, four and four, on a count that came from a man in a chair at the foot of the iron stair with his right hand on the flange of the middle rising main and his left on a glass case.

Mara did not look at him. She could see him at the edge of her eye, a dark coat and a rug and a glass on an iron stand, and Kellan Renn beside him in black and gold, counting aloud under his breath. *Fifty-seven.* She looked at her watch.

"*Draw,*" said Mara.

"*Draw,*" said Peverell, inside.

That took the longest. She had timed it on the training cells in the keeper's room until she could hear it in her sleep: a minute and a half, if every fitter was quick and nobody fumbled a latch. Each fitter opened the slot at the foot of a dark case and drew the old can out, hot, into a box, and shut the box, and set it aside. Thirty-six old cans, stepped, every one of them, that had kept the town's time since twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month. They would go to the Board's press in their boxes as evidence, with the red tags on, and nobody would ever burn them in anything again.

She heard the boxes shut, one after another, along both galleries. *Thirty-three. Thirty-four.* A latch stuck on the north gallery; somebody swore and freed it. *Thirty-six.*

"Eighteen past," said Mara. "*Set.*"

Fresh cans went in. She heard them go home, thirty-six small iron sounds. Each fitter laid a hand flat on the glass of the case in front of them, palm down, fingers spread, as she had taught them at the keeper's table with three cells and a lamp. They had their jars at their feet in straw. They had their cards in their aprons, thirty-six cards in thirty-six different hands, each with its own count on it and not two alike in the house. Nobody salted. Nobody was to salt until she said.

"*Town or mine,*" said Mara.

"*Town,*" said a fitter on the south gallery, at once. Then another. Then all of them, along both galleries, one after another, not loudly, as people say a thing they have been told they will have to say. *Town. Town. Town.*

That was right. The fresh cans had nothing in them yet but fresh ash, and they were sitting in cases joined to pumps that were going four and four on a man's pattern. They would hear the pumps. They would say the town's count, because it was the only count in the house. It meant nothing yet. In a moment she would say *let go,* and the pumps would stand, and the house would go quiet, and every fresh can in it would be quiet too, for the minute it took to salt it with ash that had never heard a beat, and give it a card, and let it find its own.

She looked at her watch. Nineteen minutes past, and ten seconds.

She looked down the iron stair at the man in the chair.

"Mr. Halden," said Mara. "Let go."

---

Kellan Renn had his mother's watch open in his left palm and his right hand ready to count, and he saw the Proprietor take his right hand off the iron flange of the middle rising main at nineteen minutes past six and eleven seconds, exactly on the word, the way he had taken it off on Tuesday.

The pumps went on.

Kellan heard them. He heard them before he saw anything, because he had been counting their strokes aloud under his breath for three minutes, *fifty-seven* at the turn of every minute, and the count did not stop in his mouth when the hand came off the flange. It did not need to. Up and down, behind the wall at his back, four and four. The six outfalls went on pouring into the hand of water on the mud below the landing.

"*Town,*" said somebody inside, on the gallery. "*Still town.*"

The Proprietor did not move.

He sat in his chair at the foot of the stair with his right hand lifted an inch above his knee, where he had brought it, and his left hand flat on the glass, and his face exactly as it always was. Kellan saw that it was exactly as it always was. He had sat at that face's table four times. He had watched it say *I'm sure of my reading* to a Board. He knew it as well as he knew a column of figures, and he saw it now do nothing at all, which in that face meant that something had happened behind it that it had not expected.

"Sir," said Kellan.

"I've let go," said the Proprietor. His voice was quite pleasant. "My hand's off it."

"It's still going."

"I can hear it, Mr. Renn." The Proprietor looked down at his own right hand, an inch above his knee. Then he looked at his left, flat on the glass, where it had lain since 2611. Under it, in the brass-framed case, the old cell that the foundation called the first turned its light, small and yellow, four and four, as it had turned for two hundred and ninety years under one Proprietor's hand after another.

Kellan saw him understand it. He did not know how he saw it. Nothing in the face changed.

"It's one hold," said the Proprietor. "Not two." He said it quite slowly, to the glass. "I've held yards with my right hand and kept this with my left for seventy years, and I'd thought they were two things. They're not. It goes from here—" he did not lift the left hand; he looked at it— "through me, into whatever my other hand's on. On Tuesday there were lit cells under that pump to take it back. Today there's nothing under it but me." He was quiet a moment. "You asked me on Tuesday what happens when I let go of a pump that's only got me. I said it was simpler. I was wrong. I can't let go of the pumps without letting go of this."

On the landing above them, at the rail, Mrs. Rowan had not moved. She stood with her watch open and her face, Kellan thought, like a woman reading a fault report that had come out the way she had been afraid it would.

"*Nobody salts,*" she said, over her shoulder, to the doorway. Her voice was dry and exact and did not hurry. "Hold the jars. Hands stay on the glass. It's still town and it'll stay town till I say."

"*Nobody salts,*" said Peverell inside.

Then Mrs. Rowan looked down the stair again.

"Mr. Halden," she said. "Your clerk will tell you the time. I'll tell you the rest, once. The slack ends at half past. The salt and the cards and the count take four minutes, if my fitters are as good as I think, and the pumps three more to start. If you've let go by twenty-four minutes past, there's time. At twenty-four, if you've not, I'll call it off. I'll say *stand* and Peverell will put the old cans back, and the bank will go on as it's gone since the autumn, and the low town will be dry, and none of it will have been for anything, and it'll be on my name." She did not change her voice. "I'll not ask you twice. I'll not touch a cell to make it go faster. That's all I've got."

She looked back at her watch.

"Twenty past," said Kellan. He heard his own voice say it, quite level.

The Proprietor did not answer. He was looking at his left hand on the glass.

Kellan had read about that hand in his mother's newspaper when he was eight. Everybody in Meridian had. *The Proprietor's hand has not left the foundation cell since 2611.* He had thought it was a figure of speech, the way the Mercury said a thing had not been out of a man's mind. Then he had sat at the long table and seen that it was not. Adlam had told him, the first night, in the street afterward: once a year, on the first of the first month, the glass was taken off its frame and cleaned by two clerks with soft cloths, and for that, and only that, the hand came up. Under a minute. Adlam had held the watch on it every year for nine years. Fifty-one seconds was the longest.

"What happens to it," said Kellan, "if you lift it for longer?"

"I don't know." The Proprietor did not look up. "Nobody's ever lifted it for longer. Not me. Not the man who held it before me." He was quiet. "I think it goes to its own count. I think the first stepped cell in this country, that's kept the foundation's time since 2391, goes to whatever it would have kept if nobody had ever salted it. I think that's the foundation's oldest property gone in a minute, and I'm the man who let it go." He breathed in. "And I think something else. I've kept that count in my own chest for seventy years, Mr. Renn. Fifty-seven. My heart's kept it. I don't know what I am if it stops."

"Twenty-one," said Kellan.

He found that he had his right hand on the old man's left wrist, the free one, with two fingers on the pulse, the way Talley had taught him on a boat because Finch had taught Talley. He did not remember putting it there. The pulse under his fingers was going fifty-seven to the minute, steady as a clock, four and four.

"You asked me," said Kellan, "to tell you what you wouldn't like to hear. On the card. Not counsel's. Yours." He did not take his eyes off his mother's watch. "Seventy years is twenty-five thousand days, and something. This is three minutes. If you lift at twenty-two, there's time. If you lift after twenty-four, there's not. If you don't lift, the low town's dry this morning, and the bank keeps your count, and the next time anything in this harbour falls into step it'll be because it was never got out, and every keeper in the country will know it was got as far as the salt and stopped because a man couldn't take his hand off a glass." He breathed. "That's the sum. It's the same number whichever way I do it. I've done it four ways since you said *one hold.*"

The Proprietor looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time, for perhaps four seconds, with his lean face and his grey temples and his eyes that had looked out of a window in 2604 at the lamps of the Long Quay going white. Kellan held the look. He did not know what was in it. He thought, afterward, that he had never in his life been looked at so carefully by anybody, and that it was the look of a man checking a sum.

"Twenty-two," said Kellan.

The Proprietor lifted his hand.

He lifted it off the glass slowly, an inch, the way a man lifts a dressing off a wound. The palm came away white. It came away the shape of the glass, flat, the fingers spread and stiff, as though it had been pressed in a book. On the glass where it had been there was a print of it, five fingers and a palm, in a faint fog that had been there so long it did not fade. He held the hand up an inch above the print. It shook.

Under the glass the first cell's light went on turning, four and four. Then it missed. It missed the way a man misses a step on a stair he has gone up every day of his life, and caught itself, and missed again. And then it was not turning four and four any more. It was turning slower, three and then two and then four, uneven, with a long pause, like a thing remembering how to walk.

The pumps stopped.

Kellan felt them stop through the stone of the walk, in the soles of his boots. Six iron pumps behind the wall at his back stopped on the down-stroke and stood. The six outfalls below the landing gave one last gulp each into the hand of water on the mud, and stopped pouring. For the first time since the boat came in under the sea gate, Kellan Renn stood in Saltmere and heard nothing going four and four anywhere at all.

Under his two fingers the old man's pulse stumbled.

It went fifty-seven. Then it went sixty-one, and forty-nine, and something fast and ragged that Kellan could not count, and the lean face went grey from the mouth outward, and the Proprietor's right hand closed hard on the arm of his chair. Adlam said something sharp behind them and started forward. Counsel said "*Sir*—"

"No," said the Proprietor. He did not look at either of them. "Leave it. Let it find something."

He was looking across the basin.

Kellan looked where he was looking. Across the drained basin, past the seam in the mud where Ivey's crew were getting up off their knees, past the first stake and the second and the long empty sill of the Graving's mouth, to the grey ward that hung across the mouth from wall to wall, and the tall thin figure in grey standing in front of it with her back to the dock and her hands folded.

Something went out of the old man.

Kellan felt it go through the two fingers on his wrist. It was not the pulse. It was what the pulse had been keeping. It went out of him the way heat goes out of an iron when it is set down on stone, all at once, and across the basin, and Kellan, who had never in his life felt anything that could not be written in a column, felt it go across the mud and the water and the seam, toward the Graving's mouth, toward the grey ward and the woman standing in front of it.

The Proprietor's lips were moving.

He was counting. Kellan could see it. He was not counting aloud, and his eyes were open and on the far side of the basin, and his lips were moving on a count, four of something, and a rest, and four.

Kellan looked down at his mother's watch.

*Twenty-two minutes past six,* he would write afterward, *and eleven seconds. His lips counted. Four, and a rest. I don't know what.*

---

Kiva was sitting on the dock floor forty-five paces in from the sill with her dead hand in her lap when it came across the basin.

She did not know what it was. She knew afterward, because the whole town knew afterward, that it was twenty-two minutes past six and that the Proprietor had lifted his hand. She did not know it then. She knew only that her doors, which had been lying down since the cut, flat and quiet as dogs on a hearth, lifted their heads all at once. And she felt something come across the mud and in at the dock mouth, toward the ward, the way a wave comes across a bay toward a wall.

It was a count.

It was the town's count. It was the bank's, and the Regiment's, and the outfalls', four and four, the count that had come up into her fingers from every bollard and grating and bed rail in Saltmere for three weeks. But it was not in the stone any more. It was not in the water. It was coming across the basin by itself, loose, the way the held water had come down off the chime when the cask was cut, with nothing holding it.

It came to the Master's ward.

It did not go through. Kiva saw the grey across the dock mouth shiver from wall to wall, once, as a sheet shivers when a door is opened somewhere in the house. And she saw the Master, on the sill, with her back to the dock and her hands folded at her waist, go perfectly still. She had been still before. She went stiller. The ward did not move. The count lay against it, outside, the way the held water lay against it inside, and for a moment there were two things touching across that grey, and Kiva felt both of them, with her doors, from forty-five paces, at once.

Every lamp in the Graving went to a point.

There were three rows of them along the tiers on either side, white lamps in glass globes on iron brackets, lit since five, and all of them did it at once. The flame in each globe went small. It went small the way a flame goes when the wick is turned down, except that it did not go dim. It went to a single point of light in the middle of each globe, very small and very bright, as bright as the whole flame had been, brighter. It was not white. Kiva did not know what colour it was. It was not out. Every lamp on the tiers, three hundred of them, was a point, hanging in the middle of its glass like a star in a well.

Through the grey of the ward, faintly, she could see the Long Quay beyond the basin, and the white lamps on their fluted posts that the lamp-men had not yet put out. Every one of them was a point. Along the low town's edge, the yellow lamps in the windows of Ropery Row. Points. On the iron landing over the grating at the head of the basin, the oil lamp Peverell kept lit there. A point.

On the tiers, three thousand people drew one breath and did not let it out.

Kiva found that her lips were moving.

She was counting. She had not decided to. She could hear herself, under her breath, very low, on the dock floor: *one, two, three, four.* And a rest. *One, two, three, four.* She did not know what she was counting. It was not the town's count; that was outside the ward, lying against it. It was not the cask's; the cask was a drift of grey ash in a shallow pool. It was a count. She was keeping it. She could not stop.

Rhea was standing over her. Kiva did not know when Rhea had come. Rhea was looking down at her face, at her mouth, and Rhea's own face was doing nothing at all.

At the sill, forty-five paces off, the Master's sister stood at the Master's side, close enough to see her face. Kiva could not see the Master's face. She could see the sister's, turned toward it, white.

*One, two, three, four.*

Then the points went out.

They did not go out. The flames came back. Every lamp on the tiers was a lamp again, white, an ordinary flame in a glass globe, as though somebody had turned three hundred wicks up together. The lamps on the Long Quay were lamps. Peverell's oil lamp on the landing was yellow. And the count that had come across the basin and lain against the ward was not lying against it any more. It was not anywhere. Kiva felt for it with her doors and it was gone.

Her lips stopped.

"*Nine seconds,*" said Noa, at his desk under the lane mast, ten paces off. His voice was very small. "*By the mast clock. From twenty-two minutes past six and eleven seconds.*" He was looking at his tape. "*A hair. On every pen. Nobody on the mast.*" He did not say anything else, and he did not draw anything.

Kiva put her right hand inside her shirt and found the disk.

It was cold. It had been cold beside a Glory master at the far end of a polished table, and on a bench in a dock, and through two fevers, and through two crossings this morning. It was cold now. She held it for a moment against her chest, and it was a round flat cold thing on a cord, and nothing else.

"You were counting," said Rhea.

"Yes."

"Out loud. Low. Four, and a rest." Rhea did not take her eyes off Kiva's face. "I've written it." She touched the slate on her belt. "I'll not write what. I don't know what."

"Nor do I," said Kiva.

At the sill the Master had not moved. Her ward hung across the dock mouth, grey, from wall to wall. Her sister had taken a small book out of her sleeve and was writing in it, standing, very fast.

Mara Rowan heard the pumps stop, and saw the lamp on the landing come back yellow from a point, and did not let herself look at either.

"Twenty-two," she said, "and twenty seconds. *Salt.*"

"*Salt,*" said Peverell inside.

Thirty-six jars came up out of the straw on two galleries. Mara could not see them. She could hear them: thirty-six grey seals with two hands on them broken by thirty-six thumbs, and thirty-six pinches of ash that had never heard a beat going in at the tops of thirty-six fresh cans, in thirty-six dark cases, in a pump house where for the first time in six months nothing at all was keeping time. Not the pumps; they stood. Not the cells; they were dark. Not the man at the foot of the stair, who was sitting in his chair with his white hand held up off his glass and his face grey, and Kellan Renn's fingers on his wrist, and Adlam on one knee beside him, saying something low.

"*Cards,*" said Mara.

Thirty-six cards came out of thirty-six apron pockets. Each fitter laid their own under the handle of their own case. On each card, a count. Three and a quick. Five slow. Two and two and one. A hymn tune's first line. A girl's own pulse, taken at the keeper's table on Monday night and written down. Not two alike in the house. Mara had read every one of them herself at midnight and initialled it.

"*Town or mine,*" said Mara.

There was a silence on the galleries.

It went on for a long time. It went on for the length of three breaths. She stood at the landing's rail with her watch in her hand and her reading hand in her coat pocket, flat against her side, and did not take it out. It wanted to come out. It had wanted to come out since Sunday. It wanted to go up that stair and along the south gallery and lay itself flat on a dark case and *know,* the way it had known the Ferren bank on a hill in the autumn, the way it had gone white to the wrist knowing.

She had decided on a kitchen floor four years ago that she would never ask the girl anything about the dream, only write it down when it was told. She had decided in the pump hall a week ago that she would not touch a cell in this house. She found that it was the same decision, and that it was harder the second time, and that she was going to keep it.

"*Mine,*" said a fitter on the north gallery.

It was the heavy man with the burn scar on his wrist. Hart's mate. Mara knew his voice.

"*Mine,*" said another. "*Mine.*" Then they came along both galleries, one after another, not together, because they were not keeping time together any more: *mine,* and *mine,* in thirty different voices, some loud and some hardly louder than a breath. Mara counted them. She did not look at her watch while she counted. *Twenty-nine. Thirty. Thirty-one.* The man at the foot of the stair had shut his eyes. *Thirty-three. Thirty-four. Thirty-five.*

There was a silence.

"*Nineteen,*" said Mara. "*Case nineteen.*"

"I can't tell," said Loveday Hosken, from the south gallery, through the door. Her voice was very high and quite steady. "Mrs. Rowan. I can't tell. It's not town. It's not anything. It's so quiet I can't—" She stopped. "I can't feel my hand properly. It went when the pumps went. I've kept it on."

Mara did not move from the rail.

"Then say you can't tell," she said. "That's a word as well. Say it, and keep your hand where it is, and wait."

"I can't tell," said Loveday Hosken.

They waited. Mara stood on the landing and looked at the second hand of her watch go round. Hart had not taken his bad hand off the grating. Below her the outfalls stood dry, six iron mouths over a hand of still water, and the water did not move.

"*Mine,*" said Loveday Hosken. "*It's mine.* It's slow. It's—Mrs. Rowan, it's doing the thing on my card. It's doing my mother's hymn."

"Thirty-six," said Mara. "Twenty-five past." She took a breath and found that it went all the way down, for the first time since three. "Mrs. Peverell. Your pumps."

Peverell started them herself, one at a time, each on its own six cells.

Mara heard the first one go at twenty-six minutes past. It did not go four and four. It went in a long slow stroke and a short one, and a pause, and the first outfall below the landing coughed and poured into the hand of water on its own count, unlike anything. At twenty-seven the second went, faster, three and a rest. Then the third. Then the fourth, which was slow and heavy as a man walking uphill. Then the fifth. At twenty-nine minutes past six the sixth pump started on its own six cells, and six iron mouths below the landing poured into the basin on six different counts, and no two of them agreed, and the water under them went every way at once and did not keep any time at all.

"It sounds like a fair," said Hart, from his knees, after a while. He sounded astonished. "It sounds like six bands at a fair."

"It sounds," said Mara, "like a pump house."

She shut her watch. She stood at the rail for a moment longer with it closed in her hand. Then she put it in her pocket, and took her reading hand out of the other pocket, and went up the stair and in at the door and along the south gallery between the cases to case nineteen.

Every cell along the gallery was lit again, and every one turned its light on a count of its own, slow and quick and uneven, so that the long gallery looked like a street of windows at night with a different family behind each. Loveday Hosken was standing at nineteen with her right hand flat on the glass. Mara looked at the hand. It was white. It was white from the fingertips to the wrist, the way Mara's own had been white on a hill in the autumn, starved, every bit of warmth gone out of it into a cell that had needed it more.

"Take it off now," said Mara gently.

The girl took it off. It did not close.

"I kept it on," said Loveday Hosken. "You said. Hands stay on the glass."

"You did right. I'll have the healer for it." Mara looked at the case. It was not hers to touch. Every one of the thirty-six was somebody else's, and had been since the jars came along the quay at half past five in thirty-six pairs of arms. She had not touched a cell in this house in three weeks, and she had not needed to, and the bank was out, and the low town was taking water for seven minutes in Ropery Row on a count nobody had given it.

She laid her reading hand on case nineteen.

She did not read it for anything. It was done. She laid her hand flat on the glass, palm down, the fingers spread, because she had decided to, now that it was nobody's need, and she wanted to know. The light inside turned slow and yellow on a hymn's first line. Under her hand, through the glass, there was the cell. And under the cell there was nothing at all but more of itself.

"Mine," said Mara Rowan, very quietly, and took her hand away, and went to send for Aske.

---

Hart said *ache* at thirty-one minutes past six, and the flood came in under the sea gate.

Kiva heard it from the dock floor, faintly, through the Master's ward, like breath. She had heard it at the end of every slack since she came. She had never heard it come into a basin where nothing was keeping time. It sounded the same. She did not know why she had thought it would sound different.

She sat on the wet stone with Pike beside her and Rhea standing over them and her dead hand in her lap, and watched the flood come.

It came across the basin from the harbour, brown and quiet, over the mud and the first stake and the seam that Ivey's crew had shut, and up to the sill of the Graving's mouth, and against the Master's ward. It did not come through. It rose against the grey from the outside, a hand, a foot, the brown harbour water standing higher and higher against nothing that Kiva could see but grey, while inside the dock the shallow pool of the Barrel's held water lay still and grey against the other side of it and did not rise at all.

The Master did not move.

She stood on the sill with her back to the dock and her hands folded at her waist and the harbour rising against her ward at her feet, and she did not move, because she could not. Kiva had heard the sister say so on Monday at the trestle in the pump hall. *She can't hold it and move.* Now she watched it. The tall grey figure stood on the granite with the sea climbing the other side of her door, and did not shift her feet, or turn her head, or unfold her hands. Once Kiva saw her shoulders go up and down, slowly, as a person breathes who has been asked to carry a thing one more stair. That was all.

At twenty to seven the Company's two boats came out from the outer quay with the gate between them.

They brought it in on the flood the way they had taken it out on Tuesday, on lines, a great black wall of timber and iron floating level on the brown water, until it lay across the mouth of the Graving outside the Master's ward with a man's length of water between. Then the Company's men began to let the harbour into its hollow belly through its own sluices, to sink it onto its seating on the sill.

It went down very slowly.

At ten to seven the Master's hour was up. Kiva knew it, because the sister had told her so on the basin wall at ten to six, and she had done the sum then and seen that everybody had done it. At the sill the sister turned her head and spoke up the dock, flatly, in the low clear voice that did not need lifting.

"She says she'll be late. She's saying it first."

Nobody on the dock floor answered. On the tiers above, three thousand people who had seen every lamp they could see go to a point and come back did not say anything either. The gate went down a foot. The Master stood.

At five to seven the gate's foot touched the granite outside the ward, and the Company's men on it shouted to each other, short Company words, and let in more water, and it settled. The Master stepped back.

It was the first time she had moved since a quarter past six. She stepped back off the sill onto the dock floor, one pace, with her hands still folded and her ward still up, so that the gate could come home onto the seating where she had been standing. The grey across the mouth shivered from wall to wall as she went, and the flood against it came a hand nearer before it held. She stepped back as a person steps who does not trust the floor. Her sister put a hand under her elbow and she let it stay there.

At four minutes past seven the gate seated.

Kiva heard it. Everybody in the Graving heard it: one long deep sound of timber and iron coming down onto granite under the weight of the harbour, and stopping. The black gate stood in the dock's mouth as it had stood all through the Measure, holding the harbour out, with the flood standing brown against its far side and the Barrel's held water lying grey against its near side, in a shut dock, still.

The Master let go.

Kiva felt the ward go. It went out of the dock mouth the way a held breath goes out of a chest when there is no more need to hold it, and her doors, which had been lying down for forty-eight minutes, got up all at once, one after another, and stood, and shook themselves. She felt her Ember turn over under her breastbone. She felt her still door come up through her boots into the stone, wanting nothing. And she felt, through the stone of the dock floor, from a long way off, from the head of the basin, six counts at once, all different, none of them hers, none of them anybody's in particular, going on.

The Master of the Order of the Even Hand stood on the dock floor a pace inside the shut gate, grey to the lips, with her sister's hand under her elbow and her own hands at last unfolded at her sides. She did not fall. Kiva watched her not fall for a long time.

Then the second sister came down the steps from the outer quay with the Order's flat iron-wheeled cart, empty, that had carried thirty-six jars along the Long Quay on Saturday and back to the Mole and back again, and the two sisters helped the Master onto it. She sat on the cart's bare boards with her hands in her lap and her long grey face turned toward the shut gate.

Kiva got up off the dock floor. She did it badly, with one hand, and Pike helped her with his half-closed one, and she walked down the dock to the gate on legs that had waded slurry and carried a Barrel and did not want to walk anywhere. Her doors stood up as she went and did not lie down, because there was nothing to lie down for. The Master was only a woman on a cart.

She stopped at the cart's side.

The Master looked at her. Her grey eyes did not search. They looked at Kiva's face, and then down at the left hand hanging at the end of Kiva's arm, and up again.

"You counted," said Clemency Thrale. Her voice was very low and very tired and still came to Kiva as though there were nothing in between. "My sister says I did. Your officer will say you did. The man on the outfall walk will have a clerk who'll say he did." She was quiet for a moment. "I don't know what. Do you?"

"No."

"Then neither of us will say." The Master turned her face back to the gate. "Write that you counted, and that you don't know what. My sister's written it for me, in my words. That's all anybody should write." She shut her eyes. She opened her eyes and looked at the sister at the cart's handle. "Take me home. I've been in this town an hour and fourteen minutes, and I'd like to see the sea."

The sisters turned the cart round and pushed it up the long ramp at the dock's south side toward the outer quay with the Master sitting on it with her eyes shut, and the iron wheels made the only sound in the Graving.

Kellan Renn held the board on his knees at twenty-five minutes to seven, on the outfall walk at the foot of the iron stair, while the Proprietor wrote on it.

He held it because the Proprietor had asked him to, and because Adlam's hands were shaking and counsel's were not, and the Proprietor had looked at counsel's and then at Kellan's and said, "Mr. Renn. Would you." The board was a clerk's writing board from the keeper's room. On it was a sheet of the pump house's fault paper, because it was the nearest paper, and Peverell had brought it down the stair herself and laid it there without a word and gone back up to her pumps.

The Proprietor wrote with his right hand. It was steady. His left lay in his lap on the rug, palm up, white still, the shape of the glass, the fingers half curled and not closing. He had not put it back on the case. The case stood beside him on its iron stand with the print of his hand fogged on its glass, and under the glass the first cell turned its light on a count of its own, slow and uneven, three and a pause and two, like nothing the foundation had ever owned.

His pulse, under Kellan's fingers a quarter of an hour ago, had found something at last at twenty-six minutes past, when the first pump started. It had found sixty-four. It had stayed there. Aske, when he came, would say that it was a perfectly ordinary pulse for a man of fifty-five, and that he could not speak for a man of a hundred and three.

"*The Halden Foundation,*" wrote the Proprietor, "*relinquishes, from this day, its claim on all ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.*"

He stopped. He looked at it.

"Counsel will tell you I can't do that on fault paper," he said, not looking up. "Counsel will be right. He'll draw it up properly this afternoon, in nine pages, and I'll sign the nine pages, and they'll go to the Compact's court tonight with your figures." He touched the line with the end of the pen. "But this is the one I mean. I'd want it written where it happened, by the hand that's still working, on the paper this house writes its faults on."

"Why?" said Kellan.

He had not meant to ask. It was not his to ask.

The Proprietor looked across the basin. The flood was coming in over the mud, brown and quiet, past the seam and the first stake to the grey ward across the Graving's mouth. Below the landing the six outfalls poured into it, each on its own count, every way at once.

"Because I felt it go," he said. "Out of me. Across that water. And it was never mine." He was quiet. "Your figures said so on Friday, in front of the Board. Adlam's books said so. Forty-eight a minute in 2402 and fifty-seven today, and the foundation never laid a finger on it. I've known since Friday that it wasn't ours. I've known it in my head." He looked down at his left hand in his lap. "I didn't know it here until twenty-two minutes past six. The foundation found counting ash in 2391, Mr. Renn, the way a boy finds a coin in the road. We've been claiming the road ever since."

He wrote again.

"*It was never ours. We found it. A. Halden, the Proprietor, at the Saltmere harbour pump house, on the outfall walk, at twenty-five minutes to seven on Wednesday the 23rd of the third month, 2681.*"

He held out the pen.

"Witness it," he said. "Under your own name. Not the foundation's clerk. Not Halden License's blade. Yours."

Kellan took the pen. He wrote under the old man's line, in his own small careful hand, *Witnessed, K. Renn,* and the time by his mother's watch, which was four seconds behind the Proprietor's, because it lost four seconds a day and he knew which four.

"It doesn't settle anything," said the Proprietor, taking the board back and looking at the two lines. "I'd like you to know I know that. It doesn't say I was wrong. They didn't go white this morning, the lamps. They didn't go out. I don't know what they did." He folded the fault paper once, very carefully, with one hand and his knee. "I've believed one thing for seventy-seven years, Mr. Renn. I believe it still. I'm only no longer the man who owns it."

He gave the folded sheet to Kellan.

"Give that to the Clerk," he said. "In your own hand. Then go and find your four. I've kept you from them all morning, and they'll want to know what you counted."
