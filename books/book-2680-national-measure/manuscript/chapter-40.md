# Chapter 40 — Kiva: For Want of a Better

The Board's table stood on the floor of the Graving at two on Sunday, on the chalk of the pen's middle line, where the Commodore had lain on Saturday with his tarred hat over one eye.

It was the same long deal table from the Salt Hall, carried along the quay in the morning by the Board's carpenters and lowered eighty feet down the dock on a rope. The Clerk sat at the middle of it with the two Board members on either side. On the table in front of them, in a row, lay the things the Board had asked for. A grey box tied with tape, with a number on its end and a red card in its holder. A black calf book with a straw in it, from Ferren. A larger calf book with a straw in it, from the press behind the keeper's desk at the harbour pump house. Noa's roll of paper with its four columns, and his ledger, and a sheaf of yellow forms. A thin sheaf of cream paper in the Board's clerk's hand, which was everything that had been said at a long table on the Long Quay at nine that morning.

Mara sat on the first bench in front of the table with a single sheet folded in three in her lap, brown wax and black wax across its fold, and her reading hand flat on it.

The tiers went up round them on three sides to the iron and glass roof, and every place on every tier was full. Kiva had never seen so many people in one place. She had thought the Graving full on Friday for Carrow, and on Saturday for the reading. It had not been full. People sat on the steps between the tiers now, and stood along the lip at the top, eighty feet up, against the rail where she had laid her fingers last night, and leaned over it. The green was there, and the grey, and every circuit's colours. The Ropewalk's four sat together on the second tier with Lyle at the end. The Tarn sat in its knot. Meridian sat in the middle, all four of the first company and their reserves: Halden in black and gold, Old Four, Twenty-Eight, and Seven.

Seven was not on the tiers. Seven was on the second bench on the floor, behind Mara, because the Clerk had said so. Perrin sat at the end with his hand pressed to his side. Anwen sat next to Kiva on her left. Jude sat on her right with the book under his good arm, and Pru beyond him with her capped hands folded in her lap.

Kiva was warm. She had been warm since noon. It was not a fever yet. It was the thing that came before one, a heat in the hands and the backs of the knees and a lightness behind the eyes, and she knew it as well as she knew the order of her own doors. She had eaten, and drunk water, and sat in the shade of Quiller's shop with the door shut, as Mara had told her. It had not stopped. She had not expected it to.

Aske was at the end of the bench behind her, with his bag on his knees. He had held her wrist at half past one in the shop and said *ninety-four,* and written it, and not said anything else.

At two, by the clock on the Board's table, the Graving went quiet from the top down.

It went quiet as a field goes quiet when a hawk comes over. It began at the lip, eighty feet up, at the landward end, and came down the tiers row by row, and Kiva turned on the bench to see why, and felt her doors lie down.

The Master of the Order of the Even Hand was coming down the Graving's steps.

She came slowly, a step at a time, with her hands folded at her waist, in plain grey, with a grey cap, tall and thin and very upright. A sister walked a step behind her on either side. The tiers made room for her without being asked. She did not look at them. She looked at her feet on the stone steps, and once, halfway down, she stopped and stood quite still for a count of three with her eyes shut, and Kiva saw the town's count go through her, from her feet up, as cold goes up through a boot. Then she came on.

She did not come to the bench. She walked along the floor of the dock, past the Board's table, past Seven's bench, all the way to the seaward end, where the floating gate held the harbour out, and stood there with her back to its black timber and the sea breathing behind it.

"The Board will put you where you like, Master," said the Clerk.

"Here," said Clemency Thrale. "With the sea at my back. It keeps no time. It helps." Her voice came the length of the dock floor as though there were nothing in between. "I'll stand. I've an hour. I'd be glad if the Board would take me inside it. After it, I'll go back to the Mole."

"The Board will take you in it." The Clerk looked at the clock on her table. "It's two. You'll be gone by three."

"The Charter Board sits again," said the Clerk, "on the matter Miss Rowan put on its table on Friday night. It has before it a sealed page, a notebook, two keepers' books, the Proprietor's evidence taken this morning, and the returns. It'll hear them in that order, and then it'll hear the Master, and then it'll say what it can." She folded her hands. "The page first. Reader."

The Reader of the Compact Library stood up from the end of the first bench, with her two pairs of spectacles and her bright eyes, and came to stand beside Mara.

"Lavinia Arkwright," she said. "Reader, the Compact Library, Meridian. Since 2649." She took the folded sheet out of Mara's lap, without asking, and held it up so that the Board could see the fold. "I took this into my press on Monday the 25th of the sixth month, 2680, at eight minutes to two in the afternoon. It was shut when it came to me, under Mrs. Rowan's own wax and the Works' reconciling stamp. I put my wax across hers and wrote the hour through the join. It's been in my press since, in the dark, with the Library's settlement on the Annick papers, which is the safest place I have." She turned it to the tiers so that the nearest could see the black wax over the brown, and the figures across the join. "It's not been opened. My wax is whole. I've looked at it every hour for two days, and I'm satisfied."

"And when was it written?" said the heavy Board member.

"I don't know, sir. I've never read it. I can say when it was shut." She looked at Mara. "Mrs. Rowan can say when it was written."

"The 29th of the fifth month, 2680," said Mara. "At ten at night, at my kitchen table in Coper Street, in pencil, in my hand. In her words, as she said them, with a wet cloth on her chest, after a fever. The same night." Her voice was dry and exact. "I sealed it at my table on a Sunday night in the sixth month, and took it to the Library on the Monday. The Reader put her wax over mine before Master Annick's box was opened. Then my niece read the notebook, in the Library's room, with the page already in the press. The Reader saw her read it."

"I did," said the Reader. "Her lips counted. I wrote it down."

The Clerk looked at Mara.

"Open it, Mrs. Rowan."

The Reader gave the page back to Mara. She took a small knife out of her satchel, a bone-handled thing with a thin blade, and held it out handle first. Mara did not take it at once. She turned on the bench and looked at Kiva.

Kiva put her left hand on her aunt's right, over the knife's handle. Her hand was hot. Mara's was cold. Neither of them said anything. They put the blade under the black wax together, and the Reader's seal lifted off the brown with a small dry sound like a twig, and then under the brown, and Mara's own seal and the Works' stamp came away in two pieces and fell on the dock's stone between Mara's boots.

Mara unfolded the page.

It was pencil, in her own small exact print, on one side of the sheet. Kiva could see it from the bench. She had not seen it since the night it was written. She had not read it then. She had lain on her bed with the cloth on her chest while her aunt wrote it at the kitchen table, and Mara had sealed it on the Sunday without reading it over, because she said she knew what was on it.

"Read it," said the Clerk.

Mara read it.

She read it in her ordinary voice, the voice she used to read a fault report across a counter, dry and level and exact, and every word of it went up the stone walls of the Graving into the tiers and the iron and glass roof, and nobody in four thousand people made a sound.

*"The 29th of the fifth month, 2680. Ten at night. K.R., told at the kitchen table after a fever, in her words. Written by M.R."*

*"She was high. Above things, looking down. There were roads, from every side at once, and lights on them, a great many, coming. The lights were all unlike. She says they were people. She didn't know them. She knew what they were, because she knew what she was."*

*"There were notes. That's new. She tried to count them and lost her number at four."*

Somewhere high in the tiers, very small, somebody said *four* under their breath, and somebody else said *hush.*

At the end of the Board's table, Noa had not moved. Kiva saw his pencil come down on his ledger and write, small. She knew what he was writing. *N.B.* She knew he would not write anything after it.

*"The lights touched. When they touched, she couldn't tell where one stopped. There was one note, then, that had been a great many."*

*"She looked up. There was no sky. Not dark. Nothing. And in it, something enormous and close. Not heavy. Full. She says it was paying attention."*

*"Every lamp in the city answered."*

*"And under it, at the very end, something small. It ticked. Once. She says it's the word she'd use for a bag, and for a drum pen, and for this. I've written: the word she'd use for both."*

Mara lowered the page.

*"Sealed, M.R."* she read, from the foot. *"That's all there is."*

She folded it again, along its old folds, and held it in her lap under her reading hand, and did not look at anybody.

The Graving did not make the warm sound, or the long sound, or the sound it had made for Carrow. It did not make any sound at all for so long that Kiva could hear the sea against the floating gate, three hundred paces off, where the Master of the Order stood with her back to the timber. She could hear, under it, through the stone of the bench, the town's count, faint, four and four.

She sat with her hot hands in her lap and found that she was not ashamed. She had thought, on the bench, that she might be. She had thought that hearing her own words read out in her aunt's voice, the words of a girl of seventeen with a wet cloth on her chest who did not know what any of it meant, would be like being stripped in front of a town. It was not. It was like hearing a fault report read: a thing that had happened, written down the same night by somebody who knew how to write things down, and kept in the dark so that it could be believed.

She thought it was the best page her aunt had ever written. She thought she would tell her so, sometime, when it would not make her angry.

"The notebook," said the Clerk. "Reader. Master Thrale."

The Reader had untied the grey box's tape. She took out a notebook with a cracked green cover, no bigger than a field book, and held it in both hands. Then she turned, not to the Board, but down the length of the dock floor, to the woman standing at the gate.

"Master," she said. "This is your Order's. It came to my Library by a theft eighty years ago, and the Order's been asking for it back for eighty years, and the settlement of 2611 says it's read in the room and not copied. I've brought it two hundred miles under my own custody because the Board asked." She held it a little higher. "I'll not read it aloud if you object. I'll take it home unread and write in my book that I was asked not to, and by whom. Nobody will think the worse of either of us."

Thrale stood with her hands folded and the sea behind her.

"She told nobody in her house," said the Master. "She wrote that. I've read it. Once, in your room, in 2670, when I was made Master, under your rule, with a Halden clerk by the window watching my lips." Her voice did not change. "She said they'd make it into doctrine by spring, if she told them. She was right. They would have. We would." She was quiet a moment. "Read it. In front of everybody, so that it belongs to nobody. And don't make it anything."

The Reader opened the notebook at a slip, and read.

She read it as she had read a night clerk's margin to Kiva in a reading room in the summer, in exactly the same voice, without looking up until she had done. *Fever again. Fourth time with this dream, so I will write it. Somebody should.* The roads. The lights, each a different light. *I know what they are, because I know what I am, and they are that.* The sky not torn. *As if someone had taken the roof off a house in the night and the house woke and found itself under the stars.* What came down, not crushing, full, like standing under a wave that does not break. *It is paying attention.*

*"Every lamp in Meridian answers at once. Some go out. Some go white. One turns a colour I have no word for."*

Kiva kept her face still. She had kept it still in the reading room when her lips counted. She kept it still now, on a bench in a dry dock with the whole town round her, and she did not look along the bench at Mara, and she did not look up the length of the dock at the gate.

*"I do not know if we gathered to hold it shut, or to break it open, or to take it, or only to stand there together when it came. I wake before I find out. Every time."*

*"The sky unroofed. That is the only way I can say it."*

The Reader shut the notebook.

At the gate, Thrale had not moved. But her folded hands had moved. One thumb crossed the other, and went back.

The keepers took less time, and they were worse.

The Reader read the Ferren book for 2604, because Lumb had sent it in her keeping and was two hundred miles away being uncivil about it. *Keeper took ill in the evening; a fever, three nights. Entered here because the needles did it too. Dreamt that the roof was off the house and there was nothing over it. Not dark. Nothing. And all four-and-twenty stood up together, and were looking.*

Peverell read her own house's book herself. She stood up at the end of the first bench with her spectacles on and the calf book open on her two small crabbed hands, and read the page that she had found for Mara on a Tuesday in the third month, in a keeper's hand that leaned the other way and made its *s* long. *Dreamed there was no roof on the house nor on anything, and nothing over. The nothing was not empty and it was not dark. Something was attending.* And the lamps. *The refinery's lamps on the Long Quay went white about three in the morning and stayed lit. The low town's went out.*

She shut the book and sat down without a word.

Noa read the returns. He read Westerley, and Tarnfoot, and Holloway, as he had read them on Friday, and the Graving was quiet for every one of them, and nobody laughed. Then he picked up one more yellow form, which was new.

"This came in at eight this morning," he said. His thin voice was very clear. "From the Holloway lamp office. I wired the warden myself on Friday night after the Salt Hall. I told him what had been said in the hall, and by whom, and under what name. I asked him to tell the lamp-man." He did not look up from the form. "I'd no business to. It's not a reader's place. I'd like it written that I did it, so that nobody thinks the warden thought of it."

"Read it, Mr. Bexley," said the Clerk.

He read it.

*HOLLOWAY LAMP OFFICE. THE NIGHT LAMP-MAN HAS ASKED THAT IT BE WRITTEN NOW. HE SAYS NO ROOF. NOTHING OVER. SOMETHING THERE ATTENDING. THE LAMPS ON THE RING WHITE AND OUT BOTH. HE SAYS IF THE GIRL CAN SAY IT TO A BOARD UNDER HER NAME HE CAN SAY IT TO HIS WARDEN UNDER HIS. HIS NAME IS ON THE FORM. HE ASKS IT NOT BE PRINTED.*

Noa put it down.

"His name's on the form," he said. "I'll not read it, because he's asked. The Board can see it."

Kiva did not hear anything for a moment after that. She was aware of Anwen's shoulder against hers on the left, quite hard. She was aware of Jude on her right, very still, with the book under his arm, and of his hand, which had come off the book and lay on the bench between them, flat, not touching. She was aware of the heat in her own hands, and of the page folded in Mara's lap, and of a man two hundred miles off who had lit lamps for a living and had a fever and had been laughed at once in a gallery, and who had read in a wire that a girl had stood up, and had stood up.

She had not done it for him. She had done it so that the thing would not live in one head. She had not thought, once, that it would get another head to say it.

The Board's thin clerk read the Proprietor's evidence from the cream sheets, in a flat careful voice, every word that had been said at the long table on the Long Quay at nine. The window. The fever. *I dreamed that night.* Eleven times since. The Library in 2610, and the settlement. *Some go out. Some go white.* His reading: every lamp in the country white and not out. *I'm sure of my reading. I'm not certain I'm right.*

And then the question, and Kiva's answer, in her own words, as he had asked.

*Some had gone white. On the ferry. I was lower than I'd been. I don't know what it means. I'll not have it said I saw more.*

And under it: *A thing she has no word for. Not put on the table.*

The Graving heard it. Kiva heard it hear. There was a sound from the tiers like wind in a field, a long way off, and then it stopped. Halden's counsel, at the end of the first bench, did not move, and did not write anything in his folder. Two benches back, Kellan sat with his hands in his lap and his face quite white, looking at the cream sheets in the thin clerk's hands.

"That's Mr. Halden's evidence," said the Clerk. "Under his own name." She looked at the clock on the table. "It's twenty-five to three. Master."

Clemency Thrale did not come forward. She spoke from where she stood, with her back to the floating gate and the sea breathing behind its timber, and her voice came the length of the dock as though there were nothing in between.

"Clemency Thrale," she said. "Master of the Order of the Even Hand. Glory. One door, for life: a ward. I've kept it since I was nineteen, and every other door I have shut." Her folded hands did not move. "I've not been past my house's gate in eleven years. I came past it on Wednesday, for a cellar, and I find I'm to say a thing in a dock as well. I'll say it once."

The Graving was so quiet that Kiva could hear her own heart, which was going a little fast, and a little hot.

"I've dreamed it," said Thrale. "Three times. At nineteen, the night I took my door. In 2670, the night I was made Master, in my own house, with nothing in it that kept time. And on the 17th of the ninth month of last year, at my window over the river, at twenty past two in the afternoon, with a fever that came on me at the window and was gone by dark." She did not look at anybody. "No roof. Nothing over. Not empty. Something paying attention. I'll not describe it further. The Master Annick described it better, and so did a girl of seventeen, and so, I find, did a man I've been told for eleven years is my house's enemy."

On the bench, Kiva felt her doors, which had been lying down since two, lie flatter still, as dogs do when the person they do not need to watch sits down.

"I've told nobody," said Thrale. "Not my house. Not the sister at the gate, who's standing behind me and has just heard it with everybody else." She did not turn round. "I'd my reasons. They were the Master Annick's. A house that keeps one door each would make a dream into a rule by spring, and I've spent my life making sure it hasn't any rules but the ones it needs." She was quiet for a moment. "Mr. Halden reads it one way. I've heard his reading now, from the Board's clerk. He wants every lamp in the country white, keeping one time, so that whatever comes finds them lit."

She looked down the length of the dock, past the Board's table, toward the Long Quay, which could not be seen from the Graving's floor.

"I read it the other way," she said. "The Master Annick didn't say white was good. She said some went white and some went out, and one turned a colour she'd no word for, and she woke before she found out what any of it was for. I don't know what it's for either. I know that it asks. It pays attention. It comes in where there's a door open to it." Her voice did not rise. "I'd have one door, and one quiet thing, and let nothing in that asks. That's my house's reading. That's why we keep no clock. I don't say Mr. Halden's a fool. He was at a window and I wasn't. I say I'd sooner a house where nothing answers."

She stopped.

"Is that the Order's position, Master?" said the heavy Board member.

"It's mine. The Order hasn't got one. If it had, I'd have to resign." Something moved at the corner of her long mouth, and was gone. "That's all I'll say of the dream. Now the cellar, because that's what I came for, and I've a quarter of an hour."

"Thirty-six jars," said Thrale.

She did not move from the gate. One of the sisters behind her stepped aside, and Kiva saw the low flat cart standing in the shadow of the gate's timber with its iron wheels and its grey cloth, where nobody had noticed it come down the dock's ramp. The sister drew back the cloth. The jars stood in their straw in rows, small and round and stone-coloured, each with a grey seal at its neck with a mark on it like two hands laid flat side by side.

"There were forty-one in my cellar when I was made Master," said Thrale. "There were forty-four before that. There were forty when Miss Rowan left my house in the second month with a dram and two-tenths in her tin, to make good a hand. There are thirty-six on that cart. There are four in my cellar." She did not look at the jars. "It's never heard a beat. Some of it was tinned before the Halden patent. Some before there was a Halden. It's the last of what my house has. And I've not given it. I've brought it."

The Graving waited.

"My house has never sold," said Thrale. "Nor given to a bank. Nor to a floor, nor a pump, nor anything that keeps time. We make good a hand. That's all we've ever done with our cellar, and it's the only rule we have that isn't about doors." Her voice was quite level. "Mrs. Rowan asked me for thirty-six jars for a bank. Not for a hand. A bank of thirty-six cells in step, in a pump house, in a town that keeps one count in everything. If I give them, I break my house's rule. I'm the Master. I keep the house. And I'll break its rule on a dock floor in front of a town I've never seen."

She looked along the dock at Peverell, on the first bench.

"Keeper. I said on the quay you'd hear the price first. Here it is." She did not raise her voice. "My house breaks its rule, and writes in its own book that the Master broke it, on this day, in this dock, and why. That's mine to pay, and I'll pay it. And four jars are what's left for every hand that's hurt on the road south, for as long as there's no more plain in the country. That's my house's to pay." She was quiet a moment. "And this is yours. If those thirty-six jars go into your cells on Wednesday, those thirty-six cells are never stepped again. Not by Mr. Halden's house, nor the Works, nor the Committee, nor you, nor any keeper after you, while your pump house stands. Each on its own count, not two alike, for good. Written in your book and in mine, under your name and the Committee's." She looked at the Committee's bench. "You'll keep a slower bank, and a dearer one, and your fitters will curse it every winter for the rest of their lives. That's the price. It's not ash. It's that."

Peverell stood up.

She stood for a long moment at the end of the first bench with her spectacles on their cord and her apron on, because she had come from the pump house, and her small crabbed hands at her sides. Then she took the keeper's book for 2604 off the Board's table, where she had laid it, and opened it at the back, at a clean page, and took the pencil out of her apron pocket.

"I'll write it," she said. "In this. My father's father's book. The page after the night the lamps went white." She wrote, standing, against the table, in her small crabbed hand. "*Received from the Order of the Even Hand, thirty-six jars of plain. In return, the harbour bank never to be stepped again while the house stands. Each cell its own count. Peverell, keeper.*" She held the pencil out to the chairman of the Harbour Committee without looking at him. "Mr. Chairman."

The chairman looked at the pencil.

"A slower bank," he said. "A dearer one."

"And the low town dry on its own count, and not on anybody's," said Peverell. "Sign it or don't. If you don't, it's my house, and I'll sign for the house, and you can take me to the magistrate in the morning." She held the pencil out. "I've not slept since Tuesday. I'd sooner you signed."

The chairman of the Harbour Committee took the pencil. He signed. His hand was not quite steady.

At the gate, Thrale bowed her head an inch.

"Then they're given," she said. "Not brought. Given." She looked at the sister on her left. "Write it in our book tonight, at the hut. In my words. *Given to a bank, by the Master, who broke the house's rule to do it, and said the price first.*" She was quiet. "And write that I'd do it again."

It was ten to three by the clock on the Board's table. Kiva saw that the Master's long pale face had gone grey, and that her folded hands were no longer quite still. One thumb crossed the other, and went back, and crossed again.

"Master," said the Clerk. "You've ten minutes."

"I've five," said Thrale. "I'll go now, if the Board's done with me. I'd like to walk back to the Mole while I can still walk it." She unfolded her hands for the first time since she had come down the steps, and let them hang at her sides. "Miss Rowan."

Kiva stood up on the second bench. She did not decide to. Her knees decided.

"Your lips counted," said Thrale, down the length of the dock, "when you read her. The sister wrote it down. I've thought about it since the summer. I said at my house that I'd not decided what I thought." She looked at Kiva across three hundred paces of stone with her grey eyes, which did not search. "I've decided. I think you were counting the roads. I think that's all. I think it's a good thing to count, and a hard one to stop."

She turned and walked out of the Graving by the seaward ramp, with the two sisters a step behind her, and the empty cart left standing by the gate, and the thirty-six jars on it in their straw.

Kiva's doors got up, one at a time, as she went, and stood, and shook themselves. The town's count came back up through the bench to where it always was. And the heat in her hands, which had been held, she understood now, by the same thing that had held her doors down, came up all at once like water when a hand is taken off a pipe.

She sat down rather quickly.

"Rowan," said Aske, behind her, very low. He had her wrist already. "A hundred and two."

"I know."

"Sit. Don't stand again." He did not let go. "I'll take you out when the Board's done. Not before. You'd never forgive me."

The Clerk had not moved through any of it. She sat at the middle of the Board's table with her hands folded and her small grey face giving nothing away, and when the Master had gone up the ramp and out of sight, she let the silence stand for a count of ten.

"Mrs. Rowan," she said. "The Works. You stood on Friday and said what you'll do on Wednesday. The Board's heard the Proprietor's reading and the Master's. What's the Works'?"

Mara stood. She still had the page folded under her reading hand.

"The Works hasn't got one either," she said. "The Works doesn't believe anything that hasn't happened. Mr. Rusk will tell you so." She did not look at Rusk. "I'll tell you mine, since you've asked. It's the Home Line's as well; a foreman told me so in the autumn, on a hill, about a drum." Her voice was dry and exact. "I don't know what's coming. Nobody here does. The Proprietor thinks it'll find stepped lamps white. The Master thinks it'll find nothing to answer in a house with one door. I don't know which of them is right, or whether either is." She looked at the Board. "I know one thing, and I've known it since a bank on a hill went into step with a pump through the water. Whatever's coming, don't be one engine when it comes. Not one count, in everything, that one hand can drive and one fault can stop. Thirty-six counts, not two alike, each with a hand on it that isn't mine." She sat down. "That's not a reading. It's a fitter's rule. It's all I've got."

The Clerk of the Measure stood up.

She stood behind the middle chair with her gold chain on her coat, and when she spoke everybody heard her, from the Board's table to the lip, eighty feet up, where people were leaning over the rail.

"The Board will say what it can," she said. "It'll not say more."

She did not look at a paper.

"The Board records that a thing has been dreamed, with a fever, by people who never heard of each other. By the Master Annick of the Even Hand, between 2446 and 2450, in a notebook she showed nobody. By the keeper at Ferren and the keeper at Saltmere, on the same night in 2604, in their remarks columns, because a fever isn't a fault. By the keeper at Westerley, that night, in the morning, to his wife. By Mr. Ambrose Halden, that night and eleven times since. By the Master Thrale, three times. By a lamp-man at Holloway on the 17th of the ninth month last year, who's asked his name not be printed, and who'll have his wish. And by Miss Kiva Rowan, of Meridian, at thirteen and since, whose account the Board has had read to it from a page that was shut before she could have borrowed it from anybody." The Clerk paused. "The Board believes that page. It's had thirty years of pages in a great many rooms. It knows a shut one."

The Graving did not make a sound.

"The Board can't say what was dreamed," said the Clerk. "It's heard three readings of it this afternoon, and they don't agree, and none of the people who gave them say they're certain. The Board isn't going to be certain on their behalf." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, and was gone. "The Board has no name for it. It's been called a fever and a fault and a dream and a remark. None of those is right. The Master Annick had a phrase for it. The Board heard it read this afternoon from her own notebook, and it'll borrow it, for want of a better, and give it back the moment anybody finds one." She folded her hands. "What the Master called the sky unroofed. The Board will call it the unroofed sky, and it'll write that down, and it'll not say what it is, because nobody in this dock can."

Kiva sat on the second bench with Aske's hand on her wrist and her own hands burning in her lap, and heard four thousand people take a breath at once and not let it out.

"The Board warrants a Barrel response," said the Clerk, in exactly the same voice, "at the harbour pump house, at the dawn slack on Wednesday the 23rd, on the keeper's leave, which she's given. Under the Saltmere Company's officers. With the Municipal Works' reader on the landing, and the keeper's fitters on the cells, and the Order's thirty-six jars, given, at the price the Master said, which is written in the keeper's book and the Committee's name. Any four on the national roll may stand in it, by name, on the Board's warrant and the Board's ash, if it chooses. No four is obliged." She looked round the dock. "The sealed prediction in the Board's press will be opened at noon on Wednesday, as it was always going to be, in front of whoever Mrs. Rowan names and whoever the Board does. It'll be found right or wrong. The Board will write down which."

She looked at the clock.

"The foundation's claim is for the Compact's court. The Board has Mr. Renn's figures and Mr. Adlam's books, and it'll send both to the court tonight, with a line saying it read them." She looked at Noa. "Mr. Bexley's four columns go up together. The Board's looked at them for three days and it won't draw a line between them either. It'll send them to every pump house and lamp office on its roll, as they are, without a line." She paused. "The Board rises."

The Chair did not rise with it.

She sat at the end of the long table where she had sat all afternoon, with her ledger open in front of her, and while the Graving stood up round her in pieces from the top down and began to talk all at once, Kiva saw her turn to a page near the back of the ledger and take up her pen.

It was a page that had only two lines on it. Kiva had seen it once, in the autumn, upside down, across the Chair's desk. It was headed *Witnessed,* because there had been no column for her, and there was still no column for her.

The Chair wrote a third line. She wrote it sitting, in her level hand, while the Graving stood up around her, and blotted it, and turned the ledger round on the table, and held it so that Kiva could read it from the bench.

*Sunday the 20th of the third month, 2681. Put it on the table herself, under her own name, before anybody asked. Y.C.*

Kiva read it twice.

Then Aske stood up behind her, and put his hand under her arm, and said *now,* and she went.

---

The fever came up along the Long Quay, in the white lamps, as it had come a week ago.

She had expected it. She found that expecting it did not help. By the third bollard she was shaking, and by Quiller's door she could not have climbed the stair if Jude had not taken one arm and Perrin, against every card he had, the other, until Aske said *Cade* in a voice Kiva had never heard him use, and Anwen took Perrin's place without a word.

Aske had her on the bed by the north window inside a minute, with the candle lit and his watch out and Pru on her knees on the other side with her capped fingers on Kiva's wrist, very lightly, because she could not grip.

"A hundred and four," said Aske. "Going up." He put the back of his hand against her cheek, as her aunt used to. "Two of them. In one day. A man with his hand on a glass in the morning, and a woman with her back to the sea in the afternoon." He wrote it on the card, standing, against the window frame, and Kiva watched him write it and could not read it for the heat. "*The third this year. Two Glory masters inside six hours.* That's all I'll write. It's all I know."

"I knew," said Kiva. Her teeth were together. "On the quay. Before."

"I know you did. You sat in the far chair this morning and you stood up for her this afternoon anyway." He sat down on the upturned bucket. "I'll not say you shouldn't. I'll count."

She lay with her eyes open and her jaw shut and waited for the roof to go.

She knew how it would come, if it came. She had felt it come on a ferry with her hands on the rail. She lay with the candle burning and Aske counting and Pru's light fingers on her wrist and the quay lamps in a row along the black water outside the window, and the fever climbed through her like a tide coming in over flats, and she waited for the nothing and the roads and the lights, and for whatever was paying attention to pay it to her.

It did not come.

There was the loft's ceiling, dark, with its beams. There was the candle. There was the count, under the floor and the chandlery and the quay, four and four, going on. There was Aske's voice, saying a number every few minutes, and Pru's breathing, and somewhere at the far end of the loft somebody turning over who was not asleep.

At a quarter to seven, by Aske's watch, with the fever at a hundred and four and her teeth together, she took her left hand out from under the blanket and laid the first two fingers on the iron rail of the bed. She did it on purpose. It was the hour.

*It keeps time.*

"Write it," she said. "Please. I can't."

Pru took the book off the sill and the pencil on its string. She wrote it, with her capped fingers, very badly, worse than Kiva's left hand. *Sunday the 20th. A quarter to seven. The bed rail, the loft. Yes. Written by Finch.*

The fever broke a little after eleven, in a long sweat that soaked the blanket through, as it had on the barge and on the night after the Counting House. Aske said, "There," and wrote it, and Pru sat back on her heels with her hands held up in front of her face.

"Nothing," said Kiva.

She said it aloud, as she had said it a week ago. Her voice was very hoarse.

Aske looked at her over his spectacles in the candlelight.

"Nothing came," he said. It was not a question.

"Nothing came." She shut her eyes. "That's all. I'd have told you if it had. I'd tell anybody now."

He did not say anything to that for a while. Then he wrote one more line on the card, small, and this time he turned it round and showed her, because she had asked nobody to keep anything from her any more, and he seemed to know it.

*Fever, the third. Nothing came. Said so.*

---

At ten o'clock, while the fever was at its height, the Mercury's boy had come up Quay Street with his bag, and Jude had gone down and bought the late sheet, and stood at the window with it under the candle for a long time, reading, with his good arm on the frame.

He did not read it to her. He folded it and laid it on the sill. She saw it there when she opened her eyes at midnight, with the fever gone and the blanket soaked and Aske asleep at last on his bucket with his chin on his chest.

The headline went across the top of the folded sheet in letters as big as her thumb, and the candle lay on it.

*THE UNROOFED SKY.*

And under it, smaller: *The Board Names What Nobody Can Say. Two Masters and a Catcher. The Order Gives Its Cellar to the Bank. The Board Warrants Wednesday.* And under that, in a column with a line round it: *"Some Go Out. Some Go White." "I'll Not Have It Said I Saw More."*

She looked at it for a long time.

It was a name. It was not hers. It had been a Glory master's phrase in a notebook nobody had been let read, and a clerk had borrowed it in a dock for want of a better, and now it was in letters as big as her thumb on a penny sheet that a boy with ink on his thumbs had sold up and down Quay Street. By the morning it would be in every town on the coast. By the end of the week it would be in Meridian, on Coper Street, in the shop.

Everybody in this city knew that she dreamed. Everybody would know by Wednesday. Nobody knew what it was. The Board had said so, and so had the Proprietor, and so had the Master, and so had she.

She found that she did not mind it. She had thought that she would, for four years. She had kept her mouth shut for four years so that she would never have to lie in a bed and look at a sheet like this. And now she was lying in a bed looking at a sheet like this, and it was only a name. It did not say what the thing was. It did not say what she was. It said that a thing had been put on a table where anybody could see it, and that nobody owned it now, not her, not the Proprietor, not the Order, not the Board. It belonged to the lamp-man at Holloway as much as to anybody.

She put her hand inside her shirt and found the disk.

It was cold. It had been cold at the far end of the Proprietor's table that morning, and cold on the bench in the dock while the Master stood with her back to the sea, and cold through the fever. She had looked each time. She would write it in the morning, on the left, with her left hand. *Cold. Two masters. As before.* There was no crossing, so the fourth column was owed nothing. She would write it anyway.

At the far end of the loft somebody was still not asleep. She could see the shape of him at the south window, sitting up. Kellan. He had a card in his hand, a white one with a Halden mark, and he was looking at it in the light from the quay. She did not know what was on it. She would ask him in the morning. She thought he would tell her, and that it would come out as a sum.

Beside her bed, on the next one, three feet off, Selka Dray was lying on her back with her eyes open, looking at the ceiling. She had been there all evening. She had not said anything. Now, as Kiva turned her head, Dray turned hers.

"Wednesday," said Dray, very low. "If they ask the roll."

"Yes."

"I'll be there." Dray looked back at the ceiling. "On my feet. I'd sooner you heard it from me."

She shut her eyes.

Kiva lay with her hand on the cold disk and her eyes open.

Outside the window, under the white lamps on the Long Quay and the yellow ones in the low town, Saltmere went on keeping its count. Four and four. Fifty-seven to the minute, or faster. At the end of the Mole, in a swept hut on the sea side where nothing kept time, a woman in grey who had broken her house's only rule was lying awake with her hands folded, and two sisters were writing it in a book. On a cart at the seaward end of the Graving, under a grey cloth, thirty-six jars stood in their straw, given. On the second floor of a pump house a keeper had written a line in her great-grandfather's book and gone to bed for the first time since Tuesday. In a tall grey house on the Long Quay, every window lit, a man of a hundred and three sat with his left hand flat on a glass case and his right over his eyes, and nobody saw him.

In the Board's press, in a black iron box with three locks, there was a paper with a seal on it and the hour, which would be opened at noon on Wednesday. At dawn on Wednesday, if two people were right, the basin would go still at nine minutes past six. There were three days.

And after that there was Rhea Sorn's last night on the van, and the last of the third month, and a card that had not been written yet, and either of them might say no.

The Measure was read. Seven was seventh. The unroofed sky had a name and no meaning.

She slept at last with her hand on the disk, and dreamed of nothing at all.
