# Chapter 10 — Kiva: Uphill

There were twelve juniors on the Old Yard at half past six on Tuesday morning, in grey smocks, with their breath going up, and every one of them had been told that she was a husk.

"You stamp on one," said Kiva. "All together. Then you lean, on two, toward the sheds. Back on three. And four's a stamp, but lighter. Like this."

She did it. She did it badly, because her knee was strapped and she had never in her life marched in step with anybody; but they watched her the way juniors watch a second-year, with their mouths a little open, and when she had done it twice they did it after her, and on the third time the twelve of them came down on the frozen dirt together with a crunch like a cart going over gravel.

"That's horrible," said the sandy-voiced one, with great satisfaction. "Do it again."

Her name was Bettony. She had a plait to her waist and a gap in her teeth, and she had sat on the shed step in the autumn and asked what *declared* meant, and she had come up the hill in the dark this morning with eleven others behind her because somebody had told her that the Marshal might come.

"The Marshal's not coming till Thursday," said Kiva.

"Then we'll be good by Thursday."

They were put in three files of four, each file a husk, with a junior at the front to hold the count and three behind to keep it. Kiva gave them the rule that she had worked out on the tram, and that Coombe had not given anybody, because Coombe had not stayed long enough to be asked. *When one of you is cut out of time, that file sits down. The other two files stop dead, and you count to three under your breath, and then you find your count again and come on. When one of you is cut in time, nobody sits down. You lean in, and you go on.*

"How do we know if it's in time?" said Bettony.

"You'll know. You'll be stamping when it comes."

Jude stood at the north end of the lane with a broom handle in his fist for an edge. He was still limping. The Wren House had put a white salve on the burn and bound it, and it had stiffened in the night, and he moved like a man getting out of a cold bath; but he had come up the hill at six without being asked and stood on the frost waiting, and when Kiva asked whether he ought to be on his feet, he said that a Kill who couldn't stand on a Tuesday wasn't going to be much use on the twelfth.

Perrin stood on the shed step with nothing in his hands.

He had been told to do that. He had told himself, on Monday night, aloud, at the kitchen table, and he had come up the hill and stood on the step and put his hands in his pockets, and he looked like a man who has been asked to wait in a corridor while a thing is done to somebody he loves.

"I'd like it known that this is the hardest thing I've ever been asked," he said. "Harder than three-eighty. I'm made to lift things. I'm not made to stand on a step."

"Count," said Anwen.

"I'm counting. *One, and. Two, and.*"

The three files stamped. Jude walked in among them with his broom handle, slow, his head on one side.

"*Three, and. Four, and—*"

He tapped the front junior of the nearest file on the shoulder, on the *and.*

The file sat down. It sat down so suddenly that two of them sat on each other, and somebody shrieked, and the other two files stopped dead with their feet in the air, exactly as they had been told.

*One. Two. Three,* said twenty-four lips, under twelve breaths.

Then the middle file stamped. The right-hand file stamped half a beat after it. And for perhaps four counts there were two husks on that yard that were not keeping time with each other at all, one heavy and one late, stamping and leaning and treading on their own heels; and Kiva, who was on her knees on the north side with a cloth on her shoulder and no ash under it, felt something in her chest go tight and glad.

"There," she said. "Look. They've each found a count, and it isn't the same one."

"Which one do I count?" said Perrin.

Nobody answered him.

He stood on the step with his hands in his pockets, and the two files stamped out of step with each other in front of him, and he looked from one to the other with his lips moving and nothing coming out of them. Then he said, quite loudly, "*One*—" on the middle file's stamp, and then "*One*—" on the right-hand file's, and stopped.

"You can't count two," said Jude, from among them.

"I know I can't count two. I'm finding that out."

"Then pick one."

"Which?"

"The one that's nearest me," said Jude. "I can't cut two either."

It went like that all morning. It went badly, and then it went less badly, and then for a quarter of an hour it went worse than it had gone at the start, because the juniors had learned to change their count when a file sat down, and twelve children were a great deal better at changing a count than three husks had been. By eight Perrin had lost the count four times and found it three. Jude had cut eleven juniors on the *and* and three on the beat, and the three on the beat had leaned in on him with their arms out, cackling, and pretended to freeze his boots. Anwen had stood behind Perrin with her eyes on the files' feet, not on him, counting under her breath, and twice when Perrin lost it she had said *yours* to him, and he had said *mine* back, and she had taken it up without a gap, off the feet.

"She was counting before," said Pru, who had a turnip watch and a slate. "Before he lost it. Like I said."

"You said it on Monday," said Kiva. "It's on a sheet."

"I know where it is. I'm only saying it worked."

Bettony came over at eight, red-faced and limping in the way that the juniors had all begun to limp, out of sympathy with Jude.

"The tall one cuts heavy," she said. "He doesn't mean to. But when he taps you, it's like being leaned on by a door."

"He's a Kill."

"I know. I'm only saying." She looked at Kiva's knees on the frost, and the empty cloth. "Why are you down there? There's nothing to catch."

"There's nothing to catch today. When there is, I'll be here."

Bettony considered that. Kiva watched her decide whether it was a good answer, and then decide that it was an answer, and then go back to her file. It was a thing that she had noticed about juniors: they took one at its word. One could not stand in front of twelve of them and say a thing that was not so; they would do it.

She wrote it down on the shed step, while the juniors went in to breakfast and Perrin sat with his head against the doorpost and his eyes shut.

*Pack drill, with juniors. Went wrong: a broken pack comes back as two counts, not one. The captain can count one. Second try: he counts the one nearest the Kill, and says which, aloud, so that the Kill knows which he's on. A.P. counts behind him off their feet, and takes it by "yours" and "mine."*

"*Says which,*" said Perrin, without opening his eyes. "I'd like it known that I found that one."

"You found it."

"Put my initials."

She put them.

---

Coombe came to the Old Yard on Wednesday at two, and she did not say that she had come.

She was simply on the shed step when Kiva looked round, as Noa was always simply at the end of a notice board, with her grey coat buttoned and a fresh length of tarred rope in her hand. Orla was on her crate. Beside Orla, on the other end of it, in his coat, with his two arms laid along his knees and bound white to the elbow, sat Pike.

He had not been asked. He had come on the Kiln tram and walked up from the corner with Greta Stroud at his elbow, and Stroud had gone back down again.

"I'm not here," he said.

"You said that on Saturday," said Orla.

"It was true on Saturday."

The Marshal looked at the two of them on the crate, and at the bound arms. She did not ask. She tied a knot.

"Show me," she said.

So they showed her the sheet.

It was Mrs. Pryce's double, grey with washing, with its seam down the middle; it had come back from Saturday unharmed and been washed again on Monday as a matter of principle. Dilys had come up the hill with it in a basket, as she had come on Saturday, and stood at the far end with Anwen, a corner each. Gerald hung from his pole across the trestles. There were four sandbags by the south row.

Pru threw the cloth. Jude, limping, slung a sandbag onto the far hem before Gerald dropped, as Pike had said that he would try. Gerald came down in the upper middle of the sheet with his sound of a dropped mattress.

"*Dark! One!*"

Kiva had the left hem. She said nothing. She was looking down the lane at the far edge, where Dilys stood grinning with her corner, and the far edge did not lift, because it had a hundredweight of sand lying across it.

"*Corners,*" said Pru, at her ten.

The neck came up. The cord went round. It was clean.

"Again," said Coombe.

It was clean again. It was clean five times out of five, with the weight first and the cloth after and Kiva's two words never said, because there was never anything to say them about.

"Very nice," said the Marshal.

Kiva did not like the way that she said it.

"Now," said Coombe. "Make it walk."

---

"Walk?" said Perrin.

"It walks. The thing that laid that sheet. It's on its feet." Coombe had not moved off the step. "It laid its skin down behind it on a wharf because it was standing on a wharf, and it stood still because there was a gate in front of it that it liked. On the Longshore there's nothing to like. They walk, and the skin comes after, as a cloak comes after a man. Show me what your sacks do when the thing walks."

They looked at one another.

"Talley, Cade," said Coombe. "You're the string. Take your hundredweight on its pole between you. Tie that sheet's top hem to your belts with a yard of cord, so that it trails behind you on the ground. Walk up the yard. Somebody cuts your rung when I say. Rowan's on the far edge. Go."

It took them five minutes to rig. Jude and Perrin stood shoulder to shoulder with Gerald's pole across both their necks, like a yoke, and the sheet tied to their belts behind; and when they walked, the sheet came after them up the frozen dirt with a soft dry hiss, and the four sandbags that Jude had slung onto its far hem came with it, dragged, bumping, like a sledge.

"Cut," said Coombe.

Pru let Gerald go. He came down behind them, on the sheet, as a rung comes down at a string's heels.

"*Dark! One!*"

And the string kept walking.

It did not mean to. Perrin and Jude had been told to walk, and nobody had told them to stop, and so they went on, two paces, three, with the sheet tied to their belts; and the sheet went with them, and Gerald on it, and the sandbags along its far edge; and the whole lot slid up the yard toward the sheds, away from Pru's hands, away from Kiva's knee, like a tablecloth pulled off a table with the supper still on it.

"*Far edge!*" said Kiva. It came out of her before she knew. "It's going!"

It was. It was all going. The far edge had a hundredweight of sand along it and it did not lift, and it did not fold, and it did not need to; it simply went, with everything on it, away from everybody who had meant to catch it.

"Stop," said Coombe.

Perrin and Jude stopped. Perrin looked over his shoulder at the sheet, a yard behind his heels, with Gerald lying on it and four sandbags at its far end and nobody's hands within three paces of any of it.

"Oh," he said.

"That's a rung gone," said Coombe. "On a moving thing, with your weight on its skin, you've pinned the skin, and the skin's tied to the thing, and the thing's walking. So your weight goes with it." She tied a knot. "On the Longshore we lost a cloth that way the first season. Then we lost a man's hand, the second, trying to get it back. It's the same lesson that your catcher learned on a wharf. A thing that folds from the far edge doesn't need to fold if it can walk away with the whole cloth."

Nobody said anything.

Kiva was looking at the yard. She had been looking at it for some time, while the others looked at the sheet.

The Old Yard was not flat. Nothing in Meridian was flat; the city was built on a hill, and every yard in it fell somewhere. This one fell toward the bottom wall. She had known it since the spring. She had stood on it a hundred times with her still door open, and she had felt it through the soles of her feet as one feels which way a ship is listing; a hand in ten paces, perhaps, down from the sheds to the wall, and the water that ran off it in a thaw ran down the lane and out under the wall through a drain the size of a fist.

On the wharf the ground had fallen south, a hand in two paces, to the river. Pike had read it with his heel. *If a rung runs here, it runs to the river.* And the white skin had lain downhill of the thing's legs, on the river side, where the rung would run.

"Ma'am," she said.

"Rowan."

"Which way does the rung go? When it's cut. On a wharf, it ran downhill. On the cloth. Since the skin was downhill."

"The rung goes where rungs go," said Coombe. "Down, and then to the low side, while it's open. Your catcher said so. He's written it; it's in your columns."

"Then the skin's only under the rung if it's downhill of the thing."

"Yes."

"And it's downhill if the thing's facing uphill. Since it trails behind."

"Yes."

"So if the thing were facing downhill—" Kiva heard her voice go up, and brought it down again. "If it were facing downhill, the skin would be behind it, uphill. And the rung would come down at its heels and run the other way. Away from the skin. Onto ground."

The Marshal did not say *yes.* She did not say anything. She looked at Kiva for a long moment with her pale steady eyes, and then she turned them on Pike.

Pike was sitting on the crate with his bound arms on his knees, looking at the frozen yard. He was looking at it as Kiva had seen him look at a road, before he threw a cloth. His square grey face had gone very still.

"Make it carry its own sheet uphill," said Coombe. "That's what we say on the Longshore. We've said it for three seasons. Nobody's written it down, and the Board's printer has a page of mine on it that it won't print until the Measure's over, in case it's wrong." She tied a knot. "How do you make a thing face downhill when it wants to face something else?"

"You give it something to face," said Anwen.

She was at the far end, with her corner. She had let it go. Kiva had not seen her let it go.

"A gate," said Anwen. "It liked the gate on the wharf. It walked up to it and leaned. So you put the gate where you want it to face, and it walks to it, and it faces the way it walked."

"And if it's already facing the wrong way? Already walking? With its skin laid down?"

"Then you move the gate." Anwen had gone a little pale. "You walk it. Round. While it's leaning on it. It follows the wall. And when it's facing downhill, you stop, and it stops, and its skin's behind it, uphill."

"Who calls that?"

Anwen opened her mouth, and looked at Kiva, as she had looked at Kiva for every call since the spring.

Then she shut it.

"I'd want to," she said. "I'd want to call it myself. I'm in the wall. I can feel where it's leaning. I'll know before anybody whether it's following or not." Her voice was quite level and very quick, the way it went when a wall had a thin place in it. "Rowan reads the fall. She says *downhill's south,* or whichever. Then it's mine. I walk it round and I say *stopped* when it's facing, and nobody cuts until I say."

"Then do it," said Coombe.

---

It took them the rest of the afternoon, and it cost Anwen two-tenths and more off the book.

They rigged it again. Jude and Perrin were the string, with Gerald on his yoke and the sheet on its cords behind them; and this time Anwen brought a wall up in front of them, waist high, silver, across the lane at the shed end, and they walked up to it, as they had been told that a string would, and leaned their yoke on it.

Kiva opened her still door on the lane under her right heel, wide, by its corners, and felt the yard go down away from her toward the bottom wall, as plain as a stair.

"South-west," she said. "A hand in ten. The bottom wall's low."

"South-west," said Anwen. "Mine."

She began to walk.

Kiva had seen her walk a wall before. She had seen her come down the length of Mace's line on a night in the autumn, step by step, with the silver going along in front of her like a curtain on a rail. This was not that. This was turning a thing that was leaning on the curtain, with its whole weight, and wanted to stay where it was.

The wall moved a foot. The yoke came with it. Perrin and Jude shuffled, as they had been told to shuffle, a foot, with their heads down, following the light.

Then Anwen's wall moved another foot, and Jude, who had been told to be a string and not a student, leaned the other way.

The wall bent. It did not break. It bowed in the middle like a sail, and Anwen's heels went back in the frost, and her face went white and set.

"*He's leaning off,*" she said. "Talley. Off."

"I'm a string," said Jude. "I've decided I don't like being turned."

"Then I'll turn you anyway." She took a step, and the wall went with her, and the yoke came round another foot. "Rowan. Where's south-west?"

"Half round. Another quarter."

"*Mine.*"

It took a long time. It took, by Pru's turnip, two minutes and twenty seconds to walk a lazy string through half a circle on a frozen yard, and at the end of it Anwen was standing at the bottom end of the lane with her hands shaking and her wall in front of her, and Jude and Perrin were leaning their yoke on it, facing the bottom wall, and the sheet lay behind them on the frost on its cords, uphill, toward the sheds.

"*Stopped,*" said Anwen.

"Cut," said Coombe.

Pru let Gerald go.

He came down behind the string's heels, on the sheet's near edge, and lay there; and Kiva, who was kneeling a pace downhill with her own cloth, thought for one sick moment that it had not worked. Then Pru, who had been told to make the rung *run* as an open rung runs, put her boot to him and rolled him off the near edge of the sheet and down the fall of the yard, a yard, two yards, to the low side, onto bare frozen dirt.

Kiva's cloth went over him at the first cast.

"*Dark! One!*"

There was nothing under him but ground. There was nothing behind her to fold, and nothing at the far edge of her cloth but the bottom of the Old Yard, and she knelt on the hem with her two dead fingers lying on the canvas and called the corners at her ten, as a catcher does on a quiet night.

"*Corners.*"

Pru tied. It was clean.

"Again," said Coombe.

They did it again. They did it five times in all, and it went wrong twice. Once Anwen's wall bowed so far that it broke in the middle and she had to bring it up again from nothing, and the string walked off south in the meantime, dragging its sheet, and Kiva had to say *far edge* and Coombe said *stop.* Once the turn went right and the cut was early, before Anwen had said *stopped,* because Pru's hand on the hoist had slipped; and the rung came down half on the sheet and half off, and lay across the near edge with its top on the skin, and Kiva looked at it and could not tell which way it would go.

"*Far edge,*" she said. "I don't know. It's on the hem."

"Then you don't throw," said Coombe. "You wait. You let it run, and you throw where it runs to. If it doesn't run, if it lies on the skin, you put your weight on the far edge and pray. Weight's not a mistake. Weight's what you do when the turn's failed." She tied a knot. "Your catcher's notion was a good one. It's the second line. It isn't the first."

On the fifth, it went as it had gone on the first.

Jude and Perrin unyoked themselves, and sat down on the frost, and Perrin put his hand to his ribs and said that they were telling him things. Dilys came down the yard with her corner of the sheet bunched up in her arms and said that she had never seen a bedsheet do so much and that she'd not dare tell her mistress. Anwen stood at the bottom of the lane where her wall had been, with nothing up, looking at her own hands.

"*Cost,*" said Jude, from the frost. He had the book open on his knee. "Pryce. Three turns and two halves. I'd call it two-tenths."

"Two and four," said Anwen. "I'll not be rounded."

"Two and four." He wrote. "*Held, eight and eighty-one.*"

Coombe came down off the step at last.

She did not go to Anwen, or to Kiva, or to the sheet. She walked along the south row, with her rope in her hand, to the crate where Orla and Pike were sitting, and stood in front of Pike.

"You put your heel on it," she said.

"I did."

"I had a man on the Longshore did the same, three seasons gone. Twenty years a catcher, and a good one. He called it ground and laid on it." The Marshal's voice did not change. "He's a lock-keeper on the canal at Hollins now. He keeps a very tidy lock."

Pike looked at his arms.

"Was there a page," he said, "in your book, on it? When you had him?"

"There was a line. *A hem that lies down is ground.* The same line as yours. It was the right line for forty years." Coombe tied a knot. "I've written a page. The printer has it. It says to make the thing carry its sheet uphill, and if that fails, to put dead weight on the far edge. It says the weight was Graywater's, by name, at a wharf below the Hythe. I had that off your Torch's slate. I don't steal."

"The weight was a laundress's."

"Then I'll have the printer put a laundress." For the first time Kiva saw something move in that weathered face, at one corner, and go again. "And the far edge?"

"The second has it," said Pike. "Two words, once. My Torch wrote that on a slate at four in the morning, and I've thought since that it was the best thing she ever wrote."

"Whose second?"

Pike nodded down the yard at Kiva, without turning his head.

Coombe looked. She looked for some time.

"I've never heard it put so," she said at last. "I've stood on the Longshore for thirty years and never heard anybody put it so. We give the far edge to the corner-hand on that side. He's got a corner in his fist and he's hauling, and he hasn't the time to look." She turned back to Pike. "I'll put that in too."

"Put her name."

"I'll put the second's. It's a place, not a person. A place is what the printer wants." The Marshal looked at the two white bound arms. "How long?"

"Six weeks. Four to go."

"And you came up a hill on a tram to sit on a box and watch somebody else's four turn a bedsheet."

"I'm not here," said Pike.

"No," said Coombe. "I can see that you're not."

She went back up the yard. On the shed step she stopped, as she had stopped on the Pit's stair, and said over her shoulder, "Pryce."

Anwen's head came up.

"That was yours. You called it, and you said it before anybody asked. On the Longshore the wall leads in a turn, and the catcher reads the ground, and the blade waits. I'd have told you that on Thursday if you hadn't found it." She tied a knot. "You found it. I'll not tell you."

---

Anwen Pryce went down to the Graywater hall yard that night at eight, alone, with her coat buttoned to the neck and her hands in her sleeves, and she did not tell anybody that she was going.

The hall yard was lit under its one hooded lamp. Doran Mace had his line out on the cinders, as he had most nights that the van was not called: six wards in slate blue, Tibb and Stiles and Greta Stroud and three more, walking a wall along the length of the yard and back, slow, as a crew rows when the boat is going nowhere. She had walked forty paces of that wall in the first month. She knew how it went. She had not come for that.

"Pryce," said Mace. He did not stop the line. "It's Wednesday."

"I know what day it is."

"You've a place for the summer. It's the summer you've a place for. It's the second month."

"I want to learn to turn it," said Anwen.

He looked at her then.

He was a big slow man with a board under his arm, and he had been the ward-chief on that van for longer than she had been alive, and in the autumn he had left her name where it was on his line, with a note beside it that said she had said so as she went. She had never asked him for anything. She had told him things. She had found, since the spring, that it was easier to tell people things than to ask them, and she had been thinking about that on the tram.

"Turn it," he said.

"A wall. With a thing leaning on it. Round, so that it faces where I want." She kept her hands in her sleeves. "I did it today, on a yard, with two boys and a sandbag. It took me two minutes and twenty seconds to get half round, and the wall bowed, and once it broke. A string's heavier than Talley. It's heavier than Talley and Cade together."

"It is."

"On the coast they say the wall leads in a turn. The Marshal said so."

"Did she." Mace said it without any expression. "I'd heard she was in the city."

"She was in our yard."

"Then you've had better teaching this afternoon than I could give you in a month, and you've come down here at eight at night to ask me for more." He held up his hand, and the line stopped, and six wards stood along the cinders with their wall up and looked at the two of them with frank interest. "Why?"

Anwen had known that he would ask. She had decided on the tram that she would answer the true thing and not the tidy one, because she had found that she minded when other people gave her the tidy one.

"Because I didn't know I'd want to call it until I opened my mouth," she said. "I've been in a wall since I was fourteen. A wall stands where it's told. In the spring it was told by Rowan, and before that by the Hold-master, and in the summer by you. I'm good at it. I've the best Hold in the Third Division, and I've stood where I was put for three years and been proud of it." Her voice was quite level. "Today the Marshal asked who'd call the turn, and I looked at Rowan, as I've looked at Rowan for every call since the fifth month. And then I didn't. I wanted it. I wanted to be the one who said *stopped.*"

"And now you've said it, you're frightened you'll be wrong."

"No," said Anwen. "Now I've said it, I'm frightened I'll be slow."

Mace was quiet for a moment. Then he said, without turning his head, "Stroud. You've the end."

Greta Stroud came off the line with her hands still up, and the end of the wall went with her, silver, a yard of it, bending at the corner where it left the others.

"Bring it here," said Mace. "Pryce. Put yours against it. Lean."

Anwen brought her wall up.

It came at her hips, low, as it always came, and she put it against the end of Stroud's and leaned, and Stroud leaned back. It was like leaning on a door with somebody behind it.

"Now turn her," said Mace.

She tried. She took a step to the left and the wall went with her, and Stroud's end bent and slid along it, and stopped. Stroud was a Graywater ward of fifteen years and a woman who had held the end of a line on a coal wharf with a string's whole weight against it. She did not mean to be turned.

"You're walking," said Mace. "That's your fault. You think a wall goes where your feet go. It doesn't. It goes where your hands go, and your feet catch up." He came and stood behind her, not touching. "Your hands. Not your feet. Turn the wall from the far end first. The end that's away from you. Bring that round, and the near end will come of itself. A wall's a sheet, Pryce. It's a long one, and it's stiff, and it's standing up, but it's a sheet."

Anwen stood very still.

"Fold it from the far edge," she said.

"I've no notion what you're talking about. I'm telling you how a wall turns." Mace had his board under his arm. "Try."

She tried. She kept her feet where they were. She brought the far end of her wall round first, a hand, and then another, as one brings the far corner of a sheet over the top on a laundry table, with her arms wide; and the near end of the wall pivoted on its own foot, at her hip, and came round after, and Stroud's wall came with it, sliding, and Stroud said *oh* under her breath, and was turned.

"Again," said Mace.

She did it again. She did it four times, and the fourth time she did not look at her feet at all, and she brought the far end round and said *stopped* before she knew that she was going to, and Stroud's wall stopped on the word, as though it had been told.

"That's the trick," said Mace. "You'll not have it on a string by Thursday week. You'll have it by the summer, if you come down here on nights that aren't yours and annoy my line." He held up his hand, and the line began to walk again behind him. "Pryce."

"Yes."

"You wanted it. You said so. I'd have you say it to Rowan as well, before you say *stopped* on a floor in front of a Blaze. She's your catcher. She's used to having the call."

"I know she is," said Anwen. "That's why I'll say it."

She went up the hill on the last tram with her arms aching to the shoulder and her coat open, because she was warm. She was thinking about her mother's hip against a table, and a far corner brought over the top, and the queer comfort of having been told by a big slow man with a board that the thing she had been proudest of for three years was a sheet.

She had not known that a wall could lead. She had supposed, without ever saying it to herself, that a wall was the thing that stood in front of the people who did.

She thought that she might tell Kiva on Thursday, before the yard. She found that she did not want to wait until Thursday. She got off at Coper Street instead of going on, and went up the shop stair, and knocked.

---

Kiva was at the kitchen table, writing the turn into seven columns, when Anwen came in with her coat open.

"I want it said," said Anwen, before she had sat down. "I'm calling the turn. On a floor. On our floor, on the twelfth. Not on your word. On mine."

Kiva put down the pencil.

"I know. You called it today."

"I called it today on a yard. I want it said before there's a Blaze watching, so that you can say no." Anwen sat down, rather suddenly, as though her knees had decided. "You've had the call since the fifth month. On the litter, on the hill, at Stopgate. I've stood where you said for a year. I'm not saying it was wrong. I'm saying I've found a thing that's mine, and it's in the middle of a call you used to have, and I'd not take it off you without asking."

Kiva looked at her.

She looked at the brown coat, open, and the hair pinned for a ward line, and the hands that had stopped shaking at last; and she thought of a tool shed in the spring, and a girl who would not speak to her for six weeks, and a ward that had held at the end of a line on a hill while the litter went over.

"It isn't a thing I had," she said. "It's a thing nobody had. I read the ground. That's mine; I've a door that does it. The wall's yours. I never knew where a wall was leaning. I'd have had to ask you."

"You never did ask."

"No. I said *mine* and you held. I never once asked whether you could feel which way it was going." Kiva turned the sheet round on the oilcloth, so that Anwen could read it. "There. I've written it as yours. Under *How.* I'd written it before you knocked."

Anwen read it.

*How: R. reads the fall, aloud, by the compass. A.P. walks the wall round from its far end, and the thing follows. A.P. says "stopped." Nobody cuts until she has. If the turn fails, weight on the far edge (Pike's), and the second has it.*

She read it twice. Kiva watched her go red, slowly, from the collar, exactly as her mother had gone red at a laundry table.

"*From its far end,*" she said. "How did you know?"

"I didn't. I watched you do it this afternoon. You brought the end away from you round first, every time it worked. The two times it failed, you walked."

"Mace told me that tonight. He said a wall's a sheet."

"Then he's right," said Kiva, "and I'll put him in the seventh column."

Mara, at the far end of the table, did not look up from the tide table, but Kiva saw her pencil stop. It stayed stopped for some time.
