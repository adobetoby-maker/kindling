# Chapter 29 — Kiva: The Longshore

"Warm," said Aske. "Three mornings."

It was six on Wednesday, and the loft was dark except for the candle on the sill by the north window, and the window was grey. Aske held the hand for two minutes by his watch, as he always did, and then for one more, and then he let it go and wrote.

*Warm at six on Wednesday the 16th. Three mornings. Thursday, if wanted, and the price said first.*

He looked at the line.

"Today's your Ember," he said. "Declared, single, all day, off your own thimble. It's not your hand. It's not a crossing. There's no gap in you anywhere today, and I'd like there not to be." He capped his pen. "If anybody on that mud asks you for a second door, you'll say no, and if they ask twice you'll send them to me."

"Nobody will ask."

"Somebody always asks," said Aske. "On a beach, with the tide coming. I've been on beaches." He put the card in his bag. "I'll be at the Board's tent on the causeway with the other healers, a quarter of a mile from anywhere. I'll be no use to anybody till they're carried to me. Remember that."

He went to Perrin's bed. Perrin was sitting on it in the half dark with his shirt off and Jude winding the linen round him again, tight, and Perrin's face set at the hinge of the jaw.

"Morning and night," said Aske, and put his hands flat on the linen and listened. "As before. Don't breathe deep. Don't laugh. Don't, above all, lift anything." He took his hands away. "You may hold a glass. A small one."

---

The Longshore began where the Mole ended.

Kiva had walked out along it once before, at two in the morning with Jude and a lantern, and then it had been black flats and a pale line of sea. Now, at half past seven, it was grey. The causeway ran out from the end of the Mole across the flats, an old raised track of stone wide enough for a cart, and on either side of it there was nothing but mud and sky. The mud was grey and wet and shining, and cut everywhere with creeks, some as narrow as a ditch and some as wide as a street, all running seaward full of brown water. The tide was going out. It had been going out since six and would go on going out till noon. Far off, at the edge of everything, there was a line of white where the sea was.

The wind was in the south-west, off the sea. It came across the flats in gusts, cold, smelling of salt and weed, and when it came it pushed the water in the creeks back a little against the ebb, so that the brown surface wrinkled and stood up in little ridges and then lay down again.

"*Gusty,*" said Jude, at the causeway's edge. He had his arm back in its linen, and he was looking at the creeks as other people look at a crowd, to see which way it would go. "The bosun said it'd be gusty. He said a gusty ebb is a bad ebb, because the water doesn't know which way it's going."

The Board had put its tent at the landward end of the causeway, where it left the Mole. It was the same grey tent from the timber pond, carried out on a cart. There was a comb table in front of it, and a row of clerks, and every four in the Measure stood in a queue along the causeway and laid out its tins to be combed empty. Behind the tent there was a second, smaller tent, with a red cross painted on its flap and a row of stretchers outside it. Aske was already there, with his bag, talking to a Company healer in green.

And along the top of the Mole, all the way back to the town, there were people.

They stood along its parapet in a dark line against the sky, as far as Kiva could see, the low town and the high town both, in shawls and caps and coats, with children on shoulders and glasses to their eyes. There was a boat in the harbour mouth with the Mercury's flag on it, and a man in its bow with a long glass on a stand. There were more people on the causeway itself, roped off at the tent, a hundred, two hundred.

"They've come to see the worst of it," said Orla.

She had come out on the Circuit's cart with Aske and the clerks, and she sat on the cart's tailboard with her stick across her knees. She had not been asked. Nobody had expected her not to come.

"The worst?"

"The Hunt's the only board you can lose somebody on," said Orla. "They know that. They've known it longer than we have. That's why they come." She looked out at the flats. "There's nobody in the low town who hasn't got somebody in the Company, or had."

Coombe came along the causeway at a quarter to eight in her grey Board coat, with the rope in her hands.

Kiva had not seen her since the second month. She looked exactly the same. She walked as if the causeway were a quay she was counting heads on, slowly, and her pale eyes went over the fours in their colours one after another without stopping on any of them. She stopped at the comb table. The Clerk was there, in her own grey coat with the gold chain, and they did not greet each other. They stood side by side, two women in the Board's grey, and the Clerk read the rules.

"The Hunt," said the Clerk. "The third board. Twelve beats on the Longshore, two fours to a beat, one officer to each, by the Board's draw. A four works on its officer's word and on nothing else. Nothing is lit without it. Nothing is cut without it. A four does not go onto the mud below a form on running water. A person down on the mud is the officer's crew's; the fours stand off. Students close no tear." She did not look at a paper. "Every bag is combed at the gate. A four is marked on its quarter of its clean yield, after the Board's half and the officer's company's quarter, and less a dram on the mark for every remark on its officer's slate. The Hunt closes at three, or when the flood is over the flats, whichever comes first."

She stepped back. It was Coombe's turn.

Coombe did not step forward. She stood where she was by the comb table with her rope, and she began to tie a knot in it, and while she tied it she spoke.

"Who has your far edge?" she said.

Nobody answered. It was so sudden that nobody knew whom she had asked.

"Every catcher on this causeway," said Coombe. "In order of the draw. Say who has your far edge. Say it aloud, so that your officer hears it, and your four, and I do." She did not look up from her knot. "Gannet Creek. Old Four."

There was a moment. Then Old Four's catcher, a thin boy Kiva did not know, said, "The corner-hand, ma'am. On the far side. By the book."

"By the book," said Coombe. "Westerley."

"The corner-hand, ma'am."

"Brine Reach."

It went down the causeway, four after four. *The corner-hand. The corner-hand, ma'am. By the book.* Once a Holloway catcher said *me,* and Coombe said *and who has your heel,* and the catcher said *nobody, ma'am,* and Coombe said *say that to your officer, not to me.* Twenty-Eight's catcher was Marten, who said *the corner-hand* and then, after a moment, *and Dray, if she's free,* and Dray's face did what it always did, which was nothing.

"Mallow Creek," said Coombe. "Team Seven."

Kiva had known it would come. She had known what she would say since Coombe opened her mouth.

"Finch, ma'am," she said. "The second. She kneels at my other side and has the far edge, and says it once."

Coombe's hands stopped on the rope.

"The second," she said. "A place."

"A place, ma'am. Today it's Finch."

Coombe looked at her for the first time. It was a long look, the pale steady eyes going over Kiva as they went over a street, and then going on to Pru, who was standing beside her with the cord at her belt and her face going slowly scarlet from the neck up.

"Finch," said Coombe.

"Ma'am," said Pru.

"You'll say it once."

"Once, ma'am. Two words." Pru's voice did not shake. "I've had it from Pike."

Coombe tied her knot. She went on to the next.

"The Tarn."

There was a pause. Kiva turned her head. Bridie Hebden was standing at the front of the Tarn's four in her brown and buff, with her plaits pinned up round her head and a cord at her belt that was too long for her, wound twice round her wrist. Trewin was beside her with his hat in his hands.

"The corner-hand, ma'am," said Bridie. Her quick northern voice was very clear. "By the book."

"By the book," said Coombe.

She did not say anything else. She tied another knot. Kiva watched her hands, and saw them stop on the rope for the space of a breath, and then go on.

"Skerry Pill. Halden License."

"The corner-hand, ma'am," said Yarrow, for Halden.

"The Ropewalk."

"Me," said Treen.

She said it at once, small and dark, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her quick eyes on Coombe's face. "I have the far edge, ma'am. And the call. Run and lay. I always have."

"And who's watching the lodger in the pill's mouth?"

"Me, ma'am."

"That's two places," said Coombe.

"I've two eyes, ma'am."

"So had a man of mine," said Coombe. She did not say it unkindly. She said it as a fact about the tide. Then she went on down the causeway to the next beat, and the next, until every catcher in the Measure had said it aloud, and then she stopped at the end of the line and looked back along the whole length of it, with her rope in her hands.

"My page is with the Board's printer," she said. "It's been there since the second month. It's not the book. I wrote it, and I'd do what it says, and it's not the book." She looked along them all. "Your officer's slate is the book today. Your officer's word. I'll be on this causeway. I'll not be on your beat unless you're in trouble, and if you're in trouble, I'll be late. A quarter of a mile is a long way on mud." She coiled the rope. "That's said once. I'll not say it again."

She went away to the Board's tent and did not come out.

---

Perrin declared at the comb table at eight, in his flat clear voice, with his coat buttoned over the linen.

"*Team Seven. Rowan, Ember, declared, by her captain's voice. Finch, ward, catcher, for Cade. Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Cade stands down, on his healer's card, and captains from the hard.*"

A murmur went along the causeway. It was not the warm sound. It was the sound of people telling each other what they had heard. *Ember. Not stance. Ember.* Kiva heard somebody in green say *what's she want an Ember for on a beach,* and somebody else say *they've no plant, they've got no plant at all.*

The clerk at the comb table wrote it down. He combed Seven's tins, all six, one after another, and they were empty, and he said so.

"Mallow Creek," he said. "Yeo's. Out past the second groyne and right along the spur. You'll see the Company's flag."

---

Yeo was waiting at the end of the spur with her notebook shut.

The spur was an old cart track of stone that went off the causeway at a right angle, a quarter of a mile out, and ran along the east bank of Mallow Creek toward the sea. The creek ran beside it, thirty paces wide, brown and fast, going out. On the west side there was nothing but mud and marsh grass to the edge of sight. On the east side there was the spur, raised a yard above the mud, and on the spur there was a little group of people in green, and a flag on a pole, and two punts drawn up on the stone.

Kiva saw the stows before she saw anybody's face.

There were two of them, sixty paces apart along the creek. The nearer one, upstream, was Seven's. The further one, downstream toward the sea, was the Tarn's. At a distance they looked like two pairs of grey posts standing in the brown water, eight paces apart across the channel, with something slung between them. Closer, she saw what it was.

It was a mouth.

Between the tops of the two posts, and down into the water, there hung the open end of a long grey skin, as a net hangs between two poles across a river. Behind the mouth the skin went away downstream with the water, under the surface, like a sleeve, or like the long bag of a windsock blown out by a wind. It was ten paces long, perhaps twelve, narrowing all the way, and closed at its far end. The ebb poured in at the mouth. It came out through the sides of the sleeve, all along its length, in a frost of white plates that broke up on the surface and went away downstream toward the sea. And the cold came off it. Kiva could feel it on her face at fifty paces, against the wind.

"Stow," said a voice beside her. "That's what we call them. After the old stow-nets my grandad used to set in the Reach. He'd stake a net across the tide with its mouth open, and the tide would fish it for him."

It was Penhale.

He was standing on the spur in his thigh boots and his canvas smock that had been black once, with his red beard blowing sideways in the wind. He was grinning at her. Kiva had not seen him since the dawn at Gannet Creek, when she had walked away along the causeway in the dark without looking back.

"Miss Rowan," he said. "Second to Pike. You'll not remember me."

"I held your pole," said Kiva. "In the dark. For an hour."

"So you did. Your two fingers on my ash pole, and the girl taking your wrist every quarter, and nobody telling me what you were doing." His grin got wider. "Pike told me afterward. A gauge, he said. Two fingers that tell you nothing, and they told you the slack." He shook his head. "I've been a catcher on this coast since I was fifteen, and my dad before me, and I never had a gauge but my eyes."

"I'm not a second today," said Kiva. "I'm Seven's catcher."

"I know you are. I heard your captain at the table." He jerked his head toward Yeo. "I'm the officer's reserve. I stand on this spur and do nothing, and if anybody goes down on the mud, I go and get them. That's the rule. You stand off, and I go." He looked at the creek. "I've been officer's reserve on four Hunts. Never gone onto the mud yet. It's very dull, and I'm paid for it, and my mother likes it."

Yeo had turned round. She came along the spur toward them, tall and grey and brisk, with her notebook in her hand.

"Seven," she said. "And the Tarn." The Tarn's four had come along the spur behind them, in a square, without any of its corners changing place. "I'm Yeo. You'll have heard of me, some of you." She looked at Jude, once, and away. "This is my beat. This is my crew. Penhale, reserve. Ivey, catcher to the closing crew." A short broad woman in green nodded from beside the punts. "The closing crew, four, under their own officer, with their own licence. They'll close the tears when the forms are down. Your forms. Two stows. Upper's Seven's, lower's the Tarn's. Under each, in the creek bed between the posts, there's a tear. The Company marked them at the evening low. Nobody's touched them."

She opened her notebook. She did not read from it. She held it open in her hand as Rhea held her slate.

"Before," she said. "Two stows on Mallow Creek, working the ebb. Tears in the bed under each. Nothing lit within the beat but what I say. The ebb runs till the slack, and the slack's at the bottom of the tide, which by the gate's table is twenty-one minutes past twelve. On the flats it'll be earlier, and long. It's the neap; the neap slack on this creek runs forty minutes or near enough." She shut the book. "You'll cut in the slack. Not before. Each stow on two posts; cut both and the stow's dead and the skin lies down. You'll set your purses under the posts at the end of the ebb, when the channel's low enough to stand in. Penhale will show you. Anything shed before then, I'll say what's done with it." She looked along them. "Questions."

"Ma'am," said Trewin. "The lower stow. It's in deeper water."

"It's in the bend. The channel's deeper on the outside. You'll want the punt for the far post." Yeo nodded at it. "My boatman's yours when you want him. Anything else."

Nobody said anything.

"Then that's said," said Yeo. "You're on the Board's ground and the Company's beat, and you're on my word. I'm glad to have you." She glanced at Perrin, standing at the back with his coat buttoned to the throat and his hand pressed to his side. "Captain. You'll stand with me, on the spur. You'll have the glass."

"Ma'am," said Perrin.

---

For two hours they watched the stows work the ebb.

It was the longest two hours Kiva could remember. She sat on the edge of the spur with her legs over the mud and her book on her knee, and wrote, and watched, and did not write anything that she had not seen. Pru sat beside her. Anwen stood a little way off with her hands in her sleeves, watching the creek. Jude walked up and down the spur, limping, with his left arm in its linen and his eyes on the posts.

The stow was always moving.

From a distance it had looked still, two posts and a hanging mouth. From the spur Kiva could see that it never stopped. The posts leaned with the ebb, both of them, a little, the way the kiddle's posts had leaned at Gannet Creek. The mouth bellied in the current. The long grey sleeve behind it stirred under the water like a living thing breathing, filling and slackening, and all along its sides the white plates came out and went away. When a gust came off the sea and pushed the water back, the sleeve slackened and the posts came upright an inch. When the gust passed and the ebb ran again, they leaned again, and the sleeve filled.

"It's like a thing drinking," said Pru, very low. "It's drinking the creek."

"It's working," said Penhale, from behind them. He had come and sat down on the spur too, with his boots hanging over the mud. "That's what we say. It's working the ebb. When it turns, it'll swing round on its posts like a vane on a church, and work the flood the other way. Its mouth to the sea and its tail inland." He sniffed. "They've only been on the flats since the winter. Before that it was kiddles. Now it's kiddles and stows, and Lord knows what by the summer."

"How do you know when it'll swing?" said Kiva.

"When the tide turns. Same as everything." Penhale looked at her sideways. "You'd know before me. With your fingers."

At half past ten, Pru said, "There's something under the mouth."

Kiva looked.

Under the surface of the brown water, between the two posts of Seven's stow, where the mouth went down into the creek, there was a darker place. It was not the stow. It lay in the bed of the creek, under the mouth, a long dark shape like the shadow of a log, and the water went round it in a little ruffle.

"Tear," said Penhale. "That's what's feeding it. The Company found it at the evening low. Under each stow, in the bed. Like a tap." He was quiet a moment. "Ivey's crew'll close that. Not you."

"Not us," said Kiva.

---

At ten minutes to eleven, a gust came off the sea that was bigger than the others.

Kiva saw it come. She saw it on the flats first, far out, a darkness going over the shining mud like the shadow of a cloud, very fast, coming toward them. She saw it reach the mouth of Mallow Creek, and the brown water in the creek stand up in ridges, and the ridges come up the creek against the ebb, toward the Tarn's stow and past it, toward theirs.

The Tarn's stow slackened and leaned and came back. Theirs did not.

Theirs had been leaning further than the Tarn's all morning; the upper creek was narrower and the ebb ran harder in it. When the gust hit, the water in the narrow creek did not slacken. It stopped dead against the gust and stood up, and then, as the gust went on, it came back. It came back up the creek in a long low lift, a surge, the wrong way, inland, against the ebb, and it went into the stow's mouth from behind.

The sleeve bellied out the wrong way. It bellied up out of the water, grey and streaming, and the two posts leaned hard inland.

And the top of the near post came off.

It came off on its own. Nobody had cut it. It cracked like a branch going in frost, and a yard of it fell into the creek on the downstream side, and it was a heap of grey ash as it fell. It hit the water and did not sink. It spread.

It spread on the brown water like milk going into tea, grey and fast, and the ebb, which had come back now that the gust had passed, took it and carried it downstream. Toward the Tarn's half. Toward the sea.

"*Pryce,*" said Yeo, from the spur. "*Flat.*"

Anwen was already moving.

She went down off the spur onto the mud of the east bank, in two strides, and her wall came up at her hip and went out over the water. It did not stand. She laid it down. She laid it flat on the brown surface, a hand deep, as Mace's line had laid six walls on the black water of Gannet Creek in the dark. It went out across the creek from the east bank like a boom of logs, silver, nearly to the west bank, and the grey fan came down the creek and met it and stopped.

Most of it stopped. It piled against the silver in a grey crust, steaming.

But some of it went under. Kiva saw it go. It went down under the edge of the wall, where the wall lay only a hand deep in water that was six feet deep, and it came up on the far side, thinner, and went on downstream toward the Tarn's stow.

"*Down a hand,*" said Kiva.

She did not know she was going to say it. It was Mace's. It was what Mace had said to Stroud and Tibb at Gannet Creek, and she had heard it from the hard, and it had gone into her somewhere and stayed.

Anwen heard it. Kiva saw her heels go into the mud. She saw the silver of the wall go down into the brown water, deeper, a hand, two. The grey stopped going under. It lay against the silver, steaming, and the ebb pushed on it, and Anwen leaned on her heels on the east bank with her teeth set and held it.

"*Net,*" said Yeo.

Ivey was already in the punt. The boatman pushed off. They came along the edge of the silver with a long-handled net, like a shrimper's net, and Ivey scooped. What came up was grey sludge, steaming, and she tipped it into a tin in the punt's bottom, and scooped again, and again, until the water against Anwen's wall was brown.

"*Lift,*" said Yeo.

Anwen lifted. The wall came up off the water and shut. She stood on the mud with her arms hanging and her face white.

"That's the creek's," said Yeo. She had her notebook open and was writing in it, standing, fast. "*A post shed a rung uncut on a surge against the ebb, before the slack. Held in part on a wall laid flat on the water by the Hold of Team Seven, on the officer's word. Netted. A part lost downstream.* That remark's the creek's, not yours." She looked down at Anwen. "Pryce. Who taught you to lay a wall on water?"

"Doran Mace, ma'am. Graywater. At Gannet Creek, in the dark, I watched him."

"You watched him once."

"Once, ma'am."

"And you did it." Yeo wrote something else. "And somebody told you down a hand."

"Rowan, ma'am."

Yeo looked at Kiva. Then she looked at the stow, which stood with one post whole and one cut short, a yard of it gone, leaning with the ebb again as if nothing had happened. The sleeve had gone back under the water. The white plates came out of its sides.

"It's still working," said Penhale, quite quietly. "On one and a half."

"The near post's still standing," said Yeo. "Short. It'll hold the mouth. You'll cut what's left of it at the slack, with the other." She shut her notebook. "That rung would have gone straight down the creek into the Tarn's stow, and through it, and out to sea. I'd sooner have it in a tin."

"It's wet," said Jude. He had come down to the edge of the spur and was looking at the tin in the punt. "It's been in the water. On the ebb."

"It's been in the water on the ebb," said Yeo. "It'll comb red. Everything that's been wet on a running tide since the autumn has combed red. We don't know why, and it's not mine to say." She looked at him. "It's still not in the sea."

Jude looked at the tin a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly.

Kiva was looking at the stow.

"Pru," she said. "Did you see it lean? Before the gust hit?"

Pru had been watching too. She was frowning.

"It leaned before," she said. "I think. The near post. It went inland a hair before the water stood up. As if it heard it coming."

"That's what I saw."

"Lord," said Penhale. He had heard. He was looking at the stow with his red beard blowing and his face gone quite still. "Lord. Things like that don't hear."

"I don't know that it heard," said Kiva. "I know it leaned first."

She wrote it down, on the left, where the things went that she had seen. *The upper stow leaned before the gust reached it. Finch saw it too.* She did not write anything on the right.

---

At half past eleven the channel was low enough to stand in.

The ebb had taken the creek down. It ran now in a narrow brown thread through the bottom of a great grey channel of mud, ten feet deep from the top of the banks, and the two stows stood in it with their posts up out of the mud like the stakes of a fence. Their long sleeves lay half out of the water now, grey, streaming, still working what was left of the ebb, still letting the white plates out of their sides. The tears in the bed under them were dark places in the mud, each with the thread of water ruffling round it.

"Purses," said Yeo. "Penhale. Show them."

Penhale went down into the channel first, in his thigh boots, to the knee in the mud, and Kiva and Pru went after him in theirs.

The mud was cold. It was cold through the boots at once, and it sucked at every step, so that she had to pull each foot out of it with a noise like a cork. Pru beside her went down to the shin and said something under her breath that she would not have said in front of Aske.

"You'll get used to it," said Penhale. "Or you won't. My brother never did. He went to sea instead." He was carrying a purse over his shoulder, a grey canvas sheet laced to an iron ring as wide as a cartwheel, with a drawstring threaded through eyes all round its edge. "That's why I'm a catcher and he's in a boat. He couldn't stand the mud. Said it got in his soul."

He laid the purse down in the mud under the near post of Seven's stow, a yard downstream of the dark place of the tear, open, flat, with the iron ring pressed into the mud all round so that it would not lift. The drawstring's two ends he brought together and knotted to a line, and the line he carried up the channel's side to the top of the bank, to an ash pole that one of Yeo's men had driven into the mud there.

"Under the post," he said. "Straight under. At the slack the rung comes straight down. No flood, no ebb. It falls like a stone into still water and lands in the purse, and you haul, and the drawstring closes, and it's shut." He showed Pru how to take the line in her fists. "One fathom. Two fathom. Three. That's how you know it's shut; three fathom of line and it stops. My dad counted it so. Everybody on this coast counts it so."

"Fathoms," said Pru.

"It's what a line's measured in. Your arms out, so." Penhale put his arms out wide. "That's a fathom. Three of those and the purse is closed." He grinned at her. "You'd make a coast catcher, miss. I told you at Gannet Creek."

"You did." Pru went red and did not mind it. "I've been thinking about it since."

They set the second purse under the far post. Then Penhale stood in the mud with his hands on his hips and looked at them both.

"There," he said. "That's yours set. I'll go and see to the Tarn's. Their girl's never set a purse in her life, and she's fifteen, and their captain's a lovely slow man who'll take a week to show her." He began to wade off down the channel toward the lower stow. Then he stopped and turned round in the mud. "Miss Rowan."

"Yes."

"What's *nobody?*"

She looked at him.

"I heard the Tarn's captain at the comb table," said Penhale. "Saying it under his breath. *Nobody, nobody.* Like a prayer. And your captain said you'd an Ember declared, on a beach, where nobody needs to carry anything anywhere." He scratched his red beard. "I've been wondering."

Kiva thought about how to say it. She did not want to say it the way it was written in the columns. She wanted to say it the way she would say it to anybody who might need it on a beach.

"It's a way of passing a thing," she said. "A thing that folds onto whoever's holding it. One person catches it on a ward and lets go. For a quarter of a count, nobody's got it. Then I take it, with one door, and carry it, and let go. Nobody again. Then a still door plants it, somewhere away from people." She held up her right fist. "Nobody ever holds it long enough for it to fold. And nobody ever holds it at the same time as anybody else."

Penhale looked at her fist. Then he laughed, loud and cheerful, so that a curlew got up out of the marsh grass on the far bank and went away crying.

"Nobody," he said. "On this coast nobody passes anything to nobody. We hand it, man to man, both hands on it. You'd put half the Company out of a job." He shook his head, still laughing. "I'd like to see it. I'll not, likely. I've been reserve on four Hunts, and never been onto the mud yet."

"I'd sooner you didn't see it," said Kiva. "It's only for when it's wanted."

"Then I'll hope not to." He touched his forehead to her, a little salute, with mud on his fingers. "Coombe's page. The far edge to the second. They say it's at the printer."

"It is."

"We give it the corner-hand. It's in the book. Has been since my dad." He looked down the channel toward the Tarn's stow, where Bridie was standing in the mud beside its posts with a purse over her shoulder, looking at it as if it might bite. "When it's printed, I'll read it. I'm not one for reading. But I'll read that."

He waded off. Kiva watched him go, broad and red-bearded, with the mud to his knees, talking already. She could hear him from fifty paces, low and cheerful, telling Bridie Hebden about his brother who couldn't stand the mud.

---

She went back up the channel's side to the pole on the bank, where the line of the first purse was tied.

It was an ash pole, as thick as her wrist, driven into the mud at the channel's edge. It went down into the mud, and the mud went down to whatever was under the creek, and the creek went out to the sea.

Pru knelt at the second pole, three paces off, with the line in her fists. Anwen was on the bank above with her hands in her sleeves. Jude stood at the channel's lip with his edge unlit and his good hand open at his side, looking at the near post of the stow, the one that had been cut short. On the spur behind them, Yeo stood with her notebook and Perrin stood beside her with a brass glass to his eye.

Kiva took her left hand out of her coat. It was warm. It had been warm three mornings. She laid the first two fingers on the ash pole, at a light touch, above the mud, and did not lean.

*Something's pushing.*

And then, because she held them there, the other thing.

*It keeps time.*

It was the ebb. It was long and slow and uneven, a long lift and a short one, the way the flood had been at Gannet Creek in the dark. Only it was going the other way, outward, toward the sea, and it was fading. Each lift was shorter than the last. And under it, faint, so faint that she was not sure, there was the town. Four and four.

Pru reached over without being asked and took her right wrist.

"Seventy-two," said Pru, after a while, with her eyes on her own watch. "You're calm."

"I'm not calm."

"Your pulse is." Pru let go. "It's the water. Not you."

Kiva kept her fingers on the pole. Down the channel, Penhale had finished with the Tarn's purses and was wading back up toward the spur, still talking. Bridie stood in the mud beside the lower stow's near post with the line of her first purse in her fists, and Trewin stood on the bank above her with his hat on and his hands clasped. The thread of water in the channel's bottom ran seaward, slower now, and slower.

The ebb lifted. It lifted less. It lifted less again.

At twelve minutes past twelve, between one breath and the next, the fingers said nothing.

Not *pushing.* Not *keeps time.* Nothing at all.

Kiva lifted her right hand and held it up, flat, so that Yeo on the spur could see it.

Yeo looked at the creek. Nothing had changed that anybody could see. The thread of brown water lay in its bed. The two stows stood in the mud with their sleeves half out of the water. And then, slowly, all together, the four posts on Mallow Creek came upright out of the mud and stood straight, like the stakes of a fence on a still day, and the sleeves lay down.

"*Slack,*" said Yeo. "Twelve minutes past. Seven. Your stow."
