# Chapter 6 — Kiva: Seven Columns

She wrote him up on Sunday, at the kitchen table, on the Works paper that was ruled in seven.

It took the whole afternoon. She had nine things by the end of it, and each of them had a line to itself: what it was called, and what he did, and how. The last column she left empty all the way down. That was his.

The eighth entry took longer than the other eight together.

*The third turn of the cord.* She had seen it three times, on three bags. *What: he changes hands. The right has the first turn and the second. On the third the right only holds, and the left takes the long end under.*

It was there. She had watched it as she had watched a laundress's hip. But a hip was a trick, and this did not look like one. It looked like a man sparing something.

He had said that he did not want what was wrong. He had also said *which hand I use, and when,* in so many words, by a stove.

"You've had that pencil in the air for ten minutes," said Mara.

"I don't know whether it's what he does or what's the matter with him."

"Is it what you saw?"

"Yes."

"Then it's what he does. What's the matter with him is his to put in the last column." Her aunt did not look up from the tide table, which had come by Friday's boat and was spread across her half of the oilcloth like a railway guide. "I've had Rusk's thumbs described to me by three different fitters. He's never thanked one of them. He's also never dropped a can."

Kiva wrote it in.

---

Pike read them on Tuesday, by the stove in the mending room, with his spectacles on.

She had not known that he owned spectacles. He took them from a tin in his apron, and hooked them on one ear at a time, and held the sheet a long way off.

"You brought me a laundress first," he said.

"You said somebody who'd been good at a thing for longer than was decent."

"*Hip pins the near hem. Folds from the far edge; always has the last of it.*" He read it without expression. "That's a ward-fold."

"It's Finch's. I wrote that in the corner."

"So you did." He laid the ticket on his knee and took up the sheets. "Now me."

He read as he sewed. He set his eye to a line and pulled it through, and did not go on to the next until it was fast.

"*The heel. Twice, a pace apart, before the cloth.*" He took her pencil out of her hand, without asking, and wrote in the empty column at the right, in a square slow print. "Why. Since ash runs to the low side while it's open. Lay the seam up the fall and it runs to the neck. Lay it across and you've a heap in your corner-hand's lap." He went down a line. "*The cord in the coat.* Wax. *Kneels with the gate at his back.* That's the book; I'll not write the book out. *Left knee on the hem.*"

"I put *so that he has both hands.*"

"Under *how.* It's well enough. There's more." He wrote. "A knee hears a hem. Not so well as a hand. But a hand's wanted, and a knee's doing nothing."

"That's how you had the proud corner."

"That's how I had it. I'll come to what you wrote about that." He turned the sheet. "*His eyes are level. He does not look at the thing. He looks at something at the height of a boot.*"

"I couldn't see what."

"Her heel. Sorn's. The left." It was the nearest that she had seen him come to being pleased. "A blade's feet settle a count before her arm goes. Hers come down flat when she's ready and not before. I've called *now* off that heel for eleven years. She doesn't know it."

"Shall I write that she doesn't know?"

"Write that the catcher watches the blade's feet. Leave whose." He wrote it himself.

Then he came to the wait, and stopped.

"*After* dark, one, *he does not call the corners. He waits between eight and eleven counts. He looks at the heap.*" He read it twice. "I said in the van that I didn't."

"You said that you watched it lie."

"So I do. I've been thinking since Saturday what for." He had the pencil over the last column. He did not put it down. "It's open for a slow sixty. Everybody knows that. In the first ten it's at its loosest, and it leans. It leans to whatever's nearest that keeps a pattern. That's why the wall goes out over it, all but the one end; I'd not have it take a ward's shape. And if it leans to the end that's left lit, and settles, then there's nothing else on that ground, and I bring my corners in."

"And if it leans somewhere else?"

"Then there's a thing on my floor that I wasn't told of. A box somebody's left lit in a cellar. A blade not gone dark." He looked at the stove. "I'd move the cloth. I'd have my corners haul from the other side, and get the neck between the heap and whatever it was."

"Have you?"

"Not above four times. It's leaned to the lit end, or it's lain flat, for six-and-twenty years." His square face did not change. "Since the thaw it leans a hair every time. To nothing that I can find. I'd not thought about that until you made me say what I was looking at."

He wrote in the column. It was the longest that he had written. *Why: to see what the heap is listening to, before I put my people's hands near it.*

"That's a good one," he said, grudgingly. "I'd have gone to my grave not telling a corner that."

He went on. He came to the eighth.

Kiva watched him read it. She saw him get to the end of the line, and go back to the beginning, and she saw the grey come up over his collar.

"That's not a habit."

"It's what was there. On all three bags."

"I tie right-handed. I've tied right-handed since I was sixteen."

"You tie two turns right-handed. On the third the right holds and the left goes under."

"Since when?"

"I don't know since when. I've only seen three."

He said nothing. He took his right hand out from where it lay on the canvas, and held it to the stove, and shut it. It shut as a stiff gate shuts. The fingers went, and the thumb came after, late, by the width of a breath.

"The autumn before last," said Pike. "It's the cold. It closes behind the rest. I can't trust it to pin on a third turn, so the left does it, and I'd have told you on oath that nobody could see."

"I didn't write that it was wrong."

"No. You wrote *he changes hands on the third.* In a column headed *how.*" His voice was quite flat. "A corner-hand reads that. He thinks, that's how Pike does it, that's the way, and he spends a year learning to tie cack-handed for the sake of an old man's thumb."

"Is it the way?"

"It's my thumb."

"Then that's the last column," said Kiva. "I left it empty. It's yours."

He looked at her over the spectacles. It was not a friendly look. He had asked for a thing, and been given it, and it had turned out to have an edge.

He wrote. He bore down on the pencil.

*Why: his right thumb is slow. This is not method. Do not copy it.*

"There," he said. "That's a chartered catcher's hand, in writing, saying what's the matter with it. I've not told Sorn. I suppose that she'll read it."

"Nobody reads it unless you say."

"Don't be soft. If it's not to be read, it's not worth the paper." He turned to the ninth, roughly. "*Went wrong. Second bag. Hobday's corner proud at about the twelfth count. He had it with his knee. I saw it at the same count and would have spoken.*"

"That's what went wrong."

"Nothing went wrong. It was mended inside a count."

"It was proud for a count."

"And that's a man nine years on my hem, with his name in a column." He struck through one word and wrote another over it. *A corner.* "It's not about him. It's not about me, come to that. It's about you. *Would have spoken.*"

"I put it since it was so."

"Then leave it. I'd sooner have a second who writes down that she nearly opened her mouth than one who pretends that she never wanted to." He squared the sheets on his knee. He took the spectacles off, an ear at a time. "Nine. And two that I'd not have known I did, and one that I'd have paid you not to see."

"Is that all of them?"

"No."

She waited.

"There's one that you've missed. It's the one that matters most to anybody kneeling where you kneel." He nearly smiled, and thought better of it. "I'll not tell you. If I tell you, you'll look for it, and then you'll see it whether it's there or not. You'll find it or you won't."

He gave her the sheets back. He kept the laundry ticket.

"I'm having this," said Pike. "I've a notion to read it to Finch."

The Charter Board's conditions went up on Friday, on the same board, under the same seal.

Kellan read them aloud. He had not been asked to. He began in his ordinary voice, as one reads a tram notice.

"*Entries by Saturday the 30th of the first month, at the office of each circuit, under each four's own hand and its captain's.*

"*A captain shall be a classified candidate of the four, in a declared role. The captain is the one voice that a referee or a marshal hears. The captain declares every role and every change, and calls a withdrawal.*

"*A four is four and one reserve. A candidate stands for one four only, declared at entry. There are no changes at the gate.*"

He stopped there. It was not for long. But Kiva was standing next to him, and she saw his ears go white.

"*The warrant,*" he said. "*Three drams a head, the reserve included, for passage, bed and the gate comb. Paid with the entry.*"

"Three," said Anwen.

"I said two. In the tenth month, at your table. I said that it wouldn't be less." He was writing on his cuff, quickly, as though it were a relief. "Five heads is fifteen. We hold five-and-twenty and twenty-one hundredths, if nobody lights anything tomorrow."

"We're lighting tomorrow," said Perrin. "Old Four's coming to the yard."

"Then we hold less. Go on, there's more." He found his place. "*The Hunt Marshal, D. Coombe, Blaze, will come to each circuit in the second month to prepare its first company for the Hunt. Her floors are at each four's own charge, at a dram a head.*"

"A Blaze," said Pru. "I've never seen a Blaze."

"There isn't one on the Circuit's roll," said Noa. He was at the end of the board again. "Sorn's a Torch. So's Miss Dane. It's the rank above. Coombe ran mixed fours on the coast for thirty years, and the Board's had her write its hunt rules." He went pink. "I asked the clerk."

"A dram a head," said Kellan. "Four heads on a floor. If she gives us one floor, that's four. I don't know how many she gives." He added it. "So it's fifteen and four. That's nineteen, before anybody's so much as bought a cord. Out of five-and-twenty."

"*A captain,*" said Jude.

They had all been not saying it.

"A classified candidate," said Kiva. "I'm not classified. I've a rank with a clause in it and a board that goes to dashes."

"I'm not on the book," said Jude. "I'm a head's share and a name."

"I'm a reserve," said Pru. "I'm a first-year."

That left three. Anwen had the team book under her arm, and she did not open it. Perrin was looking at the ceiling. Kellan was looking at the line above. *A candidate stands for one four only.*

"We've a fortnight," said Anwen. "Fifteen days. I want it said that I'd sooner this were settled at a table than in a basement, with half of Stonehand reading over our shoulders."

"I've to think," said Kellan.

"For how long?"

"I don't know. It's thinking."

"It looks a good deal like sulking from the bench," said Jude, quite gently.

Kellan did not answer him. He put his pencil away, and he went up the stairs two at a time.

Kiva watched him go.

She had done the sum that he had done, and she had done another, which was not on the board. If Kellan Renn stood for Halden License, then Seven was a catcher, a Hold, a carry and a Kill by name. If he stood for Seven, then a Works yard with a string in a cage would have a peg with nothing on it, and the best feet in the School would have turned their back on it twice.

She did not know which she wanted. She found that she was rather ashamed of how much she wanted to be asked.

---

Old Four came to the Old Yard on Saturday at two, in their coats, with a dummy of their own on poles.

"It's a yard," said Orla. She was on her crate, a pace north of the south row, with a sand-glass on her knee. "It's not sanctioned and it's not read. There's no mast on anybody. So I've no need of the Chair's paper, and I'll have my own instead. Rowan. Say it."

"One door. The still. Declared for all three carries."

"And if you open another?"

"Then I've lost the carry and I've told you that I've no business on a floor."

"That'll do." Orla turned the glass over in her fingers. "Circuit rules. Three for the litter over your own line. One for a down. If the glass runs out, a point to whichever half it's lying in. Seven's line is the sheds. Old Four's is the gap. Three carries."

The litter lay in the middle of the lane, twenty paces from either end, between the two rows of black posts. It was Gerald. Somebody had given him a hat.

Kiva stood on the scraped line by the sheds with her four.

It was her four, for that afternoon. Perrin had the middle, out of his jersey for the first time in three months, with his sleeves rolled. Anwen was on his left with her hands low. Kellan was on the right. He had come down the lane at five minutes to two, in his own coat, and laced his cuff, and said nothing about anything.

Jude and Pru had the shed step. There were juniors on it as well, a dozen of them, in grey smocks, packed like sparrows on a gutter.

"Is she going to use all three?" said a small sandy voice.

"She's declared one," said Pru.

"What's declared?"

"It's when you say what you'll do, aloud, and then you've got to."

"That's hard."

"Yes," said Pru. "It's meant to be."

"*Go,*" said Orla, and turned the glass.

Kiva did what she always did.

She went up the lane at a walk, on the north side, and four paces short of the litter she stopped. She opened the still door on the packed dirt, by its corners, and shut it. *Clean.* Then she went on to the litter's flank, and Perrin had the front poles already, and she came back to her tested ground as he lifted, and planted.

The yard took her.

Old Four did not come at a run. They never had. Lowen Brask walked to the head of the litter, square and quiet, and set his feet in front of Perrin, and opened a door of his own.

The lane boomed. It was like two drays meeting in a gateway.

He did not strike. He leaned. A still door did not push the man in front of it; it pushed through him, as a hand pushes through a row of books, and what moved was whatever stood at the far end. That was Kiva. She felt it come in at her shoulder, where it touched Perrin's back.

It stopped there. She was planted. It went down through her into the ground, as a corner had once, and Perrin did not move by the width of a boot.

"*Mine!*" said Anwen. A wall of silver came up on the left, cart-wide, and Edda's came against it from the other side with a noise like a shut drawer.

Kellan had gone.

She did not see him go. He was there, at her right hand, and then there were two long low bounds along the north row, with the struts running down his calves, and a line of blue-white behind a post.

"*Now?*" he said.

He said it to her. He was three paces off, behind Old Four's blade, with his edge lit, and he looked over his shoulder at his catcher.

"Now."

He touched Gower between the shoulders. It was as light as a tap on a door.

"Down," said Orla. "Gower. Kneel."

After that it was only walking. Kellan took the foot poles. Brask stood where he was, a post with nothing to lean on, and they carried Gerald home past him with his hat on.

"Three and one," said Orla. "Seven, four. Old Four, nothing. Set."

"He asked her," said one of the juniors.

"He always asks her," said Jude. "He learned it. It took him a spring."

---

Brask had his notebook out.

Kiva saw that from the line. He did not write in it. He only looked at something that was there already, and shut it, and said three words to Wace.

"*Go.*"

She walked up the north side. She stopped four paces short, and tested, and shut. *Clean.* She went on to the litter.

When she turned round, Wace was standing on it.

He had walked there. He had simply come up the lane at his own pace, as she left the mark, and put his two boots where hers had been and opened his door. He was a big placid boy, and he stood on her tested ground like a cow in a gateway.

"That's mine," said Kiva.

"It was," said Wace. "I've been a post before."

Brask and Gower had the litter. They had taken it while Perrin was looking at Wace. They were walking it east.

She needed ground.

She had one door, and it was no use in the air. She looked for the nearest place to stand that was not under somebody, and it was south of the litter, two paces off, hard by the south row.

She opened the door on it.

There was a room underneath.

She had known. She had known as she did it; she had written it in a proof sheet, in pencil, with her initials. The main came along under the wall to the leaning post and went on west beneath the south row, eight feet down, bricked. Nothing in it kept time any longer. But it was hollow, and her stance found the top of it and rang, and for half a count she had her weight on nothing.

She shut it. She did not plant.

"*Rowan!*" That was Perrin. He had got a hand to a pole. Brask leaned on him, and this time there was nobody behind.

He went back three paces on his heels, like a wardrobe on castors.

"Down," said Orla. "Cade. On your back counts."

It was Gower who had done it, with a touch as he went by. Kellan had Wace a moment after, since Wace was planted and could not have dodged a tram. It did not matter. The litter was over the line at the gap, and Old Four's ward had not so much as lit.

"Three and one," said Orla. "And one to Seven for Wace. Seven, five. Old Four, four."

Kiva stood in the lane with nothing open.

She had been read. It was as plain as that. She had a habit, and it was a good one; she tested her ground before she stood on it, every time, and she had never landed on a room since the fifth of the tenth month. And a boy with a notebook had watched her do it since the Pit, and had seen that she did it in the open, in front of everybody, a full count before she used it.

It was a signpost. She had been putting one up at every floor that she stood on.

And he had known about the drain. He had felt that post in the dark, on a night with a litter. He had left her the south side on purpose.

"*Go.*"

She tested farther back, nearer her own line. Edda walked onto it. She tested a third place, and Wace was there before she had shut the door.

"Stop it," said Perrin.

He had come up beside her. He was breathing hard, and he had dirt down his back.

"You test, and then we all look at it. *He* looks at it." He jerked his head at Brask. "I'm a still door. I can stand on a bit of ground as well as Wace can, and I'm on your side. Test it under me."

"What?"

"I stand first. You open your door under my heels. If it's clean, you say so, and I don't shift until you've said *mine.* Then I say *yours,* and I go to the poles." His big red face was quite serious. "It's the wall. It's what you made us do with you on the litter. Caught, and then let go."

She stared at him.

It was hers. She had lain on her back on the cobbles and said it, in the eleventh month, with a bag of sand across her shins. She had not once thought of it as a thing that could be done to a piece of ground.

"North of the litter," she said. "Two paces. Go."

He went. He planted, and the lane boomed. Wace, coming up the north side, found a carry of Stonehand's second team on the place that he had meant to stand, and stopped, and looked at Brask.

Kiva opened her door under Perrin's boots.

*Clean.*

"Mine."

"Yours," said Perrin, and stepped off forward, and she was planted before his heel had left the dirt.

Nobody had seen her test. There had been nothing to see.

Brask leaned. It came down the litter and into her and stopped.

But it had taken time. There had been three false marks and a conversation, and Old Four had dragged Gerald a full pace into their own half before she was set. Anwen had come off her wall to cover Perrin's back while he stood. Gower touched her as she turned.

"Down," said Orla. "Pryce."

The last grains ran out of the glass with nobody moving. The litter lay a yard east of the middle.

"One for the half, and one for the down. Old Four, six. Seven, five." She set the glass on the crate. "That's the afternoon. If you've ash left and want a fourth for nothing, I'll turn it again. It doesn't go on any sheet."

"Turn it," said Kellan.

They ran the fourth on the last of his ash and Anwen's.

Perrin was standing north of the litter before the sand had begun to fall. Kiva tested under him, and said *mine,* and he said *yours.* Brask leaned on a thing that did not move, and went on leaning. Kellan asked her *now?* from behind a post, and she said it, and he touched Gower, and came back for the foot poles at a walk.

It was the best carry that they had made since the summer. It was not on any sheet.

Brask came down the lane afterward with his notebook in his hand.

"You always test first," he said. "I've had it written since the Pit. It's the best habit in the Third Division. I'd not have you drop it."

"You had Wace stand on it."

"I'd have stood on it myself, only I was wanted at the pole." He was not apologizing. He was a boy who read forms for a living, and he had read one. "And I left you the south. I felt that post once, with a litter. I knew what was under it."

"That's fair."

"I thought so. I'd expect a Saltmere four to see it by the second carry, and they'll not have the drain." He looked at Perrin, who was sitting on Gerald. "That was good. Holding it for her. Whose was that?"

"Mine," said Perrin. "I'd like it known. I thought of it with dirt down my neck."

"It costs you your carry for three counts."

"I know what it costs. I counted them." He got up, with care. "We've one who can stand on a thing, and I'm wanted at the front. It wants a fifth, and a lane's four."

Nobody had an answer to that. Kiva wrote it down as it stood.

*Went wrong: tested in the open. They stood on it. Second try: held first, by Cade; tested under him; "mine" before "yours." It works. It costs the carry three counts, and there is nobody to spare.*

They signed on the lid of the quench box. Kellan read it, and it was short.

"*Saturday the 16th. The Old Yard. Three carries against Old Four, and a fourth for nothing. Lost, six to five. Lit: Pryce, 0.21. Cade, 0.12. Renn, 0.24. Rowan, eleven stances, off her own thimble. Spent: 0.57. Held: 24.64. Sealed, medical, W: 0.8.*"

He signed first. Then he sat with the pen in his hand, and looked at the three names that would go under his.

"That adds," he said.

It was all that he said. He gave Anwen the pen.

Kellan Renn counted the rivets on Thursday.

He had not meant to. He had not done it since the ninth month. But he came in under the refinery gate at four, with his own cuff in his pocket, and found that he was at two hundred and six before he had told himself to stop.

That was a bad sign. He counted things when he had already decided and did not care for the answer.

The license yard was as it always was. There was the blue brick, and the lattice house at the north end, and the paid wards standing easy along the wall. There was the hum coming up through his boot soles, slow and even, the tread of a great many people going by on the far side of a wall. His feet wanted to fall in with it.

He counted threes against it, as he had since the autumn, and they steadied.

"Top rung," said Birch. "Yarrow has the cloth. You'll ask him."

"Yes, sir."

He went up the back of the string in two bounds, dark, on his struts. It was the same string. It had been in that cage since the thaw, and he had taken its top rung twenty-six times. He knew how it stood as he knew his own stair.

"*Now?*"

"Not yet. It's swaying. Now."

He lit. It was four-tenths, to a hair. The edge went through level, and he was dark before his feet found the brick, and sixty drams came down on Yarrow's cloth with the sound of sand.

"*Dark. One. Corners.*"

Pettit and Hesketh hauled. It was very clean. It was cleaner than anything that he had seen on a Circuit floor; these three had had a contained string to themselves two days in the week since the thaw, and it showed in every hand.

"Sealed," said Yarrow. "Forty-one."

A grader in a black coat held a comb over the neck. Kellan could not hear the reed from where he stood. He could see the tag that the man wrote, and it was red.

It was always red on this floor. He had stopped noticing that.

He noticed it now, since he had spent a fortnight with a printed sheet in his head. *Marked on clean yield.* Clean was under trace on the seventh reed. This was a yard that kept step, with a bank under the brick that had been salted to a beat before he was born, and every rung that had ever fallen on it had been open for a slow sixty with that under it.

He had the figures. He kept everything. Nine rungs since the new year, and nine red tags.

It did not matter on a license yard. Halden did not tin what fell here; it was a practice string, and the ash went back into the Works' own stock, where the Step was wanted. It would matter a great deal on a tidal flat, with a marshal's comb at the gate.

He put that in his cuff, in two figures, and did not say it. He did not yet know whom he would be saying it to.

"Renn," said Birch.

The foreman had come to the rail. He had a board under his arm, with a printed sheet clipped to it, and the seal at the head of the sheet was one that Kellan had been reading for three months.

"That's our entry. It goes to the Chair's office on Saturday week." Birch turned it round. "Yarrow, catcher. Hesketh, Hold. Pettit, carry, and captain. I've a line that says *blade,* and I've had nothing to put on it since the fifth month, when ours went to the Line. You cut for these three at the Regional. You've cut for them twice a week since."

"Nothing owed. You said."

"Nothing's owed. I'm not calling it in. I'm asking, which is a thing that I do about twice a year and dislike." He scratched his neck. "The Works pays the warrant. It's three a head. It sends its four down by station transit on the twenty-eighth of next month; I'm told that it's a day and a half, with a bed."

"Seven goes by the road. And a ferry, and the river, and the coast boat."

"I dare say that it does."

Kellan looked at the line.

It was a very ordinary line. It had a ruled space after it, the width of a name.

He made himself do it properly, as a sum, with the working shown.

Halden License had been first at the Regional. It had been first with him on the peg. It would go to Saltmere as one of the three or four fours in the country that might win outright, and whoever cut for it would be read by every draft board on the coast, on a blade that he had never drawn so true as on this brick. He was seventeen. He was a Fire. He had a Draw of eight-two and feet that a foreman looked at before he looked at a face.

On the other side there was a kitchen table.

There was a team that had been sixth by yield, in brackets, and was fourth by no better title than that it had stood on a hill in the dark. There was a catcher with one door declared and a rank with a clause in it. There was a Kill who came down heavy and had seven drams and eight-tenths by name. Seven would not be worse at the cut without him. He had seen Talley on a tenth knot. It would be worse at one thing only. It would have a blade who had not spent a spring learning to say *now?* over his shoulder.

And he had declared for them. He had done it at the Regional, under rule nineteen, in front of eleven hundred people, with this yard's jacket on his back. He had taken the jacket off.

*I'd do it again tomorrow.* He had written that to Birch.

He found that it was still true. He would turn his back on this floor again, for that four, on any day when it was the four or the floor.

It was not that kind of day. Nobody was asking him to wear anything. There was a board with ninety-six marks on it, and a roll that took eight, and he wanted to know what he was. He wanted to stand in the best four in the country and find out whether he was the weak corner of it. He would never learn that at a kitchen table, where he was the one whom the others looked at when a thing wanted cutting.

That was the whole of it. It had no shine on it whatever. He turned it over, looking for something better underneath, a reason that he could have said to Pryce without his ears going white.

There was not one.

*I want it said,* she would say. And he would have to say it.

"You'll have my name," said Kellan.

"I'd thought that I might."

"Not today. On Tuesday. I've to say it at a table first, on Monday, to four people and a first-year. I'll not have them read it off a board."

Birch looked at his feet. He looked at them for some time.

"They've not got worse," he said. "I'd have said that it wanted more nerve to stop where you were."

"It would. I've looked. I haven't it."

"No." The foreman took the board back under his arm. "Well. There's not many would have told me so."

Kellan went and unlaced his cuff.

He did not count the rivets on the way out. He had what he had come for, and it was exactly as heavy as he had expected, and there was a tram to Coper Street on Monday that he would have to be on.
