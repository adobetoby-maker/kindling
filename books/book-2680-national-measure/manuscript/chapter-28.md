# Chapter 28 — Kiva: The Second Plant

Aske wrote the card at the long table in the loft at two o'clock, with Perrin sitting across from him in a clean shirt and the four of them standing round.

He wrote it in his small square hand, slowly, as he wrote everything that mattered. Then he turned it round on the table so that Perrin could read it, and then so that the rest of them could.

*Cade, P. The seventh rib on the left, cracked across, not through; the sixth and eighth sprung as before. Tuesday the 15th, at the Inner Lock, under load. No plant. No lift. Nothing carried heavier than a cup. Strapped, to be seen morning and night. A fortnight at the least. W. Aske.*

"A fortnight," said Perrin.

"At the least." Aske capped his pen. "I've written *at the least* so that nobody can come to me on the thirtieth and say I promised."

"The Lane's Thursday."

"The Lane's Thursday, and you'll be at the hurdle with your hands in your pockets, declaring." Aske did not raise his voice. He never did. "You may captain. You may talk. You may stand on the hard tomorrow on the Longshore and watch your four with a glass, if the Board will have you there, and I think it will. You may not go onto the mud. If you go onto the mud and something goes wrong, you'll be one more thing on the mud that somebody has to carry, and you'll be the heaviest."

Perrin looked at the card for a long time.

"I knew," he said. "On the lip. Last night. I said the price. Rowan said it."

"Then you knew," said Aske. "That's the best I can say for it. You knew, and you paid, and you came first." He got up. "I'll see it again at eight."

He went down the room to his own bed. The four of them stood at the table and looked at the card.

"Finch stands," said Perrin.

He said it before anybody else could say anything, flat and clear, as he had said things at the marshal's box.

Pru was by the stove with her cord in her lap, winding it as she had been since they came in, and she stopped.

"I'm the reserve," she said. "It's on the entry. I stood the Bench. I stood the Tarn on Saturday." She went red. "I'll stand. I'd like to stand. I want to say that before anybody's careful with me."

"Nobody's being careful with you," said Perrin. "There's nobody else." He put his hands flat on the table, either side of the card. "Rowan, catcher. Finch, catcher. Pryce, Hold. Talley, Kill. That's the four on the mud. I captain from the hard."

"Two catchers," said Anwen. "And no still door."

"No still door."

"On the Longshore." Anwen had her hands in her sleeves. "Where the ground's mud, and the water's never still, and you've told us since Coombe that the far edge wants weight on it." She looked at him steadily. "What's behind it? Two catchers and no plant. I'm asking because somebody will."

"Rowan's a still door."

"Rowan's whatever you declare her. One door." Anwen did not look at Kiva. "If you declare her stance, she's our plant, and she can put her boots on a far edge as she did this morning, and we've got weight. That's what a still door is on the Longshore. Coombe said so. On a moving thing, weight on the far edge."

"I know what Coombe said."

"Then what's behind it?"

Perrin did not answer at once, but looked at his own hands on the table.

"I'm going to declare her Ember," he said.

There was a silence, and Jude, at the end of the table, put down the book.

"Ember," said Anwen.

"Ember." Perrin did not look up. "Declared, single, by her captain's voice, off her own thimble. It's on her card. She can light it and nothing else, on that board, all day." He drew a breath, carefully. "Which means she's not our plant. She can't put her boots on anything. She's a catcher with a carry's door, and a still door shut in her that she's not let open."

"Why?"

"Because if somebody goes under a skin tomorrow," said Perrin, "there's one thing I know of on this coast that takes it off them without folding whoever holds it. Coombe said it. She said she'd seen mixed fours pass a load hand to hand on the Longshore, badly, two people grabbing at once and hoping, and twice it killed somebody. She said she'd never seen it called." He looked up at last. "It's three people. Finch folds. Rowan carries. Somebody plants. We've two of them standing tomorrow, and the third was me, and the third's cracked."

Anwen was quiet.

"I'm not saying it'll be wanted," said Perrin. "I'm saying if it's wanted, and we haven't got it, I'll have spent the day on the hard with my hands in my pockets knowing I could have had it declared and didn't. I'd sooner lose the weight on a far edge than that." He put his hand to his side, without noticing. "That's what's behind it. It's a thing that might not happen. I'm declaring for it so that if it does, we're not grabbing."

"And the plant?"

"I've been thinking about the plant since Sunday," said Perrin. "I asked for a second plant on Sunday, at eight, at this table. You all heard me. I said if my ribs went on the Bench or the Carry, there'd be no method." He almost smiled. "They went on the Carry."

"Trewin," said Jude.

"Trewin." Perrin nodded at the door. "He did it once on a yard, a count late. That's once more than anybody else who isn't us."

Kiva had not said anything, and found that they were all looking at her.

"It's mine to say?" she said.

"It's your door," said Perrin. "I declare it. You light it. I'd not declare a door of yours you didn't want lit." He looked at her straight. "Is it yours to say?"

She thought about it. She thought about the far edge of a grey sailcloth under her boots that morning, and the lead in it pulling, and how good it had felt to be the ground. She thought about the Longshore at midnight with Penhale's purse pole under her two fingers, and a fold coming up out of black water on the far side of a skin like a page turning. She thought about Coombe on the Low Lane with her rope in her hands. *It's the thing she does that she can't do, done by three who can.*

"It's yours to say," she said. "You're captain. But I'd say it too, if you asked me. Ember."

Perrin let out a breath.

"Then it's Ember," he said. "Talley, write it."

Jude wrote it. "*Tuesday. For the Hunt: Rowan, Ember, declared. Finch stands for Cade. Cade captains from the hard, on his healer's card.*" He looked up. "Pryce?"

Anwen took her hands out of her sleeves.

"I wanted it asked," she said. "I've asked it. I'll stand with it." She looked at Perrin. "I want it said that I'd still sooner have a plant on the far edge than a method in a pocket. But you're captain, and it's a good reason, and I've not got a better one."

"That's fair," said Perrin.

"It's fair," said Anwen. "I'd not say it if it weren't."

---

The board went up at the tent at six, under the lamp, with the seal on it, and the whole of the Measure came down the boards in the dusk to read it.

Kiva went with Jude. Perrin had been told by Aske to lie on his bed until eight, and was lying on it, under protest, reading the Mercury's afternoon sheet with his name in it upside down so that he would not have to look at it the right way up. Anwen had gone up Tar Lane by herself again. Pru had gone to sleep sitting up on her bed with her boots on.

There were two sheets on the pole. The first was the Carry, top to bottom, twenty-four lines, with Seven at the top of it in the clerk's careful hand. *1. Team Seven, Meridian. Three minutes and eight seconds; clean.* Kiva read it twice. She had seen it written before, in her head, on a quay. It was different on a pole.

The second sheet was the standings.

*After two boards. Marked by place, twenty-four to the first and one to the last, and added.*

*1. Halden License, Meridian. Forty-six.*

*2. The Ropewalk School, first four. Forty-five.*

*3. Team Seven, Meridian. Forty-three.*

*4. Old Four, Meridian. Forty-one.*

*4. Holloway, first four. Forty-one.*

*6. The Tarn, first four. Thirty-eight.*

Kiva read it three times.

"Third," said Jude. He said it very quietly. "Kiva. Third. Of twenty-four."

"After two boards."

"After two boards." He scratched his jaw. "We were seeded twelfth. On Saturday. We were the exact middle of the whole board, and Cade said it was honest and he'd take honest." He looked at the pole. "This is honest as well."

"It is."

"I know it is. I'm saying it." He was quiet for a moment. "Six on the Bench, and first on the Carry. And tomorrow's the Hunt, and the Hunt's the one that's alive."

People were looking at them, and Kiva felt it. It was not like the looking on the quay that morning, which had all been at Perrin. This was a quieter kind. It went past her to the pole and came back to her, and to Jude in his Seven colours with his book under his good arm, and went away again. A girl in green said, low, to the boy beside her, *that's the drained jacket, that's the ladder,* and the boy said, *that's the one who doesn't stand at the Bench.*

Under the standings there was a third sheet. It was newer. Kiva had to step closer to read it in the lamplight.

*The Hunt. Wednesday the 16th, on the Longshore, from nine. Twelve beats, two fours to a beat, one officer to a beat, by the Board's draw.*

She read down it. *Gannet Creek: Old Four; Westerley, first four. Officer, Sorn, of Graywater.* She read it again; Rhea had a beat. *Brine Reach: Team Twenty-Eight; Holloway, second four.* And then two lines lower.

*Mallow Creek: Team Seven; the Tarn, first four. Officer, Yeo, of the Saltmere Company.*

*Skerry Pill: Halden License; the Ropewalk School, first four. Officer, Bray, of the Saltmere Company. A lodger marked in the pill's mouth by the Company at the evening low; the fours on the beat have been told.*

Jude had read it too, and Kiva heard him draw breath.

"The Tarn," he said. "On our beat."

"Forty paces off, likely. Two fours to a creek."

"Trewin." Jude looked at her. "Cade asked for him on Sunday. Before there was a draw. Before anybody knew."

"I know."

"That's luck," said Jude. "I know it's luck. We didn't do anything to get it." He looked at the line again. "And a lodger on Skerry. With the Ropewalk. Laid."

"*The fours on the beat have been told,*" said Kiva.

"Everybody's been telling them since the gate," said Jude. "Coombe told them in the second month, before she ever met them. Pryce told Lyle a fortnight ago, up Tar Lane. The Company's told them." He shut the book under his arm. "I'd not know what else anybody could tell them."

---

Kellan came up the loft stair at ten past six and stood at the end of Kiva's bed.

He did not sit down; he was in his black and gold, with his coat over his arm, and he had the look he had when he had been doing a sum that would not come out.

"Skerry Pill," he said.

"I saw."

"Bray's beat. With the Ropewalk. And a lodger in the mouth." He looked down the room at the south end, where Pettit was sitting on his bed with Yarrow and Hesketh, talking low. "Pettit's been to Bray. She says the lodger's lying in the mud at the pill's mouth where the Company left it, and it's not moved since five, and they'll not kill it before the Hunt, because it's the Board's beat and the Board wants it as it lies." He shifted his coat. "She says the Ropewalk's been told. Carrow said, *we've been told.* That's all she said."

"Then they've been told."

"They've been told." Kellan looked at her. "I'm not saying anything's going to happen. I'm doing a sum. A lodger goes to a laid door. Coombe showed that. The Ropewalk lays everything they've got; they laid a lock this morning. A pill's mouth on a neap ebb with the wind where it is—" He stopped. "I'm on the same beat. I'm a blade. I've watched one in Coombe's cage. I've never seen one loose."

"Jude cut one. In the Pit. Through the cord on stone."

"I know. I read his line." Kellan was quiet for a moment. "Pettit says Halden's work is Halden's work, and the Ropewalk's is theirs, and Bray's is Bray's, and that's the rule on any beat, and he's right." He put his coat on. "That's Pettit. I'm telling you because I wanted to tell somebody who'd not tell me I was being foolish."

"You're not being foolish."

"No," said Kellan. "That's what I was afraid of."

He went down the room to his own bed and did not look back.

Anwen came up the stair at a quarter past six, as Kiva was going down it.

She had been up Tar Lane. Kiva could tell by her boots, which had tar on the heels, and by her face, which had the closed careful look it wore when she had said a thing she had made up her mind to say. She stopped two steps above Kiva on the narrow stair and put her hand on the rail.

"I went to the Ropewalk," she said.

"I know. Jude said."

"I saw the board first. Skerry Pill." Anwen looked down the stairwell, at nothing. "Lyle was in the long shed with the little ones, like before. I told her what the board said. She'd read it. They'd all read it. Bray told them at five."

"What did she say?"

"She said Carrow says a laid boom's the only boom on this coast that holds. She said Mace's walls let a third of it under at Gannet Creek; she'd heard that from the Company. Hers don't let anything under, because her feet are in the mud under them." Anwen's hand tightened on the rail. "She said Wenna's night was in the dark, on the flood, with a crew that didn't know. This is daylight, on the ebb, on a Board beat, with Bray, and everybody's been told. She said Treen will be watching, and if Treen calls *run,* she'll walk."

"Then she'll walk."

"She said she would." Anwen was quiet for a moment. "I've said it twice now. Once a fortnight ago and once tonight. I'll not say it a third time. A third time's not telling somebody. It's minding their business for them." She looked at Kiva at last. "I want it said that I know the difference. I've only lately learned it."

"It's said."

"Good." Anwen went on up the stair past her, and at the top she stopped. "She's teaching a boy of eleven to lay. He's very bad at it. She's very patient." And she went in.

---

The Tarn's four lodged over a cooper's on Quay Street, in a long room that smelled of new oak and wet ash hoops, and Trewin came down to the yard at half past six to meet them with his hat in his hands.

Perrin had come. Aske had let him go on condition that he walked, and that he walked slowly, and that Kiva walked beside him and stopped him if he tried to carry so much as a door handle. So he had walked down Quay Street at a pace that made a woman with a pram overtake them twice, and now he stood in the cooper's yard among stacks of barrel staves with his hand pressed to his side.

"I heard it," said Trewin. "From the stair. On the eleventh." He nodded at Perrin's side. "I'm sorry. It was a very good climb."

"Yours was better," said Perrin. "You planted on the floor. Every step. For fifty paces."

"Mine was slower." Trewin turned his hat round in his hands. "I'd thought of it on Sunday. Coming down the Long Quay from your yard, saying *nobody.* I thought, if he can plant on a word that isn't his, I can plant on a step I've only half taken." He looked at the cobbles. "I'd not have thought of it without the yard. I wanted you to know."

Perrin did not say anything for a moment. Kiva saw him go the strange colour again, the one from the quay.

"We're on your beat tomorrow," he said at last.

"I saw the board. Mallow Creek." Trewin nodded. "Yeo's. She's very good, they say."

"I'm not standing," said Perrin. "I'm on the hard with my hands in my pockets. Finch stands. Rowan's declared Ember."

Trewin looked at him, and then at Kiva, and then at Pru, who had come too and was standing by the staves with her cord at her belt. He was slow, but he was not stupid. Kiva watched him understand.

"For the pass," he said.

"For the pass. If it's wanted." Perrin made himself say it. "If anybody goes under anything, on either half of that creek, and the officer says. We've a catch and a carry. We've no plant." He held out his hand. "I'm asking you to be the third. If it's wanted. Which I hope it won't be."

Trewin did not take the hand. He looked at it for a long time, with his gatepost face quite still. Then he looked past it, at the yard gate, where a tall plaited girl of fifteen was standing in the brown and buff of the Tarn, listening.

"Bridie," he said. "Come here."

The girl came. She had her two plaits pinned up round her head, and her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and Kiva knew her. She was the reserve who had unpacked the Tarn's box at the gate on the first day, and laid out a stone jar, and said *plain* to the clerk, proudly. Her grandfather had kept the chandlery at Tarnfoot for fifty years. The jar had been cut with soot. Kiva had watched her chin begin to shake.

"Hebden," said Trewin. "Bridie Hebden. Our reserve. She's catching tomorrow." He said it slowly, as he said everything. "Our catcher turned her ankle on the stair this morning, coming up after me. It's twice the size it should be. She can't stand on the Longshore."

"I can catch," said Bridie. Her voice was quick and northern and very determined. "I've caught all winter. On the yard at Tarnfoot. I've never caught on a coast."

"Nobody here has," said Pru, by the staves, and went scarlet.

Trewin turned back to Perrin.

"She's my catcher tomorrow," he said. "She's fifteen. If you're asking me to be ready to plant a thing that Rowan carries across our beat, then I'm asking you something back." He nodded at the girl, quite gently. "If it's her. If she's the one who's gone under. You'll not stop to ask me whether I'm ready."

"No," said Perrin.

"Then I'm the third." Trewin took the hand at last. He shook it properly, once, as he nodded. "If the officer says. And if it's wanted, which I hope it won't be, I'll not wait to see her land."

---

They did it dry, in the cooper's yard, among the staves.

They lit nothing. Aske had said one go, on Sunday, and that had been the go; and there was a Hunt in the morning, and nobody's door was to be spent on a cooper's cobbles the night before. So they did it with words and hands and feet.

Pru stood at one end of the yard with her hands held low at her hip, palms up, as though her ward were under something. Kiva stood beside her. Trewin stood eight paces off, by a stack of staves, with his feet square.

"I'll say it twice," said Kiva. "Once when Finch lets go, from where I'm standing. Once when I'm in the air, before I land. You open on the end of the second one. Not when you see me land. Not when you feel it come. On the word."

"I know. I've had it said."

"I'm saying it again. You'll want to wait. Your whole body will want to wait. It did on Sunday." She looked at him across the yard. "Don't."

Trewin nodded, slowly.

"*Nobody,*" said Kiva, short, with the swallow.

Pru's hands opened. Kiva went across the yard at a walk, eight paces, with her right fist closed as if something rode along it. Three paces from Trewin she said it again.

"*Nobody.*"

Trewin opened his hands.

He opened them a count late. Kiva saw it. He had waited to see her stop.

"Again," she said.

They did it again. And again. On the fourth time he opened half a count late. On the seventh, a quarter. On the tenth, he opened on the end of the word, and Kiva saw him do it, and saw his face as he did it. It was white. It was the face of a man who has just stepped off a wall into the dark because somebody he hardly knows has told him there is a floor.

"That's it," she said. "That's the word."

"It's horrible," said Trewin, quite politely.

"It is. It gets less horrible."

"Does it?"

"No," said Pru, from the other end of the yard. "It gets more usual. That's not the same."

Trewin looked at her, and then he laughed. It was the first time Kiva had ever heard him laugh. It was very deep and slow, like a cart going over a bridge.

The customs house clock struck the three-quarters, a long way off down Quay Street. Kiva took her left hand out of her pocket and laid the first two fingers on the nearest barrel stave where it stood on end against the wall, lightly, without leaning.

*It keeps time.*

She wrote it in the back of the book by the light from the cooper's window. *Tuesday the 15th. A quarter to seven. A stave in a cooper's yard on Quay Street. Yes.*

They did it ten times more. Bridie stood by the yard gate and watched every one, with her arms folded and her plaits pinned up and her eyes going from Pru's hands to Kiva's fist to Trewin's feet and back again.

When they had done, and Trewin had gone to fetch a lamp, Bridie came across the cobbles to Kiva.

"What happens in the gap?" she said. "When nobody's got it. When she's let go and you've not got it, and you've let go and he's not got it. What happens to the thing?"

Kiva thought about it. Nobody had ever asked her that. They had asked her how long the gap was, and what it cost, and whether it was a stack. Nobody had asked her what happened to the thing.

"Nothing," she said. "For a quarter of a count, nothing. It hangs. It doesn't belong to anybody, so it doesn't do anything to anybody." She looked at her own right fist. "That's the whole point of it. A thing that wants to fold onto whoever holds it can't fold onto nobody."

Bridie looked at her fist too.

"Our book says you pass a load hand to hand," she said. "Two people holding it at once. So it's never dropped."

"That's the book."

"It sounds safer."

"It sounds safer," said Kiva. "Two people holding a thing at once is two doors on one thing, agreeing. There's a cord on the Longshore that goes to that like a dog to a bone. Our Marshal had one in a cage. It went to two people on a sack." She stopped. She did not want to frighten a girl of fifteen the night before a Hunt. "I'm not saying your book's wrong. I'm saying nobody's is right about everything."

Bridie considered that with her chin up.

"My grandad used to say that," she said. "About his scales." Then her chin did a small thing, not quite a shake. "He said nobody's scales were right about everything, and that's why you weigh twice." She went back to the gate.

---

They walked back along Quay Street in the dark, slowly, at Perrin's pace.

The low town was going to bed. The gratings rattled in the gutters on the count, four and four, and in the windows above the street the lamps went out one by one, yellow, ordinary lamps, oil and wick, nothing like the white ones on the Long Quay. A man was singing in a public house. A dog barked at them from a doorway and stopped when it saw how slowly they were going.

Pru had gone ahead, and Kiva walked beside Perrin with her hands in her coat.

"I've declared you onto a door you'll not use," said Perrin. "And Finch onto the mud. And Trewin onto a thing he's done once on a yard and twenty times dry tonight, for a girl of fifteen who's never caught on a coast." He walked a few paces. "And I'll be on the hard with my hands in my pockets watching. If it goes wrong, it's mine. All of it. My declaring."

"That's captain."

"I know it's captain." He breathed, carefully. "I didn't want to be captain. You know that. I minded it so much I couldn't sleep. And then I found out it meant you got to decide things." He was quiet. "It means you get to decide things for people. That's the other half. Nobody tells you that half."

"You've been told it now."

"I've told myself. It's not the same as being told." Perrin almost laughed, and stopped, and put his hand to his side. "Ow."

They walked on.

At the corner where Quay Street came out onto the Long Quay, the white lamps began. They stood on their fluted posts all the way along the water to the Counting House, without a flicker, and their light lay on the black harbour in long still bars. Behind them the basin was coming in on the flood, quiet, and the gratings had stopped rattling for a moment.

Jude was standing at the end of the quay by the first lamp, looking at the sea.

He had his left arm in its linen again and was standing quite still, with his good hand on the bollard and his face toward the Mole and the dark beyond it, where the sea was breathing, every count at once. Kiva and Perrin stopped beside him, and he did not look round.

"Three and a half," he said. "In the book. And the four's quarter of whatever's clean, tomorrow." He was quiet for a moment. "I've been standing here since the board. I've been thinking about what it's for."

"The ash?"

"The ash. The Hunt. Tomorrow." He did not look round. "Yeo said it's always the same thing. A kitchen in the low town. Keeping the sea out." He lifted his good hand off the bollard and put it back. "We'll go out tomorrow on the Longshore and kill what's there, and comb it, and nearly all of it will be red, because everything is now. And the red can't go in a pump house. It can't go anywhere. It'll sit in a bag with a tag on it for evidence." He was quiet. "So what's it for? The Hunt. If what we bring in can't keep anybody's kitchen dry."

Perrin did not answer, and Kiva did not know the answer.

"I don't know," said Jude. "I've been looking at the sea for half an hour and I don't know. And I'm going out on it tomorrow anyway." He turned his head at last. "That's all. I'll go to bed now. I'd want it said I thought about it."

"It's said," said Kiva.

He went away along the quay toward the chandlery, limping a little, under the white lamps, and Perrin watched him go.

"He'll stay," said Perrin. "Here. When it's done. I'd put money on it."

"Yeo hasn't asked him."

"No. She's watching." Perrin looked at the black water. "That's how they do it on the coast, I think. They watch you till they're sure, and then they ask once."

He turned and went back up the quay toward the loft, with his hand pressed to his side. Kiva stood a moment longer by the bollard where Jude had stood.

She took her left hand out of her pocket, but did not lay the two fingers on anything. She looked at them in the white light: the first two, which bent and gripped and told her when a thing was pushing, and told her when a thing kept time, and on the coast, at the slack, told her nothing at all.

Tomorrow they would be on the Longshore. She would have them on a purse pole again, as she had had them on Penhale's pole in the dark at Gannet Creek, with the flood going inland and the town's count under it. She would know the slack before anybody else on the beat. She would know when it ended.

It was not much: two words, once, to the person who needed them.

She put the hand back in her pocket and went up to bed.
