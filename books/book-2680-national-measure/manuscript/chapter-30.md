# Chapter 30 — Kiva: Slack

Kellan Renn had the sum done before the rung hit the water, and it did not come out.

He was standing in the mud of Skerry Pill at twenty-five past eleven with the line of Halden's second purse in his left hand and a mallet in his right, driving its pole. The pill was narrower than a creek and deeper, a cut in the mud no more than twenty paces across, running seaward between banks of marsh grass. Two forms stood in it. Halden's was a stow, two grey posts and a sleeve, forty paces above the kiddle. The Ropewalk's was a kiddle, seven posts across the channel nearer the sea, leaning on the ebb with the white plates coming out of its skins. And in the pill's mouth, where it opened onto the flats, there was the lodger.

It lay on the mud at the water's edge where the Company had left it. It was grey, and long, longer than a man, as thick as a wrist, and it did not look like anything. It looked like a cable somebody had dropped off a boat. It had not moved since dawn. Bray had said so at the spur, in her voice like a foghorn, and every one of them had looked at it, and then not looked at it, which was harder.

The gust came off the sea at twenty-five past.

Kellan saw it on the flats, a darkness going over the shining mud like the shadow of a cloud. It came up the pill against the ebb, and the brown water stood up in ridges, and the Ropewalk's kiddle leaned hard inland all at once, all seven posts, and the fourth from the north bank cracked at the top.

A yard of it fell. It was a heap of grey ash as it fell. It hit the water and spread.

"*Maud,*" said Carrow. "*Boom.*"

She said it at once. Kellan saw her say it, and saw that she did not think about it. It was a word she had said a hundred times, on this coast, at this kind of moment, and it came out of her the way *down* comes out of a marshal.

Lyle was on the south bank, twenty paces below the kiddle. She stepped down onto the mud at the water's edge and laid.

Kellan had seen her lay in a lane and at a lock. He had never seen her lay on water. Her ward came up at her hip and went out over the pill, silver, and lay down flat on the brown surface across the whole width of the channel, bank to bank, and her feet went into the mud under it in the same breath. He felt them go. Everybody on the beat felt them go. It was like a post being driven into the ground beside you.

The grey fan came down the pill on the ebb and met the silver and stopped.

All of it stopped. Nothing went under. Graywater's walls had let a third of a rung under at Gannet Creek; Lyle's let nothing under. The grey piled against her laid ward in a long steaming crust from bank to bank, and the ebb leaned on it, and she stood on the mud with her feet in the floor of the pill and her wall in the water and did not move an inch.

It was beautiful. Kellan thought so, and was ashamed afterward that he had stopped to think it.

"*Net,*" said Bray, from the spur.

Then Treen said, "*Run.*"

Kellan turned his head.

Treen was on the north bank, small and dark, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her eyes on the pill's mouth. She had not shouted. She had said it the way she said it in a lane, short and flat, for one person.

He looked where she was looking.

The lodger had lifted its tip.

It lay along the mud at the water's edge as it had lain since dawn, grey, dull, like a dropped cable. But its tip was off the mud now. It was up, a hand's breadth, and turned, and pointing. It pointed up the pill. It pointed at the silver lying flat across the water forty paces away, and at the girl standing on the bank behind it with her feet in the mud.

Kellan had watched a lodger's tip turn in Coombe's cage in the second month, from the rail of the Pit, and had thought that he understood it. He had not understood how fast it would be.

The lodger came off the mud into the water. It went up the pill along the south bank, under the surface, the way an eel goes, straight, without any of the bends a snake makes. Kellan could see it as a ripple and a darkness. It was coming at a man's walking pace, and then faster.

"*Run,*" said Treen again. "*Maud. Run.*"

Lyle did not run.

Kellan saw her hear it. He saw her head turn a fraction toward the north bank. And then he saw her look at the grey crust lying against her wall, forty drams of the Board's ash, steaming, with the whole of the ebb leaning on it. If she shut, it went. It went down the pill on the ebb, straight into the mouth, onto the flats, into the sea. It went into the water where the lodger was.

She looked back at the crust. She did not move.

"*Maud,*" said Carrow. "*Run. Shut and run. Leave it.*"

It was too late for that. Kellan knew it was too late even as Carrow said it. The ripple was ten paces from Lyle's boots.

He dropped the mallet.

He did not decide to. He found afterward that he had done the sum, and that the sum had come out, and that he had not waited for it. The sum was that he was a blade, sixty paces off, with an Ember door that would light on his right fist in less than a count, and that the lodger was a cord, and a cord can be cut. And there was nobody else on the beat who was a blade and nearer. Halden's sum was something else. He would do Halden's sum later.

"*Renn,*" said Pettit, behind him.

He was already running.

He ran across the mud of the south bank toward Lyle. The mud took his boots to the ankle at every stride, and the ripple in the water was going faster than he was. He saw it reach the bank below Lyle's boots. He saw it come out of the water onto the mud, grey, dripping, and go up over the toe of her left boot, and lie there.

Its tip went into the place where her ankle met the mud.

Kellan saw her wall go dark.

It did not shut. A ward that shuts goes in on itself, folds, closes like a hand. This went out. It went out the way a lamp goes out when the oil is gone: it was silver, and then it was grey, and then it was nothing, and the water lay brown where it had been. The grey crust that had been piled against it went down the pill on the ebb all at once, in a long fan, steaming, past the girl, past the mouth, onto the flats.

Lyle did not fall. She was still standing. Her feet were still in the mud. Her stance was still open; Kellan could feel it, a post driven into the ground. But she was standing quite still with her hands open at her sides, and her face had no expression on it at all.

He reached her.

The lodger lay across the mud from the water's edge to her boot. Its tip was still in the place at her ankle. Its body went back into the pill, grey, as thick as a wrist, lying on the mud in a long curve like a cable dropped off a boat.

Kellan lit.

His Ember came down his right fist. He did not cut toward the girl. He cut down, into the mud, a pace from her boot, through the grey thing lying on it. He cut as Jude had cut one on the coping of a race in the Pit in the second month, by lighting above it and letting the edge come straight down. The edge went through the lodger and into the mud under it, and the mud hissed.

The lodger did nothing. It did not writhe. Its two halves lay on the mud, and went grey, and then greyer, like ash cooling. The tip came out of the place at Lyle's ankle. It fell onto her boot, and lay there, and did not lift again.

Kellan shut.

He stood in the mud a pace from Lyle with his right fist dark and his heart going very fast, faster than it had gone at the Proprietor's table, faster than in any lane. He looked at her face.

"Miss Lyle," he said.

She did not look at him. She was looking at the water where her wall had been.

"It's quiet," she said.

Treen got there first after him, and then Carrow, running with nothing open, and then Tolver, and then Bray, who did not run but walked very fast and seemed to cover the mud as if it were a floor.

Lyle had shut her stance. Kellan felt the post go out of the ground. She stood in the mud on her own feet, with nothing open, and she lifted her right hand to her hip, where a ward comes up, and held it there.

Nothing came up.

She held it there for a long time. Kellan watched her face. It was the square careful face that he had seen at the mast and on her knees in a lane. It did not change.

"Maud," said Carrow.

"There's nothing there." Lyle lowered her hand. "I can't find it. It's like reaching for a step in the dark and it's not there." She put her hand back to her hip. "It's not shut. It's gone."

"Try."

"I am trying." Lyle's voice did not rise. "I'm opening my stance. That's there." Kellan felt the post go into the mud again, and come out. "I'm opening my ward. There's nothing to open." She looked at Carrow at last. "It's quiet. Juniper. It's all quiet. I always said laid was quiet. It isn't. This is."

Carrow did not say anything. Kellan watched her. She stood in the mud with her hands at her sides and her quick blunt face gone very still, and she did not reach for Lyle, and she did not look away from her.

"I called boom," said Carrow.

"You always call boom."

"I called it laid." Carrow's voice was quite steady. "I didn't say laid. I didn't have to. I've never said boom and meant anything else." She was quiet for a moment. "Treen called run."

"I heard her."

"Why didn't you?"

"Forty drams," said Lyle. "And the lodger was in the mouth, and if I'd shut, it would all have gone down onto it." She looked down the pill at the grey fan, which had gone out onto the flats now and was spreading on the shallow water there, steaming, a long way off. "It went anyway. And so did I."

Bray had reached them. She stood over the halves of the lodger on the mud and looked at them, and then at Lyle, and then at Kellan.

"Who gave the word?" she said. Her voice was so deep that Kellan felt it in his chest.

"Nobody, ma'am," said Kellan.

"Nobody." Bray took a notebook out of her coat. It was the same as Yeo's, a Company book, green. She wrote in it standing, quickly. "*The Hold of the Ropewalk's first four laid a wall on the water on her captain's word, to hold a rung shed on a surge. A lodger in the pill's mouth went to it. The catcher called run. The Hold held. Entered at the left ankle. The ward gone; not shut.*" She stopped writing and looked at Lyle. "Gone, Miss Lyle?"

"Gone, ma'am."

Bray wrote it. "*The blade of Halden License left his own work and cut the lodger on the mud, on no officer's word.*" She looked at Kellan. "That's a remark. I'll say whose it is. It's the blade's." She closed the book. "And I'd have given the word. I'd want that read, by whoever reads it. I'd have given it if I'd been a breath quicker, and I wasn't."

"Thank you, ma'am."

"Don't thank me. I've just cost your house a dram on the mark." Bray put the book away. "Get back to your purse. The slack's in three-quarters of an hour, and you've a pole half driven."

---

He went back across the mud to Halden's stow.

Pettit was standing at the purse pole where Kellan had left it, with the mallet in his hand. He had driven the pole himself. The line was tied off. He looked at Kellan coming across the mud with his heavy fair face quite still, the sleepy face that made people underestimate him, and he did not say anything until Kellan was beside him.

"I said your name," said Pettit.

"I heard you."

"I didn't say it to stop you." Pettit gave him the mallet. "I said it because I was going to go myself, and you were nearer, and I wanted you to know somebody was coming after you." He looked down the pill at Lyle, standing in the mud with Carrow and Treen. "That's all. I'd have gone. I'm a still door. I'd have been no use. I'd have gone."

Kellan took the mallet.

"It's a dram on the mark," he said. "Bray's remark. And I left the second line half set. If it's not right at the slack—"

"Then it's not right at the slack, and Yarrow will haul what he can, and we'll be a place lower on a board." Pettit shrugged. He did it slowly, as he did everything. "I can count. I know what a place is worth. I've been counting places all week." He was quiet for a moment. "I'd expect nothing else of you. You know that. That's what you say."

"I say it about other people."

"I know. I've heard you." Pettit almost smiled. "I'm saying it about you."

Kellan did the sum, at last, aloud.

"Forty drams of the Board's ash gone onto the flats, wet, on the ebb. It'll comb red if anybody nets it, and nobody will. A dram off Halden on the mark, mine. A line half set, so maybe a part of our second rung in the pill at the slack, and a remark for that as well, mine. The Ropewalk's whole rung gone, and a remark, theirs." He stopped. "And a door."

"And a door," said Pettit.

"It doesn't go in a sum," said Kellan. "I don't know what column it goes in."

"No," said Pettit. "Nor do I."

He took the line of the second purse in his hands and began, slowly, to check the knot.

---

At twelve minutes past twelve, on Mallow Creek, Kiva's fingers said nothing, and the four posts stood up out of the mud.

"*Slack,*" said Yeo. "Twelve minutes past. Seven. Your stow."

Kiva did not hear any of what had happened on Skerry Pill. She would not hear it until the cart, on the causeway. On Mallow Creek there was only the slack, and the stow, and the mud.

It was very still. It was stiller than Gannet Creek had been at midnight, because there was daylight on it and she could see how still it was. The thread of water in the channel's bottom lay in its bed like a ribbon, not running either way. The marsh grass on the west bank stood up straight and did not move. The white plates that had been coming out of the stow's sleeve all morning had stopped coming; the last of them lay on the mud where the ebb had left them, melting. And the stow stood in the channel, its two posts upright, one whole and one cut short a yard, with the mouth hanging slack between them and the long grey sleeve lying down on the mud behind, downstream, as flat as a coat dropped on a floor.

"Talley," said Kiva.

Jude went down into the channel.

He went down the bank on his good leg and his bad, carefully, into the mud to the shin, and waded across to the near post, the short one. He stood beside it. It was the height of his own shoulder now, a yard shorter than it had been. He lit his edge on his right fist, short and bright, and held it at his side, and did not lift it.

Kiva was on her knees at the first purse pole on the bank, with the line in her fists and her two fingers resting on the ash pole above them. Pru was on her knees at the second pole, three paces downstream, with her own line. Anwen was on the bank behind them with her hands out of her sleeves. On the spur, Yeo stood with her notebook and Perrin beside her with the glass.

Kiva looked at Jude's heel.

It was in the mud. She could not see a heel in mud; she could see his boot, and the line of his leg above it, and the way his weight sat. She watched it. She watched it come down off the ball of his foot onto the heel, the way it did before he cut. She had been watching him cut since the second month. She knew it as she knew her own name.

"*Not yet,*" she said.

He did not look round. She saw his head go a little on one side, listening.

The weight settled. The knee came straight.

"*Now.*"

The light went level across the channel, and the top of the near post came off.

It fell straight down. There was no ebb to take it and no flood. It fell like a stone into the still shallow water at the post's foot, and the water closed over it with a grey bloom, and it went down onto the purse lying open in the mud.

Kiva hauled.

The line came in through her fists, fast, hand over hand, the way Pru had hauled at Gannet Creek. Under the water the drawstring would be closing the purse round the rung like the neck of a bag. She hauled until the line stopped coming. Then she put her weight back on it and held.

"One fathom," she said. "Two fathom." She did not know she was going to count it. It was Penhale's count, and it had gone into her somewhere and stayed, like Mace's *down a hand.* "Three. Shut. It's shut."

"*Shut,*" said Yeo, from the spur, and wrote.

"*Far edge,*" said Pru.

Kiva had not looked up yet. She was still on her knees with the line in her fists. She looked up now.

The near post was a stump. The mouth of the stow had hung between the near post and the far one; now, on the near side, it had nothing to hang from. It came down. It came down slowly, a grey curtain falling into the slack water. And behind it the sleeve, the long grey sleeve that had been lying down flat on the mud downstream like a dropped coat, moved.

Its far end lifted.

The closed end, the end furthest from the posts, came up off the mud. It came up in a long grey lip, streaming, frosting the mud as it left it. And it bent over as a page turns. It was turning toward the nearest line. It was turning toward the first purse's line, and toward the girl on her knees at the end of it.

Kiva let go of the line.

She let go and stepped back on the bank, without turning round, as Rhea had stepped back off a skin in the Corn Exchange yard, as Penhale's Company man had stepped back on the hard at Gannet Creek. The purse was shut. Shut was shut. A shut purse is a sealed bag, on this coast.

The fold came over and came down. It came down on the mud and on the slack line where it lay on the bank, flat, with a wet slap, and lay there steaming, a yard from her knees.

Then it lay still. It went greyer by degrees. It did not lift again.

"*Hold,*" said Yeo. She had stepped to the edge of the spur. She was looking at the fold, and at Kiva on the bank above it, and at Pru on her knees three paces off. "Seven. Who called that?"

"Finch, ma'am," said Kiva. "My second."

Yeo looked at Pru for a long moment.

"Finch," she said. "From where?"

"From here, ma'am." Pru had not moved from her knees. Her face was scarlet and her voice was steady. "I was watching it. Not the post. The far end of the sleeve. I've had it from Pike. The second watches the far edge and says it once." She swallowed. "I said it once."

Yeo wrote. She wrote for some time.

"*The near post cut at twelve and fourteen,*" she said aloud, as she wrote. "*Shut. The mouth fell; the far end of the stow lifted and folded toward the catcher's line. The second called far edge, once, from three paces. The catcher came off the line. The fold lay down on the bank where the catcher had been.*" She stopped. "I've not seen that before. I've seen the far edge come. I've not seen anybody watching for it."

"It's a place, ma'am," said Pru. "Not a person. Coombe says."

"So she does," said Yeo, and went on writing.

The far post came down at twenty-four minutes past.

It was the tall one, whole, standing in the middle of the channel with its half of the mouth hanging from it and the sleeve, now, lying in a heap where it had folded. Jude waded to it through the slack water. Kiva watched his heel.

"*Not yet.*"

His weight settled.

"*Now.*"

The top of the far post came off and went straight down into the still water, onto the second purse. Pru hauled. She hauled hand over hand, scarlet to the ears, with the line coming in through her fists, and leaned back on it when it stopped.

"One fathom," said Pru. "Two. Three. *Shut.*"

And the stow died.

Kiva had never seen a thing die all at once like that. The kiddle at Gannet Creek had been cut three posts at a time, and then four, and had gone to ash in the mud slowly, over the low water, while she was walking away from it in the dark. This went at once. The two stumps went grey and soft and slumped in the mud like candles in a hot room. The mouth went grey. The sleeve, lying in its fold on the bank and across the mud, went grey all along its length, and then thin, and then it was a film of ash lying on the mud like dust on a sill.

Only the dark place in the creek bed was left, under where the mouth had been. The tear. The thread of slack water lay round it in a ruffle.

"*Dead,*" said Yeo. "Seven's stow. Twenty-nine minutes past. Two posts, two purses shut, and a rung in the net's tin from the ebb." She shut her notebook. "That's clean work. I'll say so on the slate." She looked down the spur. "Ivey. Your crew. The upper tear's yours."

Ivey came along the spur with the closing crew behind her, four Company men in green and their officer, a short grey man with his sleeves rolled. They went down into the channel past Jude, who was coming out of it on his good leg and his bad with his edge shut and his face white. They knelt in the mud round the dark place in the bed, with their hands out over it. The light under their hands was steady and white.

Kiva sat back on her heels on the bank.

She looked at her hands. They were shaking. The left one, the warm one, with its two fingers that bent and gripped and told her nothing, was shaking as much as the right. She put them both flat on her knees.

"Pru," she said.

"Don't," said Pru, three paces off, still on her knees with the second line slack in her fists. "Don't say anything. I'll go red and I'll not be able to haul anything for an hour."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"Yes, you were." Pru looked at her sideways. "You were going to say *well called.* I could see it in your face. Don't. I'd sooner you said it to Pike, in a letter, and let him say it to me."

Kiva did not say it. She sat on the bank with her hands flat on her knees and looked at the film of grey ash on the mud where the stow had been, and at the Company crew kneeling round the tear with their hands out and the white light under them.

She put the first two fingers of her left hand back on the ash pole. Lightly. Without leaning.

Nothing.

The slack held. It was twenty-nine minutes past twelve, and the slack had been holding for seventeen minutes, and it would hold for twenty minutes more, near enough, if the wind held. The channel lay quite still. Down at the Tarn's half, sixty paces off, the lower stow stood in the bend with its two posts upright and its sleeve lying flat on the mud behind it.

---

The Tarn cut their near post at twenty-five to one.

Kiva watched it from the bank. Their blade was a big red-haired boy she did not know, and he cut from the mud as Jude had, standing beside the post. Bridie called it. Kiva could hear her from sixty paces, high and quick and northern. *Not yet. Now.* The rung went straight down into the slack water. Bridie hauled.

"*Shut,*" she called. "*One fathom, two, three. Shut.*"

She had Penhale's count. Kiva heard Penhale laugh on the spur, loud and pleased, and say something to Yeo that she could not hear.

The near post was a stump. The mouth fell. Kiva watched the far end of the Tarn's sleeve, lying on the mud downstream of the posts on the west side of the channel, and waited for it to lift.

It did not lift.

It lay on the mud. It lay as flat as Seven's had lain before the cut, flat as a dropped coat, and did not move. The Tarn's corner-hand, a thin boy in brown and buff on the west bank, was standing at the far side of it with the line of their second purse in his fists, and he was looking at the sleeve too, and it lay still.

"It's not folding," said Pru, beside her, very low.

"It's still on a post." Kiva looked at the far post of the lower stow. It stood on the west side of the bend, in the deep water, upright, whole, with its half of the mouth hanging from it. "I don't know why. Ours folded. It should."

"So it doesn't fold."

"So far it doesn't fold."

The Tarn's blade was getting into the punt.

That was the bend. Yeo had said it at nine. The channel was deeper on the outside of the bend, and the far post stood in the deep side, and at the bottom of the slack there was still a yard of water round its foot. A blade could not stand beside it in the mud. He had to be beside it in a boat. The boatman held the punt against the post with his pole. The red-haired boy stood up in it, unsteady, with his edge unlit, and Trewin stood on the east bank above with the punt's line in his two hands, holding it as he held everything.

It took time. A punt is a slow thing in a yard of water and a foot of mud. The boatman pushed it off the bank and it stuck, and he pushed again, and it came free and slid out toward the post and went past it, and he had to bring it back. Kiva watched the clock in her own head. Twenty to one. Eighteen minutes to.

The second purse's line came up on the west side of the channel. It came up from the purse lying open in the mud under the far post, across the mud, and up the west bank to its pole. The lying sleeve lay across that mud. The corner-hand on the west bank had the line at the pole. But the line had caught. Kiva could see it from where she sat; it had caught on something in the mud, half way, and lay in a sharp bend instead of a curve, and if the purse were hauled like that it would not close.

Bridie saw it too. Kiva saw her see it.

She was on the east bank, with the first line slack in her hands. She put the line down. She called something across to the corner-hand, and he shook his head, and called back, and pointed. To clear the line, somebody would have to go down onto the west mud, across the thread, and walk out along the line to where it had caught, and free it. And the line lay across the sleeve.

Bridie looked up the spur at Yeo.

Kiva could not hear what she said. She saw her say it. She saw her point at the sleeve lying on the mud, and at the caught line across it, and say a few words, a question.

She saw Yeo look at the sleeve.

Yeo looked at it for a long breath. Then she answered. Kiva could not hear the words, but she knew what they were. She had heard them before, at a coal wharf in Meridian in the frost, from a man with his heel on a white patch of hem. *A hem that lies down is ground. That's the book.*

Bridie went down onto the west mud.

She went across the thread of slack water in two strides, in her boots, with her plaits pinned up round her head and the long cord wound twice round her wrist. She went out along the caught line. She stepped onto the sleeve where it lay across the mud, flat and grey and still, and walked on it to where the line was caught, and knelt on it, and began to free the line with her fingers.

It held her. It was hard. It did not take a boot. It was ground.

Kiva had her two fingers on the ash pole. They said nothing. The slack held.

"Pru," she said.

"I see her."

"It's still on a post."

"I know." Pru had not moved. "Kiva. I know."

The red-haired boy was beside the far post in the punt. Trewin had the line taut. The boatman leaned on his pole. Yeo had come along the spur to the Tarn's half and stood above them on the stone with her notebook open, and Penhale stood beside her, red-bearded, in his thigh boots, with his hands in the pockets of his smock.

Bridie had freed the line. Kiva saw it come straight in the mud, a curve again. Bridie stood up on the sleeve and turned to come back.

The far post of the lower stow moved.

It was a quarter to one. Kiva knew afterward that it was a quarter to one, or a minute before, because Perrin had the glass and the watch, and he wrote it down. She did not know it then. She knew only that her two fingers were on the ash pole and they said *nothing,* still, *nothing,* the slack holding, the water still, the grass still, the thread lying in its bed like a ribbon. And the far post moved.

It leaned.

It leaned inland, a hair, the way the upper stow's post had leaned at ten to eleven before the gust had reached it. Only there was no gust. There was no flood. The water had not moved. Kiva's fingers on the pole said that the water had not moved, and the grass on the west bank stood up straight, and the thread lay in its bed. And the far post of the Tarn's stow leaned inland on the turn of a tide that had not turned.

And the stow swung.

It swung on the post as a vane swings on a church when the wind comes round. The half of the mouth that still hung from it came round, and the long grey sleeve that had been lying flat on the mud downstream came round after it, its closed end last. It lifted off the mud. It lifted the whole of its length at once, streaming, grey, and swung round on the standing post like a door on its hinge, out of the downstream and inland over the west mud, its mouth to the sea that had not yet begun to come in.

Bridie was standing on it.

She went down. She went down on the mud of the west side as the sleeve came up from under her, and the sleeve came round over her. It came up and over in a long grey fold, frosting as it came, and it came down. It came down on the mud on the west side of the channel with Bridie Hebden under it, flat, with a wet sound that Kiva heard from sixty paces.

The corner-hand at the pole had the far edge, by the book. He was hauling. He had the second purse's line in his fists, and he was hauling it, because the line was clear now and the purse was under the post and the post had not been cut, and he did not know what he was hauling. He had his eyes on the line.

"*Far edge,*" said Kiva.

She said it as loud as she had ever said anything. It went across sixty paces of still mud and still water, and the corner-hand did not turn his head, because it was not his name and it was not his voice, and nobody on that half of the creek was listening for it.

"*Down,*" said Yeo, on the spur. "*A person down. Penhale.*"

Penhale went.

He went off the spur and down the east bank into the channel in two strides, the way he had gone into the mud a hundred times. He went across the thread of water in his thigh boots and up onto the west mud. He did not hurry and he did not hesitate. He went to the fold as a man goes to a gate he has opened every day of his life.

He went to the near edge.

That was the book. Kiva knew it as she watched him. She had never read the Company's book, but she knew it, because it was the same as every other book: when a person is under a skin, the catcher goes to the near edge, and gets his ward under it, and lifts, and the person rolls out under the ward. It was a page and a half. It had been the right page for forty years.

Penhale's ward came up at his hip. It was broad and low and grey, a coast catcher's ward, and he knelt and put it under the near edge of the fold where it lay on the mud, and he lifted.

The fold came up. It came up like the cover of a book. Kiva saw Bridie's arm under it, her sleeve, a plait come loose, the brown and buff of her coat. She saw Penhale lean in under the fold with his ward up over his head like a roof, and reach for the arm with his free hand, and take it.

The fold had two ends.

Kiva saw that too late. Everybody saw it too late. It was a sleeve. It had a mouth, at the post, and a closed end, a long way off down its length. The fold that had come down over Bridie was its middle. The closed end was still coming round. It was still swinging round on the post over the west mud, the last of the long grey sleeve, the far end of it, the end that had been lying furthest downstream on the mud all morning while everybody watched the mouth.

It came round over the near edge. It came round over Penhale's back.

It came down.

It came down on top of the fold and on top of the man kneeling under it with his ward over his head and his hand on a girl's arm. It came down flat, grey, frosting, with a sound that Kiva did not hear, because she was on her feet by then and running, and Pru was running beside her, and somebody on the spur was shouting her name.

The far post of the Tarn's stow stood up straight in the channel's bend.

And under her boots, on the mud of the east bank, as she ran, the slack held. Her fingers were not on anything; she did not need them. The marsh grass on the west bank stood up straight and did not stir. The thread of water in the channel's bottom lay in its bed like a ribbon, running neither way. Nothing on Mallow Creek had turned but the stow.
