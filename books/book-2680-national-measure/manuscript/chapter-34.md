# Chapter 34 — Kiva: Who Has It

The returns were on yellow forms, eleven of them, and Noa had laid them out on the sloping top of his reading desk in a row, weighted at the corners with whatever he had in his pockets.

"The Chair asked for these on the 12th," he said. "It was on her third sheet in the autumn. *A Chair can ask who else has a keeper with a book.* Lumb's notion. She asked the Clerk, and the Clerk asked the Board, and the Board wired every pump house and lamp office on its roll on Saturday morning." He did not look up from the forms. "One question. *Look in your remarks column. Any night on which the whole house did one thing together that was in no column. Wire the date and the line.* The wire office has been bringing them to me since Tuesday, because I'm the one with the columns."

Polglase was at his own desk, a yard off, rolling the day's tape. He did not pretend not to listen.

"Eleven," said Kiva.

"Eleven so far. There are a hundred and forty houses on the Board's roll. Most of them haven't looked yet. Some of them won't." Noa touched the first form with the end of his pencil. "Eight say nothing found. That's a return too. I've written them all down."

He moved the pencil along to the ninth.

*WESTERLEY HARBOUR PUMP HOUSE. KEEPER'S BOOK 2604. 11TH THIRD MONTH. AT THREE THE STEPPED LAMPS ON THE QUAY WHITE. TOWN LAMPS OUT HALF HOUR. KEEPER ILL OF A FEVER, HIS WIFE SAT UP. HE SAID IN THE MORNING THE ROOF WAS OFF. NOT FAULT.*

Kiva read it twice. She was aware of Polglase's tape going round on its spool, and of the timber pond's water moving under the boards, low, on the ebb.

"Westerley's three hundred miles from here," said Noa. "Up the coast. They've a refinery on their quay as well. Not Halden's. A smaller house, under licence." He moved the pencil. "The tenth."

*TARNFOOT WORKS. 2604. 11TH THIRD MONTH. THE FOUR DRUMS STOOD UP TOGETHER A LITTLE AFTER THREE AND LAY DOWN. NO FAULT FOUND.*

"No fever," said Kiva.

"No fever written. That's not the same as none." He did not move the pencil for a moment. Then he moved it to the last form, and left it there.

*HOLLOWAY LAMP OFFICE. 17TH NINTH MONTH 2680. AT TWENTY PAST TWO IN THE AFTERNOON EVERY LAMP ON THE HOLLOWAY RING ONCE. NIGHT LAMP-MAN TOOK ILL THAT EVENING, A FEVER TWO NIGHTS. ASKED BY THE WARDEN WHAT HE SAW IN IT. WOULD NOT HAVE IT WRITTEN. SAID HE WOULD BE LAUGHED AT. A FEVER IS NOT A FAULT.*

Kiva stood at the desk with her bound right hand against her coat and her left hand flat on the wood beside the form.

Twenty past two on the 17th of the ninth month. She knew that minute. Everybody who had come south knew it. It was the minute that four husks stood a square in the Pit at home, and Selka Dray went under a corner with her leg broken, and thirty-six cells at the head of this harbour fell into step and had not come out. It was on a bill on every warehouse door on the Long Quay. *IN STEP SINCE TWENTY PAST TWO.*

And two hundred miles off, a man who lit lamps for a living had taken a fever that evening, and had seen something in it, and had not let it be written down.

"He'd be laughed at," she said.

"That's what the warden wired." Noa's voice was quite level. "I've written it as it came. I'll not add to it. I'll not take from it." He took the pencil off the form and put it behind his ear. "There's one more thing. It isn't a return. It's mine."

He unrolled a short length of the gate mast's tape across the forms. It was the night's tape, from midnight to dawn, eight lines running flat along it from the eight pens, because nobody had stood on the mast's crosses since Monday. At one place there was a mark across all eight, top to bottom. It was no taller than a hair is wide.

"Eight minutes to one this morning," said Noa. "By the mast clock. Nobody on the mast." He laid his finger beside it. "I asked the Company's officer at noon whether anything happened on the Longshore in the night. She said her closing crew shut the lower tear at Mallow Creek at the bottom of the ebb, with a guard on the bank. I asked her when. She looked in her book." He did not take his finger away. "Eight minutes to one."

"And yesterday's," said Kiva. "Twelve forty-four."

"Twelve forty-four, when a stow turned. Eight minutes to one, when a tear shut." He rolled the tape again. "That's two. I've looked at every tape since. There's nothing else on them. Two is not a column. I've written them both down, on the same page, and I've not drawn anything between."

"He's begun to look," said Polglase, from his desk, to nobody in particular. "Put that down as well, Mr. Bexley. You've two hairs and an old man who's begun to look."

Noa did not smile. But the corner of his mouth moved.

"I'm putting a fourth column on the sheet for tomorrow night," he said to Kiva. "The Board sits at seven, at the Salt Hall, on the Chair's three. I've people, and rifts and spawn, and Saltmere. I'm adding ash." He touched the ledger on the desk's ledge. "What the comb says. How much of every year's yield sang on the seventh. It goes up with the others. It went up faster from the fifth month, the same as the others." He looked at her. "I'll draw the four on one sheet with one rule along the bottom for the weeks. I'll not draw anything between them. If the Board wants a line, it'll have to draw it itself."

"They'll ask you to."

"They can ask." He began to gather up the forms, one at a time, in their order. "I'd be a fool to draw it. I've drawn one wrong before."

She walked along the boards to the end of the timber pond and back, twice, with the Holloway form in her head.

*Would not have it written. Said he would be laughed at.*

She knew exactly how he had felt. She had felt it on a ferry, with a hand on her wrist and a voice counting, and something in her that had learned at a hem to keep two words behind her teeth until they were owed. She had kept her mouth shut on the ferry and the barge and the coast boat. She had kept it shut at the Proprietor's table with her jaw aching. She had been, she thought, rather proud of how well she kept it shut. It had seemed like a thing she had learned, like a catcher learns not to speak until the bag is sealed.

But a catcher's second keeps her mouth shut so that she can say the thing afterward, to the person who needs it. That was the whole of the rule. Two words, once, and then you said what he had done that he did not say aloud. A second who never said it at all was not a second. She was only somebody standing at a hem.

A lamp-man in Holloway had kept his mouth shut, and nobody would ever know what he had seen, because nobody had been sitting at a kitchen table with a pencil and a clean page. A keeper's wife in Westerley had sat up with her husband, and he had said *the roof was off* in the morning, and somebody had written it in remarks and put *not fault* under it. A keeper at Ferren had written it in brown ink because the needles did it too. A keeper here had written it because his father had told him to write what the needles did. Every one of them had put it in the remarks column, which was where you wrote a thing that was not a fault and not anything else. Not one of them had been believed, because there was no way on earth to tell whether a man with a fever had borrowed it from somebody.

Except that there was one page in the country that had been sealed before anybody could have borrowed anything. It had been written at ten o'clock at night in the girl's own words, on the same night as the dream. It was shut under a reader's wax with the hour written through the join, in a press in a basement two hundred miles away, before the girl had ever opened a grey box and read *the sky unroofed.* Mara had done that so that nobody could ever say Kiva had borrowed. Kiva had thought, last spring, that the seal was there to protect her. She saw now that it had never been for that. A seal is not for keeping a thing safe. It is for being opened in front of people, so that they can believe what is inside.

She stopped at the end of the boards by the Graving's old wall, where she had stood at a quarter to seven with two fingers on a mooring ring.

It was hers. Mara had said so on these same boards a week ago, with a gull on the black water. *You needn't tell anybody. That's the only thing I know for certain about any of this. It's yours.* It was hers to keep, and she had kept it for four years.

It was also, she thought, a thing with no handles. It lived in one head, the way the three doors lived in one hand. It was worth nothing to anybody while it stayed there. It was worth nothing to a lamp-man in Holloway who would not have it written down.

She would put it on the table.

She did not decide it all at once. She found that she had decided it somewhere between the mast and the wall, and that she was only now noticing. It sat in her as the week's crossing had sat that morning. It was not like a door being shut. It was like a coin being put down on a counter, where anybody could see it.

Then she thought about what it would cost, because that was the next thing a person did, and she made herself do it properly.

Everybody in the city would know. Everybody on the tiers would look at her tomorrow in the Graving and see a girl who dreamed, and not a catcher. The Mercury would have it. Halden's counsel would have it, and would want to know what she had seen, and so would the Order, and so would the Proprietor, who had sat across a table from her a week ago and told her about a window in 2604 while she held her teeth together. Dray would have it. Carrow would have it. Her four would have it from somebody else, if she did not give it to them first.

That was the first thing, then. Her four, before anybody.

She went back along the boards toward Quiller's, in the dusk, and she did not hurry. The quay lamps were coming on along the Long Quay, white and steady, one after another, and she counted them as she walked without meaning to, and stopped herself at four.

---

She asked them to come down to the shop at eight.

Quiller had let her have it before, for Ivey. He let her have it again, without asking why, and went up to his own rooms at the back with a candle and shut his door. The shop was dark and smelled of tar and new rope and the paraffin in the stove. Kiva lit the one lamp on the counter, the one Ivey had read by, and set it between the coils.

They came down the outside stair one at a time. Pru first, with her capped hands held up in front of her and two of the caps fresh, because Aske had redone them at five. Then Jude, with the book under his good arm. Then Anwen, who had been somewhere and had tar on her boots. Perrin came last, a step at a time with his hand on the rail, and stood in the doorway for a moment looking at the lamp and the four faces round it, before he came in and shut the door behind him.

"This is like the night before the Pit," he said. "At home. When you told us about the three."

"It's like that," said Kiva. "It isn't that."

There were no chairs in the shop. Perrin leaned against the counter, very carefully, with his weight on his heels. Anwen sat on a coil of rope. Jude stood. Pru stood beside him with her hands up, because there was nowhere to put them.

Kiva did not sit. She stood on the other side of the counter, where Ivey had stood, with the lamp between them.

"I dream," she said.

It was very quiet in the shop. Outside, a cart went along Quay Street on the cobbles, and the gratings in the gutter rattled on the count.

"Not like anybody dreams," she said. "With a fever. When I was Kindled, at thirteen, in a yard. On Classification Day, in the hall. In the Pit last spring, and my aunt wrote that one down the same night. And on the ferry below the Order's house, last month." She kept her voice level, as she would have kept it at a hem. "It's always the same, more or less. I'll tell you what it is, once, and then you'll know, and I'll not have to wonder what you'd think if you knew."

She told them.

She told it as she had told it to Mara at a kitchen table at ten o'clock at night, with a wet cloth on her chest. She told them about the height, and being above things, looking down. About the roads, from every side at once, and the lights on them, which were all unlike, and were people. About not knowing who they were, and knowing what they were, because she knew what she was. About looking up, and there being nothing there. Not dark. Nothing. And something in the nothing that was enormous, and close, and paying attention. She told them about the notes, and how she had tried to count them once and lost her number at four, and the lights touching. She told them about the lamps of the whole city answering.

She told them that on the ferry she had been lower than before, and that the lights had been nearer, and that some of them had been going out.

She did not tell them the rest of the ferry. She found she had not decided, yet, whom that was for. It was not a thing to be said in a shop to four people who had come down a stair at eight o'clock because she asked. It might be a thing for somebody else. She held it back, and knew that she was holding it back, and let them see that she knew.

"There's more about the ferry," she said. "I'll say it where it's asked for. I'm not keeping it from you. I don't yet know who it's for."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Pru had gone scarlet, slowly, from the collar. She did not look away.

"I knew something came," she said. "On the barge. You had your teeth shut so hard I thought you'd break them. And at the Counting House, after, you said *nothing came,* out loud, and I knew that meant it came the other times." She swallowed. "I wanted to know so badly I couldn't sleep. I said I'd not ask. I didn't."

"I know you didn't. That's why you're the first."

"I'm not the first. Your aunt's the first." But Pru's face had changed. "I'm the first in this shop. I'll take that."

Anwen was sitting on the coil of rope with her hands in her sleeves, looking at the lamp.

"What's behind it?" she said.

"I don't know." Kiva met her eyes. "Nobody does. I'm not going to say what it is, because I can't, and I'd be lying if I tried. It's just there. It's been there since I was thirteen, and it's in a Glory master's notebook from two hundred years ago, and in a pump keeper's book at Ferren, and a pump keeper's book here, on the same night in 2604. And this afternoon it came in on a wire from Westerley, and another from Holloway." She told them about the Holloway form. *Would not have it written. Said he would be laughed at.* "That's why I'm telling you. I've been the lamp-man for four years. I'm going to stop."

"Stop how?" said Perrin.

"Mara wrote it down, the night it came, last spring. My words. She sealed it and had a reader at the Library put the hour on it, through the wax, before I'd ever read the notebook. It's in a press in Meridian." Kiva put her left hand flat on the counter. "It's the only page in the country that nobody can say was borrowed. I'm going to ask the Board to open it."

"In front of everybody," said Anwen.

"In front of whoever the Board sits in front of."

Perrin had not moved from the counter. He had his hand pressed to his side under his coat. He looked at her for a long while with his face going the colour it went when he was thinking.

"They'll look at us," he said at last. "All of us. Tomorrow, in the Graving, with Carrow at the other end of the pen. Everybody on the tiers will be looking at you and not at the litter."

"They already look," said Kiva.

"They look at the girl with three doors. That's different. That's a thing you can do." He breathed carefully. "This is a thing you are. They'll look at you as though you'd been sent for. I know you. You'd hate that worse than anything." He paused. "I'm not saying don't. I'm the captain, and it's not a captain's to say. I'm saying I know what it'll cost you, and I'll be on the hurdle with my hands in my pockets when they look."

Kiva found she could not say anything to that, so she nodded.

Jude had not spoken. He was standing by the door with the book under his arm, scratching his jaw slowly with his right hand, and looking at the counter, at her hand on it.

"I asked everybody what ash was for," he said. "Since the autumn. I asked the sea." He stopped. "I never asked what you were for. I thought you were a catcher with three doors, and that was enough to be going on with." He looked up. "I'd want it said that I'm glad you told us before you told the Board. That's all I've got."

"It's enough," said Kiva.

Anwen got up off the coil of rope. She did not come round the counter. She put her hands flat on it, opposite Kiva's, as she put her hands on a card before she signed it.

"I want it said too," she said. "When they look at you tomorrow, I'll be at your back with my wall up, and if anybody says a word on that floor that isn't about the litter, they'll be saying it to me." She did not go red. "And when you tell the Board, I'll be there. Not to say anything. To be there."

"So will I," said Pru. Then she went scarlet all over again. "I'd like to see Halden's face."

Perrin laughed. It hurt him; Kiva saw it hurt him. He laughed anyway, with his hand pressed hard to his side, and then he stopped.

"Who else?" he said. "Before the Board."

"My aunt. Tonight. She's to send for the page, because it comes out to her hand and nobody else's." Kiva counted it on her fingers, the left ones. "Aske, in the morning. Orla. Kellan, before the Lane." She looked at Perrin. "The Board, at the sitting tomorrow night. I'll stand up and ask."

"Not the Clerk first?"

"No. If I ask the Clerk at her tent, she'll write it down and put it in the press and say it'll be heard when it's heard. I want to ask in the room." Kiva took her hand off the counter. "That's where the sheets are. The returns. Noa's columns. That's where it goes. In the first column, with the others. Before."

Nobody argued. Jude opened the book under his arm, and found the page, and took the pencil from its string.

"I'll not write what you told us," he said. "It's not the book's." He wrote one line, and turned the book round so that she could read it.

*Thursday the 17th. At Quiller's, at eight. Rowan told us a thing of her own. We'll stand where she stands.*

Under it, without being asked, the other three signed. Perrin signed last, with his hand pressed to his side and his face scarlet, and then gave her the pencil.

She signed it left-handed. It was very bad. Nobody said so.

Mara Rowan had her left hand, her reading hand, flat on a glass case on the keeper's table at the harbour pump house at ten that night, and it was not one of the thirty-six.

It was the training cell. Peverell had had it carried up from the fitting shop on Wednesday, an old glass case on an iron stand that had been in the Regiment until a crack in its foot put it on the bench in the eighth month. It was not in the bank now. It was not in the water. It sat on the keeper's table under the lamp with its own light turning inside it, slow and yellow, on its own count, salted on Tuesday out of a soot-cut jar that somebody had sold the Company in the winter as plain. Soot-cut plain was no use for a bank. It was good enough to teach with.

Beside it on the table there were two more cells. One was keeping a count Peverell had given it with her own hands that morning, three slow and a quick, like a woman beating a rug. The other was in step. Peverell had salted it from the pump house's own store, which was all the bank's, and it went four and four, fifty-seven to the minute, like everything else in this town.

Round the table, on stools, sat twelve fitters in their aprons, with their hands on their knees: the first watch of Peverell's thirty-six, who would come to the table in three twelves before Wednesday.

"Again," said Mara. "Hart's mate. You."

The man she pointed at was a heavy, careful fellow with a burn scar on his wrist. He got up and came to the table and laid his right hand flat on the first case, as she had shown him, with his fingers spread and his palm down on the glass.

"Whose is it?" said Mara.

He shut his eyes. That was allowed. Most of them did it the first week.

"Its own," he said, after a while. "Slow. It doesn't care about anything."

"And that one."

He moved his hand to the second. "Peverell's. The rug." He moved it again, without being told, to the third, and his face changed. "That's the bank," he said. "That's the town."

"How do you know?"

"It's in my teeth." He took his hand away and looked at it. "It's in everything. When I put my hand on the other two I could feel the bank under them, a bit, through the table. When I put it on that one there wasn't anything under it but more of itself."

"That's right," said Mara. "That's exactly right. Write that down, somebody. *There wasn't anything under it but more of itself.*" One of the fitters, a girl of nineteen with ink on her apron, wrote it on a slate. "On Wednesday, at the word, every one of you will have a hand on a cell like that. You'll not be asked to change it. You'll be asked to say whether it's still the town's, when the can goes in. *Town* or *mine.* That's all. Out loud, to the person next to you, and then to me." She took her own hand off the training cell. "Thirty-six people, thirty-six words. I'll not touch a case. I'll stand on the landing with a watch."

She said it, she noticed, as though it were nothing. It had taken her a fortnight.

"And if we're wrong?" said the girl with the slate.

"Then you'll have said *town* when it was *mine,* and the cell will go back in, and you'll have learned something that I couldn't teach you on a table." Mara folded her hands on her apron. "Mr. Hart will have you at the grating tomorrow night at the slack, all twelve. It's the neaps. The quiet walks with the table at the neaps, he tells me, and comes when the sea gate says it will and not a minute before. On Wednesday it won't. On Wednesday it'll come at nine minutes past six whatever the sea's doing, if we're right, and nobody in this room can tell you why." She looked round the twelve faces. "That's not a thing to be frightened of. It's a thing to write down."

Somebody knocked at the keeper's door.

Mara knew the knock before it came again. She had heard it on the shop door in Coper Street for twelve years, at every hour of the day and night, two knocks and a pause and one more. She did not get up. She found that she could not, for a moment, and that her reading hand had gone flat on her apron of its own accord.

"Come in," said Peverell, from the press.

Kiva came in. She had her right hand in fresh linen against her coat and her book under her left arm, and her face was the face she had worn at thirteen on the morning after the yard, when she had come to the kitchen table to say what she had seen. It was white and quite steady.

"I'm sorry," she said to Peverell. "I'd like a word with my aunt."

"Have the room," said Peverell. "Out, the twelve of you. Tomorrow at seven. Bring your slates." She took her spectacles off their cord and put them in her apron, and looked at Mara over the training cell, once, with her small eyes. Then she went out after her fitters and shut the door.

Mara stayed on her stool. She did not ask anything. She had promised herself on a kitchen floor four years ago that she would never ask the girl anything about that, only write it down when it was told, and she had kept the promise through a fever on a ferry that she had heard of by letter, and another on a quay that she had heard of from Aske. She folded her hands.

"I've told my four," said Kiva. "Tonight. At Quiller's. All of it that's on your page, and that it came on the ferry."

"I see."

"I'm going to ask the Board to open the page. Tomorrow night, at the Salt Hall, when they sit on the Chair's sheets." Kiva stood on the other side of the keeper's table with the three cells turning between them. "It comes out to your hand, the Reader said, or a magistrate's. Nobody else's. I'd like you to send for it."

There were a great many things Mara could have said. She knew all of them. She could have said *are you sure.* She could have said *you needn't.* She could have said that the Proprietor would take it to the Board as evidence for stepping the country, and that the Order would take it as evidence for shutting every door but one, and that the Mercury would take it as a headline, and that none of them would ask the girl what she meant by it. She could have said that she had sealed that page so that nobody could ever accuse the girl of borrowing, and not so that a hall full of strangers could read it aloud. She could have said that she was afraid.

Every one of those things would have been true. Every one of them would have been a hand on the choice, and the choice was not hers.

"Who first?" she said instead. "After me."

"My four. Aske in the morning. Orla. Kellan. Then the room." Kiva's voice did not shake. "I'll keep back one thing from the ferry. I don't know yet who it's for."

"That's yours as well." Mara stood up. Her knees had decided, and so she let them. "The wire office at the gate is open all night for the Board. The Reader's at the Library from seven. If I wire her now, she'll have it when she unlocks the door." She took a sheet of the pump house's fault paper from the press, because it was the nearest paper, and sat down again, and wrote.

*L. ARKWRIGHT, READER, COMPACT LIBRARY, MERIDIAN. THE PAGE IN YOUR PRESS UNDER YOUR WAX OF MONDAY 25TH SIXTH MONTH 2680 IS WANTED BEFORE THE CHARTER BOARD AT SALTMERE ON SUNDAY. TO MY HAND. IF YOU WILL BRING IT YOURSELF I WILL PAY THE TRANSIT. IF YOU WILL BRING ANNICK PERSONAL UNDER YOUR OWN CUSTODY THE BOARD WILL ASK FOR IT. M. ROWAN.*

She read it over once. Then she took a second sheet and wrote a second.

*LUMB, KEEPER, FERREN PUMP HOUSE. THE BOOK FOR 2604 IS WANTED BEFORE THE CHARTER BOARD AT SALTMERE ON SUNDAY. GIVE IT TO THE LIBRARY READER IF SHE COMES. IF SHE DOES NOT, BRING IT. M. ROWAN.*

"Sunday?" said Kiva.

"The transit's a day and a half. If she takes the morning one, she's here on Saturday night. The Board won't open a sealed page on a Saturday night in a dock with the Measure being read." Mara folded the two sheets. "You'll ask tomorrow, in the room, and the Board will say Sunday, and that's what it'll say because there's no other day it can say. I'm only writing what's so."

Kiva looked at the two folded sheets on the table.

"You're not going to ask me anything," she said.

"No."

"Not even whether I'm sure."

"You came to the door and knocked twice and once." Mara put the sheets in her apron pocket. "You've been sure since you knocked. I've heard you knock on the shop door like that since you were five. You only ever did it when you'd decided something." She stood up again. "I'll take these to the wire office myself. Now. Go to bed. You've Carrow at eleven."

Kiva did not go. She stood by the keeper's table looking at the three cells turning in their cases, the one on its own count, the rug, and the town.

"The man in Holloway," she said. "The lamp-man. He'd not have it written."

"I read the form. Noa brought it to the Works' lodging at six."

"He'd nobody with a pencil," said Kiva. "That's all. I had you."

Mara found that her reading hand had gone flat on her apron again, and that she could feel the weave of the cloth under it, and every thread. She left it there.

"Go to bed," she said. "I'll wire."

The girl went. Mara heard her boots go down the iron stair to the outfall walk, quick and uneven on the bound side, and then along the walk toward the low town. She stood by the keeper's table with the two folded sheets in her pocket and did not move for some time.

She had not owned it. She had not said one word that put a hand on it. She had written two wires because they were hers to write, and asked one question, and she would carry the wires to the office now, at a quarter to eleven at night, along the Long Quay under the white lamps, and pay for them out of her own purse.

Tomorrow night, at the Salt Hall, she would have to stand up in front of the Board and say what she meant to do on Wednesday, under her own name, with Halden's counsel watching and the Harbour Committee counting the minutes the low town would be wet. She had known that since the afternoon. She had thought it was the hardest thing she would do this week.

She took her hand out of her apron pocket and laid it flat on the training cell, the one on its own count, and held it there. It was slow and yellow and did not care about anything. Under it, through the table and the floor and the old stone of the pump house, faint, there was the town.

"Mine," said Mara Rowan, to nobody, and took her hand away, and went to send the wires.

Aske held the hand at seven on Friday morning and said, "Warm. Five mornings." He wrote it. Then he looked at her over his spectacles, because she had not let go of his sleeve.

"On the ferry," said Kiva, "something came. With the fever. On the 11th, at the Counting House, nothing came. That's what I said aloud, after. You wrote something on the card I didn't see."

Aske did not move.

"I'm telling you because I'm telling the Board tonight," said Kiva. "I wanted you to hear it from me at this window, not from the Salt Hall."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he took the card out again, and turned it over, and showed her the line on the back that she had not seen on the 11th. It was in pencil, in his small cramped hand.

*Expected something to come, and it didn't. Has had it come before. Will not say what. Do not ask.*

"I'd guessed the shape of it," he said. "Not what. I'll not ask what now. You'll say it where you've chosen to." He put the card away. "I'll be there. I'll sit at the back. If you go a colour, I'll come forward, Board or no Board." He shut his bag. "That's not a price. It's only what I'll do."

Orla Dane was on her crate at the gate at half past seven, with her stick across her knees and the Mercury's morning sheet folded on the boards beside her, unread.

Kiva sat down on the boards beside the crate. It was cold. The mast stood up black over them against a white sky, and Noa was not at his desk yet. There was nobody near enough to hear.

"You read Annick," said Kiva. "In 2664. Your name's in the Reader's book. *Read in the room, one afternoon.*"

Orla did not look round.

"I did," she said. "Seventeen years ago. I'd a candidate who'd gone off the Bench and taken his hand with him, and I'd stopped sleeping, and I went to the Library to read about wards, and the Reader put a red card in front of me." She was quiet. "I read it. I thought it was an old woman's fever. I've thought so for seventeen years, most days."

"It isn't only hers."

"I know it isn't. I read the Ferren keeper's leaf over your aunt's shoulder in the autumn and didn't say so. I read the Saltmere one in your book, on the left, in the third month." Orla's crooked face did not change. "I'd wondered why you'd gone the colour of paper reading a keeper's remarks. I didn't ask. I've a rule about asking."

"I dream it," said Kiva. "I've dreamed it since I was thirteen. It's on a page in the Library press, in my words, sealed before I ever read her." She kept her eyes on the mast. "I'm going to ask the Board to open it. Tonight. I'm telling you first, because you're my sponsor, and because you read her and never told me what she said, and I read her and never told you."

Orla was silent for so long that Kiva thought she was not going to answer.

"Well," said Orla at last. "That's a sentence I'd not have bet on hearing on a timber pond." She put her hand on her stick. "Is it a door?"

"No."

"Is it on your certificate?"

"No. It's not anything. It's only a thing I am."

"Then it's not mine to sponsor and it's not mine to stop." Orla took her hand off the stick again. "I'll say one thing, because I'm the one person on this coast who's read that notebook and isn't from the Order or the Library. She wrote that she told nobody in her house. She said they'd make it into doctrine by spring." She looked at Kiva for the first time. "Don't let anybody make you into doctrine. Not the Order. Not that man on the Long Quay. Not the Board. Not me. Say what you saw, and then go back to your hurdle."

"That's what I mean to do."

"I know it is. That's why I'm saying it before, and not after." Orla picked up the Mercury and unfolded it and looked at it without reading it. "Carrow at eleven."

"Carrow at eleven."

"On one door." Orla turned a page. "So are you. You'll find that's a different lane to the one you lost on her floor. Go and find out how."
