# Chapter 19 — Kiva: The Fitter with the Watch

Ruddock took Pike's dressings off at eight on Friday morning, in the Company's van yard behind the Long Quay, with the van's tailboard down for a table.

The Graywater van had come in on Thursday night by the coast road, three days from Meridian, with the rings whining and its slate-blue paint grey with dust. It stood now in a corner of the yard between two of the Saltmere Company's own vans, which were green, and longer, and had a kind of boat lashed upside down on the roof of each. Doran Mace's line had slept in the Company's barracks. Rhea Sorn had not slept anywhere that Kiva could discover. She was in the yard at eight, by the tailboard, with her hands in her pockets, watching.

Kiva had come because Pike had sent for her. He had sent a boy to Quiller's at seven with four words on a card. *Dressings off. Eight. P.*

"I wanted a second," he said. He was sitting on the tailboard with his arms out in front of him, palms up, on a folded towel. "There's nobody else in this yard has seen them go on."

Ruddock unwound the linen. He did it from the elbow down, slowly, a turn at a time, with his tongue between his teeth. The band at each wrist came last. Under it the skin was grey and new and shone, as skin shines on a burn that has healed too fast. The hands themselves were thin. They had been big hands once; Kiva had watched them tie three hundred bags. Now they lay on the towel as though they had been left there by somebody else.

"Close them," said Ruddock.

Pike closed them. The fingers came in, slowly, all ten. They came about halfway to the palm and stopped there, trembling a little, and stayed.

"Half," said Ruddock. "Last week it was a quarter."

"I can count," said Pike.

"Open."

The fingers went out again. They went out more easily than they had come in. Pike looked at them for some time.

"A cord's three turns," he said. "And a thumb to pin. And the little finger across." He did not say it to anybody in particular. "The first wants them all the way to the palm, and the second wants the thumb hard. I've neither."

"You'll have the first in a month," said Ruddock. "I'd not promise the thumb."

"I never asked you to promise it." Pike lifted his eyes to Kiva. "You've a hand up to here, I'm told." He touched his own forearm, just below the elbow, with one grey fingertip. "Your healer's been telling the whole quay."

"Mid-forearm, at seven this morning," said Kiva. "Aske says it'll be the wrist by four."

"And yours'll come back." Pike looked down at his own. "Well. We're a pair."

He nearly smiled. It did not come all the way, but it was there.

Rhea had come across the yard. She stood beside the tailboard and looked at Pike's hands, and then at Kiva.

"There's a Board warrant," she said. "We came south on it. The Company's stretched to the last man with the springs coming on, and four tears on the Longshore this week, and the Board's sent for vans from inland to stand on Company ground under Company eyes." She took a slate out of her pocket and did not look at it. "I've two nights left on your card. If the Company calls us on Monday, it'll be one of them. Pike rides as a voice. They'll lend us a catcher. You're Pike's second, as before."

"If T7 doesn't want me."

"If T7 doesn't want you." Rhea put the slate away. "That's the card. I wrote it."

---

Aske was right. At four on Friday afternoon the hand was cold to the wrist and no farther, and Kiva went out of the loft by herself.

She had decided at noon, sitting on the edge of her bed with her book on her knee. She had read Hart's second card again, which she kept between the last page and the cover. *What do the two fingers tell you through iron? Try it properly, and write down how it comes out, whichever way.* She had tried it properly. She had tried it on her aunt's surface plate and in her aunt's vice, on a free Saturday in the twelfth month, forty-nine times, and written down every one. Six in seven. She had never written back to him with the answer. She had meant to. It had seemed better said than written.

She did not tell Orla. Orla had a stick and a bad knee and a habit of coming. She did not tell Perrin, because Perrin would have wanted to know why, and she was not certain why. She took her coat and went down Quay Street and along the Long Quay toward the head of the basin.

The low town began where the quay ended.

She had seen it from the boat, a wide flat country of roofs below the hill. She had not understood what it meant until she walked into it. The street went down a ramp from the quay, and then it was level. The houses on either side were small and close and neat, with their doors painted, and their steps whitened, and boxes of something green in the windows. And behind the houses on the right, where the harbour was, there was a wall.

It was taller than the houses. It was grey stone, with a parapet along its top, and from down here she could not see over it. She could see the masts of ships going past above it, as though the ships were sailing along the roofs. At high water the sea on the other side of that wall would be above the chimneys.

There were drains in the street. They were iron gratings every twenty paces, and from every grating came the sound of water running away underground. And under the sound of the water there was the other sound, the town's count, much louder here. She could feel it in her boots without kneeling. Four and four. The gratings rattled very slightly on it.

A child was sitting on a doorstep with a slate, drawing soldiers. Kiva knew what they were before she was near enough to see.

---

The pump house stood where the low town came up against the head of the basin.

It was the long stone building with the organ-pipe chimneys. From here it was enormous. It stood on a bank of masonry with the brown water of the basin on its seaward side and the low town's roofs on the other, and it hummed. The door was open. There was a brass plate beside it, green with salt. *SALTMERE HARBOUR PUMP HOUSE. BUILT 2398. RE-CELLED 2611.*

Kiva went in.

It was a hall. It was taller than the Ferren pump house and three times as long, with windows high in its roof from which the light came down onto two rows of cells, eighteen on each side, that stood along a gallery at the height of a first floor. The cells were the old kind, iron drums the size of barrels, each with a hood and a gauge and a brass handle. Below the gallery, on the floor of the hall, were the pumps. There were six of them, great black iron things like the legs of a beetle, going up and down. They went up and down together. Every one of them, on the same stroke.

It was very beautiful. That was the thing that Kiva had not expected. Six great pumps and thirty-six cells, keeping time with each other so perfectly that it looked like one machine breathing.

There was a desk under the gallery stair, at the end of the hall, with a green-shaded lamp on it and a calf book open, and a small woman in spectacles writing in it. And at the desk, with the tide table spread across half of it and a pencil in her left hand, sat Mara.

Kiva stopped.

Her aunt looked up. She did not drop the pencil. She did not stand. She looked at Kiva across the length of the pump house, past six iron pumps going up and down, and her face did something that Kiva had seen it do perhaps twice in her life. It went still in a way that was not calm at all.

"Ah," said Mara.

---

They went out onto the outfall gallery, because there was nowhere else in the pump house that was not full of the noise of the pumps.

It was a stone walk along the seaward side of the building, with an iron rail, above the basin. Below it, the pumps' outfalls came out of the masonry in six great iron mouths, side by side, and poured the low town's water into the harbour. The water came out on the count. It came in four great gulps, and stopped, and came four more. The brown water of the basin went away from the outfalls in rings.

"I came on Wednesday," said Mara. "By the Works' transit. With Rusk. I wrote to the officer here in the second month, the night I wrote to the Order."

"The night of the twenty-third."

"Yes."

"You didn't say."

"No." Mara had her hands on the rail. The left one, the reading one, lay flat on the iron; the right one held it. "I meant to tell you on the cart. Then I meant to write to you at the house of the Even Hand. Then you had a fever on the ferry, and I heard of it at the kitchen table with my bag not yet packed, and I thought, I'll write it to Saltmere. And then I thought, she'll have had a week of standing in a strange town on one door, and here's a letter to say her aunt's coming to read the bank that everybody's proud of." She did not look at Kiva. "I decided it at the kitchen table in the middle of the night, and I didn't tell anybody until it was too late for them to argue. I've done that all winter. I don't like it in myself. I've not stopped."

"You don't have to read it," said Kiva.

"I said I wouldn't promise."

"I know what you said. You said it to me in the autumn, at Ferren, with your hand white to the wrist." Kiva found her voice was not quite level and made it so. "Thirty-six cells. You did four-and-twenty, and at the eighteenth you stopped feeling the iron. You've one hand."

"I've one hand," said Mara. "I know how many I've got."

Kiva stopped.

She had been about to say something more. She had been about to say *don't,* or *you mustn't,* or *if it goes as the right one went.* She had all of it ready, the way she had the parts of a crossing ready, in order.

She looked at her aunt's left hand, flat on the iron rail, reading the grain of it out of habit. She thought of a kitchen table, and a woman reading a card and laying that same hand flat on it. *You'll want my letter with it.*

It was not hers. It had never been hers. Her aunt had sat across a table all winter and watched her make a choice about her own hand, and had said what she thought, once, and then had not said it again. She had not owned it. She had let it be Kiva's.

"It's yours," said Kiva.

Mara turned her head.

"It's yours. Whether you read it or not. I'll not say anything else about it." Kiva put her own left hand into her coat. It was cold to the wrist. "I'd like you to know that I minded. That's all."

Her aunt looked at her for a long moment.

"That's the most grown-up thing anybody's said to me since the autumn," she said. "I find that I don't care for it at all." She took her hand off the rail. "Come in. There's somebody who'd like to meet you. He's been on the floor since one, and he's pretended not to know you were here for a quarter of an hour, which is the most tact I've seen in this town."

---

He was on the pump floor, between the second pump and the third, with a can of oil and a long-necked oiler in his left hand.

He was tall. That was the first thing. He was taller than Perrin and much thinner, and he stood with his shoulders a little forward, as though he had spent twenty years under the gallery and had got used to keeping his head down. He wore a fitter's blue jacket gone pale at the seams. There was a watch on a leather strap round his left wrist, on the inside, where he could see it without turning his arm. His hair was black and cut short, and it had gone grey over the ears.

His right hand was inside the front of his jacket, between the second button and the third.

Kiva saw that and felt something go through her. It was where she kept hers.

He put the oiler down on the pump's base and came across the floor to the stair. He did not hurry. He came to the foot of the stair and looked up at her, and she saw his face properly for the first time. It was long and brown and lined round the eyes, and the eyes were brown and set deep. It was a face that listened.

"You're the one who writes everything down," he said.

His voice was low and slow. The coast had got into it; it said its words in a long flat way, as Carrow's did. But under the coast there was something else, something she knew, and it took her a moment to find it. It was Meridian. It was the way the Old Yard said *floor,* with the *r* almost gone.

"Yes," said Kiva. "Rowan."

"Hart." He did not offer his hand. He did not have one to offer; the left was black with oil and the right was in his coat. "You came."

"You asked a question. On a card. I've the answer." She came down the last two steps. "I thought I'd say it, not write it."

"Then say it."

"Forty-nine times, on a surface plate and in a vice, on a Saturday in the twelfth month. Through iron, at a light touch, the two fingers say a thing keeps time. Six in seven. They never say what time. If I lean, they say my own pulse. If I'm frightened, the same." She heard herself saying it as she wrote it, in order, the way she had laid it out on the Works paper. "It's a reason to look. It isn't a reading."

He looked at his watch.

It was a small thing. He turned his left wrist an inch and glanced down at the inside of it, as a man glances at a watch before he answers a question. Then he looked back at her.

"Six in seven," he said. "That's a good instrument."

"It's two dead fingers."

"It's a barometer." He took the oiler up again. "A barometer doesn't know what weather is. It knows that the air's pushing, and how hard. That's all it knows. You could hold it in your hand for a year and it would never once tell you *rain.*" He went back to the second pump. She followed him, because he seemed to expect it. "But if you look at it at eight every morning, and write down where the needle is, and look out of the window, and write down whether it's raining, then after a year the book knows what weather is. The needle never does. The book does."

He bent and put the long neck of the oiler into a hole in the pump's casing, and squeezed, once, and drew it out. The pump went up and came down. Four, and four.

"You asked what they tell you," he said. "That's the wrong question. That's the one I asked for ten years." He straightened. "Ask them the same question at the same hour, every day. Don't ask what. Ask yes or no. Write it down. Never ask on purpose, when you want an answer. Ask when it's time to ask." He looked at her. "It'll never tell you what time a thing keeps. Your book will."

Kiva stood on the pump-house floor with her cold hand inside her coat.

She had spent a year trying to make the two fingers tell her something. She had tested them and measured them and written down six in seven and called it a reason to look. She had never once thought of them as a thing that could be read like a gauge. She had thought of them as a loss with a small amount left over.

"How long?" she said.

"For mine? Three years before the book knew anything. Eight before I trusted it." He took his right hand out of his jacket.

---

It was a whole hand. She had not known what she expected, and that was the first surprise. It had four fingers and a thumb, and the shape of it was a hand's shape. But the skin was wrong. It was grey-white, like the wax of a candle, and smooth in a way that skin is not, without lines at the knuckles. The nails were ridged and yellowish. And the fingers lay curled toward the palm, not tightly, as a sleeping child's fingers lie, and they did not straighten. He did not try to straighten them.

"The Bench," he said. "At the Regional, twenty-one years ago, in front of four thousand people. My doors fought on a live cell, and the cell ran away, and the backwash came up this arm before the quench took." He said it as a man reads out a fault. "Your healer will have a word for what it did."

"Turned out," said Kiva. "He says he can ease a hand that's starved. He can't ease one that's turned out."

"He's right. I had ash put on it for two years, by people who meant well. It did nothing." He looked at the hand without much interest. "It doesn't feel. Not hot, not cold, not a pin. It doesn't hold. I write with the other, and badly; you've seen. But it aches."

"When?"

"When something near it keeps time." He held it out a little way, toward the pump. "It aches now. It's ached for six months, every hour of every day, since twenty past two on the seventeenth of the ninth month." His voice did not change at all. "Before that it ached at the pumps and nowhere else. Now it aches everywhere in this town, because the whole harbour's keeping time."

Kiva's left hand had come out of her coat without her telling it to.

"Mine said *keeps time* on the quay," she said. "On the first night. Through a bollard."

"It would. It'd say it through anything here." He put the hand back inside his jacket. "But there's twenty minutes a tide when it stops."

She looked at him.

"Come," he said.

---

He took her out onto the outfall gallery, past Mara, who had gone back to the desk with the small woman in spectacles and did not look up.

On the gallery, at its north end, there was a little iron landing, and from it a ladder went down the masonry to a grating at the water's edge. It was a heavy iron grating, set in the stone, over a culvert that ran under the pump house. Beside it, bolted to the masonry, there was a tall white board painted with black lines and numbers. *0* was at the bottom, at the mud, and the numbers went up in feet to *24* at the top, above the gallery's rail.

"The tide staff," said Hart. "The basin's. Not the sea gate's. They're different." He took a book out of his jacket pocket with his left hand. It was a small thick book with a cloth cover, very worn. "The table they print in the town is for the sea gate. Slack at the sea gate comes three-and-twenty minutes before slack at this grating, because the water has to come in under the gate and fill the basin before it stops. Your aunt found that on Wednesday night. She'd been reading my six minutes against the wrong table all winter."

"She said she couldn't make them agree."

"Nobody could. I couldn't, for a year." He opened the book and held it so that she could see. "I gave up on the table. I used the hand."

The pages were ruled in four columns, in his round childish hand. *Date. Slack, by the watch. Hand. Staff.* There were two lines for every day, one for each high water and one for each low, going back and back. In the third column every entry was one of two words. *Ache. Quiet. Ache. Quiet.*

"Every slack, I come down that ladder and lay the bad hand on this grating," he said. "With the pumps running. Four and four. It aches. Then the tide stops, and for a little while it's quiet. Then the tide turns and it aches again. I write down the minute it goes quiet and the minute it comes back." He turned a page. "Twenty minutes, near enough, at the springs. Longer at the neaps. Never the same twice. Never what the table says."

"But the pumps are running."

"The pumps are always running. They're keeping the low town dry." He shut the book. "I don't know why it's quiet. I don't know what the hand's listening to that stops. I only know that it does, and that I can tell the Works the minute it stops and the minute it starts. Nobody else in this town can, to the minute. Not with a table. Not with a gauge." He put the book back in his pocket. "So your aunt came. She's the first who asked."

"The cells that held," said Kiva slowly. "On the twenty-first of the eleventh month. The forty-one minutes."

"They were filled at twenty to four. The basin's slack began at three thirty-eight." He did not look at his watch this time. He did not need to. "They held while it was quiet. The one that held longest was filled the closest to the start. They went in when the hand began to ache again." He was quiet for a moment. "I wrote that down for your aunt. I didn't write why. I don't know why."

Kiva looked at the grating, and at the brown water going away from the outfalls in rings.

She had a question. She had had it since the autumn, since the night at Ferren with a keeper's book under a desk lamp, and a line in brown ink that she had read twice. *Somebody at Saltmere had a temperature as well.* This man had known what the bank was before anybody told him. He had known it with the bad hand. He had sat in this pump house at twenty past two on the seventeenth of the ninth month, and she wanted to know, very badly, whether he had been hot that night, and whether he had slept, and what he had seen if he had.

It was there behind her teeth. It would have taken six words.

She did not ask it.

If she asked, and he said yes, then it was hers. It would have come home to her by boat, and it would be worth half. She had decided that in the autumn at a kitchen table, with her hand on a pencil. She had thought then that it was a great deal to give up. She found now, standing a yard from him on an iron landing, that it was a great deal more.

"Are you all right?" said Hart.

"Yes."

He looked at her as he had looked at her from the foot of the stair, with that long listening face.

"You've a shut door," he said. "You told me so on the card. One line left out on purpose." He turned his watch on his wrist, an inch, and back. "I've never asked what was behind it. I've got some of my own."

"I know you have."

"Then we'll do very well," said Hart. He turned to the ladder. "Come at the springs, if you want to see the hand go quiet. Tuesday and Wednesday, at about six in the morning. There's a thing at the outfall at the springs that you'll want to see as well." He set his foot on the first rung. "I'll not tell you what. I'd sooner you saw it first and wrote it down."

He went up the ladder one-handed, with his right hand in his jacket. At the top, on the gallery, he stopped and looked down at her.

"Your sponsor's in town?" he said.

"Yes."

"I thought she might be." He did not say anything else. He went in.

---

The second seeding lane went at two on Saturday afternoon, in the Ropewalk, against the Ropewalk School's second four.

They were younger than the first four, and they knew it, and they had watched Seven put Lyle on her knees on Wednesday. Kiva saw that in the first minute. Their catcher was a tall fair boy who called *run* and *lay* in a loud clear voice, as the book said, and their Hold and their blade were both laid. When Seven went wide along the north wall at the bell, the Hold did not run. She stood in the middle of the pen, laid, with her wall across, and let them go past.

"*Over,*" said Perrin, at the hurdle.

Their blade shut and ran for the hazel to stop it, and Jude was there first and put the flat of his edge on him before he could open again. Then they went over. Anwen took the poles from under, and Jude, and it was three to Seven and a down before the glass was half run.

"That's the wall that doesn't walk," said Perrin, coming back. "She saw what happened on Wednesday and she's not running. So she's no use to them at all where she stands."

The second four changed in the pause. Kiva watched their catcher talk to their captain, a stocky boy with a blade, very fast, with his hands. When the bell went for the second carry, their Hold did not lay. She came into the pen with her wall up and her stance shut, on one door, and walked it.

She walked it badly. She walked it from the near end, the way everybody did before somebody taught them otherwise, and the far end swung about like a gate in the wind. But she walked it, and it was in Seven's way, and Seven had to go round. Their blade came after Jude on one door too, unlaid, quick and loud. It touched Perrin on the poles.

"*Down.*"

Then their carry and their catcher took the Commodore and went for their hurdle, and Anwen's wall was a pace too slow, and they went over. Three. Three and a down. Four all.

"They've stopped laying," said Jude.

"They've stopped laying because we made it walk." Kiva was watching their Hold, who stood behind her hurdle with her forearms shaking, as Anwen's had shaken on Wednesday. "They're not as good on one door as we are. Nobody here is. They've never had to be."

"Then we stay on one," said Perrin. "We're better at it than they are. We've done nothing else since we were thirteen."

In the third carry, Seven was better at it. Anwen walked her wall from the far end, as Mace had taught her, and it swung round across the pen in a quarter circle and stopped where she wanted it, and the second four's Hold walked hers from the near end and it did not. Jude stood between their blade and the pass and lit, and touched him as he came. Perrin tapped once, and let his shoulders go, and Anwen took the poles. The Commodore did not go over; their catcher was at the hazel and would not be shifted. But he lay in Seven's half when the glass ran, and there had been a down.

"*Time,*" said the marshal. "A point to Seven, and a down. Six to Seven. Four to the Ropewalk."

There was a little sound from the rails. It was not the warm sound. It was quieter and more puzzled, like a crowd that has watched a familiar dog do a trick it did not know.

Perrin went past the hurdle without a word. He went across the pen to the second four's captain and shook his hand, and said something that Kiva could not hear. The stocky boy laughed, rather ruefully, and said something back.

"What did he say?" said Kiva, when Perrin came back.

"He said they'd never walked a wall in a lane in their lives." Perrin was rubbing the back of his neck. "He said, *We don't have to, here. Nobody makes us.* I told him I was sorry for it. He said not to be. He said he'd learned more in that glass than in a month."

---

There was a woman at the rail in Saltmere green with gold on her collar.

Kiva had never met her. She knew the gold on the collar, and she knew the name, because it had been on a letter under the brass weight on her aunt's dresser. The woman was tall and grey-haired and brisk, and she wrote in a notebook with a pencil while she watched, without looking down at it. She was not looking at Kiva. She was looking at Jude.

He had gone out of the pen by the side door, to the steps that went down from the Ropewalk to the lane. He was sitting on the top step with his left arm back in its linen and his face toward the harbour, which lay below the whole length of the ridge, brown and flat inside the Mole, with the grey sea beyond.

The woman went out after him. Kiva went to the door. She did not go out. She stood in it, where she could hear, and nobody seemed to mind.

"Talley," said the woman.

Jude looked up. He did not get up. "Ma'am."

"Yeo. Saltmere Company." She did not sit down. She stood on the step above him with her notebook shut. "You stood between their blade and the pass in the third carry. You did it before he moved. What did you see?"

"His feet," said Jude. "His weight came off the back one. I've been watching people's feet since the second month." He looked at the harbour again. "A friend of mine taught me. He's on another four now."

"Renn."

"Yes."

Yeo wrote something without looking down.

"You've been looking at the sea," she said.

"I've been looking at it since Sunday week. From the boat, mostly." Jude scratched his jaw with his good hand. "I'd wanted to see it since I was small. I'd want it said that it isn't what I thought. I thought it would be still, mostly, and then waves. It's never still."

"No," said Yeo. "It isn't."

"Ma'am. What's it for, here? The ash."

Yeo looked at him.

"I've asked that of everybody," said Jude. "Since the autumn. A Marshal, and a foreman, and my own four. At home it's a hand, if it's quiet, and a floor, and a cell, and the Works. What's it for on the coast?"

Yeo was quiet for a moment. Kiva saw her look down the hill, past the high town and the low town, to the long stone pump house at the head of the basin with its chimneys shivering.

"Keeping the sea out," she said. "That's what it's for. All of it, in the end. Every dram the Company tins goes to a cell somewhere that keeps a pump going that keeps somebody's kitchen dry." She put her pencil in her notebook. "You'll hear people say it's for the Company, or the Board, or the Measure. It's for that. A kitchen in the low town, at the bottom of a spring tide, with the sea on the other side of the wall above the chimney."

"Then it's always the same thing."

"It's always the same thing." Yeo turned to go back in. "That's why I've asked the Works twice for its reader. If the thing that keeps the kitchen dry is a hazard, I mean to know before the sea does." She paused on the step. "You stood well, Talley. On that leg."

She went in past Kiva, and nodded to her without stopping, and went back to the rail.

Jude sat on the step. After a while he said, without turning his head, "You were listening."

"Yes."

"I'd want you to have heard it." He looked at the harbour. "*It's always the same thing.* I've been asking since the autumn. Nobody's said that."

On Sunday at seven, in the loft, by the north window, Aske held the hand for two minutes by his watch and said, "Warm."

He wrote it standing, against the window frame. *Warm at seven on Sunday the 6th. A sulk of three nights. Five of six.*

He looked at the line he had written for a moment, and then he put the card in his bag and went down the stair for his breakfast. Kiva sat on the bed and opened and shut the hand, and all three fingers that reported came in to the palm when she told them, and the two that did not came in with them.

Three nights. She had known it would be.

She took her book out from under the pillow. It was the first Sunday, and it went to Orla at four.

---

Orla Dane read the book on Sunday afternoon on her fish crate at the foot of the gate mast, with her stick hooked on the crate's edge and her bad knee straight out in front of her.

The gate yard was half empty. The boards were wet. Noa's reading desk stood under the mast with a tarpaulin over it, and the queue was gone, and a Board clerk was sweeping. She read slowly, as she always read the book, with her lips shut.

It was a long week in it. She had not had it since the Sunday before the road, a fortnight ago in the Old Yard, and it was all here, in the square pencil hand: the house of the Even Hand, and a Master who stood at a window, and a tin with two seals. The comb, and the seventh reed silent. The ferry.

*Friday the 27th. The ferry below the house of the Even Hand. Fever, from the middle of the river. A hundred and twelve, by Finch. Aske counted it all night. Broke Saturday at four. I can sit up.*

*Cold, the disk. Before and after. I looked.*

And on the right, where the reasons went, one line, small.

*I kept my mouth shut. I'd learned how.*

Orla read that three times.

She did not know what it was about. She knew the shape of it, at least, as one knows the shape of a thing under a sheet. She had read a book in a room in the Compact Library in 2664, with a Reader beside her and a pencil she was not let use, and there had been a line in it about a fever, and she had never told the girl what it said. The girl had read the same book in the sixth month, and had never told her.

She had not been told that the girl dreamed. Nobody had told her. And here was a line on the right of a page that said *I kept my mouth shut,* about a fever on a ferry, a mile from a Glory master's door.

She could ask. She was the sponsor. The book came to her by a Chair's order, and a sponsor could ask what was behind a line.

She did not ask. She sat on the crate with the book open on her knee and thought about why not.

It was not kindness. She had learned to distrust whatever reason she would have given aloud, and she would have said *kindness* aloud. It was that the girl had written *I'd learned how,* and that was a thing Orla had tried to teach a boy twenty years ago and had never managed. She had taught him everything else. She had built him a step at a time and explained every step, and she had never once let him keep anything back from her. She had thought that was what a sponsor was for.

The girl had learned it from a van, and a hem, and a man with burned hands. And she had written it down for her sponsor to read, plainly, so that the sponsor would know there was a door, and would know it was shut.

Orla shut the book.

She turned forward to Thursday, and the fifth, and the notch. *A notch at seven seconds. Every pen. Mr. Polglase says it's the harbour.* And Wednesday. *The lay. It has a gap after all. It's in the walking. I don't know if it's a stack. I know it's not a miss.*

She would give it back on Thursday, after the sixth, if the healer said yes. She would sign at six and not before.

She sat on the crate for some time after that. Then she got up off it, and hooked the stick over her arm, and went down to the harbour.

---

The pump house was lit when she got there. It was a little after six, and the light was going, and the long hall glowed through its high windows like a chapel.

She did not go in. She had come to the door four times since Tuesday and not gone in. She went along the outside of it instead, by a stone walk with an iron rail, to the seaward side, where the pumps' outfalls poured into the basin on the count.

He was on the gallery. She had known he would be. He was at the north end of it, on an iron landing, with his back to her, looking down at a white board with black numbers on it. The tide was making. She could hear it coming in under the sea gate, a long way off, like breath.

She stopped ten paces from him.

She had thought about this for five months. She had thought about it on the transit, for a day and a half, with an officer of the Circuit snoring opposite. She had decided one thing in all that time, and she had told the Chair so. *I'll not use her to say it.* She had decided nothing else. She had found that she could not decide anything else until she saw his face.

He turned round.

He was forty. She had known that, and it was still a shock. She had last seen him at nineteen, in the infirmary behind the Measure Hall, with his right arm under a wire cage and a sheet, and his face turned to the wall. Now he was a tall stooped man with grey over his ears and a fitter's jacket, and his right hand was inside the jacket's front, and he was looking at her as one looks at weather coming.

"Dane," he said.

"Jessamy."

Nobody had called him that, she thought, in twenty years. She saw it land.

"You came by the transit," he said. "With the officers."

"My knee won't sit a cart."

"No. It never would." He did not move from the landing. "You've come to the door four times this week. Peverell told me. She thought you were from the Works, come to stop the pumps."

"I came to see you."

"I know what you came for."

She had planned what to say first. She had planned it on the transit. She was going to say that she had been wrong, plainly, without decoration. Then she was going to stop talking. She had decided that stopping was the hard part, and she had been right.

What she said instead was, "You should be at the gate."

He looked at her.

"They've a reader from the Chair's office at that mast," she said. "Bexley. He's reading every candidate with two doors, and he's reading a notch on every pen that nobody can explain. Their own man calls it the harbour. You know when the harbour's still, to the minute. You should take your book to that mast and sit with him and—"

She heard herself. She stopped.

It was the old thing. She had walked down the hill to tell him she had been wrong to do his thinking for him, and she had opened her mouth and told him what to do.

Hart said nothing at all for a moment. The tide came in under the sea gate, far off.

"I've been telling the Works for six months," he said. "Peverell's been telling them. Yeo's been telling them. The first who came was a woman with one hand, on Wednesday. Nobody's told me where I *should* be in twenty years." His voice did not rise. "You're the first. That's how I knew it was you, before you'd said my name."

"I didn't come to say that."

"No. You came to say you were wrong." He turned his watch on his wrist. "You wrote in the ninth month. Four lines. I read them on this landing more times than I'll tell you, and one of them was about her, and none of them said it." He looked at her. "You've come two hundred miles to say it. To my face. So that you'll have said it."

"Yes."

"Then say it."

She found that she could not. It was there, ready, in the words she had planned, and she opened her mouth and they were not the right ones any more. They were hers. They were for her. They would be said, and she would feel better, and he would stand on his landing with his hand in his coat exactly as he had stood before.

"She's—" said Orla.

She stopped. She stopped so hard that she felt it in her jaw.

He had heard it. She saw that he had. One word, and the beginning of the girl, and she had caught it and shut her teeth on it. But it had been said. It had been in the air between them for the length of a breath.

"There," said Hart, very quietly.

"I didn't—"

"I wondered," he said. "When her copy came, in the eleventh month, with three squares ruled off and one line left out on purpose. I wondered whether you'd come for me or for her. Whether you'd come to tell me about her. Whether you'd stand there and tell me what a good candidate she was, and how you'd done it right this time, and how she wrote everything down because you'd learned to let her." He was not angry. That was the worst of it. He said it as he would have read a gauge. "I wrote to her and not to you. You know why."

"Because I'd have answered."

"Because you'd have answered first." He turned back to the rail and the white board. "She came on Friday. She stood on this landing. She had a question behind her teeth that she wanted to ask me so badly that I could see it in her neck. She didn't ask it." He was quiet for a moment. "Did you teach her that?"

"No."

"No. I didn't think you had." He did not look round. "Good night, Dane."

Orla stood on the stone walk with her stick.

There was a thing she could have said then. It was short, and true, and it would have cost her something. She knew what it was. She had known for twenty years. She did not say it. She stood there until the light was gone off the water, and he did not turn round, and at last she went back along the walk, a step at a time, the way she had come.

At the corner of the pump house she stopped and looked back. He was a shape on the landing against the last of the sky, with one hand on the rail.

It had gone badly. She had known that it might. She had not known that the worst of it would be that he was right, about every word, and that she had said none of the right ones, and that she had come two hundred miles to say them.

She went up the hill to the officers' lodging in the dark, and did not light a lamp, and sat by the window with the book in her lap until it was time to sleep.
