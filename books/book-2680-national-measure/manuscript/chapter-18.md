# Chapter 18 — Kiva: Laid

The Ropewalk was a quarter of a mile long and twelve paces wide.

It ran along the spine of the ridge above the high town, a single tarred shed with a low roof and a window every ten paces down both sides. Kiva saw it from the bottom of Tar Lane at half past eight on Wednesday morning, and she thought at first that it was a wall. Then she saw the chimneys along its ridge and the smoke from the far end, where somebody was boiling tar, and understood that it was a building, and that it went on for as far as she could see.

"They made rope in it," said Jude. "For two hundred years. A man walked backward down the whole length of it, spinning, with the hemp round his waist. The bosun said."

"And now?"

"Now they make fours." He had his left arm out of the sling for the first time since the moor. Aske had taken the linen off at seven, and turned the arm over, and bent it, and said, *Use it in the lane. Don't lean on it. It goes back in the linen after, or I'll put it there myself.* Jude held it a little away from his side, as though it belonged to somebody else and he had been asked to carry it.

The near end of the shed was the School. The doors stood open. Inside, the floor was old boards, black with two hundred years of tar, and the light came in at the windows in long bars and lay across them. There were rails down both sides for the people who had come to watch, and the people who had come to watch were three deep, in green.

The pen was chalked on the boards at the shed's near end. It was forty paces long, which was the length of the pen at home. It was twelve paces wide, because that was the width of the shed, and there was nothing to be done about it. There was a hurdle at each end, of hazel, the height of a man's hip. There were posts down the middle of each side, between the pen and the rails. There were chalk squares on the boards, four on one side of the middle and three on the other.

In the middle of the pen lay the litter. It was a canvas dummy on two poles, the size of a man, stuffed with something heavy. It wore a tarred hat with a broad brim, and somebody had painted a moustache on its face.

"That's the Commodore," said a boy at the rail, to nobody in particular. "He's never been carried over a hurdle by a gapper in his life."

Perrin looked at the Commodore.

"Twelve paces," he said. He said it as a man says the price of something. "Pryce. Twelve."

"I know," said Anwen.

She was standing at Seven's hurdle with her hands in her sleeves, looking down the pen. Kiva knew what she was looking at, because she was looking at it too. At home the pen was twenty paces wide. A wall that stood across it from post to post was a long wall, and it took a good Hold to keep it up, and there was always room to go round one end of it. Here a wall ten paces long would stand from one side of the shed nearly to the other. There would be a pace at each end, and no more.

"That's a door," said Anwen. "Not a wall. A wall here is a door with somebody standing behind it."

---

The Ropewalk four came out of the crowd at the far end at five to nine.

Carrow walked first, with the rope on her sleeve. Lyle walked half a pace behind and to one side. Behind them came a long-armed boy with a carry's shoulders and a cheerful face, who was Tolver, and a small dark girl with a cord at her belt and her sleeves rolled up, who was Treen and was their catcher. They stood behind their hurdle and did not look at Seven. They looked at the Commodore, as people look at a thing that belongs to them.

The marshal was a Board man, grey-coated, with a glass. He stood on a box at the rail with his hand up.

"Board rules," he said, "which are the Circuits' rules, which the four of you know. Three for the litter over your own hurdle. One for a down. A still door doesn't plant in a square. A foot planted in a square is a down and the planted one kneels. A litter goes over a hurdle on a pass, two to two, top to top. If it touches the hazel, it's dropped and it's anybody's. If the glass runs, a point to the half it's lying in. Three carries." He looked at the two captains. "Declarations."

"Ropewalk," said Carrow. "Carrow, edge and stance, laid. Lyle, ward and stance, laid. Tolver, stance. Treen, ward."

"Team Seven," said Perrin. "Cade, stance. Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Rowan, stance, declared, by her captain's voice."

There was a murmur along the rail at *by her captain's voice.* Kiva heard it and let it go past her.

"Seven," said the marshal. "Your catcher's under a mast rule, I'm told."

"She is. She's been read. She'll be read again on Thursday."

"Then I'll not look at her any harder than I look at anybody." He turned the glass in his hand. "Ready."

Kiva went to her place behind the hurdle, on the north side, where she could see the whole pen. Perrin stood at the hurdle's middle with his hands on the hazel. Anwen was on the south side and Jude beside Perrin, with his left arm held out from his side and his right hand open.

The plan was a good one. They had made it on the boat, and again in the loft last night with a slate and a candle, and Kiva knew every word of it. Perrin to the poles, as fast as he could go. Jude to plant two paces north of the litter's head, where the boards were clean. Kiva's door under him, so that nobody could shift him. Anwen's wall up on the south side, to keep Tolver off the poles. Then the tap, and the shoulders, and over.

It was a plan for a pen that was twenty paces wide.

"Go," said the marshal, and turned the glass.

---

Lyle was in the pen before Perrin was over the hurdle.

She did not go to the litter. She ran straight down the middle of the pen, past the Commodore, past the chalk squares, to a place on the boards five paces short of Seven's hurdle. She stopped there, square, facing Seven, and opened.

Her wall came up at her hip. It was silver and low, and it went out on both sides of her across the pen, and it stopped a pace short of the shed's wall on the north and a pace short on the south. And her feet went into the boards under it in the same breath. Kiva felt them go. They went down through the black planks into whatever lay under the Ropewalk, which was the ridge, and they held.

Perrin was over the hurdle. He was two strides into the pen, going for the poles, and there was a wall in front of him.

He stopped. He had to.

Anwen's wall came up behind Lyle's, on Seven's side, as she had been taught to put a wall up against a wall. The two met in the middle with a noise like a shut drawer. At home, that was where the leaning started. Two walls leaned on each other, and whichever Hold was the stronger walked the other back, a pace at a time, until something gave.

Nothing leaned. Lyle's wall did not move. It did not move at all. It stood on the boards as though it had been built there, because Lyle's feet were in the floor under it, and Lyle's wall and Lyle's feet were one thing.

Anwen leaned. Kiva saw her heels go into the boards. Her wall bowed. Lyle's stood.

"*Talley,*" said Perrin. "North end."

Jude went for the gap at the north end of the walls, between Lyle's wall and the shed. It was a pace wide. He went through it sideways, on his bad leg, with his left arm held up out of the way.

Carrow was on the other side of it.

Kiva had not seen her come. She had been watching Lyle. Now Carrow stood two paces past the end of the wall, on clean boards, with her edge lit on her right fist and her feet in the floor. She did not move toward Jude. She did not have to. He came out of the gap into her reach, and she put the flat of her edge on his good shoulder, as lightly as a woman puts a hand on a child's head.

"*Down,*" said the marshal.

Jude went to his knee in the gap.

Behind the walls, Tolver and Treen had the Commodore. They had not hurried. They lifted him by the poles and walked him back down the pen to the Ropewalk's hurdle, quite gently, as two men carry a sleeping friend upstairs. At the hurdle, Treen put the head poles on the hazel and Tolver put the foot poles on. On the far side, nobody took them. Then Carrow was there.

She had shut. Kiva saw it. Both doors went together, edge and stance, in one breath; and then Carrow was running back up the pen with nothing open at all. She ran past her own litter. She vaulted her own hurdle. She turned on the far side and took the head poles from under, still with nothing open, and Treen came over after her and took the foot.

The Commodore went over the hazel as smoothly as a tray goes over a counter.

"*Three,*" said the marshal. "And a down. Four to the Ropewalk."

The rails made a sound. It was not a cheer. It was the low warm sound that a crowd makes when a thing has gone as it was expected to go.

---

Seven stood behind its hurdle and did not say anything for a moment.

Lyle had shut her wall. She walked back up the pen to her own end without hurry, and she did not look at Anwen as she went. Anwen was white. She had both hands in her sleeves again, and her forearms were shaking inside them.

"I couldn't move it," she said. "I couldn't move it an inch."

"Nobody could," said Perrin.

"I could have, if you'd planted at my back." Anwen did not look at him. "I'm not asking. I know what we said. I'm saying that's how they do it. She's got her own still door at her own back. She doesn't need anybody."

That was it. Kiva had seen it as soon as Lyle's feet went into the floor, and she had not had the words for it. Now she had them.

Lyle was a wall with somebody standing behind it. Carrow was a blade with somebody standing behind it. At home that took two people. You put a Hold on her feet and a still door at her back. Then there were two doors on one thing, agreeing, and a lodger turning its tip toward them, and Perrin shouting *nobody stands together* across a pit. The Ropewalk did it in one body. Two doors, one person, one line on the tape.

"There's no gap," Kiva said.

They looked at her.

"In them. When they're laid." She was looking down the pen, at Carrow and Lyle standing behind their hurdle. "Everything we do is gaps. A crossing's a gap. The handed crossing's two gaps and three people in between. The tap's a gap. We've spent the whole winter getting good at the space between one thing and the next." She heard her voice go flat, and let it. "They don't have one. Not while they're standing still."

"Then we're beaten," said Jude.

"No." Kiva did not know she was going to say it until she had. "Carrow had to run."

---

The second carry went worse.

They changed the plan at the hurdle, in a quarter of a minute, with the marshal waiting. Perrin would not go for the poles. He would go for Lyle's place, the place five paces short of their hurdle where she had planted, and plant there first. If he was standing on it, she could not. Jude would go to the litter with Anwen. Kiva would stay behind the hurdle with her door ready, to put it under whoever needed it.

It was a good change. Carrow read it before they had made it.

At the bell, Perrin ran for the place and planted on it. Lyle did not come anywhere near him. She ran down the south side of the pen, past the litter, to a place three paces short of the Ropewalk's own hurdle, and laid there, facing the Commodore, with her wall across the pen. And Tolver and Treen did not go for the litter at all.

They went for Jude and Anwen.

They did not touch them. They could not; neither of them was a blade. They stood in the way. Tolver was a big long-armed boy with a still door, and he planted on the north side of the litter's head, where Jude wanted to be. Treen went to the south side of the foot, where Anwen wanted to be, and her ward came up low and small, a tray at her hip, the width of a doormat. It was a catcher's ward. It would not have stopped a child. It was in the way.

Jude and Anwen had to lift the Commodore round them. They did it. They got him up, crooked, and turned, and began to carry him toward Seven's hurdle, toward Perrin planted in the middle of the pen like a post.

Carrow walked out from behind Lyle's wall.

Kiva saw her come. She came up the pen with nothing open, running easily, past the chalk squares. Then, a pace from the litter, she opened both doors together and was standing. Her feet were in the boards and her edge was on her fist. The litter was coming toward her because it had to; there was nowhere else for it to go in twelve paces.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. It was Anwen. Carrow had put the flat of the edge on her forearm, on the pole.

Anwen knelt. She had to let go to kneel. The foot of the litter went down on the boards with a thump.

Jude dragged it. He dragged the Commodore backward by the head poles, alone, with his bad arm and his bad leg. He got him two paces, three, toward Perrin and the hurdle. Carrow shut and ran and opened again in front of him. She did not touch him. She stood in his way, laid, planted, with her edge lit. He stopped, because the alternative was to walk into her edge, and the glass ran out.

"*Time,*" said the marshal. "The litter's in—" He looked. The Commodore lay a yard on the Ropewalk's side of the middle line, with Jude holding the head poles off the floor. "In the Ropewalk's half. A point. And a down. Six to the Ropewalk."

Perrin stood planted in the middle of the pen, on a piece of floor that nobody had wanted, with his still door open into the ridge and nothing to do with it.

He shut. He walked back to the hurdle. He was a deep dull red, all the way up into his hair.

"I planted on her place," he said. "And she went and stood on another one."

"She doesn't need a place," said Kiva. "She can make one anywhere there's floor."

"Then what was she running for?"

Kiva had been waiting for somebody to ask that.

"Watch Treen," she said. "Not Carrow. Watch the catcher."

---

They watched in the pause between carries, while the marshal turned his glass and the people at the rails talked.

Treen was standing behind the Ropewalk's hurdle with her cord at her belt and her ward shut. She was small and dark, with very quick eyes, and while her four rested she did not rest. She was watching Seven. She watched Perrin's hands and Anwen's feet and Jude's bad arm. Kiva knew how that felt from the other side. She had done it herself, on a hundred floors.

"In the second carry," said Kiva, "when Carrow ran up the pen to the litter, Treen said something."

"I didn't hear anything," said Jude.

"No. It was a word. She said it before Carrow shut. And when Carrow was a pace off the litter, she said another." Kiva had not been sure, at the time. She was sure now. "*Run.* And then *lay.* She calls it. She calls when Carrow can shut and go, and when she has to stand again."

"So?"

"So when Carrow's running, she's got nothing open. Not the edge, not the stance. Nothing." Kiva felt the thing come clear as she said it, the way a sum comes clear when you say the numbers aloud. "A laid door hasn't got a gap. But you can't walk laid. Your feet are in the floor. To go anywhere, you have to shut both and run and open both again. And while you're running, you've got the biggest gap there is. A whole count. Two."

Perrin was looking at her.

"That's what Treen's for," said Kiva. "She's watching for when it's safe to run. And they only run when nobody's near enough to do anything about it."

"So we get near," said Jude.

"No. We make them run when we *are* near." Kiva looked at the Commodore, lying in the middle of the pen with his tarred hat over one eye. "We don't push against a wall that's planted. We can't. We make it walk."

There was a little silence.

"*Make it walk,*" said Anwen. "That's Coombe's. That's for a sheet."

"It's for anything that's strong standing still," said Kiva.

Perrin put his big hand on the hazel of the hurdle and looked down the pen. He was not red any more. He had gone the colour that he went when he was thinking very hard and did not want anybody to see it, which was no colour at all.

"Wide," he said. "We don't go down the middle. We go along the wall. The north wall, the shed wall, all the way, with the litter. Lyle has to come off her place to get in front of us. She has to run to do it." He turned to Jude. "And when she runs, she's nothing. And you're there."

"She'll see it," said Jude.

"Then she'll not run, and we'll walk the litter down the north wall to our hurdle, and that's three." Perrin took his hand off the hazel. "Either she runs or we score. I'll take either."

---

Carrow saw it. Kiva had known she would.

At the bell, Seven did not run for the litter. Perrin and Jude went up the north side of the pen, close against the shed's wall, between the posts and the window light, and Anwen went with them on the inside. They took the Commodore by the head poles together, and lifted, and did not turn him toward the middle. They carried him straight along the north wall, at a walk, toward Seven's hurdle.

Lyle had laid in the middle of the pen at the bell, as before, with her wall across. Now there was a litter going past the north end of her wall. It was close against the shed, in the pace-wide gap between her wall's end and the boards. It would go past her, because she could not reach it from where she stood.

Treen said something. Kiva saw her mouth make the shape of it.

Lyle shut. Wall and stance went together. She ran north along the line of her own wall, to the gap, to get in front of the litter.

Jude was there.

He had let go of the head poles a stride before, on Perrin's shoulders, as Anwen had taught him. He was waiting at the gap with his edge lit on his good fist. Lyle came running to the gap with nothing open, because she could not open while she ran. And Jude put the flat of his edge on her shoulder as she came, quite lightly, the way Carrow had put hers on him.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "Ropewalk."

Lyle knelt in the gap. Her face did not change. Kiva saw her look up at Jude from her knees with the same square careful look that she had had at the mast. It was the look of somebody filing a thing away to be thought about later.

The rails had gone very quiet.

Perrin and Anwen had the Commodore moving. They walked him past Lyle, kneeling, along the north wall, five paces from Seven's hurdle, four. Kiva was at the hurdle with her door ready. Jude was coming back for the foot poles. It was going to go over.

Carrow did not run.

She stood where she was, at the Ropewalk's end of the pen, with nothing open, and watched them go. Then, quite deliberately, she walked down the south side of the pen. She walked, she did not run, past the middle line and past the chalk squares, to a place three paces short of Seven's hurdle on the south side. She stopped there. She opened both doors together.

She was standing between the litter and the hurdle. She was laid, planted, with her edge lit. The north wall was on one side of the litter's path and Carrow was on the other, and between them there was a gap of two paces. A litter was a pace and a half wide with a man on each pole.

She had not run. She had walked, in plain sight, while nobody was near her, because everybody from Seven was on the litter or at the hurdle. Then she had stood.

"*Through,*" said Perrin. "Go through."

They went through. It was the only way to go. Perrin on the head poles, Anwen on the foot, along the shed's wall, with Carrow's edge two paces off on their left. Anwen was on the left. Carrow leaned. She did not move her feet; her feet were in the floor. She leaned from the hips, as far as a planted body could lean, and her edge came out at the end of her arm and touched Anwen's elbow.

"*Down,*" said the marshal.

Anwen knelt. The foot of the litter went down. Perrin stood with the head poles in his hands, a pace and a half from his own hurdle, and the Commodore lying behind him on the boards like a felled tree.

Jude took the foot. He had come back for it, and he took it, and they lifted. Perrin backed to the hurdle and put the head poles on the hazel. There was nobody on the far side to take them from under, because Kiva was there, and Kiva had her still door declared and no hands for a litter. That was the rule, and Seven had chosen it.

"*Hold it,*" said Perrin. He was holding the head on the hazel's top, just clear, with his arms shaking. "Rowan. Don't. Talley—"

Jude could not get over the hurdle and take the head from under while he held the foot. Anwen was kneeling. And the glass ran out.

"*Time.* The litter's on the hurdle." The marshal leaned over to look. "Not over. Not touching. In Seven's half. A point to Seven. A down each way." He wrote. "Seven to two. The Ropewalk."

---

The rails made their warm sound again. It went on for some time. Then it changed, and became something else, and Kiva could not at first tell what. She looked along the rail and saw that the people in green were looking at Jude. He was standing at Seven's hurdle with his edge dark and his left arm held against his chest, very white about the mouth.

They had seen a gapper put a laid girl on her knees. They had not seen that before.

Carrow came down the pen.

She walked. She did not hurry. She stopped on the other side of Seven's hurdle and looked at Kiva over it, and then at Perrin, and then at Jude.

"That was good," she said. "The north wall."

"It got us one," said Perrin.

"It got you two. You'd have had three if your catcher could carry." Carrow tilted her head at Kiva. "You can't, can you? You've a door declared and that's all you're let open."

"That's all," said Kiva.

"In this town you'd have two, and you'd have had him over." Carrow did not say it unkindly. She said it as a fact about the weather. "Who saw Treen?"

"I did."

"I thought so. You were watching her and not me. Nobody watches Treen." Carrow glanced back up the pen, to where her catcher stood with her sleeves rolled up, coiling a cord. "She'll be pleased. She likes to be noticed. She'll not say so."

"You didn't run," said Kiva. "At the end."

"No." Carrow looked at her. "You made Maud run, and Maud got caught, and I'd seen it. So I walked. You can walk where nobody can reach you. You were all on the litter." She lifted one shoulder. "You'll do it better next time. So shall I."

She turned to go. Then she turned back, as she had at the mast.

"Your reader," she said. "The honest one. He'll be at that mast tomorrow at seven, reading you, I'm told."

"Yes."

"Polglase will be beside him. He's never read anybody from Meridian." She was quiet for a moment. "He'll be very fair. He'll write down exactly what he sees. I thought I'd tell you, since I've seen what your man wrote about mine."

She went away up the pen. Lyle had got up off her knee and was standing by the Ropewalk's hurdle, and Carrow put a hand on her shoulder as she passed. Lyle did not look at her. She was looking at Anwen.

---

They walked down Tar Lane in a row, with the Ropewalk's long roof behind them and the harbour below.

Nobody spoke until the bottom. The lane came out onto a little square with a pump in it and a view of the whole basin, brown and flat, with the pump house at its head. Its organ-pipe chimneys shivered in the air. Perrin stopped by the pump and put his hand on its iron handle and looked at the harbour.

"They beat us," he said.

"Seven to two," said Jude.

"I know the figure. I'm saying it so that it's said. They beat us, on their own floor, with their own doctrine, and they didn't cheat." He turned round. "I want that written in the book. Not *lost to the Ropewalk.* I want *beaten, fairly, by a better four, on a floor twelve paces wide that they've stood on since they were eleven.*"

"It's a long line," said Jude.

"Then write it small." Perrin looked at Anwen. "Pryce. You couldn't move her wall."

"No."

"Could anybody? At home? Brask's Hold, or Mace's line?"

Anwen thought about it honestly, as she did.

"Mace's line could. Six of them. Walking it from the far end." She took her hands out of her sleeves and looked at them. "No single wall could. Not against that. Not unless somebody was at my back, and we've said nobody will be."

"And we'll keep saying it," said Perrin. "There's a lodger in every bit of tidal mud on this coast, by what Coombe said. I'd sooner be beaten seven to two than have one of those come up a lane at you and me." He took his hand off the pump. "So we don't fight a laid wall. We make it walk. That's Rowan's. I want it in the book in her name."

"It's Coombe's," said Kiva.

"Coombe said it about a sheet. You said it about a person, in a lane, with the glass turning." Perrin started down the hill. "It's yours. I'd like it known."

Kiva walked after him.

She had been beaten before. She had been beaten by Halden six to two in her own yard, by a boy who had read every word she said. That had felt like being found out. This did not. This felt like being shown something. There was a way of doing what she did, the thing that had cost her two fingers and an arm for four days, and it was taught in a long tarred shed to children of eleven. It was cheap and quick and quiet. They were very good at it. It had a gap after all, and it was in a place that nobody in Meridian had ever thought to look, because nobody in Meridian had ever let two doors stand open long enough to need to walk.

She did not know whether it was a stack. She knew it was not a miss.

She would write that on the right-hand page tonight, where the reasons went. She would not know what to put on the left.

---

On Thursday at five to seven, the gate mast stood up black against a white sky, with the sea fog lying on the timber pond so thick that the far end of the boards was gone.

Aske had held her wrist at six, in the loft, by the light of a candle, with Pru asleep in the next bed and the stove cold. He had held it for two minutes by his watch, and then he had held it for another. Then he had written.

*Warm at six on Thursday. Five of six, if it goes.*

Now he stood at the foot of the mast with his bag between his feet, and Orla sat on her fish crate with her stick across her knees, and Noa stood at his reading desk with his tape threaded. Polglase stood at the other desk, a yard away, in Saltmere green, with his own tape. Under the mast, on the boards, someone had painted two white crosses six paces apart.

"I said I'd say it before," said Aske. "I'm saying it." He did not look at Orla. He looked at Kiva. "The fourth went to the middle of the forearm and sulked for two nights. I don't know what this one'll do. I think it'll go further. I think it'll sulk three nights, and I think that's not the end of it. I'd want you to know that I think so, and that I've written it on the card already, so that I can't say afterward that I didn't."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"That's all," said Aske. "That's the price, as near as I can say it."

Orla said nothing. She had not argued with him. Kiva saw that she had decided not to, and that it had cost her something to decide.

Perrin was standing behind the reading desks, with Anwen and Jude and Pru. He had not been asked to come. At Saltmere her declarations went by her captain's voice, and he had read that line in the Chair's conditions on the boat, three times, with his lips moving, and had said nothing about it until last night.

"*Hand,*" said Perrin now, loud and clear, to the two readers. "*To Ember. Rowan, changing on three.*"

Kiva went to the first cross.

She put up the ward at her hip. It came up as it always came, low and flat, a tray the width of a door, and Orla's stick rang on it once from the crate. The stick was too far away to reach, so Orla had leaned and struck the iron leg of the reading desk instead. It was enough. It was the sound, and not the stick, that her hands had learned.

*One.*

She folded. She folded from the far edge, as Pru had taught her and Pike had taught Pru, and brought the whole tray in on itself to a penny, warm in her palm.

*Two.*

*Three.*

She shut.

Nobody. Nobody in the hands, and the cold coming into them at once, as it came now, and a long way up. Then the Ember, glad, under her breastbone, coming up as it always came, as though it had been waiting by the door. And she went. Six paces, low, through the fog, onto the second white cross.

"One cliff," said Noa. "Twenty-six. Thirty-one in all."

"*Five,*" said Orla.

Kiva stood on the cross. The hand was white. She did not look at it. She knew how far the white went, because she could feel where the cold stopped; it stopped very nearly at the elbow. She put the hand inside her coat against her ribs, where the disk was. The disk was cold.

Noa had not looked up from his tape.

That was unusual. He always looked up when he had read a crossing. He looked at her, and then at Orla, and then he wrote. This time he stood bent over the desk with his pencil not moving. Beside him, Polglase had bent over his own.

"There's a notch," said Noa.

---

He said it very quietly, so that only the people at the desks could hear. Kiva came off the cross and went to the desk without being asked, and nobody stopped her.

The tape lay on the sloping top. There was her line, at the left: the ward, and then the cliff where the ward shut, which went down hard and flat, as cliffs did. Then the flat, the gap, the twenty-six that was nobody. Then the Ember's line, coming up out of nothing. Then it went on, level, as she stood on the cross.

And a little way along it, there was a mark. It was no taller than a hair is wide. It was not on her line. It was across it, and across the tape, top to bottom, and on every one of the eight pens.

Noa had his dividers out. He measured from the cliff to the notch. He measured it again.

"Seven seconds," he said. "Or eight. Seven." He measured it a third time and put the dividers down. "In Meridian it was eleven. Three times. Eleven, or twelve. And never after one cliff. I went back through every single crossing she's made on my mast, in the autumn and in the winter, and there was never anything after one cliff."

"There's a hair on this mast most mornings at the springs," said Polglase. He was looking at his own tape, which had the same mark at the same place. "We put it down to the harbour. Everything on this mast has a hair in it, near the spring tides. I've read forty years of tapes off it. I don't look at them any more."

"Do you write them down?"

"I write the time." Polglase looked at Noa over his steel spectacles, quite kindly. "I've a book of times, Mr. Bexley. You're welcome to it. It won't tell you anything. It's a coast thing. We've always had it."

Noa did not answer. He wrote. Kiva watched him write, in his narrow upright hand, in the ledger on the desk's ledge.

*Rowan, K. Hand to Ember. One cliff, twenty-six; thirty-one in all. A crossing; the fifth. Sponsor present. A notch on all pens at seven seconds after the cliff. First seen after a single cliff. In Meridian, eleven, after a second cliff, three times. Here, seven. N.B.*

He did not draw a line between the notch and anything. He did not write *because.* He left a space under it, and put his pencil behind his ear.

"I don't know what that is," he said to Kiva. "I'm not going to say I do."

"I know you're not."

"I'd like to know what it's a distance to." He looked at the tape again. "Eleven seconds there. Seven seconds here. That's a distance, and it's shorter here, and I don't know what's at the other end of it." He rolled the tape. "Mr. Polglase says it's the harbour."

"Mr. Polglase has been reading this mast for forty years," said Polglase. "And he's never yet seen a thing on it that wasn't the harbour. He'd like it noted that he's never looked."

Aske had the hand on his knee by a quarter past seven, sitting on the edge of the fish crate beside Orla, who had moved along to make room for him without being asked.

He held the wrist, and then the forearm, and then he laid two fingers in the crook of her elbow and held them there.

"Cold to a hand below the elbow," he said. "At a quarter past seven on Thursday." He wrote it. "I'll see it at noon, and at four, and tonight, and in the morning. It's further than the fourth. I said it would be."

"You did," said Orla.

He looked at her then, for the first time that morning.

"I'll want to see it warm before the sixth," he said. "That's the card. One in seven days, and warm before. If it's warm by next Thursday, I'll say yes. If it isn't, I'll say no, and you may sit on that crate till the gate shuts."

"I know the card," said Orla. "I've had it read to me twice a week since the first of last month." She put her hand on her stick. "She's at five. I'll not sign at five. I'll sign at six, on this mast, when you've said yes and not before." She looked at Kiva. "Is that understood?"

"Yes."

"Good." Orla got up, by the crate, in two stages, and stood leaning on the stick. "Go and eat something. Your captain's been standing behind that desk since before six with nothing in him but nerves."

Kiva went.

She did not go at once to eat. She went to the edge of the boards, where the fog lay on the black water of the timber pond, and she stood with her left hand inside her coat. The hand was cold. It was cold the way a stone is cold, from the inside out, and the cold went up her forearm nearly to the elbow and stopped there, in a line, like water in a glass.

She took out her book with her right hand, and her pencil on its string, and wrote in the fourth column. That was the column for the disk. She wrote in it within the week after any crossing, because a page in a canvas book at Ferren said she must.

*Thursday the 3rd of the third month. The fifth crossing, hand to Ember, at the Saltmere gate mast. Cold. As before.*

She looked at the line for a moment. Then she wrote one thing more, small, on the left, where the things went that she had seen.

*A notch at seven seconds. Every pen. Mr. Polglase says it's the harbour.*

She did not write anything on the right. She did not know what the reasons were. She was beginning to think that nobody did, and that the people who said *it's the harbour* and the people who said *I don't know* were saying the same thing in two different voices.

Anwen Pryce went back up Tar Lane alone at six on Thursday evening, with her forearms still aching from a wall that had not moved an inch.

She had not told anybody where she was going. Perrin would have come, and Perrin would have said something kind, and she did not want anything said. She wanted to look at a thing.

The Ropewalk was lit. Its windows went away along the ridge in a long line of yellow squares, and from the far end came the smell of hot tar. The doors at the near end were open. Inside, the pen had been rubbed out, and the boards were bare. Twenty or thirty children in green were standing in pairs down the length of the shed, as far as the light went.

They were practising. Anwen stood at the door and watched.

They were very young. Some of them could not have been older than eleven. They stood in pairs facing each other, and one of each pair opened two doors together, and the other one pushed. They pushed on a wall, or on a shoulder, or on a blade's flat. The one who had opened stood. Then they changed over. A woman in green walked down the line with her hands behind her back, saying something to each pair that Anwen could not hear.

Every one of them stood. Anwen counted. Nobody fell over. Nobody's wall bowed.

"You're the wall," said somebody beside her.

It was Lyle. She was standing just inside the door, in her shirtsleeves, with a towel round her neck. She had been sitting on a bench against the shed's wall, and Anwen had not seen her.

"I'm the Hold," said Anwen. "Pryce."

"Lyle. Maud." Lyle did not offer a hand. She looked at Anwen with the same square careful look that she had worn at the mast and on her knees. "You leaned well. This morning. You've a good wall."

"It didn't move yours."

"No. Nobody's does." Lyle did not say it proudly. She said it as a fact about a tool. "But you leaned well. Your heels were right. Most gappers' heels are wrong; they lean from the shoulders. You leaned from the floor."

Anwen did not know what to say to that, so she said nothing. They stood and watched the children.

"How long?" she said at last.

"To lay? Most of them get it in a year. It's easy." Lyle nodded at the nearest pair, a boy and a girl of about twelve. The girl had a wall up, small and wavering, and her feet in the boards, and the boy was pushing on the wall with both hands and his face scarlet. "It's easier than one door. That's the thing nobody believes. One door on its own is like holding a rope with one hand. Two laid is two hands on the rope, pulling the same way. It's quiet. You stop having to think about either."

"Quiet," said Anwen.

"The quietest thing you'll ever feel." Lyle glanced at her. "You don't believe me."

"I believe you. I've seen it." Anwen kept her eyes on the children. "I don't want to feel it. That's all."

"Why not?"

Anwen thought about telling her it was forbidden. That was true, and it was the easiest thing to say, and it was not what she meant.

"There was a thing in a pit at home," she said instead. "In the second month. A Marshal brought it in a cage, to show us. A Board Marshal, Coombe. She's run fours on this coast. She says the coast crews have a name for it. A lodger."

Lyle's face did not change. But she was very still.

"It's like a cord, with no string," said Anwen. "It lay there. It didn't care about a cell that was lit on its own. It didn't care about me with one door. Then two cells were laid together, lit and agreeing, and it went into them, and both went dark. Afterward it turned to two people with two doors on one sack. It lay down when one of them came off." She made herself go on. "Coombe said that a laid door is an open door. She said there was a girl from this school who went out on the flats in the eleventh month with two doors laid, and came back with one."

"Wenna," said Lyle.

She said it very flatly. Then she said nothing for a while. Down the shed, the woman in green had stopped beside a pair and was showing the boy where to put his hands.

"Wenna had a bad night," said Lyle. "On the Longshore. In the dark, on the flood, with a Company crew. Everybody has a bad night once. She's on one door now, and she's teaching the little ones, and she says she likes it better." Lyle's voice did not change. "That's what Meridian says about everything. That it's open. That it's soft. You say it about our wall and our blade and our whole school. You've said it for forty years."

"I'm not saying it about your school," said Anwen. "I'm saying a Marshal said it about a thing in a cage that I watched. And you're going out on the Longshore in a fortnight with a laid wall, at night, on the flood." She found that her voice had gone rather high, and brought it down. "I'd want somebody to tell me. If it were me. That's all. I want it said, and now I've said it."

Lyle looked at her for a long moment.

"You came up here to say that?"

"I came up here to see how you did it. So that I'd know." Anwen looked at the children again, at the twelve-year-old girl with her wavering wall and her feet in the floor. "And I've seen. You're better than me. In a lane, on twelve paces, your wall is better than mine, and it always will be. I wanted to say that too."

That surprised Lyle. Anwen saw it go across the square careful face and not stay.

"Carrow says I'm the best wall on the coast," said Lyle. "I'm not. I'm the best laid wall on the coast. Take my feet out of the floor and I'm a wall like anybody's. Like yours. Less, maybe." She took the towel off her neck and folded it. "I'd like to be as good as she says. I'm not yet."

"Nor am I," said Anwen. "As good as I'd like."

They stood a little longer. Then Lyle said, without looking at her, "How do you walk a wall? On your own? I saw you do it this morning, at the end. You walked it from the far end."

"A man called Mace taught me. On a van's line, at home." Anwen hesitated. "You don't push the near end. You walk the far end round, and the near end comes after it."

"Show me," said Lyle.

So Anwen showed her, at the near end of the Ropewalk, on the bare boards, with twenty children in green stopping to watch. She put up her wall, one door, unplanted, and walked it from the far end, slowly, round in a quarter circle. Lyle watched with her arms folded. Then she put up her own wall, and her feet went into the floor under it, and she tried to walk the far end round.

It did not walk. Her feet were in the floor.

She shut her stance. Only the stance, and kept the wall. Anwen saw her do it, and saw that it was hard for her, harder than anything she had done that morning in the lane. Her wall shook from end to end. Then she walked the far end round, a step, two steps, on one door, like anybody.

"It's loud," said Lyle, between her teeth. "One door. It's so loud."

"I know," said Anwen. "That's how you can hear it."

She went back down Tar Lane in the dark at half past seven. She had not told Lyle that a lodger could be killed, or how. She had not been asked. She thought about that all the way down to the harbour. She thought that she would tell Kiva what she had said, and that she would not tell Perrin, and she did not altogether know why.
