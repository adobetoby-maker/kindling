# Chapter 21 — Kiva: Read

The gate mast was lit at six on Tuesday morning by four lamps on poles, and there were more people round it than Kiva had seen at any reading in her life.

She had slept for three hours. She had come up Quay Street at two with mud to her knees and Jude's lantern going out. Aske had been awake in the bed by the door, and he had said nothing, and had held her wrist for a minute in the dark, and then turned over. Now she stood on the boards of the timber pond in a clean skirt with her hair wet from the pump. The sky was grey over the Graving's glass roof, and she could still feel the creek's cold in the bones of her knees.

The Ropewalk had come. All of it, it seemed: the first four in front, Carrow with her arms folded and Lyle half a pace behind, and behind them a crowd in green. They did not say anything. They had not come to say anything. They had come to watch Meridian be read in front of them, as their own people had been read in front of the town a week ago.

The Meridian fours stood on the other side. Halden License was there, all four, with Kellan in the black and gold, and Old Four, and Twenty-Eight with Dray on her stick at the end. Orla was on her crate. Mara was not there; Kiva had not expected her to be.

And there was a small woman in the Board's grey, with a gold chain round her neck and her hands folded in front of her, standing a little apart from everybody. Her face was the kind that has been shut in a great many rooms.

"The Clerk," said Perrin, under his breath. "Of the Measure. That's her."

"I know."

"I'd like it known that I've never seen her smile. Talley says she smiled on Saturday. I don't believe him."

Noa was at his desk with his tape threaded. Polglase was at his, a yard away. Neither of them had looked at the crowd.

"Rowan, K., Meridian," said a clerk with a list. "Under the rule."

Perrin stepped forward to the desks. That was his place now. He had read it in the Chair's conditions, and he had said it at the fifth crossing, and he said it now, loud and level, so that the green crowd could hear.

"*Rowan. Stance. One door, declared, by her captain's voice.*"

A murmur went through the green. Kiva understood it. They had heard of the girl with three doors. They had come to see three doors.

She went to the cross.

She did not do anything clever. She stood with her weight even, as Carrow had stood, and opened her still door, and it went down through the boards into the black water under them. It went down through the water into the mud, and through the mud into whatever the timber pond had been dug from, and it held. The pen dropped and drew one line.

"One door declared," said Noa. "One line."

"One line," said Polglase. He looked at his tape, and then at her over his steel spectacles, with a kind of mild curiosity. "That's all she has open, Mr. Bexley?"

"That's all she's let open. On any board. It's on her certificate."

"And the other two?"

"Shut," said Noa. "She has a crossing certified. Ember to still. She's read for that on this mast when she makes one, which is one in seven days and no faster. She made one on Thursday. You read it."

"I did." Polglase wrote. "A notch at seven seconds." He looked up again. "I've been thinking about your notch, Mr. Bexley. I've decided I don't like it."

"Nor do I," said Noa.

Kiva shut. She stepped off the cross.

The Clerk had not moved. She stood with her hands folded and looked at the two tapes, at the single line on each, and said nothing at all. Then she inclined her head an inch toward the clerk with the list, as a person does who has seen what she came to see.

"That's the candidate under the rule," said the clerk with the list. "The reading's done."

"No," said Perrin.

---

Everybody looked at him.

He had gone red. He had known that he would, and he did not seem to mind it. He stood by the reading desks with his big hands at his sides and the coil of Coombe's rope over one shoulder, where it had been since the moor.

"I'd like a thing read," he said. "Under my own name. It isn't her. It's me and Pryce."

"Mr. Cade," said Noa. His voice had gone careful.

"You know what it is. You've got it in your notebook with two questions after it." Perrin did not look away from him. "The Marshal's floor. The twelfth of last month, in the Pit at home. Pryce had a wall up with a string leaning on it, and she was walking it, and I planted at her back with my shoulder in her. My still door and her ward on one thing, agreeing. A lodger came up off the race at it, and Rowan said *nobody,* and I came off." He drew a breath as far as his ribs would let him. "There was a mast on that floor. It was the Board's. Nobody's ever said what it read. Nobody's ever said whether that was a stack, or whose, or whether it'd be an incident on Rowan's card because it was her ground I was supposed to be on and wasn't."

The crowd in green had gone very quiet.

"I've been thinking about it for three weeks," said Perrin. "I've said *nobody stands together* on every floor since. I'd like to know what I've been saying it about. I'd like it read on a sanctioned mast, with two readers, in front of the Clerk, and then I'll know." He turned his head. "Pryce?"

Anwen came out of the crowd. She did not hurry.

"I want it said I asked for it too," she said. "On the boat. He said wait till there was a mast with two readers on it. There's one."

The Clerk of the Measure looked at Perrin for a long moment. Her face did not change.

"It's two doors on one thing," she said. Her voice was dry and very precise, like a pencil on good paper. "From two candidates. Neither of whom is under the rule."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then it's not a reading under the rule. It's a reading at your request." She unfolded her hands. "Will you pay for the ash, Mr. Cade?"

"It's on our book. Talley's got it."

"Then read it," said the Clerk to the two desks. "And write down what it is."

---

They did it on the boards under the mast, between the two white crosses, with the whole of the Ropewalk watching.

Anwen put up her wall. It came up at her hip, low and silver, the width of a door, and stood. Perrin went behind her. He did not plant at once. He looked at Kiva first.

Kiva knew why. She had been watching the timber pond's black water since she came onto the boards, and the old wall of the Graving beside it, and the mud at the pond's edge where the boards ended. On the Longshore there had been a lodger in every bit of tidal mud, by what Coombe said. She did not see one here. She did not see anything lying along any edge that looked like a crack in the coping.

"Clean," she said. "As far as I can see."

"As far as you can see," said Perrin. "Right."

He planted. His still door went into the boards a pace behind Anwen, and he leaned his shoulder into her back, as he had in the Pit, and her wall stopped shaking. The two of them stood there. A ward and a stance on one thing, agreeing.

The pen dropped. It drew one line.

It was one firm single line, as Carrow's had been, as Lyle's had been.

"One line," said Noa.

"*Laid,*" said Polglase. Then he stopped, and looked at his tape again, and frowned. "Between two. Well."

"Shut," said Kiva.

Perrin shut and stepped back. Anwen let her wall down. The pen came up off the tape.

Noa stood at his desk with his pencil not moving. Everybody was looking at him, and he did not seem to know it. He was looking at the tape as he had looked at the notch on Thursday.

"Well?" said Perrin.

"It's one line," said Noa. "On a sanctioned tape, with two readers. That's what it reads." He put the pencil down. "It isn't Rowan's. She's not in it; she's on the boards with nothing open. So it's no incident of hers, and it never was, on the Marshal's floor or anywhere. That's the first thing. I'd like it said, because I've had it in my notebook for three weeks and I've not been sure."

"And the second?"

"It's not a stack," said Noa. "Not under any rule that I know. Every rule that I know, ours and the Board's, says a stack is a person's two doors. This is two people. It's not anybody's. There's no column for it." He looked at Polglase. "Is there one in yours?"

"No," said Polglase. "We lay in one body. We don't teach two. I've seen it done on boats, by fishermen, holding a net in a sea." He took off his spectacles and rubbed them. "I'd not have called it anything. I'd have called it two people holding a net."

"The tape can't tell the difference," said Noa. "It doesn't know whether it's one person with two doors or two people with one each. It's one line either way." He picked up his notebook. "I can't tell either. Not from the tape."

He wrote. Kiva could see it, because she had come to the desk and nobody had stopped her. It was under the line that had been there since the second month: *Two persons, unlike doors, one load, agreeing: one line on the tape. Q.: is it a stack? Q.: whose? I do not know. I am not going to say.*

He wrote under it, in the same narrow hand.

*Saltmere gate mast, the 8th of the third month, at the captain's request, two readers. A.: one line, sanctioned. Not hers. Not a stack under any rule; a stack is one person's. Whose: both. The tape does not know the difference. The lodger did.*

He looked at the last sentence for a while. Then he left it.

"That's what I'll say," he said to Perrin. "It's yours, if you want it. It's in my book."

Perrin let out a long breath.

"Then *nobody stands together* is right," he said. "It's not a rule about Rowan. It's not a rule about stacks. It's a rule about a thing in the mud that can't tell the difference either." He looked round at the green crowd, at Carrow and Lyle, and said it to them, quite plainly. "That's what I'll keep saying. On every floor. I'd like it known why."

Carrow had not moved. But Lyle had. She had come a half pace forward, out of the crowd, and she was looking at Anwen with that square careful face, and Anwen was looking back.

---

The Clerk's ruling went up on the tent pole at noon, under the Board's seal, in a clerk's hand.

*The readings made under the rule stand on the tape as readings. Whether a reading is an incident is for the candidate's own circuit and its rules. A lay is entered in the Saltmere circuit's column, as taught there, and is not an incident for any candidate of the host circuit. The conditions of the Meridian Placement Chair bind the candidates of Meridian. Every reading made under the rule, at the gate and on any board of the Measure, is to be posted where the draft boards and the Board may read it.*

*At the request of a captain, a reading was also made this morning of two candidates of Meridian, with two unlike doors on one thing. It read as one line. It is entered as a reading. It is in no circuit's column. By order.*

"*In no circuit's column,*" said Jude. He had come up from the loft at noon, still yawning. "That's a new one."

"It's a true one," said Anwen.

Carrow was at the tent pole when Kiva went to read it. She had read it already. She stood with her arms folded and her eyes on the line about the lay.

"Well," she said, without turning round. "We're in our own column."

"You always were."

"We were in it in our town. Now we're in it in the Board's ledger, in a Clerk's hand, under a Meridian rule." Carrow turned round. Her wind-brown face was quite serious. "Your captain stood behind his Hold this morning to be read. In front of us. In front of the whole school, when he'd no need to, so as to know what he'd been shouting about." She was quiet for a moment. "We'd not have done that. We'd not have thought there was anything to know."

"There's a thing in the mud on your Longshore," said Kiva. "Coombe calls it a lodger. It goes to two doors agreeing."

"I know. Maud told me last night. Your Hold told her." Carrow looked at her steadily. "Wenna had a bad night. That's what we say. I've laid on the flats every spring since I was fourteen, and so has Maud, and we've both still got two." She unfolded her arms. "I'll think about it. I'll not stop. I'll think about it."

"That's all anybody asked."

"No," said Carrow. "Your captain asked to be read. That's a good deal more." She went away across the boards.

---

Mara Rowan stood on the outfall gallery at ten to six on Tuesday morning, with her reading hand in her coat pocket.

She had promised herself she would not lay that hand on anything in this town until she had decided what she was going to do with it. She had been in Saltmere since Wednesday night, and she had not decided. She had read Hart's book of slacks and Peverell's fault columns and the sea gate's table, and she had found the three-and-twenty minutes. She had sat at the keeper's desk every night until midnight with her pencil. She had not touched a cell.

"It's quiet," said Hart.

He was on the iron landing below the gallery, at the grating, with his right hand out of his jacket and laid flat on the iron bars. His left hand held his watch up where he could see it. The pumps were running. Behind the wall at Mara's back, six iron beetles went up and down on the count, and the outfalls poured into the basin below her in four great gulps and four. She could feel it in the rail through her coat sleeve.

"It's quiet," said Hart again. "Twelve past six."

"The table says the slack at the sea gate's at ten to," said Yeo, beside Mara. "And three-and-twenty on top—"

"Thirteen past," said Mara. "He's a minute early."

"He's never early," said Peverell, from the end of the gallery. "The table's late."

There were five of them on the gallery. Peverell, small and upright, in her keeper's apron, with her spectacles on a cord. Yeo, in green with gold on her collar. Rusk, at the far end, with his thumbs in his waistcoat and his eyes half shut, as though he were asleep, which he never was. Hart on the landing below. And Mara.

The tide was out. It was the bottom of a spring ebb, and the basin had drained away from the pump house's foot until there was nothing below the outfalls but a hand of brown water lying on grey mud. The tide staff stood beside the grating with its black numbers going up from *0* at the mud. Ten paces out from the outfalls, in the middle of the mud, there was a place where the water lay a little darker.

It was rising.

Mara saw it come up. She had been told what she would see, by Hart and by Peverell and by Yeo, all three, in plain words. It made no difference. She saw it come up out of the mud as a thing comes up out of a dream, slowly, with the mud sliding off it in sheets.

It was a ring. It was grey, the grey of ash, with a rim of white frost along its lip. It was as wide as a cart is long, and it rose out of the mud straight up, all round at once, like the rim of a great barrel being pushed up from below. It rose a foot, two feet, three. The hand of brown water round it went white where it touched the ring's outside and stiffened into ice. It rose four feet.

It stopped.

"Four foot six," said Hart, from the landing. He had a fitter's level in his left hand, a brass tube with a bubble in it, and he was sighting across it at the staff. "And a half inch. At the springs a fortnight back, four foot one."

Inside the ring there was water.

It was brown harbour water, level with the ring's lip, four and a half feet above the mud. It should have run out. There was nothing to hold it but a ring of grey that stood on the mud like a wall round a pond. But it was held. It stood up inside the ring, flat and brown and full, four and a half feet higher than the water outside, as though the ring were a cup and somebody had filled it.

And its surface moved.

It trembled. It trembled in rings, very small, from the middle outward to the lip, and it did it four times, and stopped. Four times, and stopped. Mara watched it, and then she took out her watch and watched it with that, and she did not need to. She knew the count. She had been listening to it in the rail through her sleeve for an hour, and in the pump house floor for a week, and in the stones of the low town since Wednesday.

"It's the bank's," she said.

"It's the bank's," said Hart. "To the stroke. I've timed it at every spring since the tenth month."

The outfalls below her went on pouring into the basin, four and four, in step with the ring. The pumps behind her went up and down. And Hart's right hand lay flat on the grating, and he said it was quiet.

"How," said Mara.

"I don't know." He did not look up. "That's the third column of my book. *Quiet.* Every slack. The pumps going, the outfalls going, the hand quiet. And at the springs, when the water's low enough to see, that." He lifted the level an inch toward the ring. "It's as if the count goes out of the harbour at the slack and into that, and nowhere else. I don't know if that's what it is. It's what it looks like."

"The children call it the Hoop," said Yeo. Her voice was flat. "In the low town. They come down to the wall at the springs to see it. They think it's the Regiment's. A sort of drum."

"It's not in my fault column," said Peverell. "Nothing's at fault. The pumps are sound. The cells are sound. It's not a fault for a ring to come up out of the mud at the bottom of a spring tide and stand there." She was holding the rail very hard. "It's in my remarks. Every one. Since the twenty-sixth of the ninth month, a hand high. My first keeper taught me what goes in remarks."

Rusk had opened his eyes. He was looking at the ring with an expression that Mara had never seen on him, and she had seen him look at a great many things. He did not say anything. He took a pencil out of his waistcoat and wrote in a small book with a black cover.

The ring stood for twenty-one minutes.

At six thirty-five Hart said, "Ache," and the ring went down.

It went down as it had come up, all round at once, into the mud. The water inside it came over its lip as it sank. It came out in a sheet on all sides, brown and then white, frosting as it went, and spread across the mud in a long flat wave and was gone. The ring went down into the mud and the mud closed over it. There was a place that lay a little darker, and then there was nothing.

The flood was coming in under the sea gate. Mara could hear it, a long way off, like breath.

---

Peverell took them into the keeper's room behind the desk at seven, and shut the door, and took a book down from the press.

It was the press that every pump house had, Mara thought. There had been one like it at Ferren, behind the three pumps, with three centuries of books in calf gone black. This one was bigger. It went from floor to ceiling, and the books in it went back to 2398, when the pump house was built.

"You asked me on Thursday," said Peverell, "whether there was a night. Before the ninth month. A night when everything in this house did something together that was in no column." She laid the book on the table and did not open it. "You said Ferren had one. In 2604. You said a keeper there had a fever and wrote it in remarks because the needles did it too."

"Yes."

"I said I'd look. I looked." Peverell put her hand flat on the calf. "I didn't look for 2604. I looked for every remark in every book that said the whole house did one thing. There's not many. Three hundred years, and four." She opened the book at a straw. "This is one. I didn't know the date till I'd found it. Then I saw that it was yours."

Mara looked at the spine. *2604.*

She looked at the page.

It was a keeper's hand, as the Ferren one had been: round and careful, in ink gone brown. It was not the same hand. It leaned the other way, and it made its *s* long, in the old fashion.

*The 11th of the third month. Spring tide. At the low water in the evening a ring stood up in the basin at the outfall, a hand high, and the water in it would not go down with the rest. Lay down at the flood. Not in the fault column; nothing was at fault.*

*Took a fever after, the keeper. Two nights.*

*Dreamed there was no roof on the house nor on anything, and nothing over. The nothing was not empty and it was not dark. Something was attending. I write it here because my father told me to write what the needles did, and they did it too.*

*The refinery's lamps on the Long Quay went white about three in the morning and stayed lit. The low town's went out. They came on again of themselves at the half hour. Not grid.*

Mara read it twice.

She was aware of Hart in the doorway behind her, and of Yeo and Rusk by the window. She kept her face as she kept it over a fault report. It was harder than it had ever been.

*No roof. Nothing over. Not empty, not dark.*

She had sat at her own kitchen table in the fifth month of last year with her niece across the oilcloth, white and sweating, telling her what she had seen when she was thirteen and again on Classification Day. Mara had written it down in the girl's own words and sealed it and had the hour put on it, so that nobody could ever say that she had borrowed it. Then she had read a Ferren keeper's line in brown ink by a desk lamp, and had felt the floor go out from under her, and had said aloud that it was a comfort. *It isn't you. It's older than you. It's wider.*

And here it was again, two hundred miles from Ferren, in another keeper's hand, on the same night. *Something was attending.*

And a ring in the basin, a hand high, seventy-seven years before a ring four and a half feet high.

And the lamps.

She read the last line a third time. *The refinery's lamps on the Long Quay went white and stayed lit. The low town's went out.* The refinery on the Long Quay was Halden's. It had been Halden's since before the pump house was built. Every bank in it had been stepped since the patent, because that was what Halden's yards were. And on the night of the 11th of the third month in 2604, its lamps had gone white and stayed lit, and the low town's lamps had gone out.

A man could build a creed on that line, Mara thought. A sensible man. A man who had been alive that night and seen it with his own eyes could build his whole life on it, and be no fool.

"Mrs. Rowan," said Peverell.

"Yes."

"You've gone a colour."

"I've been up since four." Mara shut the book gently on its straw. "Keep that page, Mrs. Peverell. Under lock, if you've a lock. Don't let anybody copy it till I've written to a man at Ferren whose book says the same night."

"I've a lock." Peverell took the book back. She held it against her apron. "I'll say one thing, Mrs. Rowan, since you've gone a colour, and then I'll not say it again. I've kept this house nine years. My cells have not dropped a stroke in six months. Everybody in this town is proud of them, and so am I." She looked at Mara with her small eyes behind the spectacles. "I'll not be the keeper who stopped the pumps. If they're stopped, I'll be the keeper who wrote down that somebody else did, and why, in seven columns. I'd want that understood before you go any further."

"I understand it."

"I don't think you do yet," said Peverell. "But you will."

---

Mara went out onto the gallery alone, and stood at the rail, and took her left hand out of her pocket at last.

She did not lay it on anything. She held it in front of her and looked at it. It was a plain hand, with ink on the second finger and a scar on the thumb from a soldering iron. It had read the Ferren bank and then gone white to the wrist, and a healer had brought it back with a dram of somebody else's plain ash.

She would show the girl the page. That was the first thing, and it was not a choice; it was the girl's as much as hers, and more. She would show it to nobody else. Not Yeo, who was looking at her from the window, nor Rusk, who had written something in his black book that he had not shown anybody. Not the Board, and not the Order, and not the man at the Library press. Not yet.

And the hand. She looked at it and thought of a ring of grey with the bank's count shaking in the water inside it, and of thirty-six cells on two galleries, and of eleven minutes that the low town would bear on a making tide. She thought of twenty-one minutes of slack, which was all there was. She thought of what Hart had said: *as if the count goes out of the harbour and into that, and nowhere else.*

If that was so, then the harbour was still at the slack. All of it, except one thing.

And one thing was standing in it, keeping time.

She put the hand back in her pocket. She had not decided anything. But she had found the next question, and she knew what it was. That was more than she had known at ten to six.

---

On Wednesday at a quarter to six, Kiva went down the ladder to the grating with Hart.

Jude came too. He had asked at supper, very formally, whether he might, and Hart had looked at him over the table at the Works' lodging and said, *If you'll stand where I put you and not fall in.* Mara was on the gallery above with Peverell. She had shown Kiva nothing yet and said nothing, and she had a look on her face that Kiva knew from the year of the Ferren bank. It meant that she had something under the brass weight.

"Lay them on the bar," said Hart. "Light. The way you would."

Kiva knelt on the iron landing and laid the first two fingers of her left hand on the grating. It was cold and wet and furred with weed.

*It keeps time.* At once. Loud, louder than the bollard, louder than the pole in the creek. Four and four, the outfalls' gulps, the pumps behind the wall, the whole basin carrying it. She could feel it in her wrist.

"Seventy-four," said Jude, who had her other wrist. Pru had taught him that on the boat. "You're calm."

"Wait," said Hart.

He knelt beside her, with his right hand on the bars a foot from hers. His left held the watch. They waited. The tide was out. The mud lay grey, with a hand of water on it, and the dark place ten paces off.

At thirteen minutes past six, the grating went quiet.

It went quiet as the pole had gone quiet in Gannet Creek. Not *pushing.* Not *keeps time.* Nothing. She had her fingers on a grating a yard from six pump outfalls that were pouring water into the basin four and four, and the fingers said that nothing at all was keeping time.

"Quiet," said Hart beside her, very low.

And ten paces out, the ring came up out of the mud.

Kiva watched it rise. She did not breathe. She watched the grey rim come up, and the frost along its lip, and the mud sliding off. She watched the brown water stand up inside it, held, flat, four and a half feet above the mud. And she watched the water's surface tremble in rings, from the middle to the lip. Four times, and stop. Four times, and stop.

The outfalls gulped beside her. Four and four, in step.

And under her fingers there was nothing.

"It's not in the water," she said. She had not meant to speak. "It's not in the harbour. The count. It's gone out of the harbour, into that."

"That's what it looks like," said Hart. "I've never said it was so."

"It's still. The whole basin, at the slack. Everything's still." Kiva could not take her eyes off the ring. "Except that. All the count there is, it's in that. It's—" She stopped. She did not have a word for it. "It's taking it in. Holding it. The whole slack."

Hart said nothing for a long moment.

"Your aunt said the same thing yesterday," he said. "In nearly those words. On the gallery." He turned his watch an inch on his wrist. "I've been looking at it for six months and I never said it to anybody, because I couldn't prove it. You two have said it in two days." He did not sound pleased or displeased. He sounded like a man who had set down something heavy and was not sure yet whether he would be let pick it up again. "I'd want it in my book that somebody else said it first."

Jude was standing where he had been put, at the back of the landing, against the masonry. He was not looking at the ring. He was looking past it, at the sea gate, at the harbour, at the masts.

"Is that what it's for?" he said.

"What?"

"The slack. Twenty minutes, when it's all still, except that." Jude scratched his jaw. "I've been asking what ash is for since the autumn. Yeo says keeping the sea out. Maybe that's what the slack's for. So there's twenty minutes when you can see what's keeping time." He looked embarrassed. "I'd want it said I don't know anything about it."

"Nobody does," said Hart. "That's the first useful thing anybody's said about the slack in six months." He got up off his knees. "Write it down, Talley. In whatever you keep."

The ring stood for twenty minutes. At thirty-three past six the grating ached under Kiva's fingers, and the ring went down into the mud, and the held water came out over its lip in a white sheet and was gone.

Hart sighted at the staff with his level while it was still going.

"Four foot six," he said. "And a half. As yesterday."

---

Mara showed her the page at seven, in the keeper's room, with the door shut and Peverell gone out to her pumps.

Kiva read it standing, with her right hand on the table and her left in her coat.

She read *a ring stood up in the basin at the outfall, a hand high.* She read *took a fever after.* She read *no roof on the house nor on anything, and nothing over. The nothing was not empty and it was not dark. Something was attending.* She read it slowly, the way she had read a page in a reading room in the Compact Library with a sister of the Order at the end of the table.

She knew her lips were moving. She could feel them. She was counting, and she did not know what she was counting. She could not stop.

*The refinery's lamps on the Long Quay went white about three in the morning and stayed lit. The low town's went out.*

She stopped reading.

She was on a ferry in the middle of a brown river, with a hand on her wrist and a voice counting. There was no roof. There were roads, and lights on them, a great many, coming from every side, and some of them were bright and some of them were dull. Some of them were going out. Some of them had gone white. She had been lower than she had ever been, near enough to see that some had gone white. She had not told anybody. She had not told Mara.

She did not tell Mara now.

She stood at the table with her right hand flat on it and her lips shut, and she made them stay shut. It was harder than it had been on the ferry. On the ferry there had been nobody whose word it was. Here there was her aunt, a yard away, who had sealed a page so that nobody could say Kiva had borrowed, and who had shown her this one because it was hers.

It was not that she did not trust Mara. It was that she did not know what she had seen. She did not know whether *white* on a ferry and *white* in a keeper's book seventy-seven years ago were the same word. If she said it aloud, even here, even to Mara, then it would be a thing that had been said. And she had not decided anything.

"There's a line about the lamps," said Mara.

"I saw it."

Her aunt looked at her. Kiva looked back. Neither of them said anything for some time.

"You count when you read," said somebody in the doorway.

It was Hart. He had come up from the floor and was standing in the open door, with his right hand in his jacket. He was not looking at the book. He was looking at Kiva's mouth.

"I don't know that I do," said Kiva.

"I do it with a hand," said Hart. He did not come in. "I'll not ask what you were counting. I'll say that I saw it." He turned to go. "Peverell wants the room. There's a man from the Halden technical office at the door asking for you, Miss Rowan. He says he's been to Quiller's and the loft and the gate and the Ropewalk, and he'd like to sit down."

---

Mr. Adlam was sitting on a bollard on the outfall walk with his hat on his knee.

He got up when he saw her. He was as she remembered him, pleasant and fortyish, with a narrow gold cord at his cuff, but he looked tired, and his boots were grey with the dust of four streets.

"Miss Rowan. Forgive me. I've been chasing you round the town since breakfast like a bailiff." He put his hat on. "I've two things to say, and one of them isn't mine. I'll say that one first, because I was asked to."

"Yes."

"The Proprietor is in Saltmere," said Mr. Adlam. "He came down for the Measure, as he does. He's at our house on the Long Quay." He looked at her steadily, with real kindness. "He has asked to see the plate."

Kiva did not move.

"I told him it's held of the Line," said Mr. Adlam. "I told him what I know, which is that no hand but the bearer's is laid on it without a vote of the seven. He knew that already. He knows most things." He turned his hat brim in his fingers. "So we wired the Line's watch room on Saturday, under his name, asking the seven to vote. I'd like you to know that it was done properly and that I'd not have done it otherwise. The answer's expected by tomorrow's wire."

"And if they say yes?"

"Then it's yours to choose, I'm told. The Line's watch room was very firm about that when we asked how one asks. It said the watch witnesses and does not choose." Mr. Adlam smiled a little. "I'd have guessed it in any case. I'm afraid I read everything."

"What's the other thing?"

"Ah." He looked, for a moment, almost shy. "The other thing's mine. I've some old bench books. Our own. From the refinery here, and from the foundation's yard. Going back a long way." He hesitated. "I've found something in them that I don't understand, and I've an offer to make your four that you'll very likely refuse, and I'd like to make both in front of your captain and your book-keeper. Not on a pump-house walk." He looked past her at the basin, where the flood was coming in over the mud and there was no sign of anything having stood there. "Tomorrow, perhaps. After your crossing. I'm told it's at seven."

"Who told you?"

"Everybody in this town," said Mr. Adlam, "tells me everything. It's a great trial." He touched his hat. "Tomorrow, Miss Rowan."
