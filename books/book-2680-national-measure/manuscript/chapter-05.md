# Chapter 5 — Kiva: The Frame Again

Healer Aske saw the hand on the first morning of the year, at seven, by lamplight, with the frost still white on the Wren House yard.

He had the towel. He had the pin, and the penny, and the cup, and the scrap of waxed cord. He went through them in the same order as he had on a Monday in the tenth month, and Kiva gave him the same answers, and neither of them remarked on it.

"As before," he said. "Eight parts that report and two that tell you something's pushing. I've looked for the hair every week since the tenth of the tenth, and I've not found one in either direction." He capped his pen. "Eleven weeks and four days. I said that I'd counted them so that you needn't. I'm told that you counted them regardless."

"I checked."

"So did Cade. He was at my colleague's door at six, in a jersey." He took down his coat. "You'll come into the yard. I'll not have a door opened in a room with a bed in it. Your sponsor's below; I heard the stick."

Orla was on the flags, in the cold, with her collar up. She had brought the crate. Kiva did not ask how.

"One at a time," said Aske. "Declared, each, to me. I'll want the hand between. You've no ration until Monday, so it comes off my own card; seven hundredths, entered as medical, and I'll not be thanked for it. Which first?"

"The still."

"Why?"

"It's the one that I'd fall over without."

"That's a reason. Go on."

She stood on the frosted flags with her feet a little apart.

She had thought about this for eighty-one days, and she found that she had thought about it wrongly. She had imagined a door that had been shut so long that it stuck. It was not like that. It was more like a leg that has been in a splint: one knew perfectly how it worked, and one did not at all believe that it would bear weight.

"Still door. Rowan. A stance, for a count of five."

She opened it.

She set it by its corners, wide, as she had learned to over a cistern. It went down through the frost, and the flag, and the packed rubble under the flag, and it found the ground.

The ground took her.

That was all. There was no light with a still door, and no sound. But the whole of the Wren House yard came up under her two heels and held, like a wharf under a mooring, and for five counts she was the heaviest thing in it. There was no room underneath. She had looked for one. It had become a habit.

She shut it.

"Hand," said Aske.

She gave it to him. He turned it to the lamp, and pressed the palm and the wrist.

"Warm. Good. Next."

"The hand door. A ward, for one tap."

It came up low, at her right hip, where she had kept it since the autumn. It was silver, and no bigger than a tea tray. The first two fingers of her left hand stood clear of it, stiff, like the prongs of a fork, and the other eight held it as one holds a thing that is not heavy and might be dropped.

She had forgotten how cool it was. The hand door had always been the near one. It was close, and it liked whatever was by, and it lay in her palms now with a small bright eagerness, like a dog let out.

She folded it from the far edge. Far edge, near edge, a penny. Shut.

The Ember door had woken.

She felt it come, hot, under her breastbone. It had heard the cupboard open. And lower down the still door stirred as well, heavy and slow, because it had been used not a minute ago and saw no reason to have been put away.

They did not open. She had her back against both.

That was the summer's work, and the autumn's, and she had not known until that moment whether eleven weeks would have undone it. Three doors in one person wanted to be three open doors. She had learned, at a quarter of a dram a time, to have one and to mean it.

"Hand," said Aske.

It was cool to the knuckle, where the ward had lain. He held it a long minute.

"Sulking. A little. Less than I'd have wagered." He let go. "The last."

"The Ember door. A step. One bound."

The struts ran down the backs of her calves and along the soles of her feet. They were a dull orange, the color that her Ember's light had always been. She went six paces across the frosted yard in one long low bound, and came down light, with the three heat-lines on her chest drawn tight as wires and her bad knee saying something sharp.

She shut it at the landing. She stood on her own feet, on the flags, with every door closed.

Her breath smoked. She had expected to be glad. She had not expected to want, quite badly, to laugh.

"Well?" said Orla, from the crate.

"They're all there."

"I could see that they're there. I've eyes. I meant, what's it like?"

"It's like being let put my hands in my own pockets."

Her sponsor made a noise that might have been approval. Aske had the hand again. He did not hurry.

"As before," he said. "I've said that to you oftener than I've said good morning." He took out a card and wrote on it against the yard wall, and read it over aloud, since there was nobody in Meridian who seemed able to do otherwise. "*K. Rowan. Single doors, declared, one at a time, from this morning. A crossing: one in seven days at the most, and the hand seen by me the morning after. The first not before Thursday; I want three clear mornings. The longer thing: not on my card.*"

"One in seven at the most," said Kiva.

"At the most. It's a ceiling. I've known candidates take a ceiling for a floor." He gave her the card. "That hand came back to where it was after four days in a sling. I don't know why. I've a case, and it's one. I'd be obliged if you didn't hurry to give me a second."

"I'm read on Monday. On the Circuit's rigs."

"So I've been told. They'll want a door at a time, and that's on the card." He looked at her over his spectacles. "If the Chair's frame asks you for more than one, you'll say that your healer was disagreeable about it. I've found that it saves argument."

---

The Measure Hall was nearly empty on Monday, and that was the first thing that was not like the spring.

There had been four hundred in the gallery on Classification Day. There were perhaps thirty now, on the front benches, in their coats. It was a re-reading by appointment. The Circuit's clerk had the ledger open, and the long projected table on the east wall showed one name only, with the columns under it blank.

*Rowan, K. Draw. Hold. Rank. First door.*

"I'll say what this is," said Ysra Crest. She stood by the clerk's desk with her hands folded. "It is a reading. It is not a classification, and nothing that these instruments say this morning alters a word on the candidate's certificate. She has not been measured since the spring of last year. I've a National entry to countersign by the end of the month, and I'll not send a name to Saltmere with figures on it that are ten months stale."

"One door at a time," said Aske, from the wall. "It's on her card."

"I've read her card. So has Mr. Bexley."

Noa was at the mast. It was the hall's own, and sanctioned, and he had a white flag and a red one stuck in his belt like a signalman. He did not look round.

Her four had come. Anwen was on the front bench with the team book, and Perrin beside her in the grey jersey, which he was at last entitled to. Kellan sat at the end with his pencil. Pru was on her feet, since she could not see sitting.

There was somebody else, three benches back. She was standing. She had a stick in each hand, and her left leg was straight, and she was quite upright.

"The draw bench," said the clerk.

Kiva knew it. It was a lamp-cell under glass, on an oak stand, with a needle on the wall behind it. One held one's palms an inch above the cell and lit it, and kept it lit for sixty seconds, and the needle said what had been burned to do so.

"Which door?" said Ysra.

"Ember."

"Declared. Begin."

She held the pattern. The lamp lit a dull orange, and flickered, and she steadied it.

Then her hand door woke.

It did exactly what it had done in the spring. It lifted inside her, cool and close, reaching for the cell through her palm, because the cell was right there and the hand door liked cells. In the spring the Ember door had pushed back, and the lamp had stuttered, and the needle had swung, and somebody in the gallery had laughed.

She did not let it reach.

It was not a fight. She had thought once that it would always be one. It was more as one says *no* to a child at a shop window, for the fortieth time, without heat. The door went. The lamp did not so much as waver.

At sixty the clerk called time.

*Draw: 1.6.*

It came up on the east wall in figures a foot high.

Nobody laughed. Kellan had his pencil going before the lamp was out.

"It was three and a tenth," he said, to the bench at large. "It's a hair over half. One's standard. One point six is six-tenths over what the book says a minute of light ought to cost. A good single-door second-year pulls nine-tenths. I pull eight-two." He looked up. "So it's fallen a long way, and it's still what a clerk would call poor."

"I can read it," said Kiva.

"I know that you can. I was telling Finch."

"I can read it too," said Pru. "I'm only short."

"The hold benches," said the clerk.

There were three, in a row, each with its own load and its own needle. One opened a door against the bench and kept it open while the bench drew on it, harder and harder, until the door was taken out of one's keeping. The needle stopped where that happened.

She did the Edge first. The bar came down along her right fist, long and narrow and dark, and she lit it, and the bench leaned.

*Edge: 3.1.*

"That's a tenth," said Kellan. "In ten months. I'd not want it on a wall."

The ward was next. She put it up low, at her hip, a tray of silver with two fingers standing out of it, and the bench came against it like a hand on a door.

It was the same bench. But she had put up a ward for one tap and folded it away a great many times since the fifth month. She had done it under the arm of a thing in a pit, and under a cord on a hill. A ward that was folded and unfolded a hundred times before breakfast learned to stay where it was put.

The needle went past where it had stopped in the spring. It went on. She felt the bench take it from her at last, quite gently, like a tin lifted off a shelf.

*Ward: 4.5.*

"*Oh,*" said Anwen.

It was the only word that she said. She was hand-door and always had been, and she had held a ward on that same bench in the spring for longer than anybody in the Third Division. She knew what one and nine-tenths was, in ten months.

*Stance: 6.5.*

"Three-tenths up," said Kellan. "The class median's six and a tenth. That's the one honest figure she has, and she had it already."

"The frame," said Ysra.

The gallery had gone quiet.

It stood in the middle of the floor, as it had in the spring: four oak posts and a lintel, with brass along the inner faces, and above it the board that had flickered. Kiva had stepped into it once with three doors that she could not keep apart. It had woken all three at the same instant, and they had lit one another, and the Chair had said *shut it down.*

"One door," said Ysra. "Which?"

"Ember. Declared. The other two shut."

"The frame will ask for them. That is what it is for; it finds what a candidate has and calls it up, so that the board may see the whole. I can't instruct it to be civil." The level voice did not alter. "You will keep them shut if you can. If you cannot, you will say *off,* and the clerk will throw the switch, and I shall write what happened."

"Yes, ma'am."

She stepped in.

The air thickened. It was like walking into a room where a great many people have been talking and have just stopped. She opened the Ember door, and the bar came down her fist, and the frame took hold of it and looked.

Then it went looking for the rest.

She felt it. It was not rough. It was only thorough. It went down through her like a hand through a drawer and found the hand door, cool, behind its latch, and called to it. It found the still door underneath. The three hot wires on her chest drew tight.

*No,* she said to the first. *No,* to the second.

They pressed. It was sixty seconds, and it was a long sixty. The hand door wanted the brass. The still door wanted the floor, which was oak on stone and would have taken a stance beautifully. She had her back against both, and the Ember lit in front of her, and she did not look up.

"Time," said the clerk.

She looked up.

The board over the lintel was steady. It did not flicker. It had one word on it.

*Rank: Flame.*

"Step out, please, Miss Rowan."

She stepped out. Her legs were not quite her own. She put her right hand flat on her chest, over her tunic, and pressed until the burning went down to an ache.

Ysra had the ledger.

"Draw, one point six. Hold: Edge, three point one. Ward, four point five. Stance, six point five. Rank, with one door declared and two held shut: Flame. First door: none recorded." She read it as she read everything, without leaning on a word. "The clerk will ask me what *held shut* is doing in a rank line. I'll save him the trouble. It's there because it's so."

"It's a rank with a clause in it," said Kellan.

"It is. I've no other kind to give her." The Chair closed the ledger. "Miss Rowan. This is a reading of a summer and an autumn. You've not lit a door since the fifth of the tenth month. Whatever you have done in the three months since, no instrument of mine can see it. I don't say that there was nothing. I say that I've no rig for it."

"No, ma'am."

"Your conditions for the year I shall post with the entries. You'll have them before you stand on any floor of mine."

She went out by the side door. The clerk began to cover the lamp.

Somebody was coming along the front benches on two sticks. She came slowly, and she did not let anybody help.

"One point six," said Selka Dray.

"Yes."

"It's half what it was. It's still poor. I've a blade in my four who pulls seven-tenths and thinks nothing of it." Her sunk eyes were on the dark board. "I'd want that said to you by somebody who isn't pleased. Everybody on that bench is pleased."

"It's said."

"And *Flame.* With two held shut." Her jaw worked. "That's the first reading of yours that I've believed. One door, declared, and the frame leaning on the others for a minute, and you kept them shut. I watched your face. It wasn't easy."

"No."

"Good. I'd have distrusted easy." She shifted a stick. "I'm walking by the third month. I said so. Don't suppose that I've come round."

"I'd not."

Dray went up the aisle. It took her some time. Perrin watched her to the door.

"*Flame,*" he said. "I'm a Flame. I've been one since I was fifteen. I'd like it known that I've never before felt that there was anything to it."

---

She made the crossing on Thursday, on the same floor, with the frame's board lit and the mast at her back.

It was the one that she was certified for. It was on her sponsor's line, in so many words: *Ember to still, low, to a mark, declared to a count, on a sanctioned mast read by the Chair's reader.* She had done it six times in the summer for Orla, and once under a foreman's slate, and once, half, in a lane.

She had not done it since the ninth month. It cost the week's go. It came off her own thimble, and it left her two-tenths until Monday.

"Step first," said Orla.

"I know."

She walked to the chalk mark, eight paces out, and opened the still door on it, and shut it.

"Clean."

"Then say it."

"*Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on three.*"

Noa had his white flag up. The clerk had his pencil. The Chair stood where she had stood on Monday, and Aske by the wall.

"*One.*"

The struts ran down her calves.

"*Two.*"

She went. It was low, and long, and at the top of it she shut the Ember and did not listen for it. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs.

"*Three.*"

Nobody.

She was nobody, in the air over an oak floor, with nothing open and nothing to stand on. She had forgotten that it was like that. One never did remember it properly. It was a quarter of a dram going out of her like a breath, and her left hand went cold to the wrist as it went.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the stance. Wide. By its corners.

Her right foot came down on the chalk, and the floor took her.

"Planted," said Kiva.

"One cliff," said Noa. His voice cracked on it. "Twenty-six. Nothing open across it; I've the two lines, and there's a gap between them that you could drive a tram through. Thirty in all. That's a crossing."

"Mr. Bexley is satisfied that it is not a stack," said Ysra.

"It isn't anything like a stack, ma'am."

"Then look at the board, and tell me what it says, since I'd sooner the clerk heard it from a reader."

They all looked.

It had said *Flame* when she left the ground. She had seen it over her shoulder. It did not say so now. Where the word had been there was a short row of dashes, steady, as a clerk rules a line for a figure that has not come in.

"It went at the cliff," said Noa. "The instant the first door shut. It hasn't flickered. It's just gone to dashes and stopped."

"So it has." The Chair did not sound surprised. "Enter it as it stands. *Rank, with two doors used in sequence: none shown.* I said in the spring that I'd no column. I said it again in the autumn. I find that the board agrees with me, which is more than can be said for most of my colleagues."

"It ranked me on Monday," said Kiva.

"It ranked a candidate with one door. It will do so again whenever you care to be one." Ysra looked at her quite steadily. "I've a page, Miss Rowan, headed *Witnessed.* It has two lines on it. I don't propose to add a third for a thing that you're certified to do."

Aske had her hand already.

"Cold to the wrist. As it was in the summer." He turned it over. "Tomorrow at seven. If it's as before, then the card stands."

It was as before. He said so at seven on Friday, with the pin.

She climbed the stair that creaked that same afternoon, since it was within the week, and wrote in the fourth column with Hollis Vane to witness. *Cold. Thursday the 7th, on the Measure Hall floor. After one crossing, Ember to still, declared and read. Cold before, during and after. K.R.*

"You've a habit," said Vane.

"You said that in the autumn."

"I'll say it in the spring. It's a comfort to a watch officer, a thing that doesn't change."

---

The boy came to Coper Street at nine on Saturday night, with a slate-blue card. It was three words long. *Yard. Now. Finch.*

It was a bell on the Kiln loop. Kiva heard it from the van, over the whine of the rings, two and two, a mile off in the frost.

She sat on the side bench with her knees against a roll of canvas. Pru was beside her, with a hank of cord inside her tunic. Across the van, by the shuttered lamp, sat four wards in slate blue whom she knew by their boots: Tibb, who hummed, and Stiles, and Greta Stroud, and a fourth. Doran Mace had the bench end, with his board. Nairn was at the back, long-legged, with his blade arm across his knees. There were two men whom she did not know, in canvas aprons, with lead weights hung at their belts.

"Purvis," said the nearer. "That's Hobday. We're his corners."

"I'm his second."

"We heard. We've a shilling on how long you last with your mouth shut."

"Which of you has me lasting?"

"Neither," said Hobday.

Pike sat by the tailboard with a cloth on each shoulder. He had not spoken. He had his right hand inside his coat, against his chest, as a man carries a watch.

The van stopped. Rhea Sorn got down first, and stood with her hands in her pockets, and read.

It was the old ring kiln. Kiva had passed it on the tram a hundred times: a long low oval of blackened brick, with arched wickets all round it at the height of a man, and a chimney that had not smoked in her lifetime. Behind it the ground fell away to the clay pond, which was frozen. There was a row of kiln-hands' houses on the far side of the road, and every window in it was dark.

Something was standing in the third wicket.

It was a string, and it stood on end, as a string ought. It had three knots, each as big as a barrel, one above another, and it had frost on it from the crown to the knee. Its blunt top was turning, slowly, a quarter of a circle.

"Gate," said Rhea.

Four wards walked out and brought a wall up across the road, twelve paces from the kiln, silver from curb to curb. The string's head came round. It found the light. It walked out of the wicket on four thick legs, without hurry, and leaned on the gate like a drunkard on a lamp post.

"That's a string on a gate," said Rhea. "I've had two hundred. I'll read the slate, since I've a second who made it a condition, and since I've found that I rather like hearing it." She tilted it to the van's lamp. "*Before. A string of three, on end, at the old ring kiln. Its tear's at the clay pond, forty paces north. Nothing lit inside sixty paces but my gate; the row's dark, on the warden's word. Asked of anybody beyond their card: nothing. The second kneels at the catcher's left hem and holds her tongue until the bag is sealed.*" She lowered it. "What's there? Mace."

"A join that I like. Stiles is snug."

"Nairn."

"Three rungs. The top's skinned on the south face; I'd come at it from the north."

"Pike."

"Cobbles. They fall to the gutter, a hand in three paces." He had not looked at the cobbles that Kiva had seen. "I'll lay west of it."

Rhea did not ask Kiva. She did not look at her, either. It was on the slate.

"Then it's a top rung, on the catcher's word. Go."

Pike walked out onto the road, and Kiva went after him, and knelt where he pointed.

"My left hem," he said. "That puts the slack on your right. I was told." He did not say by whom. "Hold it down. That's all. If it comes proud under your hand, you bring it in. You don't say so."

She held it down.

It was the oddest floor that she had ever knelt on. She was a yard from the best catcher in Meridian, at a live rung, and she had nothing to do but keep two feet of canvas on the ground and look.

So she looked. She did it as she had looked at a thumb, and a hip.

He had not thrown the cloth yet. He stood over the place, and he scuffed the cobbles with his right heel. He did it twice, a pace apart. Then he threw, and the cloth lay flat at the first cast, and she saw that its long seam ran straight up the fall of the road.

He knelt. He put his left knee on the hem, a hand from hers, and it stayed there. That was why he had both hands free.

His right came out of his coat with the cord in it. It had been against his chest the whole way from the hall.

He was not looking at the string. She could see his face from below, grey in the gate's light, and his eyes were level. They were on something at the height of a boot.

"*Not yet,*" said Pike.

She could not see what he saw. She had her back half to the thing. She could hear it: a low dry creak, like a gate in a wind.

"*Not yet. Now.*"

There was a light. It was yellow, and level, and it lasted most of a second. It lit the cobbles and the hem and the two fingers of her left hand, lying on the canvas with nothing to say.

Sixty drams came down in the middle of the cloth like sand out of a sack.

"*Dark! One.*"

The wall went out over the heap, all but a cart's width at Stroud's end, which the book said should be left lit. What remained of the string turned its head and walked to that, and leaned there. The road went black, and then grey.

He did not call the corners.

She had been waiting for it. *Dark, one, corners;* she had called it herself forty times. Pike knelt there with his knee on the hem and his hands open and looked at a heap of ash, and said nothing at all.

She counted. She could not help it. She had got to eight when he spoke.

"Corners."

Purvis and Hobday hauled. The neck came up between them. It was beautiful to watch from underneath; there was no snatch in it anywhere. The hem ran in under her right hand, an inch at a time, exactly as fast as she could give it.

The cord went round. One. Two. On the third she saw his hands change over. The left took the long end under, and the right only held.

"Sealed," said Pike. "Forty-four." He had his palm on the neck. "Ward. Full. It's this winter's skin, and no louder than it ought to be."

They did the second rung, and the third.

On the second she counted eleven before he called the corners. And at about the twelfth count of the haul, the hem on the far side of the cloth lifted. It was Hobday's corner. It came up proud, a hand high, in a long loose fold.

She saw it. She had her mouth open.

She shut it. Her teeth clicked.

Pike's left knee moved. It slid an inch along the hem, toward the fold, as a man shifts his weight on a bench.

"Hobday," he said. "In a hand."

The fold lay down.

He had not turned his head. He had felt it through the canvas, with his knee, a yard and a half off, at the same moment that she had seen it with her eyes.

"Sealed. Forty-six."

"Finch," he said, at the third. "Your cord."

Pru came up out of the dark with her chin stuck out. She made three turns over a neck that a chartered catcher was holding shut for her, and Kiva watched the little finger come across, and nothing slackened.

"Sealed," said Pru. It was nearly a squeak. "Fifty-one."

"That's slow. It's also a bag." He took his knee off the hem. "You'll be at forty-five by the spring, or I'll know why."

There were two people in long coats at the clay pond, with a handcart, bent over the ice. Kiva heard one of them call *closed,* not loudly, as a man says that he has shut a gate.

Rhea read the slate in the road.

"*Closed, ten minutes past eleven. Lives: none. Works: a kiln wicket, frosted. Tinned: three bags, clean, at trace. A hundred and sixty-two drams, of a hundred and eighty by the book. Remarks: none.*" She hung it on her belt. "That's a night. Stroud, you were proud on the left for a count and mended it; I'm saying that I saw."

"I heard myself," said Stroud.

"I know. That's why I'm only saying."

---

They rode back with the bags in the rack and the lamp shuttered.

Pike sat by the tailboard. He had his right hand in his coat again. He waited until they were past the gasworks.

"Well," he said. "What did I do that I didn't say?"

Kiva had it ready. She had been putting it in order since the kiln.

"You scuffed the road with your heel. Twice, before you threw. And then you laid the seam up the fall. You told the Torch that the cobbles fell a hand in three paces, and you'd not looked at them. You'd felt it through your boot."

"That's a catcher's trick. I was shown it."

"You didn't say it. You said *I'll lay west.*"

Purvis laughed. Pike did not.

"Go on."

"The cord was inside your coat. From the hall. It's warm when it comes out."

"Wax," said Pike. "It's the first month. Any fool keeps his cord warm."

"Pru had hers under her arm. She kept it at her elbow until I saw her lose four in the cold."

"*I* keep it in my tunic now," said Pru. "I'd like that entered."

"What else?"

"You don't call the corners at *one.*" She watched his face. "You call *dark, one.* Then you wait. I counted eight the first time. Eleven the second, and nine the third. You look at the heap. Then you call them."

"I call the corners when it's down."

"It was down at one."

"I don't wait."

"Eleven," said Hobday, from the dark. "I've had my hands on that hem for nine years, cursing you. I never counted it."

Pike was silent. The van went over the tram points with a double knock.

"I watch it lie," he said at last, slowly, like a man reading small print. "I suppose that I do."

"What for?"

"I don't know that I've ever said." He took his hand out of his coat and looked at it. He shut it, and opened it, twice. "Write that one with the others. I'll read them by the stove. And the fourth."

"I've only said three."

"You've a fourth. You've had it behind your teeth since the first bag." He nearly smiled. "You shut your mouth on Hobday's corner so hard that I heard it. What would you have said?"

"*Hobday's proud.*"

"And I had it."

"Yes. Before I'd have got the words out."

"So there'd have been two voices at one hem, and the second telling the first a thing that he knew." Pike put the hand away. "That's why your mouth's shut. It isn't manners. A corner can only listen to one."

Kiva thought about that as far as the levee.

She had learned a good deal on her knees, and she had also learned the size of what she had not seen. He had found a proud corner with his kneecap, and read the fall of a road through a boot sole. There were twenty-six years in that square grey man, and she had got three things out of a whole night, and the fourth she was not sure of.

Rhea Sorn had turned round on the front bench.

"It's winter by the almanac," she said. "So you may have the piece that I've been keeping. That was a quiet night. A string came to a gate and was taken down a rung at a time by people who've done it two hundred times, and nobody was hurt, and you'll write it up beautifully."

"Yes."

"Don't mistake it for the trade."

She turned back. The rings whined. Pru had fallen asleep against the canvas, with her hand still shut on her cord.
