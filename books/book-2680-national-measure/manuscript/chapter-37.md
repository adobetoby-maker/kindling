# Chapter 37 — Kiva: A Declared Count

The draw had gone up at nine on Friday night, out of the canvas bag, with Selka Dray standing at the front of the crowd under the lamp, and it had said what everybody in the loft had known it would say since the clerk drew it.

*Saturday. Ten o'clock. Team Seven, Meridian, against Team Twenty-Eight, Meridian.*

Kiva had not been there. She had been at the foot of Quiller's stair, watching the clerk on his crate at the far end of the timber pond. She had seen the crowd go still in pieces, and then turn, and some of them look along the quay toward her. She had not needed to read it.

At seven on Saturday morning the Mercury's boy came up Quay Street with his bag, and the first thing Kiva saw from the north window was the boy, and the second was the size of the letters on the sheet he held up.

*THE FOUNDATION WITHDRAWS.*

And under it, smaller: *"Every Dram But the Dry Tenth": A Halden Blade's Figures Before the Board. Bench Books Show the Step Quicker Than It Was. The Order's Master on the Coast Boat. A Meridian Catcher Says She Has Dreamed the Keepers' Night.*

And under that, in a column of its own, in italics: *The Proprietor Asks the Board to Come to Him.*

She did not go down to buy one. Pru did. Pru came back up the stair with it under her arm and laid it on the end of Kiva's bed without a word, and went red, and went to have her caps seen to.

Kiva read it sitting on the bed with her hand in her lap. The Mercury's young man had got most of it right. He had got her name right, and the Pit and the ferry and Classification Day, and he had put *at thirteen, when she was Kindled* in a place where it read as though she had been Kindled on the ferry. He had written that she spoke *in a level voice, with a bandaged hand, without notes.* He had not said what she had seen, because she had not said what she had seen, only that it was the same thing the keepers had written. He had put in the lamp-man at Holloway, and the laugh from the gallery, and that the laugh had stopped.

At the foot of the column he had written one line of his own.

*Whether any of this means anything, the Board will say on Sunday, at two, in the Graving. The Board has never yet said that a fever was a fault.*

She folded the sheet and put it under her pillow, and found she did not want to read it again.

---

Aske held the hand at the window at a quarter past seven.

"Warm," he said. "Six mornings." He wrote it. Then he did not let go. He held her wrist and looked at her over his spectacles with his face quite still, and the loft went on dressing round them: Halden at the south end, Old Four arguing, Dray on the next bed lacing a boot with both feet on the floor.

"I said I'd say it twice," said Aske. "This is twice."

"I know."

"The fifth went below the elbow and sulked three nights. The sixth went to the elbow and sulked four. The seventh goes past it and sulks five, and I'll not be surprised by six." He did not raise his voice. "And it's one in seven days. If you cross in a lane today, the next is Saturday next. Not Wednesday. There's no crossing between, whatever's asked." He paused. "That's the price. It's the same price it was on Thursday. Nothing that happened in that hall last night has moved it."

Beside them, Dray's hands had stopped on her lace again.

"It's for Wednesday," said Kiva. "Still."

"Then I've said it twice for nothing, and I'm glad." He let go of her wrist. "I'll be on the tiers. If you go a colour, I'll come down, lane or no lane."

He went down the stair. Kiva sat on the edge of the bed with the hand in her lap and looked at the next bed, where Selka Dray was sitting very upright with a boot half laced and her face like a shut door.

It was three feet. It had been three feet for nineteen nights. They had not spoken across it above twice.

"Dray," said Kiva.

Dray finished the knot. She pulled it tight, and set her foot down on the floor beside the other one, and turned her head.

"I heard him," she said.

"I know you did. You heard him on Thursday as well." Kiva did not look away. "I wanted you to hear it from me. To your face, across the beds, before the bell. Not from the tiers after." She took a breath. "I'll not cross today. Perrin will declare me Ember at the box, single, and I'll stay Ember all three carries. No change. There'll be no count for you to wait on. The week's crossing is for Wednesday, if anybody wants it, and if nobody does I'll have kept it for nothing."

Dray looked at her for a long moment with her sunk grey eyes.

"You know what I said," said Dray. "In the autumn. In a chair."

"*I'll beat you in a lane there. On a declared count. With a needle on you.*"

"I meant your crossing." Dray's voice was perfectly flat. "Not your Ember. Anybody can beat an Ember on one door with an edge. I meant the thing you do that nobody else does, read off a mast, with your captain saying the count out loud so that I'd know the second it came. I've had that in my head since the ninth month." She looked down at her left leg, straight on the floor in its boot, and back up. "That's what I came two hundred miles on this for."

"I know."

"And you're not going to give it me."

"No," said Kiva. "It's spoken for. I'd not have you think I was keeping it from you. I'm keeping it for somebody else."

Dray was quiet for a long time. Down the room Old Four had stopped arguing, and Kiva did not look round to see whether they were listening.

"You'll lose the exchange," said Dray at last. "If it comes to me and you. On one door. I'll have you."

"Very likely."

"I'll not hold back because you've told me. I'd not want it thought I let you off."

"I'd not want it either," said Kiva.

Dray stood up. She stood on two feet, and Kiva saw her weight settle, more on the right than the left, as it always did now; and she saw Dray see her see it, and not care.

"Then it's declared," said Dray. "All of it." She took her coat off the end of the bed. "That's fair. I've not changed my mind about you." She went to the stair, and stopped at the head of it with her hand on the post, as she had stopped on Thursday. "I wanted your crossing. I'll say that once."

She went down.

---

They made the plan at the long table at eight, on Perrin's slate, and it was not the same plan as Friday's.

"One voice," said Perrin. He had the chalk in his fist and nobody else was allowed to touch it. "That's what lost us the third yesterday. Rowan said *feet* and Finch said *hands* in the same breath, and Talley stood between the two words and didn't take either. Coombe said it to the Ropewalk on the causeway. *That's two places.*" He drew a ring on the slate round a stick figure that was meant to be Pru. "Finch calls the pen. All of it. Hands, feet, whatever she sees. Talley and Pryce listen to Finch and nobody else."

"And Rowan?" said Jude.

"Rowan calls nothing." Perrin did not look at Kiva. "Rowan's on her Ember all day, and she's got Dray on her, because Dray's going to go wherever Rowan goes, and that's two of Twenty-Eight's four doing nothing but each other. Rowan, you watch Dray's feet and your own, and you keep your mouth shut about everybody else's."

Kiva looked at the ring on the slate round Pru.

It was the thing she had learned to do in a van at a hem, and the thing she had never once done on a lane floor in her life. On every floor she had stood on since she was thirteen she had been the one who said *now* and *mine* and *yours* and *set.* She had said them because she had been inside the gaps and knew when they came. It had never occurred to her that the call might be somebody else's place, and not hers.

"Finch's," she said.

Pru had gone scarlet. She did not look away.

"I'll call it as I do," said Pru. "Not as you do. I watch hands. I'll watch feet as well, today, because I watched Treen learn to off you. I'll not be good at it." She put her capped hands flat on the table. "And you'll not say a word, even if I'm wrong. Not *far edge.* Not anything. If you say one word I'll stop and listen to you, and that's two places."

"Not a word," said Kiva.

"Promise."

"I promise."

Perrin looked at the two of them, catcher and catcher, across his slate. Something went across his face and did not stay.

"Twenty-Eight," he said. "Marten's their captain and their catcher, and he's slow, and he's honest, and he calls off the litter. Their Hold's good. Their still door's big and planted and doesn't like to move. And Dray." He tapped the slate. "Dray on her feet. Turns badly to her left. She knows it. She'll stand with her left to the hurdle so she's always turning right."

"She'll have thought of it," said Kiva.

"She thinks of everything." Perrin put the chalk down. "That's the other thing. She's watched every lane we've stood since the gate. She knows the elbows. She knows the hen. She knows Pryce walks her wall from the far end." He looked round at them. "She doesn't know Finch calls the pen. Nobody does. We decided it at this table four minutes ago."

---

The Graving was full at ten, and half of the full had come to look at a girl who dreamed.

Kiva felt them as she came out of the side door onto the floor. It was not like Friday. On Friday the whole town had stood up for Carrow, and Kiva had been one of eight people on a floor. Today it did not stand. It sat, tier after tier, in green and in grey and in the colours of six circuits, and every face in the lower tiers turned as she came out of the door and followed her across the stone to Seven's hurdle, and she felt it on the side of her face and the back of her neck like the sun.

It was not unkind. Somebody in the third tier called *elbows!* as she passed, and somebody near him laughed. But under it there was the other thing, the thing Perrin had said in the shop. They were looking at her as the Long Quay had looked at her last night.

She kept her eyes on the stone.

Anwen was at her back. She did not say anything. She walked a pace behind Kiva's left shoulder all the way from the door to the hurdle, as a wall walks behind somebody, and when they got to the hurdle she stood there.

Twenty-Eight came out of the far door. Marten first, slow and square, with the litter's rules in his face. The Hold after him, a tall fair girl Kiva knew by sight from the Low Lane. The still door, very big. And Dray last, on two feet, without a stick, with her face like a shut door and her edge dark on her right fist, walking with her weight a little more on the right than the left.

The Graving stood up for Dray.

It did not stand all at once. It stood in pieces, from the lower tiers upward, the way a field of birds gets up. The green did not know Dray. They knew she had been in a chair in the ninth month, and that she had walked onto the timber pond on one stick at the gate, and that she was walking now on none. They stood for that.

Dray did not look at them. She looked at Kiva across the pen, once, and then at the Commodore lying in the middle with his tarred hat over one eye.

"*Declarations,*" said the marshal.

"*Twenty-Eight,*" said Marten. "*Marten, ward, catcher. Shapter, ward. Bannock, stance. Dray—*" He stopped. He did not say *edge.* Twenty-Eight had nobody with more than one door, and Dray had said in the autumn, in a chair, that she would always declare her own.

"*Dray,*" said Dray herself, before anybody could stop her, "*edge. Declared. Single. Read.*"

At the mast on the north side of the pen, Noa looked up from his desk. So did Polglase. Dray was not under the rule; she had one door. She had asked to be read anyway. The two readers looked at each other, and then Noa threaded a second pen, and wrote.

"*Team Seven,*" said Perrin. "*Pryce, ward. Talley, edge. Finch, ward, catcher. Rowan, Ember, declared, single, by her captain's voice, and no change. Cade, captain, standing out on his healer's card.*"

*And no change.* Kiva heard it go up the stone walls, and heard the Graving hear it, and heard it understand, in pieces, as it had understood Carrow's *single.* There was a sound like a long breath let out, from the Meridian knot in the middle tiers and then from everybody.

The marshal looked at Perrin over his box.

"*No change,*" he said. "That's declared?"

"It's declared."

"Then I'll hold you to it." He did not turn his glass yet. "You know the book, Cade. A captain may still call a change after *no change.* It's a point to the other four for the word, and it goes on her card and yours that the word didn't hold."

"I know the book," said Perrin.

The marshal turned his glass. "*Ready.*"

The first carry was Twenty-Eight's, and it showed Kiva that Dray had not been idle.

Seven went for the litter on its arms. They had done it twice now, and Kiva went for the head with her Ember lit, glad, under her breastbone, and the low bound took her to the litter a pace ahead of Twenty-Eight's still door. She got her forearms under the head poles, crossed at the wrists. Pru came for the foot.

"*Pryce, south. Talley, north, his hands,*" said Pru.

Anwen's wall came up south. Jude went north, where Twenty-Eight's still door was coming for the head with his big hands open.

Dray did not come for the litter at all.

She walked down the middle of the pen, past the chalk squares, with her edge dark, and stopped two paces from Jude. She stood with her left side toward Seven's hurdle and her weight on her right foot, and she watched Jude's left arm.

Kiva understood it a moment too late. Jude was fighting one-handed, with his edge on his right fist and his left arm held against his chest. He could not guard his left side without turning his whole body. Dray had seen it on Thursday from the tiers, and on Friday against Carrow. She stood two paces off his left side and waited, as a person waits at a door that they know will open.

"*Talley, left,*" said Pru.

Jude turned. He had to turn his whole body to bring his edge round to his left, and while he turned, Twenty-Eight's still door put both hands on the head poles beside Kiva's crossed forearms and simply stood there, planted, heavier than she was. And Dray lit her edge and stepped in on Jude's right, where he had just come from, and touched him on the shoulder.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "Seven."

Jude knelt.

Kiva felt the head of the Commodore go. It did not go fast. Twenty-Eight's still door leaned from the floor, as Lyle had leaned, as everybody on the coast seemed to have learned in a week, and the head poles came out of the crooks of her elbows a finger at a time, because her forearms were not hands and could not close. Pru had the foot on her arms. Twenty-Eight's Hold put her wall across the litter's middle, between Pru and the head, and leaned it on nobody, and held it there. Marten took the foot poles from under Pru's crossed forearms with his own two good hands, gently, as a man takes a tray from a child.

They walked it home. At the hurdle Dray shut and went over and turned and took the head from under, and the Hold took the foot.

"*Three. And a down. Four to Twenty-Eight.*"

The Graving made the warm sound. It was for Dray. Kiva heard that it was for Dray, and found she did not grudge it.

"She's on Talley's arm," said Kiva, at the hurdle.

Pru put her hand up. Kiva shut her mouth.

"*I* say who she's on," said Pru. "You promised." She was white, not scarlet. "She's on Talley's left. She's on it because he's got no edge there and she knows he's got to turn. And she's not on you." Pru looked at Kiva. "She's not been near you. She's waiting. She's leaving you be until it's you and her and nobody else."

Kiva had seen that too. She had not said it.

"So Talley doesn't turn," said Pru. "Talley, if she's on your left, you don't turn. You stand. You let her come. If she wants to touch you she's to come round to your right to do it, and she turns badly to her left, so she'll go round the long way, and that's a count and a half she's not near the litter." She looked at Anwen. "Pryce. Their still door's leaning from the floor. Like Lyle."

"I know. Everybody's had it off me this week."

"Then walk round him. Like you walked round Lyle. He's planted. He can lean but he can't follow." Pru looked at the Commodore lying in the middle of the pen. "And we don't carry on our arms. Not this carry. They've had the arms. We carry on Pryce's hands and Talley's hand, the head and the foot, and Rowan and me take it over at the hurdle on our arms, where they can't get at it."

"That's my plan," said Perrin, from outside the pen. "That's Thursday's plan."

"It's Thursday's plan and Friday's arms." Pru did not look round at him. "Captain."

"Go on, then," said Perrin. "It's yours."

The second carry was Seven's, and it was Pru's.

Kiva had stood in a great many lanes. She had never stood in one without calling it, and for the first count after the bell she did not know what to do with her mouth. She kept it shut. She went for the head on her Ember, and the low bound took her there, and she did not put her arms under the poles. She stood at the head with her Ember lit and her hands empty and watched Dray.

Dray watched her.

They stood three paces apart in the middle of the Graving with the litter between them, and neither of them moved, and the lane went on round them as though they were two posts.

"*Pryce, round him. Far end.*"

Anwen's wall came up against Twenty-Eight's still door, who had come for the head and planted. He leaned on it from the floor, and it bowed, a finger. Anwen did not lean back. She walked the far end of her wall round him in a quarter circle, as she had walked it round Lyle on Friday, and the near end came after it, and the big boy was leaning on air with his feet in the stone and nothing in front of him. He had to shut to keep his feet. Jude was there.

"*Down.* Twenty-Eight."

"*Pryce, head. Talley, foot. Walk. North.*"

Anwen and Jude had the Commodore. Anwen had the head in her two good hands; Jude had the foot in his one. They walked it along the north side, close against the posts, and Twenty-Eight's Hold came to stop them and Pru called *hands, south,* and Anwen's wall came up between, low and silver, and went down again.

Dray moved.

She did not come for Kiva. She went for Jude's left side again, fast, with her weight on her right foot and her edge lit, and Jude did not turn. He stood with the foot poles in his right hand and his left arm against his chest and let her come. Dray had to go round to his right to reach him. She went round the long way, as Pru had said, turning to her own right, because her left would not take the turn, and it was a count and a half, and in a count and a half Jude had walked the foot three paces.

At the hurdle Kiva and Pru were on the far side. Kiva did not remember going there. She remembered Pru saying *Rowan, hurdle,* and her Ember's bound, and the hazel going under her, and turning. They put up their forearms crossed at the wrists, both of them, and the Commodore came over the hazel into the crooks of their arms.

"*Over. Three to Seven. A down. Four to four.*"

The Graving did not make the warm sound. It made the other sound, the one it had made on Friday when somebody shouted *elbows.* Somebody shouted it again, and then somebody else shouted *Finch!*, which nobody had ever shouted in the Graving before, and Pru went scarlet from her collar to her hair and did not look up from the Commodore on her arms.

Kiva looked at her across the canvas.

She had not said a word. Not one. She had stood on a lane floor for four minutes with her mouth shut and her Ember lit and watched Selka Dray, and the litter had gone over the hurdle on somebody else's call.

It was the strangest thing she had ever felt on a floor, and it was not unpleasant. It was like being a door in somebody else's house.

The third carry was the glass.

Twenty-Eight had learned. Kiva watched Marten learn it in the pause, at the far hurdle, with Dray beside him, the two of them bent close, Marten slow and Dray quick. When the bell went, Twenty-Eight's still door did not go for the head. He went to Seven's end of the pen and planted three paces short of Seven's hurdle, square, in the way of anything that might come home.

Twenty-Eight's Hold put her wall across the north side, along the posts, where Seven had walked twice.

And Dray went for the litter.

Kiva's Ember took her there in one bound. She got to the head at the same moment as Dray got to the middle, and they stopped, two paces apart, with the Commodore lying between them on the stone and his tarred hat over one eye. The glass was turning. Nobody else was near. Marten was at the foot, slow, coming; Pru was calling Anwen and Jude somewhere behind; the still door was planted at Seven's end; the Hold's wall was along the north. It was Kiva and Dray and the litter, in the middle of the pen, the way Dray had wanted it since the ninth month.

The litter's head lay a pace inside Twenty-Eight's half. The middle was on the chalk.

Kiva could see what a crossing would do. She could see it as plainly as she had ever seen anything. Her hand door, the ward, low and flat at her hip, a wall between her and Dray's edge; then the count, *one, two, three,* and the gap, a quarter of a count with nothing open; and then her Ember, glad, and the low bound back with the head on her forearms, two paces, three, out of Dray's reach and across the chalk into Seven's half before Dray could come round to her left. It was her own certified crossing, hand to Ember. She had made it six times on two masts. She could feel exactly how it would go.

And Dray would be waiting on the third count. Dray had been waiting on it since a chair in the ninth month. Dray would hear Perrin say *Rowan, changing on three,* and she would know to the quarter count when the ward came down, and she would bring her edge into the gap.

Kiva did not know whether Dray would be fast enough. That was the thing. She had been inside the gap. She knew how short it was. She thought, very likely, that she would be out of it before Dray's edge came down, and the litter would be in Seven's half at the glass, and the lane would probably be Seven's, even with a point given away for the change. It would cost that point. It would cost the word Perrin had given at the box, and the one she had given Dray across the beds. And it would cost Wednesday.

Outside the pen, at the hurdle, Perrin had opened his mouth.

He had not said anything. He was standing with his hands on the hazel and his mouth open on the first word of a count, looking at her across twenty paces of stone, and she knew what the word was. He was the captain. It was his voice that declared her, and his word that would be broken, and his name the point would go against first. It was his to say, if she let him.

She shook her head. Once, very slightly.

Perrin shut his mouth.

Dray had seen it. Kiva saw her see it. Dray had been standing two paces off with her weight on her right foot and her edge lit and her sunk grey eyes not on Kiva's face but on Perrin's mouth, waiting for the count. She saw Perrin's mouth shut. She saw Kiva's head move. And she understood, and her face did something that Kiva had never seen it do. It did not open. But for an instant it was not a door. It was a person who has been told a thing she knew already, and finds that she minds it after all.

Then she came in.

Kiva bounded. She had one door and she used it. She got her forearms under the head poles and went back on her Ember's low bound with the Commodore's head on her arms, dragging, the weight of the whole canvas dummy pulling her down as the sleeve had pulled her down over the channel at Mallow, and she got a pace and a half. The head came across the chalk. The middle came after it.

Dray did not go round to her left. Kiva had thought she might, and had bounded to Dray's left on purpose, toward the turn she did not like. Dray did not take the turn. She stepped across instead, with her right foot, the long way, and it was a count and a half and Kiva was a pace and a half off with a hundredweight on her arms and nothing open but the Ember, and that was not enough. Dray's edge came down flat on her back between the shoulder blades, quite lightly, as lightly as a woman puts her hand on a child's head.

"*Down,*" said the marshal. "Seven."

Kiva let go. She had to; a downed fighter lets go. The head of the Commodore went down on the stone a foot inside Seven's half. She knelt beside it and did not look at Dray.

She counted ten.

She had lost the exchange. She had known she would, since a quarter past seven, at three feet, across two beds. She had chosen to. It did not feel like a thing that had been done to her. It felt like a thing she had chosen, in front of Dray and the tiers and Perrin at the hurdle with his mouth shut, and that was all.

"*Hands, Marten, foot,*" said Pru.

Kiva heard it from her knees. She did not look up. She heard Marten's slow square steps come round the foot of the litter to drag it back east across the chalk, and Jude's step after him, and the marshal.

"*Down.* Twenty-Eight."

"*Pryce, over it,*" said Pru.

Anwen's wall came up across the litter's middle. She did not lean it on anybody. She set it there, low and silver, a pace long, over the Commodore's canvas belly like a lid on a box, and stood behind it with her heels right. Twenty-Eight's Hold came to take the foot, and the wall was in the way of her hands, and she could not lift what she could not reach. Dray came round to the head, where Kiva was kneeling, and could not touch the litter while Kiva knelt beside it, because nobody touches a downed fighter, and Kiva was kneeling with her shoulder against the head poles, not holding them, not touching them, only there.

Kiva had not thought of that. She had not planned it. She had knelt where she was put.

Dray stood over her with her edge lit and looked down at her, and Kiva looked up at Dray, and neither of them said anything.

The glass ran out.

"*Time,*" said the marshal. He came down off his box and looked, and walked round the Commodore twice. "*In Seven's half. By a foot.*" He went back up. "*A point to Seven. A down each way.*" He wrote. "*Six to Seven. Five to Twenty-Eight.*"

The Graving did not make the warm sound, or the other sound. It made a sound that Kiva had never heard a crowd make. It was the sound of a great many people who have watched a thing they did not understand, and know that they did not understand it, and are talking to each other about what it was.

Kiva got up off her knees.

Dray was still standing over the head of the Commodore. Her edge was dark now. She stood with her weight on her right foot and her face shut, looking at Kiva, and for a moment Kiva thought she was going to walk away without a word, as she walked away from everything.

"You'd have had it," said Dray. "With the count. Hand to Ember. I was waiting on three."

"I know you were."

"I'd have had you in the gap."

"Maybe," said Kiva. "Maybe not. I've been in it. It's short."

Dray was silent. Her jaw moved, once.

"That's a thing I'll not know now," she said. "Ever. Not on this coast. You'll not spend it on a lane, this year or next." Her voice was quite flat. "You chose that. At three feet, this morning. And on the floor, with your head."

"Yes."

"I'll not thank you for it."

"I didn't ask you to."

"No." Dray looked down at the Commodore, at the foot of canvas lying across the chalk on Seven's side. "You knelt by the head. Nobody touches a downed fighter. I couldn't reach it past you."

"I didn't mean to. I knelt where I was."

"I know you didn't. That's what makes it worse." Dray turned to go. Then she turned back, as Carrow always did, and as Dray never had. "It was declared," she said. "All of it. Before the bell, and at the box. *No change.* I heard your captain say it." She paused. "That's the fairest lane I've ever lost."

She went away up the pen on two feet, with her weight on the right.

Pru was standing at the litter's foot with her capped hands held up and her face scarlet.

"Six," she said. "Six to five."

"You called it," said Kiva.

"I called it. You didn't say a word." Pru looked at her. "Not even when she was on Talley's arm and I didn't see it for a count. I saw you see it. You shut your mouth so hard I heard your teeth."

"I promised."

"I know you did." Pru put her hands down. "That's the first lane I've ever called that nobody called over me." She did not go any redder; she could not. "It's not a favour. You should know that. I'm not grateful. I'm only good."

"I know you are," said Kiva. "I watched."

---

Rhea Sorn sat on the fourth tier above the floor with her hands in her pockets and her slate on her knee, and watched the girl kneel by the litter's head and not look up, and did not write anything on the slate at all.

Pike was beside her. His hands lay on his knees, closed halfway, as they had lain for a fortnight, and would very likely lie now for the rest of his life. He had not said a word through three carries. He said one now.

"Cade," he said.

"I saw."

"He'd his mouth open on the count. She shook her head." Pike's voice was low and dry. "He shut it. That's a captain. I'd have said the count, at his age. I'd have thought it was mine to say." He nearly smiled. "She called nothing all lane. Not a word. Finch had it."

"I saw."

"Did you know she would?"

"No," said Rhea. "I'd have bet against it. I'd have bet my van."

She took her hands out of her pockets and looked at them. They were a Torch's hands, square and scarred across the knuckles from twenty years of cutting from behind a skin, and they had written a great many cards. They had written one in the autumn that said *catcher's second, to Pike,* with *either of us may say no* at the foot of it, and *no seat is promised,* and a date. The date was the 30th. That was eleven days off, and one more night on her van.

She allowed herself one piece of advice a season to anybody she trained, and no more. She had spent the winter's on the girl in the first month, and the spring's on a mud hard at Gannet Creek at one in the morning. She had held to that rule with every corner-hand and second she had ever had. It was the rule that kept them from being hers.

She had kept to the letter of it. She had broken the rest. She had broken it at the Company yard on Wednesday, telling Yeo to ask the girl for her columns before she slept, and calling it *a thing I know about her.* She had been knowing things about the girl since the first month and saying them aloud to people who would act on them. She had told herself it was only a second's due.

It was not. She knew what it was. It was the thing she had burned a card in the autumn to keep from writing. *Mine.*

"You're not writing," said Pike.

"There's nothing to write. It's not my lane."

"You've written every lane she's stood since the gate. On the back of the slate, small, where you think I can't see." He did not look at her. "*Watches feet.* *Called the far edge from sixty paces.* *Kept the week's crossing.* I can read, Rhea. I've been reading your slate since you first ruled it."

Rhea did not answer that.

On the floor, Selka Dray had turned back to say something to the girl over the head of the litter, and the girl was answering, and the Graving was talking to itself in a long low rumble about what it had seen. Rhea watched the girl's face. It was not a face that had lost anything. It was the face of somebody who had put a thing down on a table where anybody could see it, and was standing back from it.

"Twice in a week," said Rhea. "She's done that twice. In a hall last night, and on a floor this morning. Put a thing down and stood back from it." She took up the slate. "I've never in my life done that once."

She did not write on the back of it. She turned it over to the front, where the six columns were, and below them, in the clear space at the foot where she wrote a card before she copied it out fair, she began to write a card.

It was not the girl's card. It was not time for the girl's card, and it was not hers to write until the girl had been asked, and the girl had answered, and either of them had said no. That was the 30th. She would not write it a day before.

She wrote the other one.

*Graywater. Pryce, A., Hold, Meridian, Team Seven. To Doran Mace's ward line on the night van, in her own place and not a relief's, from the first of the fourth month, at a ward-hand's wage. Mace asked for her by name in the autumn and left her name where it was. A card is not a seat. R. Sorn.*

She read it over. She thought of the Pryce girl on the Ropewalk's boards on Thursday morning, teaching a laid Hold with no ward left how to lean from the floor, knowing it would be used against her own four, and doing it anyway. Mace had told her that on Thursday night at the van, laughing. *She'll give it away to anybody who asks properly. That's the best thing about her and the worst.*

Rhea copied the card out fair, onto a white card from her pocket, in Graywater's slate blue, and blew on it.

"That's not hers," said Pike, reading upside down.

"It's Pryce's."

"I know whose it is. I said it's not *hers.*" He looked at the floor, where the girl was walking back to Seven's hurdle with her bound hand against her coat and the Finch girl beside her. "You'll write hers on the 30th."

"I'll ask her on the 30th. If she says no, I'll not write it."

"And if she says yes?"

Rhea put the card in her pocket.

"Then I'll find out what I write," she said. "I don't know yet. That's the truth, and it's the first time I've said a thing like that to you." She stood up, with her slate under her arm. "I'll pin this at the baize. Come on. You can watch me do a thing I've never done, which is give somebody's Hold away to somebody else's line without asking her captain first."

"Cade'll mind."

"Cade'll mind for an hour and then he'll say he'd like it known that he's glad," said Rhea. "I've watched him all week as well."

She went down the tiers toward the floor's edge, a step at a time, and Pike came after her with his half-closed hands held a little away from his sides, as a man carries a thing he has been asked to carry and does not mean to drop.
