# Chapter 1 — The Husk Line

Six snares, and six empty snares, and Callie reset all six before she let herself think about it.

She did them in order going north up the ditch line, the way she always did, because doing them out of order was how you walked past one and lost the wire. The wire was the part that mattered. You could always find another stick. There were eleven feet of brass wire left in the tobacco tin in her coat, cut into six lengths, and when it was gone it was gone, and nobody between here and Meridian was going to sell her brass wire for what she had.

The sixth snare had been sprung and emptied. Something had taken whatever was in it and left the loop pulled out of shape, and Callie crouched over it for a while with her thumb in the grass, reading the ground. Cat. A small one. It had come down the ditch from the culvert side and gone back the same way, which meant it lived up there, which meant she could either move the whole line or go find the cat, and she had no time to find the cat.

She moved the whole line.

By the time she got back to the road the light had gone flat and yellow and her mother was awake, sitting up on the boards of the barrow with the blanket around her shoulders and her boots off, working her toes in the cold air like a woman with all day.

"Anything?"

"Not yet," Callie said.

"Not yet," her mother repeated, in a voice that took the phrase and set it carefully on a shelf where they could both look at it. "That's the fourth not yet."

"Third."

"It's the fourth. I've been counting on your behalf, since you've clearly stopped."

"I haven't stopped."

"Then say the number."

Callie set the tobacco tin down on the boards, and sat on the road edge, and pulled her own boots off because the left one had been filling with grit all afternoon. "Four days," she said. "Nine pounds of flour. A half-tin of grease. Seven matches. Two days of water if we don't wash, three if we do the stupid thing and drink the pond."

"And the wire."

"Eleven feet."

"There," her mother said. "That wasn't so bad, was it."

"You're in a mood."

"I am in a *tremendous* mood. I've been lying on a plank looking at the sky since noon. I've had thoughts. I've had opinions about clouds." She got her boots back on, slowly, and she did the left one first and then sat a moment before she did the right one, and Callie watched her do it out of the corner of her eye and did not say anything about it. "The trouble with clouds is that they're extremely pleased with themselves."

"That's the opinion?"

"That's the opinion. I'm not finished. I've got all night."

---

They had the barrow because they'd had to sell the mule at Gaunt's Reach, and they had had to sell the mule at Gaunt's Reach because the man with the brown bottles would not take anything else.

The barrow was a two-wheeled thing off a market garden, iron-framed, running on a pair of hard rubber bicycle wheels that Callie had rebuilt twice with tape and a strip cut off a boot sole. It had a yoke, which was not a yoke so much as two lengths of cargo strapping crossed over her chest and buckled behind her, and four hundred and some miles of road had worn two permanent dark lines into every shirt she owned. There were three shirts. The lines were in all of them.

Her mother could walk. That was the thing Callie kept in the front of her head like a coin she could put her thumb on. Her mother walked most of every morning and some of most afternoons, and the barrow was for the bedroll and the flour and the pot and the medicine, and only sometimes, late, for her mother.

It was for her mother more often now than it had been at Gaunt's Reach.

The medicine was in a brown glass bottle with a cork and about two fingers left in the bottom of it. The man at Gaunt's Reach had sold it as something for headaches. He had not been lying, exactly. He had been the kind of man who found the truest thing he could say and then stood behind it, and what he had said was, *This is what I've got, and it helps some people, and I don't know what's wrong with her and neither do you.*

He had also said Meridian. He had said it the way people said it up and down the whole length of the road, like a place in a story — hardened, lit, an enclave with actual doctors in it and machines that had never stopped working since before the Fall. Callie had asked him whether the doctors there were real. He had said they were real. She had asked how far, and he had gone and got a piece of slate and a nub of chalk and worked it out with her standing there, and the number he had come to was twenty-six days on foot.

That had been twelve days ago. Callie did the arithmetic every morning before she did anything else, and she did it again now, sitting on the road with one boot off, and got fourteen, and then went back and did it again more carefully and got fifteen, because of the day they'd lost at the washout.

Fifteen days. Nine pounds of flour.

She put the boot back on.

---

She found the husk line an hour before dark, which was both the best and the worst time to find one.

It crossed the road about half a mile north of where they'd stopped, and she almost walked over it, which annoyed her for the rest of the evening. It was not a track so much as a direction. A stand of dead thistle bent one way and not the other. A long shallow trough pressed through the silt where something heavy had crossed without lifting its feet all the way. Forty paces on, the rusted wire of an old field fence had been pushed flat to the ground in a run about six feet wide — not cut, not climbed, just *leaned on* until it lay down, the way a thing pushes through a fence when it has no opinion about fences.

Callie stood on the road and turned all the way around, slowly, and put it together the way her grandfather had taught her to before he died, which was to stop trying to see the thing and start trying to see the *grade*.

Husks went the way water went. That was the whole trick of them. They did not hunt across a country, they drifted through it, and they drifted in a line, and the line held for miles because nothing in them was capable of changing its mind. Find the line and you knew two things. You knew where they'd be. You also knew where they'd been, and a husk line four days old was a husk line you could walk down the middle of and sleep on.

This one was not four days old. The thistle was still bent. The silt in the trough was still dark at the bottom where the crust had broken.

And it was one trough. That was the whole of the decision, and Callie made it standing in the road with her hands in her pockets, and it took about as long as it took to say.

One husk was money. That was the arithmetic of her entire life since she was eleven, and it only ever worked in the one direction. One was money. Two was work. Four was the number that had killed her grandfather in a hayfield in the spring with a good tool in his hands and forty years of knowing better, because husks did not get cleverer when there were more of them and they did not get stronger. There were simply more of them, and it turned out that more of them was the same thing as harder, and nobody ever explained that to you in a way you believed until you were standing in it.

So you found the line, and you found the place on the line where only one of them could reach you at a time, and you did your business there, and you went home.

She went back to the barrow and got the hook.

---

The hook had come off the loading rail at the grain elevator in Thistle Ford, which had been her whole town, and which she had left in the spring and expected never to see again. It was a steel J about a foot around the curve, forged for swinging hundred-pound sacks, with a socket at the base where a wooden handle had been and a hole drilled through the socket for the pin. The wooden handle had broken in her first month on the road. She had replaced it with four feet of thin steel conduit pulled out of a wall, and a bolt through the pin hole, and a wing nut she had to keep an eye on.

Two ways to carry it. Bolt in, and it was a four-foot reach weapon she could plant and brace. Bolt out, and the hook came off in her fist alone, a hand's-breadth of curve and a cross of wrapped tape, for when there was no room to swing anything.

She had spent an evening at Gaunt's Reach sharpening the inside of the curve on a wet stone, which was the part nobody thought to do, because a hook was for pulling and not for cutting. That was the point. You did not stab a husk. Stabbing a husk was like stabbing a wall — it went in, and the wall did not care, and now your arm was inside a wall.

You opened a seam and you pulled.

Her mother watched her check the wing nut. "How far?"

"Half a mile. It's crossing north-east."

"How many?"

"One set of sign. Probably one."

"Probably."

"It's a broad line for one," Callie admitted. "But there's one trough, not three, and they don't walk in each other's steps. They can't be bothered."

Her mother thought about that. She was better at this than she let on; she had grown up in the same country Callie had, and the reason Callie knew anything at all about grade and sign was that somebody had stood behind her in a ditch and made her say what she was looking at until she got it right.

"Take the long bolt in," she said.

"I was going to."

"Take it in and *leave* it in. I've watched you get clever."

"I don't get clever."

"You got clever at the standpipe."

"That worked."

"It worked," her mother agreed, "and I've decided I'm never going to stop bringing it up."

Callie put the hook over her shoulder and went north.

---

The culvert was the thing she had been hoping for since the moment she saw the fence go down, and she'd had to keep herself from walking too fast toward it.

The road crossed a dry drainage on a low embankment, and under the embankment ran a concrete box culvert, square, wide enough for two people and low enough that Callie could touch the ceiling with a flat hand. It had silted up to about knee height at the mouth and was clear in the middle, and both ends came out into open cut ground where a husk coming down the drainage could see straight through.

The husk line crossed the drainage eighty paces upstream.

She took the mouth. She spent ten minutes on it before she made any noise at all, because ten minutes spent on the ground was worth more than anything she could do with her arms afterward. She kicked the silt shelf at the mouth into a step and packed it down with her heel so she had somewhere flat to plant her back foot. She found the two stubs of rebar standing out of the broken lip of the culvert crown and she took her coat off and she draped it over the higher one, so she'd know where it was without looking, because she had opened her own scalp on a piece of rebar at fourteen and she was not fourteen yet.

Then she went thirty paces up the drainage and hammered the flat of the hook twice against the concrete, and walked back, and stood in the mouth of the culvert with her feet where she had put them.

It came down the drainage in about a minute and a half.

She heard it first as stones. Not footsteps — there was nothing in a husk's walk that sounded like a person choosing where to put a foot — just a steady grinding forward, loose rock rolling ahead of a weight that did not go around anything. Then it came out of the dusk into the cut and she could see it, and it was one, and she let out a breath she had not been holding on purpose.

About the size of a man. That was the part nobody ever warned you about, because everybody expected bigger. Grey-brown, the colour of an old wasp nest, dried-looking, with the skin drawn tight over everything underneath so that you could see exactly how it had been put together — and it had been put together nearly right and not quite. The arms were too long. The head sat forward off the shoulders. There was nothing arranged on the front of the head at all.

It did not look at her. It had nothing to look with. It came for her the way a dropped thing goes for the floor.

She let it come to eleven paces. Then she backed three steps into the culvert mouth, put her back foot on the packed silt step, and set the butt of the conduit against the corner of the concrete where the floor met the wall.

The husk hit the mouth of the culvert and had to stoop.

That was the whole thing. That was what the last ten minutes had bought her. It came in at speed and the crown of the culvert took it across the shoulders and it had to fold down into the opening, and in folding down it lost the one thing it actually had, which was all its weight going one direction at once.

Callie put the hook in under its arm and opened it.

She did not pull across. She pulled *down and back*, dropping her hips, using her whole body the way you did with a sack, and the sharpened inside of the curve went in and found the seam of the shoulder and took it. The arm came away from the trunk with a dry tearing sound, like a board pulled off a nail, and the ash came out of the seam in a grey puff and hung there.

The husk kept coming. They always kept coming. They were not strong — that was the part it had taken her most of a season to actually believe, because it looked like it ought to be the other way around — they were only relentless, and relentless was worse, because strength runs out.

She was already moving — up off the step, out through the gap between the husk's bulk and the culvert wall, the space she had measured with her eyes before she made a sound.

Her shoulder caught her coat and pulled it off the rebar. She did not turn around for it.

Outside, on the open ground, she got the hook back on her shoulder and turned and planted, and the husk was coming out of the culvert mouth already, down on three points now, faster on three than it had been on four, which was a thing about husks she had learned the hard way in a hayfield in the spring.

"All right," she said.

It came. She stepped off the line — not back, *across*, the way you'd step out of the way of a cart — and put the hook over its back from the outside and pulled it into its own turn.

That was the seam. Not the arm, not the front. The long flat of the back, where whatever held a husk together ran thinnest, and where a thing that had just committed all of itself to a turn could not do anything at all about a weight hauling the other way.

It went over. It did not catch itself. It came apart while it was still going over, the whole grey shape of it letting go at once and from everywhere, the way a sack loses its bottom, and what had been a husk was a long smear of ash down the cut ground and a settling grey haze in the air.

Callie stood with the hook out at the end of her arms and breathed.

"Twenty-two seconds," she said to nobody, because she had counted, because she always counted, and because twenty-two was not good. Eighteen was good.

---

The ash was the whole reason.

The ash did not blow away. That was the first thing anybody learned about it and the whole reason the trade existed. It came down out of the air and off the ground and went hunting for the low places, running down the camber of the cut the way water runs, pooling in the wheel ruts and the cracks and the long seam where the old concrete apron met the dirt. Where enough of it gathered it thickened and took a faint shine, like something wet that wasn't.

You worked the seams. Callie had the tin out of her coat before the haze had finished settling — a flour tin with a screw lid, which had been a flour tin in somebody's kitchen sixty years ago — and went down the seams on her knees and lifted it with two fingers and the flat edge of her hand, unhurried, the way you'd skim cream off milk. Hurrying put grit in it. Grit was the first thing a weighmaster looked for and the first thing he'd knock you down for.

Two fingers deep in the tin when she was finished. That was a Handful, which was what a husk was and all a husk ever was, and it took about nine of them before anybody at a wall would say a number out loud. She had four saved in a screw-top jar at the bottom of the barrow under the flour.

This made five.

Ash bought things. That was all Callie really understood about it and all she needed to. Anyone at any gate anywhere would weigh it and take it, and pay in coin or flour or a night inside a wall, and the people who could actually *use* it — the ones with an Ember, or a door that had opened onto something — paid the most and asked the fewest questions. She had been lifting since she was eleven. She had never once asked what it did for them. Asking made you look like a person who didn't know what she was carrying.

She scraped the last seam clean rather than leave it, which was not greed. Ash lying in the open brought more. Everybody said so, and everybody said it the same flat way people say things they have watched happen.

She screwed the lid down, and sat back on her heels in the dark cut, and shook for about fifteen seconds, which she also always did, and which she had also stopped being embarrassed about.

Then she went and got her coat out of the culvert.

---

She was three hundred paces back down the road before she saw them.

It was the last of the light, the flat gold kind that came sideways and made everything to the west a silhouette and everything to the east unnaturally clear. And to the east, on the long spine of the old rail grade where it ran parallel to the road about half a mile off, there were people standing still.

Callie stopped walking.

Four of them, and a wagon, and two animals in the traces. They were up on the grade, which put them a good fifteen feet above the flat, which meant they had been looking down into the drainage cut the entire time.

They were not moving. That was the part that got into her chest. People on a road at dusk were doing something — making camp, watering, arguing, walking. These four were standing in a line along the edge of the grade facing her direction, and they had been standing there long enough that one of the animals had put its head down to graze.

She counted them again to be sure it was four. It was four.

She did not wave. She thought about it — she was thirteen, and she had two and a half handfuls of fresh ash in a tin, and there was a long hard list of reasons a person up on a grade might be interested in a person down on a flat.

One of the four raised an arm. Not a wave. Held it up, flat, and held it there, the way you hold something up to be seen.

Then all four of them turned and walked north along the grade, and the wagon went with them, and inside of a minute the light was gone off the rail spine and there was nothing up there at all.

Callie got back to the barrow at full dark. Her mother had a fire going, which she should not have been up doing, and had the pot on it, which she should not have been carrying.

"Well?"

"One," Callie said. She set the tin on the boards and did not sit down. "A big one."

"You're filthy."

"I know."

Her mother looked at her for a moment across the fire, and Callie let her, and kept her hands still, because her mother could read her the way she could read a ditch.

"What else," her mother said.

"There were people on the rail grade."

"How many?"

"Four."

Her mother nodded slowly and turned the pot a quarter turn on the coals with a stick, and the fire found the underside of her face, and she looked, in that light, exactly as tired as she was.

"Then we'll want the fire small," she said, "and we'll want you to eat something before you fall over, and we'll want to be off this road by first light."

"I know."

"And Callie."

"What."

"Sit down."

Callie sat down.
