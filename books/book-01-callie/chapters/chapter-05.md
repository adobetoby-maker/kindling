# Chapter Five

The answer came up the east rock before Katori gave it.

Callie heard it first. It was the grinding sound of loose stone rolling ahead of a weight, twenty feet below the lip of the causeway and a little behind her. It was not coming up toward the wagon or the gap at the wheel, and it was not heading for the long silver pools of ash along the low edge of the deck. It was coming straight up the slope toward the place where she was standing.

She turned. The long grey sweep of the hook turned with her, and it went through the wagon's rear hoop on the way round.

It did not catch. It didn't even slow down. The hoop was a bent ash-wood rib as thick as her wrist, and the grey edge went through it the way a wire goes through soft cheese. The top half of the hoop sagged into the canvas and hung there, and the whole of the back of the wagon went lopsided in the dark.

Callie stood looking at it with her mouth open.

"Down," said Katori. "Callie. It's on the rock, *down.*"

The husk came over the shoulder of the embankment on all four points with its head low. Callie did not swing, because she did not trust anything that swung any more. She stepped across its line the way she had stepped across a hundred of them and put the hook over its back from the outside.

She had forgotten how long the hook was now. The curve landed a full arm's length past where she had aimed it, down past the husk's hip, and there was nothing under it to pull against. The husk kept coming. It hit her at the chest.

It stopped against her. There was the same flat enormous note she had heard when the horse's hoof struck, only smaller, a mallet on a stump instead of a maul. The husk folded against nothing a finger's width off her coat, and she did not move at all. She simply watched it happen, like a girl looking through a window at weather.

She pulled then, short, with the part of the hook nearest her hands. The husk went back over the lip and down the rock, and it came apart before it reached the bottom. Its ash came down the slope in a grey hiss.

It was quiet again. Somewhere up the wagon Duchess was breathing like a bellows.

"It came for you," Ines said. She was standing on the crate stack with the skillet held low against her leg. "Not the wagon. It went past the wheel. It went past *me*. It came straight for you."

"I know," said Callie.

"Both," said Katori.

Callie looked at her.

"You asked me which one," said Katori. "It's both. That's your answer. I'm sorry it took me as long as it did." She lifted her right hand, the one with the blade in it. "Now watch me, because I'm going to do this once, and then I want you standing with your back to that wagon."

She did not open her fingers. The blade simply wasn't there any more. There was the flat grey slab in her palm, rounded at the corners, and she turned it over once and put it inside her coat and buttoned the coat over it.

"There," she said. "That's one of us dark. Your turn."

---

Callie could not do it.

She did not know what she was trying. That was the first problem, and it was a bigger problem than she would have guessed. She tried to let go of the hook in her head, the way you would let go of a rope. She tried to want it shorter. She shut her eyes and tried to feel for the edge of the thing closed over her and push it off like a blanket, and there was no edge to it. It was just on her, everywhere, the way cold is on you.

"I can't," she said.

"No," Katori agreed. "I didn't think you could. It was worth one try."

"Then what —"

"I'm thinking." Katori had come the rest of the way down the wagon and stopped a pace off, closer than she had been, and she was looking at Callie the way she had looked at her on the first morning, over the top of the fire, as if she was reading a snare. "Put the hook down on the deck. Don't throw it. Set it down."

Callie crouched and set it on the boards of the road, in the ash. Her fingers opened. That was something. The steel came out of her hand the way any steel did. The grey sweep did not go anywhere. It lay across the deck with the ash parting around it, four feet of dull nothing coming off the end of her grandfather's hook, and it did not look any less hers for being on the ground.

"Step back from it."

She took one step. It was fine.

She took a second step, and it was not fine. It was like the moment when you walk away from a fishing line and find the hook is still in your sleeve, except that the sleeve was somewhere inside her ribs. It pulled. It did not hurt, exactly. It was worse than hurt. It was *wrong*, the way the wrong foot in the wrong boot is wrong, and her whole body turned back toward the hook before she had decided anything.

She picked it up. The pulling stopped.

"All right," said Katori quietly. "That's something."

"What's something?"

"It's yours. You can set it down but you can't walk off from it." Katori turned her head and looked north along the causeway, into the dark. "Which is a pity, because I'd hoped to leave it on the road and let it call them there instead of here."

Callie had worked that out a second before Katori said it, and it went through her like cold water.

*They come at worked power.* She had said it herself on the sixth day, in a gully, with her voice flat and scared. A lit person on a road at night was the brightest thing in the country, and everything that hunted came at it. Katori had put her own light away inside her coat. Callie could not put hers anywhere. She was standing in the middle of two miles of causeway, over a pan that drained the whole country, and she was lit at both ends.

"It's me," she said. "They're coming at me."

"Yes."

"You should go on without me. Take the wagon and go on, and I'll come behind when —"

"Don't finish that," said Katori, "because the next word is going to be stupid, and I'll have to answer it, and we haven't got the time for either of us." She said it without heat. "Here is what we've got. We've got a wagon hanging off a rail stub by one wheel. We've got a dead mule on the end of a trace, pulling a horse over a cliff. We've got a man with his arm open and a woman with no arrows and your mother on the bed. And we've got you, and anything that comes up that rock is going to come at you, and nothing that comes at you can hurt you. I watched three of them try." She looked back at Callie. "So you're not a problem. You're a job. Get your back to the wagon and let me think."

---

Pell had not let go of the horse.

He was up at the front with both hands in her cheekpieces, and he was still talking in the same low, flat voice. He had been talking for so long that it had stopped being words and become something more like weather. Duchess stood splay-legged against the pole with her head high and her eyes showing white all the way round. Every few seconds the dead trace on the far side jerked, as the weight at the bottom of it settled in the rocks. Every time it jerked, it pulled the pole a finger's width toward the edge, and every time the pole moved, the horse screamed.

"Somebody," said Pell, in the same voice, without looking round, "cut that trace."

Otto went for it. He had his knife out in his good hand, and his other arm was wrapped to the elbow in something that had been Ines's scarf and was now black. He got down on his knees at the front of the wagon and reached in under the pole toward the singletree on the far side, where the trace was hooked. Then he stopped, because the whole thing was bar-tight and humming. Anything that cut it close would take the hand that cut it.

"It'll whip," he said. "If I cut it here it'll whip."

"Then don't cut it there."

"Where else is there?"

Callie was already moving.

She did not go near the horse. She went along the near side of the wagon to the lip of the causeway, a good eight paces short of the pole, and looked down. The trace ran over the lip in a long straight line and down the rock into the dark, and she could not see what was at the bottom of it and she did not try to.

She could reach it from here. She could not have reached it with her own arms, or with four feet of conduit, but she could reach it with this.

She put the grey sweep out over the edge, flat, and laid it against the trace a yard down the rock, where there was nothing on the far side of the line but stone.

She didn't even have to pull. The trace parted like a hair.

The end of it went down the rock with a hiss, and whatever was at the bottom of it went on being at the bottom. Up at the front, all at once, there was nothing holding Duchess to the edge of the world.

She went forward. Of course she did. She went the only way that was open to her, a hard plunging lunge against Pell's hands. The pole swung with her. The wagon swung with the pole. The off rear wheel, which had been hung on the rail stub all this time, came up off the stub and dropped, and it did not drop onto the deck. It dropped outboard, over the lip, into air.

The whole back corner of the wagon went down a foot, with a crack like a split log. The bed tilted. The crates groaned against the net. From the bed, her mother made a small sound.

Callie did not think about it. She didn't have time to think about it. She was already between the corner of the wagon and the edge, because that was where she had been standing when she cut the trace. She put her shoulder into the corner of the tailboard and her boots into the road.

The wagon came onto her. It came onto her the way the kick had and the way the husks had. She felt none of the weight, only a kind of pressure, the way you feel the wind when you are standing inside a house. She saw the tailboard stop a finger off her coat. She saw the axle stop. The wagon had been going over, and it stopped going over, and it stayed stopped, hanging off its corner at a slant against a thirteen-year-old girl, with her arms not even braced.

Nobody moved.

"Callie," said Katori, very quietly.

"It's all right. It's — it's fine. It isn't heavy."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"Otto," said Katori, in exactly the same voice. "Pole."

---

It took them four minutes, and they were the longest four minutes Callie had ever stood through, because she had nothing at all to do in them except stand.

Otto got the wagon pole off its hounds with Ines hauling on the far end. He laid it under the rear axle from the road side, with a sawn-off rail stub for a fulcrum. Then he threw his whole weight on the end of it, one-armed, with a noise like a man lifting a house. Katori was beside him with both hands on the pole. Ines had her whole body across the top of it. The wheel came up out of the air an inch at a time and scraped back over the lip onto the deck.

As it came, the weight went off Callie by pieces. She did not feel it go. She only saw the tailboard draw back from her an inch at a time, until there was road under all four wheels again and nothing on her shoulder at all.

She discovered that she was shaking. It was not the fifteen seconds of shaking she did after every husk. This was different, and deeper, and it came from further in. Her hands were cold. They had been cold for a while and she had not noticed. They were cold the way the hook was cold, like the inside of a well, and the cold had got as far as her elbows.

Otto sat down on the road with the pole across his knees and his bad arm held against his chest.

"Sorry," he said to nobody. "About before. About being sick."

"Don't," said Ines. She sat down beside him and took the arm in both her hands and unwrapped the scarf, and looked at it in the light from Katori's lamp, and wrapped it up again, tighter. "Everybody's sick the first time."

"You weren't."

"I was sick in a bucket in my mother's kitchen when I was nineteen, and I was sick again in the Warren the first time I saw a hound open a man, and I'll probably be sick tomorrow when I've had time to think about tonight." She knotted the scarf. "Nobody gets to be embarrassed on this road tonight. I've decided."

"All right," said Otto, and his voice went up at the end, and he put his head down on his knees.

Ines stood up and took the lamp off its hook under the board and went off down the causeway with it. She did not explain. Callie watched the light go along the deck at a slow walk, low, swinging left and right. Then she understood. Ines was walking back along the length of the fight, looking for arrows.

Every husk she had shot had come apart where it fell, and an arrow doesn't come apart. She was looking for them in the ash.

---

Callie went to the back of the wagon, where her mother was.

She did not get up on the tailgate. She stood below it with the hook held down and away from her, pointed along the road, because she had just watched it go through an ash-wood hoop as if the hoop wasn't there. Her mother lay on the bedding against the restacked crates with her face turned toward her, and in the thin spill of light from the lamp going away down the causeway, Callie could see that her eyes were open.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Well," said her mother. "That's new."

Callie laughed. It came out of her like a cough, and it hurt her all the way down, and it did not stop for longer than she wanted. When it did stop she found her face was wet, and she could not wipe it, because her hand was cold and there was a thing closed over her face as well as the rest of her, and her knuckles stopped a finger short of her own cheek.

She stood there with her hand up and could not touch her own face.

"Does it hurt?" said her mother.

"No."

"Tell me the truth."

"It doesn't hurt. It's cold. It's — " Callie looked for the word and there wasn't one. "It's like wearing something. I can't take it off. Either of them. And they keep coming, because of me. Because I'm lit."

Her mother was quiet for a moment.

"When you were four," she said, "you got your head stuck in the rails at the top of the grain elevator stair. Do you remember?"

"No."

"You wouldn't. It took your grandfather and two men from the scale house forty minutes and a pot of lard to get you out, and the whole time, you were furious. Not frightened. *Furious.* You kept saying you could get out if everyone would stop helping." Her mother's left hand moved on the blanket. "You've got that face on now. I just wanted you to know I can see it."

"I haven't got a face."

"You have exactly that face." She let out a breath. "Go and do what Katori tells you. We'll talk about it when we're off this road. I'm not going anywhere."

That was the kind of thing her mother said, and it was the kind of thing she would say whether it was true or not. Callie went anyway.

---

Ines came back with four arrows.

She held them up in the lamplight as she came, and she was not smiling, but something in her walk was different. Two of them were whole. One had lost half its fletching and one had a crack running up the shaft from the head, and she held that one a little apart from the others, as if she was already grieving it.

"Four," she said. "There are two on the west rock and I can see where they are, and I'm not going down there in the dark. The rest are gone."

"Four's something," said Katori.

"Four's four," said Ines. She put them in the quiver one at a time and did not look at Callie. "It's worse than nine and better than none. I've done the sum."

Katori stood at the front of the wagon with the lamp in her hand and looked north, the way she had been looking north all along, and then back south, the way they had come.

"Lift it?" she said.

Callie knew what she meant. Everybody did. There was ash lying in every seam of the causeway for forty paces in both directions. It was running silver down the camber, pooled against the rail stubs, and gathered in the ruts the wheels had made. It was more ash than Callie had lifted in her whole life put together, and she had lifted since she was eleven. Pell had said it that afternoon without any weight on it at all: *Meridian has columns.* This was a number that would fill a column.

And every bit of it lying in the open was calling more.

"No," said Callie.

Katori turned her head.

"No," she said again, and she heard her grandfather in it and did not mind. "Ash calls them to where it is. It calls them *here*, to this bit of road, and it doesn't care whether we're standing on it. If we lift it, we're here for an hour, on our knees, with a lamp, while it calls, and while I call. If we leave it, it keeps calling here, and we're somewhere else." She swallowed. "Leave it. Come back for it in the light."

Up at the front, Pell had finally stopped talking. He stood with one hand flat on the horse's neck and turned and looked at Callie over the top of her, and the lamplight made him look about a hundred years old.

"Somebody write that down for me," he said. "My hands aren't mine."

"I'll remember it," said Katori.

"That's not the same thing."

"No," said Katori. "It isn't. Callie. The back."

---

They could not put Duchess anywhere near her.

They tried once. Pell said the horse would have to go single, in the near traces, with the pole lashed across to keep it from swinging, and the only way to do it was to back her up a pace to get the lash on. Callie was standing at the near wheel with the hook held low. Duchess saw her and went straight up.

It was not a kick this time. It was just up, all four hundred kilos of her, with her forefeet pawing the dark and Pell hanging off her head. She came down sideways, still screaming, with her whole body bent away from the girl standing at the wheel.

"Back," said Pell. "Back — Callie, *get back* — "

Callie got back. She went twenty paces south down the causeway into the dark before the horse would stand. Even from there she could see Duchess's head in the lamplight, turned round and pointed at her, not moving, the way a dog points at something in a hedge.

"She kicked a wall," Pell said, from up at the front. He had got his voice back down where he kept it. "That's all. She kicked something as hard as she has ever kicked anything, and it was a wall, and it wasn't there, and it was a girl. And now she knows where it's standing. She's not going to forget that before morning, or maybe in her life." He paused. "That's not a complaint. It's a specification."

"I'll stay back here," said Callie.

"You'll stay back there," Pell agreed. "And I'll thank you to stay back there all the way over, and not come up, whatever you hear."

So that was where she walked.

The wagon went north at a crawl, with one horse on the pole and Otto walking at her head where the mule should have been, one-armed, his good hand on the cheekpiece. Pell sat on the board with the reins. Ines was up on the crates with four arrows and the skillet. Katori walked at the tailgate with the lamp, halfway between the wagon and Callie, and the lamp made a little moving room of light on the road that Callie could see but never quite get into.

Callie walked behind all of it, in the dark, twenty paces back, with the hook.

---

They came at her the whole way.

Not many, and not together. That was the thing she understood after the first half mile, and she understood it the way she understood a line through a field, by watching what it did and not what it was. They came up out of the pan one at a time, from the east rock or the west, from wherever they had been drifting when they felt her. She could hear each one coming for twenty or thirty seconds before it arrived, because a husk on riprap sounds like a barrow of stones being tipped slowly. Every one of them came up the slope straight at the place where she was.

None of them went for the wagon. She watched for it, the whole way. Not once did one of them turn toward the lamp and the horse and the people, which were right there, twenty paces up the road. They came up the rock at her, and they reached the lip, and she tipped them.

She had to learn the hook again to do it.

The grey part was too long and too sharp. The first time she put it over a husk's shoulder, she took the arm off clean before she had pulled at all. The arm went over the edge by itself and the rest of the husk kept coming, and it hit her, and stopped against her. She had to take a step back and try again. The second time she used the steel end only, the old curve her grandfather had forged, with the grey sweep held out past it and over the husk's back like something she was being careful not to touch. That time it worked. She got the old curve under the arm, and leaned, and the husk went back down the rock the way it had come, all its weight committed to going up and nothing left to stop it going down.

After that she did it the same way every time. She got the old part in and kept the new part out of the way, and she leaned.

She counted them. Of course she counted them.

At seven, she noticed that the cold had reached her shoulders.

At nine, she noticed that she was hungry. It was not ordinary hunger. It was a scooped-out feeling under the ribs, like the day at the washout when they had walked eleven hours on half a biscuit. It had come on fast.

At eleven, she stumbled on nothing. It was a flat place on the deck. She went down on one knee. The thing closed over her took the fall, and she did not feel the road at all, only the same pressure she had felt from the wagon, and she knelt there for a moment in the dark with the hook across her thighs, listening to her own breathing.

"Callie?" Katori's lamp had stopped.

"I'm fine."

"You went down."

"I tripped. I'm fine. It's — " She got up. It took longer than getting up should have taken. "I'm hungry. That's all. It's making me hungry."

The lamp stayed where it was for a moment. Then Katori came back down the road with it. She did not come the whole way. She stopped five or six paces off and held the lamp up and looked at Callie's face, and whatever she saw there, her own face did not change.

"How cold?" she said.

Callie stared at her.

"You heard me."

"Shoulders," Callie said. "It started in my hands. It's in my shoulders."

Katori nodded slowly. She looked for a long moment like a woman doing arithmetic she did not like the answer to.

"That's what it costs," she said. "It isn't free. Nothing that comes through a door is free, and you're holding two of them open, all at once, and you've been doing it for two hours. It's coming out of you. That's the cold. That's the hunger." She looked north up the causeway. "It's a mile to the far side. Maybe a little more."

"I can do a mile."

"I know you can do a mile. I'm not worried about the mile." Katori turned the lamp down a little, which Callie had never seen anybody do in the middle of a road at night. "I'm worried about what comes after it, when you still can't put either of them down, and you have to go to sleep."

Callie had not thought of that. She had not thought of that at all.

From up the road, out of the dark past the wagon, Pell's voice came back to them, flat and careful.

"Katori."

"What?"

"The span," said Pell. "We're at the span. And she won't put a foot on it."
