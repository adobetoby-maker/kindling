# Chapter 3 — Nineteen Days

"Nineteen days," said Pell.

Callie had been carrying fifteen in her head for a day and a half, the way you carry a stone in a boot you've decided not to stop and empty, and the number went into her like cold water.

"That's wrong."

"It's not wrong, it's just not yours." Pell had the atlas open on the board beside him with the pencil laid in the crease, and he did not look up from the mules. "Yours is fifteen and yours is a girl and a barrow doing eleven miles a day and never stopping for anything, and I don't doubt it for a second. Mine is nine hundred pounds of freight on a wagon with one spare wheel. Nineteen days. That's if the causeway's standing where you say it's standing, and if neither of these two goes lame on me, and if it doesn't rain the way it rained on us in the Warren." He clicked his tongue at the mule. "Three ifs. I price an if at a day. Call it twenty-two and be happy with me if I'm wrong."

"That's four ifs."

"It's three."

"You didn't count the horse."

Pell turned his head and looked at her for a long moment with something that was almost warmth in it.

"No," he said. "I don't count the horse. I've been not counting the horse for nine hundred miles and I'd thank you not to make me start."

Callie walked beside the wheel for a while and did not say anything.

She was doing the other arithmetic, the one she had been refusing to do since yesterday morning, and it came out the same however she started it. Four days. Four days more than she'd have made alone, and on the other side of the ledger her mother was lying on the boards behind her with her arm across her eyes instead of walking eleven miles on a leg that had started doing a thing Callie did not have a name for.

Four days of the road, bought with four days of her mother.

"Say it," said Pell.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing since I opened my mouth. You've a face like a weighmaster."

"It's nothing."

"Then it's nothing, and I'll shut up, and you can go on carrying it." He flicked the reins. "I'd think about it, though. There's a thing that happens to people who do all their sums privately, and it's that they start getting them wrong and there's nobody there to catch it."

---

She got used to walking out front faster than she expected to.

The job was not hard, which surprised her, because she had expected a job to feel like work. The job was: go half a mile ahead and look at things, and come back, and tell four people what she had seen in the order that mattered to them. By the third day she had learned what each of them actually wanted out of her, which was four completely different things.

Pell wanted surfaces. Grade, camber, washouts, whether a culvert head had dropped, whether a ford bottom was gravel or silt. He wanted it in numbers where numbers existed and he wanted her to say *I don't know* instead of guessing, and the first time she said *I don't know* he said "Good," and wrote something down, and she spent an hour trying to work out what he'd written.

Ines wanted ranges. Ines had never once asked her what a thing was. Ines asked how far, and then said a number back, and if Callie's number and Ines's number disagreed they walked it out and counted, and Ines was right four times out of five and said so without any pleasure in it at all.

Otto wanted stories. Otto wanted to know what was in the buildings.

"There's nothing in the buildings, Otto."

"There's always something in a building."

"There's been nothing in a building for sixty years. People went through all of them twice before I was born."

"Then why do you look in them?"

Callie opened her mouth and discovered that she did not have an answer, and that she did in fact look in all of them, every single one, and always had.

"See," said Otto, enormously satisfied.

And Katori wanted the thing that was wrong. That was all. Katori never asked a question that had a fact in it. Katori would fall in beside her at the head of the traces and walk for ten minutes and then say, "Anything?" and Callie would say the crows had gone off the north end of the cut, or there was a stretch of fence four hundred paces long that had been pushed down flat and it was not old, and Katori would say "Mm," and turn around and go back and change something about the way the party was walking, without ever once explaining what or why.

The fourth time this happened Callie ran out of patience.

"You could tell me what you do with it."

"I could."

"Then do."

Katori considered this for about eight paces. "You told me there was a line of pushed fence running north-east and it wasn't old. So Ines is on the east side of the wagon now instead of the west, and Otto's got the pole out of the bed instead of lashed under it, and we're not stopping at the water you and I both saw an hour ago, we're going on to the next one and we'll be thirsty." She glanced across. "That's what I do with it. Every time. All day. That's the entire job and there's nothing clever in it, and if you're waiting for the part where I tell you something you don't already know, you'll be waiting a while."

"That's not what I was waiting for."

"No," Katori agreed. "You were waiting to find out if I was actually listening."

Callie did not answer that, which was its own answer, and they walked the rest of the mile without either of them saying anything else about it.

---

On the sixth day the line crossed the road in front of them, and they sat down and let it.

Callie found it at midmorning — the trough through the silt, the leaned thistle, the whole familiar grammar of it, but running six or seven abreast this time instead of one, the ground churned across a width she could have laid the wagon into sideways. She stood and looked at it for a long moment. Then she went back down the road at a run.

"How many?" said Katori.

"I don't know. Twenty. It could be forty. I can't read it past about six, nobody can."

"Fresh?"

"This morning."

"Going where?"

"East and a little north. Across us."

Katori turned her head. "Pell. Off the road, into the scrub, south side, and get the canvas down over the hoops so we're not a shape." And then, to Callie, in exactly the same voice: "Where do we sit?"

Callie's mouth went dry, because it was not a test and that was somehow worse. The woman actually wanted to be told.

"Not the verge," she said. "The verge is where they lie up when they stop. Road bed's flat and open and we'd be a shape on it. There's a stand of scrub willow about two hundred paces back on the south side with a dry gully behind it. It's low and it's downwind and there's one way in." She swallowed. "And nobody makes any noise, and nobody makes a fire, and — "

She stopped.

"Go on," said Katori.

"And you don't use your Ember."

Something in Katori's face changed very slightly and did not become a smile.

"Say why."

"Because they come at worked power." Callie heard her own voice come out flat and certain and slightly frightened of itself. "That's — everybody knows that. A man at Gaunt's Reach told me a lit-up man on a road at night is the brightest thing in this country and everything that hunts comes at it, and I don't know if that's true, but he was the kind of man who'd have been there."

"It's true," said Katori.

"Then why did you open the crate with it?"

"Because it was the middle of the morning on an open road with three hundred paces of sightline in every direction and nothing on it, and I wanted you to see it once, on purpose, in the safest conditions I was going to get." Katori pulled her cap down. "It cost me something to do that and I did it anyway. Work out what I bought."

She went back to help Pell with the canvas.

---

They sat in the gully for five and a half hours.

Nothing came. That was the whole event and Callie knew it was the whole event by the second hour and still could not make her shoulders come down. Twice something went through the scrub off to the north-east, unhurried, not near. Once there was a long dry sound like somebody prying a board off a nail, very far away, and Otto's hand went around the wagon pole and stayed there, and Ines said "Eight hundred," to nobody, and the sound did not come again.

Callie sat with her back against the wagon wheel and her mother's boots against her hip and the hook across her knees, and watched Ines not move.

That was the thing about Ines she had not expected. Ines talked like a closing door and she sat like a stone. Five hours and she did not shift, did not scratch, did not sigh. She had an arrow on the string, not drawn, and her eyes went left to right along the top of the gully on a slow count and came back and did it again, and the whole time her face said nothing whatever.

"How many have you got?" Callie whispered, eventually.

"Nine."

"That's not many."

"It's nine more than anyone else has, and the man who made them is four hundred miles behind us and dead." Ines's eyes did not stop moving. "There's a reason I tell you the range. It's not to be clever. It's so that when I say a number and Katori says take it, I've already spent two seconds doing the only thing I'm actually good at, which is knowing whether I get the arrow back."

Callie thought about that.

"Did you ever have a door?" she said.

"No."

"Did it open onto nothing, or did it —"

"It opened onto nothing," said Ines. "I sat in my mother's kitchen and they said the words over me and nothing happened, and I waited two years to see if it was slow, and it wasn't slow, it was nothing." Left to right, and back. "I'm thirty-one and I can put an arrow into a hound at ninety paces in bad light. You can decide for yourself how much of a tragedy that is. Most people decide for me."

---

They came out of the gully at dusk and went on for two more hours in the dark to get clear of the line, which Callie had advised and then spent the whole two hours regretting.

Nothing came then either.

That night she had the middle watch, and when she went to wake Katori for the last one she found her already sitting up on the tailgate in the dark with her boots on, awake, which Callie was fairly sure meant she had not been asleep.

"Go on," said Katori. "I've got it."

Callie did not go on.

She stood at the tailgate with the hook in her hand and the cold coming up through her boots, and the words she had not asked on the first afternoon came up out of her without any permission at all.

"What's it like?"

Katori was quiet long enough that Callie thought she wasn't going to answer.

"That's the wrong question," she said, "and everybody asks it, so I'll answer the one you mean instead. You're thirteen and yours hasn't happened and you want to know what's coming."

"...Yes."

"Nothing's coming."

Callie's stomach dropped.

"That's not a threat and it's not a warning," Katori said. "It's the actual answer and it's the one nobody gives you, so listen. Mine happened in a room with eleven of my own family in it and somebody's grandmother saying words over the top of my head. I was thirteen and four months. I sat down and I stood up and the only thing that was different about me was that everybody in the room had decided to look at me for an hour." She shifted on the tailgate. "It's a door. One door. It opens somewhere behind you and you can't see it and mostly you can't feel it, and it's about as dramatic as a lamp being lit in a house on the other side of a hill. And then it's nine months before your Ember so much as gets warm, and then it's eleven years, and at the end of the eleven years you have one shape, and the shape is not the one you wanted."

"One door," Callie said.

"One door."

"For everybody?"

"For everybody." Katori said it the way Pell said a distance — as a thing so flatly true it was almost boring. "That's what it is. Ember, or the hand tradition, or the ones who sit on a mountain for forty years and get so they can't be knocked over. Three roads and everybody walks one, and which one you get is an accident of where your grandmother was standing. You get the one. You spend your life on the one." She looked up at the sky. "People make up a great deal of nonsense about it because one door isn't very much and they'd like it to be more."

Callie stood there holding her hook.

"What if nothing opens at all?"

"Then nothing opens at all," said Katori, "and you go and ask Ines about it, and she'll tell you the truth, and it'll be the truth, and it won't make you feel any better." She jerked her chin. "Bed. You've walked twelve miles and you're going to walk twelve more."

Callie went three steps and stopped.

"There weren't any words said over me," she said. "There wasn't anybody left in Thistle Ford to say them and my mother couldn't — " She stopped. "There weren't any words."

"I heard you the first time," said Katori.

"Does it matter?"

From the other side of the fire, out of the dark, without opening his eyes, Pell said: "No."

Callie turned around.

"I'm sixty-one," Pell said, to the underside of the wagon. "The whole business is about as old as I am. When it came for me there was nobody to say anything over me because nobody had *thought* of anything to say yet — we were all thirteen and none of our parents had ever heard of it and half the country thought we'd caught something. The words came later. Somebody's grandmother invented them about thirty-five years ago and now people say them slowly and think they're old." He rolled over. "The words are for the people in the room. The door does what it does. It has never once in sixty years asked anybody's permission and it is not going to start with you. Go to sleep."

---

Her mother went down on the ninth day, about four in the afternoon, getting off the wagon.

It was not a fall so much as the leg simply declining. She had one hand on the tailgate and she swung down the way she had swung down a hundred times, and the right leg took the weight and then did not take the weight, and she went sideways into the road and sat there hard with her hat in the dust.

Callie was forty paces ahead and she did not remember crossing them.

"I'm fine."

"You're on the ground."

"I'm sitting. It's a choice I've made." Her mother was already reaching for the hat. "The strap on that boot's gone and I've been telling you about that boot for a week."

"You haven't said anything about the boot."

"Then I've been thinking it very loudly."

And then Otto was there, and he did not ask, and he did not make any kind of face about it, he simply crouched and got an arm under her and stood up with her the way he had stood up with the barrow, and said, "There's a crate digging in, I'll shift it," and carried her to the wagon.

And her mother let him.

That was the thing. Callie stood in the road with her hook in her hand and watched her mother be carried eleven paces by a boy she had known for nine days, and not fight it, and not say one single funny thing about it, and that was the moment the cold got all the way into her.

---

She woke that night because the wagon moved.

It was a small movement and it took her a while to understand that somebody was shifting their weight up on the bed. She lay still. Then she got up out of the bedroll and came around the tailgate in her socks.

Her mother was sitting up in the dark with her back against the crates and her hair down, and she had her fingers in it, and she was trying to braid it.

She was trying with both hands and her right hand was not doing what she was telling it to. She would get two passes and the right hand would let go of its strand, and she would start again, and get two passes, and the right hand would let go. She did it four times while Callie stood there. She was not crying and she was not swearing. She was working at it with enormous patience, in the dark, at three in the morning, where nobody would see her fail at it.

Callie got up on the tailgate.

"Let me."

Her mother went very still for a second. Then she took her hands down and put them in her lap and said, "All right."

Callie knelt behind her and braided her hair. It took a long time, because she was not good at it either and because her hands were not steady, and neither of them said anything at all for the whole of it. Out past the wagon the country was flat and black and perfectly quiet, the way it got when there was nothing on it.

"There," Callie said.

"Thank you."

"I'll do it in the mornings."

"You will not."

"I'll do it in the mornings."

Her mother put her hand back and found Callie's wrist and held it, hard, for about three seconds, which was as long as either of them could stand.

"Pass me the kettle," she said.

Callie looked at the cup sitting on the crate beside her mother's knee. She picked it up, and she put it into her mother's left hand, and closed the fingers on it.

"Thank you, love," her mother said, and drank, and did not notice, and there was nothing at all in her face to show that anything had gone past her.

Callie sat on the tailgate in the dark and made herself breathe out slowly, the way you do at the mouth of a culvert.

---

She did not get back into the bedroll.

She sat with her back against the wheel until the sky went the colour of dishwater, and some time in the last of the dark she became aware of Katori moving quietly at the front of the wagon.

She was unloading it.

She had the net off and she was taking the crates down one at a time onto the road, by herself, without waking anybody, and restacking them two high along the near side instead of three deep across the bed. It took her most of an hour. It made the whole front third of the wagon flat and open, and it was going to ride worse, and Pell was going to have something to say about the weight being all on one side.

Callie watched her do it and did not offer to help, because she understood, in a way she could not have said out loud, that she was not supposed to have seen it.

When Katori finished she stood for a moment with her hands on the tailgate, looking at nothing, with her head down. And she said a name.

It was one word and it was not anybody's. It was not Pell and it was not Ines and it was not Otto and it was not Callie and it was not her mother's. Callie did not catch the sound of it well enough to keep, and knew immediately that she was never going to hear it again, and knew that she must not ask.

Then Katori picked up the lamp, and turned, and saw her sitting against the wheel.

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"Nineteen days," Katori said.

"Pell says twenty-two."

"Pell prices ifs. That's Pell's business and he's good at it." She hung the lamp on the hook under the board. "Go and get your boots on. We're leaving early."
