# Chapter Eleven

The line came up off the rock and went tight, and the weight came onto the hook.

Callie had wondered for a whole day what it would feel like. She had imagined the wagon hanging off the hook the way a sack hangs off a rail. Nine hundred pounds of crates and a wagon and a man, all of it pulling at the forged curve in the crack in the stone, and her with her hand on it. She had thought it would feel like carrying.

It did not feel like anything. It was the same as the kick on the causeway and the wagon's corner coming down on her shoulder. There was a pressure, very far away, like wind on the side of a house, and nothing else. The block creaked on the curve. The line went bar-tight down the pitch to the wagon and bar-tight up over the lip to the horse. The hook stood in the seam, pale, and did not so much as tick against the stone.

Below her the wagon began to come up.

She could see it from where she knelt. Forty paces down the pitch, the front of it lifted over a step in the rock and came on, slowly, a hand at a time. The bottom block was on the pole hounds. Pell sat on the board with his hand on the brake lever and his face turned up the hill toward her. Behind the wagon, on each rear wheel, a wooden chock bumped along on a length of rope, the old way. If the wagon ran back, the chocks would jam under the wheels by themselves. That was the idea. On soft road it would have worked. On bare rock Callie had watched Pell test them and she had seen them skid.

"Five," called Ines from the rim, high up and to the left. Her voice came down flat and clear. "Six. Seven."

The wagon came on. Over the lip, out of sight, Otto was talking to the horse, low and steady, a word to each step. The fall line ran up over the edge of the road and away across the top, and she could hear it creaking as it paid out.

"Ten. Eleven."

The cold was in her hand. It was in her wrist.

Callie watched the hook.

She could not help it. The whole wagon was on it, and Pell, and the load that was going to buy a table at Meridian. She knelt there with her palm flat on the back of the forged curve, and watched the pale in the steel the way she had watched her mother breathe every night since Gaunt's Reach. And as she watched, her fingers closed round the curve. She did not tell them to. They closed, the way they had always closed on anything that mattered, and held on.

"Fifteen. Sixteen."

The cold went up past her elbow like water up a pump.

It was too fast. She felt it. It was the two-bucket drawing she had felt under the wagon at the lay-by with the conduit, not the slow single draw of the afternoon in the crack in the boulder. The pale at the point of the hook began to shiver.

"Nineteen."

She knew what she was doing. She knew it the way she had known on the span that she was leaning. She was gripping. She was holding it. And she had spent a whole afternoon learning that the harder she held it, the faster it went, and she was doing it anyway. Her hands did not know how to do anything else with a thing that mattered.

She tried to open her fingers. They would not open. The whole wagon was on this.

"Twenty-three. Twenty-four."

The pale went thin along the curve like frost going off a window in the sun.

"*Chock!*" Callie shouted. "Pell — *chock* — "

Duchess stopped at the top. The line went slack for half a breath, and then tight again, backward, as the wagon took its own weight.

Pell hauled the brake. It shrieked on the tyre, and the wagon went back. It went back a foot, a foot and a half, grinding on the rock with the chocks skidding under the rear wheels. Then the off chock found a step in the rock and jammed, and the near one jammed behind it, and the wagon stopped.

Callie took her hand off the hook, and the pale went out of it, and the block swung on the curve with a clank.

"Twenty-six," said Ines, from the rim.

---

Callie knelt in the road with her head down and her arm dead to the shoulder. She could not feel her fingers at all.

The wagon was twelve paces up the pitch. Not twenty. Twelve.

"Well," said Pell from below, very calmly, into the silence. "That's a third pull, then."

Nobody answered him.

Katori had not moved from the lip. She stood with her back to Callie and her face to the high plain, just as Callie had told her. "Quiet up here," she called down, without turning round. "Nothing on the road for half a mile. Take your time."

Callie got the tin out of her coat. It was the last of her tenth that she had brought up the hill, and she tipped two fingers of it into her mouth, dry, because the cup was in the wagon. It tasted of nothing and stuck to her tongue. She made herself swallow it. She felt it go down into the well like rain onto hard ground.

It did not go far. She had used too much yesterday, and she had used too much just now. When she went down into herself, there was water at the bottom of the well, but not much of it.

"Callie." It was her mother's voice, from high up on the rim. It was not loud. The air in the cut was very still and it carried. "You were gripping it."

"I know."

"I could see it from here. Your whole arm went white."

"I *know*." Callie shut her eyes. "I can't stop. It's the wagon. It's Pell. I can't not hold on to it."

Her mother was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in exactly the voice she had used on a hundred nights on the road, when Callie could not get the fire to catch:

"Then don't look at it."

Callie opened her eyes.

"You said yesterday it held when you weren't looking at it," her mother said. "When you turned round and talked to Otto about the strap. I heard you tell Katori. So don't look at it. Look at me."

Callie looked up.

Her mother was sitting on the rim of the cut, high above the pitch, on the red earth at the edge of the rock, with her hat pulled down and her good hand flat on the ground beside her. Ines was standing beside her with an arrow on the string. From down here her mother was very small. She was sitting up straight, in the sun, on her borrowed day, looking down at her.

"There," said her mother. "Look at me. Let the hook do what it's going to do."

---

The second pull was different from the first the moment it began.

Callie put her hand flat on the back of the hook. She set her back foot, and dropped her weight, and braced through the steel, into the rock, and down. The pale went into the curve, steady.

Then she took her hand away.

She did it before she could think about it. She did it the way you take your hand off a baby's back once you're sure it's asleep, slowly, not quite believing. And she stood up in the road beside the hook with her hands empty at her sides. She kept her feet planted, and she turned her head, and looked up at her mother on the rim.

She did not look at the hook.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done. It was harder than the causeway or the span, or walking out of the pump house doorway. Every part of her wanted to look down and see if it was still pale. She didn't. She looked at her mother's hat, and the red earth, and the edge of Ines's bow against the sky.

"Pull," she said.

The line came tight. The weight came on.

She felt it come on, far away, like wind on a house. She felt the hook take it. She felt it the way she felt her own tongue, the way she'd felt the Edge on the causeway, a pace away from her and hers. It was not in her hands. It was not in her body at all. It was in the steel. The steel was standing in the rock the way her back foot stood in the road, and all she had to do was not argue with it.

The cold came up her arm. It came slowly this time, with one bucket, not two.

"Five," said Ines. "Six."

Below her the wagon came on. She could hear it grinding over the rock and did not look.

"Twelve. Thirteen."

"You're doing it," said her mother from the rim. "It's pale. It's steady. Keep looking at me."

"Twenty."

The wagon was past where it had been. She knew without looking. The grinding sound was nearer.

"Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine."

"Callie," said her mother.

It was a different voice. It was the voice from the causeway, the one that had come across all that noise from the wagon bed with no strength left in it to be loud with, and had said her name like somebody seeing the shape of the next four seconds.

"The top," said her mother. "The road. Past the horse. Three of them."

---

Callie heard Katori's Ember come out.

There was no sound to it. There never was. But Katori had been standing at the lip with her back to the hill and her hands empty. Now there was the grey blade in her right hand, the length of a forearm, and she was walking forward up the road, away from the lip, out onto the high plain toward whatever was coming.

She was lighting herself. Callie understood it with a lurch. Katori was making herself the second-brightest thing in the country, on purpose, fifty paces nearer to the husks than Callie was.

"Duchess has seen them," said her mother, very clearly. "She's stopping."

The line went dead.

Not slack. Dead. The mare had stopped at the top with her forefeet planted, and the fall went still, and the whole weight of the wagon came back onto the block and the hook. Callie felt it arrive. The pressure did not get bigger. It got nearer, the way wind gets nearer when a door blows open. The wagon hung on the hook twenty paces down the pitch, with Pell on the brake. It was not coming up and it was not going down, and the chocks were skittering on the bare rock under the wheels.

"Thirty-four," said Ines. Then, in the same breath, in a completely different voice: "*Forty paces. Taking it.*"

The bow went. Callie did not look.

"Otto," said Pell, from the wagon. His voice came up the pitch as level as it had come across the causeway. "Otto, lad. Listen to me. She's going to leave. She's four hundred kilos of leaving and nothing in this world will stop her. You can't hold her. Don't try. Just point her."

There was a noise from the top that was Duchess, a high, terrible, ringing noise. And there was Otto's voice under it. He was not talking any more. He was shouting, one word, the same one, over and over.

"*Up* — *up* — *UP* — "

The line went tight so hard that the block rang on the hook like a bell.

The wagon came up the pitch at a speed that no wagon had any business going up a pitch at. Callie heard it come. The wheels were grinding on the rock and the chocks were bouncing on their ropes, and Pell was saying "Easy, easy, easy," to nobody, with his hands off the brake. At the top, the mare was leaving. She was leaving as hard as a horse can leave, straight up the road away from the things coming down it, because Otto had got her head round and pointed it away from them, and let her go.

"Forty-five," said Ines. "Forty-six."

Callie did not look at the hook.

She looked instead, at last, up the road to the lip, because something had come over it.

---

It was one. Katori had one of the others. Callie could hear her fifty paces out on the high plain, working. Ines had put the second down on the road with two arrows. This was the third. It had come past all of it, straight past the lit woman with the blade, straight past the horse and the boy. It came over the lip and down the fifteen paces of road toward her on all four points, its head low and its long arms out, going for the brightest thing on the hill.

The brightest thing on the hill was not her.

It was the hook.

She saw it change its line. It had been coming straight at her, and at the last few paces it went a hand's width to the left, toward the forged curve standing pale in the crack in the rock with the block hanging off it. Both her doors were open in that steel, all of it lit, and the husk went for it the way water goes for a drain.

And Callie was standing beside it with nothing on her at all.

The conduit was lying in the road at her feet where she'd put it down. It was four feet of dead steel pipe out of a wall, with no bolt in the end and no hook on it, the temperature of the morning. She picked it up.

The husk hit the hook.

It hit it the way the hoof had hit her on the causeway. There was the same flat, enormous note, a maul coming down on a stump. It went into the steel and stopped. The hook did not move. The block did not swing. The husk folded onto the forged curve like a coat thrown over a post, with its whole weight stopped dead against a hand's-breadth of her grandfather's steel.

Callie felt it land. It landed in the steel, and the cold in her arm went up to her shoulder in one jump, like somebody dropping a bucket down a well.

The husk's arm came round.

It was not aimed at her. It was simply the direction that was available. It had hit a wall and stopped, and the long arm went on going the way it had been going. It came round in a flat sweep at the height of her chest and caught her across the ribs on the left side, and there was nothing between it and her at all.

She went into the wall of the cut.

She did not remember going. She remembered the rock of the cut wall, suddenly, at the side of her head and all down her left side. She remembered sitting in the road. She remembered the taste of blood, and not being able to breathe in. She could only breathe halfway in before something in her side said *no*.

"*Callie!*" That was her mother. "It's on three! Callie, it's on *three* — "

She looked.

The husk had come off the hook. It had come off the wall that it could not move, and it was turning. It had dropped down onto three points, the way they did, with its long arm up, and it was coming round at her, faster on three than it had been on four. That was a thing about husks she had learned the hard way in a hayfield in the spring, and she had learned it with her grandfather lying in the grass.

She was sitting in the road with a pipe in her hand.

She did not think about it. There was no time for thinking. There was only the thing her body had known since before she could remember knowing it. *You don't stab a husk.* *You step across. You don't step back.*

She came up off the road on one knee and went across its line, the way you'd step out of the way of a cart. The pipe was in both her hands. And there was the hook in front of her, standing pale in the seam, immovable. Nothing in the country could shift it, and it stood a foot from the husk's turning shoulder.

She put the end of the pipe under the husk's arm, into the seam of the shoulder where it met the long flat of the back. She laid the middle of the pipe across the back of the forged curve, the way you lay a bar across a fulcrum. And she leaned on the end.

Not the hook. The pipe. A dead pipe out of a wall, across a thing that could not move.

She put all of herself into it. She dropped her hips and pulled down and back, the way you swing a sack off a rail.

The hook did not move. So everything moved round it.

The husk came apart. It came apart all at once and from everywhere, while it was still turning, the way a sack loses its bottom. What had been a husk was a grey haze hanging in the air of the cut, and then settling, and then running in a long thin tongue down the slope of the road, past her boots, into the pitch.

Callie knelt in the road with the pipe across the hook and breathed halfway in.

---

"Sixty-eight," said Ines, from the rim. Her voice did not change at all. "Sixty-nine."

The wagon was still coming.

Callie heard it. It was close now, grinding on the rock just below her, and she turned her head and the whole front of the wagon was there. The pole was coming up over the last step of the pitch, with the bottom block on the hounds. Pell was on the board with his hat gone and his hair standing up, his hands flat on his knees because there was nothing for them to do.

The pitch eased. It eased a few paces below the seam, where the rock went over into the gentler slope that ran up to the lip. The front wheels came over onto it. The rear wheels came over onto it. And the grinding stopped, because the wagon was not going up a wall any more. It was going up a road.

"*Chock!*" Pell roared.

He was off the board before he said it. He went back along the wagon and kicked stones in behind the rear wheels, one and then another, and then he threw himself on the brake again. Up on the high plain, the line went slack as Otto finally got Duchess's head down and brought her to a stop somewhere a long way up the road.

The wagon stood on the slope below the lip, on its own four wheels, chocked. The bottom block on its hounds was a pace from the top block on the hook.

"Eighty-eight," said Ines. "Eighty-nine."

Callie did not know if she could let go. She did not know how.

There was no weight on it now. The line was slack. The wagon was standing on its own wheels. There was nothing holding the hook in the seam but her, and nothing for it to hold. And still it stood there, pale, because she had not told it to stop.

She did what she had done on the span. She did what she did at the end of every sack on every rail she had ever swung one from. She finished the pull. She let her hips come up, and her weight come back over her heels, and she came up off the steel.

"Ninety-one," said Ines.

The pale went out of the hook. It did not drain this time. It simply was not there any more, the way Katori's blade was not there when she put it away. The block dropped half an inch on the curve with a clank, and the hook sat in the crack in the rock at the top of the hill. It was a foot of old forged steel with a strip of tape on the cross, the temperature of the morning, holding nothing.

Callie let go of the pipe. It rolled off the hook and down the road.

She lay down on her right side in the road, because her left side said *no*. Somewhere up on the high plain Katori was walking back. Somewhere up on the rim her mother was saying her name, over and over, not loud, in a voice Callie had never heard her use, and Ines was saying, "She's all right, she's moving, she's all right, look at her, she's moving."

She went down into herself to look.

There was nothing at the bottom of the well at all. It was dry. It was dry right down to the stone, drier than the morning after the pump house, drier than she had known a well could get.

It did not frighten her this time. It was not gone. It was only empty. Those were different.

She lay in the road at the top of the scarp and looked up at the sky, and breathed halfway in, and halfway out, and counted.
