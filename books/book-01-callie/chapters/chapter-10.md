# Chapter Ten

She got more than seventeen by taking the bolt out.

It was Otto's idea, and he had it by accident. He was sitting on the bank on the west side of the lay-by with his sling across his chest, watching her try for the fourth time. He watched the pale go into the conduit and flicker at the bottom and drain back out of it, the pipe first and the hook after. And he said, not to anybody, "It always goes out of the pipe first."

Callie stopped.

He was right. It had gone out of the pipe first every time. The pale had been fainter in the pipe from the beginning, too, if she was honest. It was deep in the forged curve and thin in the conduit, like frost on a window that is heavier in the corners. She had not thought about it. She had been too busy counting.

"The pipe's not yours," said Otto. He went red. "I mean — it's not *hers*. Your grandfather's. The hook was his for sixty years. The pipe came out of a wall."

Callie looked at the conduit. Four feet of thin steel pipe she had pulled out of the wall of a burned house in her first month on the road, when the wooden handle broke. It was a good pipe. It had done everything she had asked of it.

It was not the thing that had gone cold on the causeway. The grey sweep of the Edge had come off the end of the forged curve. It had never come off the pipe.

She took the wing nut off and drew the bolt, and the hook came off in her fist alone: a hand's-breadth of forged curve and a cross of wrapped tape.

There was a boulder in the bank at the side of the lay-by, grey stone the size of a cart, with a crack running down its face. Callie put the point of the hook into the crack and seated it, and put her hand flat on the back of the curve. She dropped her weight and set her back foot and braced, *through* the steel, the way her mother had told her. She did not grip it. She let the steel be the place she planted.

The pale went into the forged curve like breath onto cold glass. There was nowhere else for it to go. It stood in the hook and nowhere else, and it was steady.

"Count," she said.

Ines counted.

It was not easier. The cold came up her arm the same way. But it came slower, as if she were drawing on the well with one bucket instead of two. At seventeen the pale was still steady. At twenty-five it was steady. Ines put her boot against the hook where it stood in the crack and shoved, and nothing moved. At thirty-three, Callie felt it begin to go thin, and she let it go, carefully, before it went out on its own.

"Thirty-three," said Ines.

Callie sat down on the gravel. Half the well was gone. It was the same half-well as before, but it had bought twice as much.

"Otto," she said.

"Sorry," said Otto.

"*Thank* you."

"Oh," said Otto, and went a different shade of red, and looked at his boots.

---

She did it three more times that afternoon, with ash between. She put two fingers of her tenth into the cup each time and felt it go down. Every time she drank it she thought of the gate, and the table, and the columns, and every time she drank it anyway.

The second time she got thirty-six. The third time she got forty.

The fourth time something happened that she did not understand.

She had seated the hook and braced through it, and the pale was in the steel and steady. At about twenty, Otto called something from the wagon. It was a question about the harness, whether the breeching strap would take a horse pulling the wrong way. Callie turned her head to answer him. Without thinking, the way you let go of a thing to point, she took her hand off the hook.

She said, "Ask Pell, I don't know horses," and turned back, and the pale was still in the steel.

It should have gone. In the morning, when she took her hands off the conduit under the wagon, it had lasted three breaths and then drained out of the steel like water from a sink. Now she was a pace away from it with her hands at her sides, and it was standing there on its own in the crack in the boulder, pale and steady.

She looked at it. She looked at it hard, the way you look at a thing you're afraid is going to fall. And as she looked at it, the pale began to go thin at the point. She stepped forward and got her hand back on it, fast, and it held for another four or five seconds, and went out.

"Forty-four," said Ines. "Counting the bit when you weren't touching it."

"How long wasn't I touching it?"

"Eight. Nine."

Callie sat down.

"It held longer," said Katori slowly, "when you weren't looking at it."

"It can't have."

"It did. I watched it. You turned round and talked to Otto about a strap, and it stood there. Then you turned back and looked at it like it owed you money, and it started to go."

Callie did not know what to do with that. She had spent the whole of her life learning that the way to keep hold of a thing was to hold it harder. The way to keep hold of a hook was to grip it. The way to keep hold of a sack was to watch it all the way down. The way to keep your mother alive was never to stop watching her breathe.

"I don't understand," she said.

"No," said Katori. "Neither do I. Remember it anyway."

---

They planned the hill at supper. It was the longest supper Callie had ever sat through.

Pell laid it out with a stick in the dirt, the way Callie had laid out the causeway. He drew the long straight cut and then the steep part at the top, three hundred paces of it between the walls of rock. And at the very top of the steep part, where the road went up through the solid grey stone to the lip, he drew a short fat line.

"The pitch," he said. "Forty paces. That's where she stops. Duchess can take the wagon up the lower part of the steep, if everybody who's got two good arms puts them on the back of it, and Otto puts his one. She'll take it up two hundred and sixty paces of the steep, slowly, with rests. I've watched her on hills for nine hundred miles and I'll stand behind that." He tapped the fat line. "She won't take it up the pitch. Not with one sore leg and nobody beside her. Nobody could ask it."

"The tackle," said Callie.

"The tackle." Pell drew it. "The seam you found is at the top of the pitch, just under the lip. Your hook goes in the seam. The top block goes on the hook. The bottom block goes on the front of the wagon, on the pole hounds, at the bottom of the pitch. The line runs between them twice. That's two for one. The fall comes out of the top block and goes up over the lip and along the top, and Duchess is on the end of it, on the flat, pulling away along the road. On the flat she'll pull like a team." He sat back. "Forty paces of wagon. Eighty paces of horse. At the pace she'll go with that on her, the best part of a minute and a half."

"I've got forty-four seconds," said Callie.

"I know what you've got. Ines told me." Pell drew a line across the pitch halfway up. "So it's two pulls. She pulls twenty paces of wagon and stops. We chock the wheels with stones, and you let go. You rest, and you drink whatever you have to drink, and then we do the second twenty. Forty seconds a pull, if she walks like I think she'll walk."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then it's longer than forty seconds," said Pell, "and I'll be sitting on the brake of a wagon hanging off a hook in a crack, and I'd be grateful if you'd think of something."

Nobody laughed. Callie wasn't sure he had meant anybody to.

"There's the other thing," said Katori.

Everybody looked at her.

"Everything on the high plain comes down that cut," said Katori. "Callie read it this morning. The tap drips. Whatever's on top when we're on the pitch will come down it into us. And on the pitch Callie's lit. Both doors, all the way open, and nothing on the high plain for miles will be able to miss her." She did not look at Callie while she said it. She looked at the drawing in the dirt. "So here's what I want to ask. I want her in the ward, on her body, standing in the cut at the top of the pitch, above the wagon, for the whole climb. Not the hook. Her. Anything that comes down the road comes to her first, because it'll come to her anyway, and it can't hurt her. She stands there like the causeway, and they fold on her, and she tips them. And we relay the crates up by hand behind her, and the wagon comes up empty, and it takes us three days, and nobody's life hangs off a hook in a crack."

It was quiet.

"It's a good plan," said Katori. "It's the one I'd make with anybody else. I want it said."

Callie had known it was coming since the afternoon. She had worked through her answer three times while she was drinking ash. It still took her a long moment to say it.

"No," she said.

Katori looked up.

"No," Callie said again. "I'm sorry. It's a good plan. It'd have been a good plan on the causeway. It isn't a good plan here, and I can tell you why."

"Tell me."

"On the causeway it walked with me. It was on me and it walked. Here it won't. I can stand in it and I can't take a step. So I'd be standing in the middle of the road with my feet planted, and the road's as wide as the wagon, and I'm only as wide as me." Callie put her own stick in the dirt at the top of the pitch and drew a small mark, one person wide, in the middle of the road. "They come at me. They fold on me. And the ones behind them come at me too, and they can't get to me, because there's one in the way. So they do what they did on the causeway. They lean and slide round. On the causeway there was a drop on both sides and they slid off it. Here there's no drop. There's a wall of rock, and the road goes on past me on both sides, a wagon wide. They go round me and down the road." She drew the lines, down past the mark, down the pitch. "Into whatever's below me. Into the crates and the people carrying them. For three days."

Nobody said anything.

"And it's three days lit," said Callie. "Three days of it coming out of me. I held it thirteen hours on the causeway and it took me two days to get a hand of water back in the well. I'd be empty by noon on the first day. I'd be a girl standing in a road with nothing, in front of everything on the high plain." She put the stick down. "I'll be lit as long as the wagon's on the pitch. A minute and a half. Not one second longer. Then I go dark, and nothing on the plain can feel me, and nothing comes."

Katori looked at her for a long time across the fire.

"That's a no," Katori said.

"That's a no."

"Good," said Katori. It was not what Callie had expected her to say. "You're allowed." She looked down at the drawing in the dirt, and then at Pell. "Two pulls. The hook. She's right."

Pell was writing.

"And there's a thing you didn't say," said Katori, not looking up, "which is that you'd rather your mother wasn't on this hill for three days. I'm saying it for you. You're allowed that too."

---

After that everybody wanted something. Callie had not known that they all wanted different things until they said them.

Ines wanted height. "The west wall of the pitch," she said. "The top of it. I went up this afternoon. There's a way up the red earth at the bottom of the steep, and you walk along the top of the cut. From up there I can see the road on the high plain for half a mile, and the pitch under me like a plate." She laid her six arrows on the ground in front of her, one at a time. "Six. I want height, and I want to know that when it's done, somebody walks me down to pick them up."

"I'll walk you down," said Otto.

"You'll be on the horse."

"After."

"After," Ines agreed. It was the nearest thing to a smile Callie had seen on her.

Otto wanted the horse. He said so all at once, as if he had been holding it in his mouth all through supper. "I'll be on the top with her. On the fall. She knows me. She'll go for me. She — " He stopped and looked at Callie. "She'll go for me if you're not near her. You'll be down at the hook. That's twenty paces at least, and past the lip, where she can't see you. She'll go."

"Can you hold her with one arm?"

"I've held her with one arm all week," said Otto. "I'm not going to stop now."

Pell wanted the brake. Nobody argued with that. "It's my wagon," he said, "and it's been my wagon nine hundred miles, and if it's going to go back down that hill it's going to go back down with me on it, and I'll hear no conversation about it."

Katori said she would be at the lip, on the road between the hook and the high plain. She said it as if it were a small thing, the way she had said *I have three people who can hold a line* on the first morning.

That left one more person.

"And me," said Callie's mother.

They all turned.

She was sitting against the wagon wheel with the blanket round her shoulders. She had been there all through supper and had not said one word, which should have told somebody something.

"I'm not riding up that hill," she said. "Pell's just said he wants the wagon light, and I heard him, and I'm the lightest thing on it that isn't a crate. And I'm not sitting at the bottom by myself in a lay-by waiting to be fetched, like a parcel. So I'm walking." She looked round at them all. "I'll go up the lower part early, slowly, with somebody, and I'll be at the top before the pitch. And then I'll want to be somewhere I can see what's happening. I'm not asking."

"You can't walk a mile of that," said Callie. "Your leg — "

"No," her mother agreed. "Not the way I am tonight." She looked at Katori. "Callie drank some ash this afternoon, out of her tenth. It went into her and she stood up straighter. I saw it. What does it do for somebody like me? With no door?"

Katori was quiet for a moment.

"Something," she said. "Not much. Nobody knows why it does anything at all. Somebody with no door drinks a Handful, and sometimes they have a good day. They walk further than they could have. They sleep. Their colour comes back. It doesn't last. It doesn't mend anything. It's like borrowing a day from somewhere, and you don't know who from." She glanced at Ines. "Ines won't touch it."

"It feels like wearing somebody else's coat," said Ines flatly. "I don't like it. That's me. It's not advice."

"A good day," said Callie's mother.

"Maybe."

Callie's mother looked at Callie.

"There's a jar in the bottom of the barrow," she said. "Five handfuls. You lifted all of them. I know exactly where every one of them came from, because I was lying on a plank in the road waiting for you to come back from each one." Her voice was very even. "You were going to put that on the table at the gate, so they'd see we could pay. And Katori's standing for us at the gate now, and your tenth is in Pell's book. So I want one of them. For the hill. I want one good day, and I want to spend it on walking up that hill on my own two feet, and seeing you at the top of it."

"That's — "

"It's mine to spend," said her mother. "You said it yourself, today, to Pell, about your tenth. It's mine. I'm saying it now." She held Callie's eyes. "You don't get to spend me, love. Not even on keeping me."

Callie could not say anything at all.

"There," said her mother, and looked away, and pulled the blanket up. "That's settled. Somebody put more wood on this fire. I'm cold."

---

Her mother went off in the night, a little after two.

Callie was awake. She had been lying awake beside her, under the wagon, listening to her breathe, the way she had listened most nights since Gaunt's Reach. The breathing changed. It went short, and then it stopped being breathing and became something with a catch in it, over and over. Callie was up on her elbow before she knew she was awake.

Her mother's eyes were half open. Her right hand was moving on the blanket, the same small plucking motion as on the morning before the causeway. She was picking at a thread that was not there, over and over.

"I'm here," said Callie. "I'm here. I'm here."

It lasted a little over a minute. She counted, because she always counted. Nobody else woke. Or everybody woke and nobody moved, and Callie never knew which.

Then it stopped. Her mother took a long breath, and her eyes found Callie's face in the dark.

"What," she said.

"You went off."

Her mother lay there. After a while she said, "How long?"

"A minute. A bit more."

"Longer than the last one."

"A bit."

Her mother was quiet for a long time. Callie thought she had gone to sleep.

"I'm still walking up that hill," said her mother.

"I know."

"I'm telling you so you don't have to lie there all night deciding whether to ask."

"I know."

"Good." Her mother's left hand came out from under the blanket and found Callie's wrist, and held it. It did not stop a finger's width short of anything. "Go to sleep. You've got a hill."

Callie did not go to sleep. But she lay down, and let her wrist be held, and after a while that was nearly the same.

---

At first light she went up the cut alone and read it.

There was a fresh trough down the middle of the road, a single one. It came all the way from the lip, down the pitch, down the steep part, past the lay-by, and out onto the flats and away east. It had been made in the night, while they slept a hundred paces from it, and nothing had come near them, because nothing on the high plain could feel anybody in that lay-by any more than it could feel a stone.

One in the night. The tap had dripped.

She stood at the bottom of the pitch and looked up it. It was forty paces of bare grey rock going up at a slant that made her neck ache, between walls higher than a house. The seam was at the top, a dark line across the road. Past it, the lip. Past the lip, sky.

The tap had dripped last night, and it would drip again. She could not tell when. Nobody could tell when. That was the whole thing about a tap.

She went back down and told them, and they went.

---

Her mother drank her Handful standing up by the tailgate, out of Callie's own tin cup. Callie had tipped the ash in from the jar herself, and her hand had shaken only a little. Her mother drank it the way she took all her medicine, in one go, with a face.

"Well?" said Callie.

"It tastes of nothing."

"I know. Is it — "

"Give it a minute, love. I've been dying for three months, I can wait a minute to feel better." She said it lightly. It was the first time she had ever said the word, and she said it the way you'd say it was going to rain, and she went straight on. "Where's my hat?"

Callie gave her her hat.

Then, about a minute later, her mother stood up straighter. It wasn't much. She did not look well. She still had the grey under her eyes and the right hand still lay against her leg as if it belonged to somebody else. But she stood up off the tailgate without holding on to it, and she stood there on both feet, and she put her hat on with her left hand and pulled it down.

"Oh," she said, in a slightly different voice. "Oh, yes. I see."

"Is it — "

"It's a good day," said her mother. "Somebody's lent me a good day. I don't know who, and I'm not going to ask." She looked up the long pale cut to the sky. "Ines. You said there was a way up the red earth. Take me up it. I'd like to be at the top before anybody needs me to be anywhere."

Ines looked at her for a moment, and then at Callie, and then she shouldered her bow and her quiver of six and held out her arm.

"Slowly," said Ines.

"As slowly as you like. I've got all day. It's the first day in a month I've been able to say that."

They went up the road together, the woman who shot and the woman who could not braid her own hair, arm in arm, slowly. At the bottom of the steep part they turned off onto a cattle track up the red earth at the side of the cut. Callie watched them all the way, until they were two small figures on the rim of the cut against the sky, a long way up. One of them lifted an arm. Not a wave. It was held up, flat, the way you hold something up to be seen.

Callie held her arm up back.

Then she turned round, and went to put her shoulder to the back of a wagon.

---

The lower part of the steep took them two hours.

Duchess pulled. She pulled with her head down and her sore leg short, and she stopped every thirty or forty paces, and every time she stopped they chocked the wheels with stones. Otto walked at her head, one-armed, and talked to her the way Pell talked to her, which he had clearly been practising. Pell sat on the brake. Katori and Callie put their shoulders to the back of the wagon and pushed, and Callie did not light anything, and Duchess did not look round once. That was because Callie was behind the wagon where the mare could not see her. It was the only place on the hill where that was true.

At the bottom of the pitch the wagon stopped for good. Duchess stood with her head low and her flanks going, with a forty-pace wall of grey rock going up in front of her like the side of a house. She did not even look at it. She knew.

"That's hers done," said Pell. "That's everything I'll ask of her in harness. Unhitch her. Take her up."

Otto unhitched her and led her up the pitch alone. She went without a wagon, slowly, scrabbling on the rock, and at the top she went over the lip and out of sight. Callie heard Otto on the other side talking to her, low and steady.

Then it was the tackle.

They carried the blocks up the pitch between them. They were heavy iron-strapped things, double sheaves, and the coil of line was heavier still. Callie took the top block up to the seam. She knelt in the road and cleaned the old silt out of the crack with her fingers, all the way along, until the stone was clean and sound on both sides.

She drew the bolt and took the hook off the conduit. She seated the point of it in the seam. She hung the top block from the curve, and the block's weight pulled the hook down into the crack. The hook was a hand's-breadth of her grandfather's forged steel, set in a crack in a rock at the top of a hill, with an iron block hanging off it like a sack off a rail.

Katori came up beside her.

"Pell's got the bottom block on the hounds," Katori said. "Line's run. Otto's got the fall on the horse. Your mother and Ines are on the west rim. Everybody's where they said they'd be." She looked down at the hook. "Tell me what you want."

"Count," said Callie. "Somebody count. Out loud. Where I can hear it."

"Ines'll count. She's already counting. She's been counting since the lay-by, I'd bet money on it." Katori crouched down on her heels, level with her. "Anything else?"

Callie looked up the last fifteen paces of road to the lip, and the sky.

"Go up there," she said. "Where you said you'd be. And don't come down here unless I call you."

Katori looked at her for a moment. Then she nodded once and went up the road to the lip, and stood on the top of the scarp with her back to Callie and her face to the high plain. She did not take her Ember out.

Callie put the conduit on the road beside her where she could reach it. She put her hand flat on the back of the hook. She put her back foot behind her in the road and dug it in, and dropped her weight, and set herself for the catch, through the steel, into the rock, and down.

The pale went into the hook.

Down at the bottom of the pitch, forty paces below her, Pell took the brake off.

"Pull," Callie said. She said it not very loud, because the air was still, and on the top of the scarp Otto heard her and said it again to the horse. "Pull."
