# Chapter 13 — Callie: Households

Sowerby looked at her mother on the hundred and eleventh, and Callie went with her, because her mother asked.

She did not ask in words. She stood at the door of fourteen after breakfast with her stick and her hat on, and looked at Callie, and waited. Callie put her coat on. That was how it was asked, now, and how it was answered.

They went across the yard slowly, at her mother's pace, with the hook. The wet had gone off the concrete in the night, and the wind was from the west and dry, and there was a smell in it Callie did not know: something green, from a long way off. Her mother stopped once, at the corner of the long building, and stood with her face in the wind.

"Grass," she said. "Somewhere."

Sowerby's room was the small one at the end of the east corridor, past the technician's door and the day holder's. He had the folder on the desk. It was the same folder as on the fortieth: brown card, thick, with her mother's name on the front in his hand. Her mother sat down in the chair across from him and put her left hand flat on top of it before he had opened it, as she always did.

"The swelling's down," said Sowerby. He did not open the folder. He did not need to. "Same as the fortieth. No fit since. The medicine's doing what it does." He looked at her mother. "Your right hand."

Her mother lifted it off her knee. She closed it. It closed most of the way, slowly, the fingers not quite meeting the palm.

"Same," said Sowerby.

"Same," said her mother.

"Walk to the door."

Her mother got up. The left leg first, then the right, late. She walked to the door with her stick, five steps, and turned, and walked back. The right foot came through a little after it should have, every time, and set down a little flat.

"Same," said Sowerby.

"I'll take same."

"I'll take same too," said Sowerby. "In this room, same is good news. I'd like you both to understand that." He looked at the folder under her mother's hand. "The thing's where it was. I'll not tell you more than that unless you ask."

"I'm not asking."

"I know you're not," said Sowerby. "I'm saying it every time so you know the door's open." He looked at Callie, and then away, at the window, as if he had decided not to look at her. "Next look in thirty days. Unless something changes. If something changes, you come at once. Not in thirty days."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Sowerby. "I'm saying it so it's said. The girl with the book has the whole station doing it." He picked up his pen. "That's all. Go and sit in the sun, if there is any. There's not been any since the autumn."

---

In the corridor outside, Callie said it.

She had not meant to say it there. She had meant to say it on the step, at night, with their hands in their laps, the way things were said in fourteen now. But they came out of Sowerby's door into the long corridor with its windows down one side, and the light was coming in through them, grey and thin and almost warm, and her mother stopped and stood in it with her eyes shut. Callie looked at her standing there, and at her right hand on the stick, closed most of the way, and it came out.

"I'll stay," said Callie.

Her mother opened her eyes.

"If they ask," said Callie. "On the hundred and seventeenth. *Will you come.* I'll say no. I'll say I'm staying. Here. With you." She heard her voice. It was the voice for a number. "There's Elsie to read the Sag. There's Dessa. They'll not need me at the hill. There's six who've been inside, and five can go."

Her mother looked at her.

She looked at her for a long time, in the thin light, with the dust going round in it. Then she said, slowly, each word with its space in front of it:

"Don't you dare."

Callie stopped.

"Don't you dare," said her mother again. Not loud. She did not raise her voice. She never had. "Not for me. Not because of me. Not because I walked to a door and the right foot came through late."

"That's not—"

"It is," said her mother. "I saw your face in there. When he said *same*. You heard *same* and you thought *same for how long*." She leaned on the stick. "I know that face. My father had it. Every morning for forty years at that scale house."

Callie did not say anything.

"He stayed for me," said her mother. "Your grandfather. At Thistle Ford. He should have gone. There was work on the river when I was twelve, good work, weighing at the big wharf, and he'd have been good at it. And my mother was dead and I'd nobody else." She looked down the corridor. "He stayed. Forty years. He read that beam for every farmer in the valley and he was the best there was and nobody ever knew it but the farmers. He stayed because of me." She was quiet. "He never once said so. I knew anyway. I've known all my life."

"He loved you."

"He did," said her mother. "And I spent forty years being the reason. You don't know what that's like. To be the reason." She put her left hand out and took hold of Callie's sleeve. "I'll not be it. Not for you. Not this time."

Callie stood in the corridor with her mother's hand on her sleeve and could not think of anything at all.

"So you'll decide," said her mother. "On your own. For your own reason. Not mine." She let go of the sleeve. "And then I'll say mine. Tomorrow. In the long room. In front of everybody. The Director said any household." She turned toward the door at the end of the corridor, where the light was. "I'm a household."

---

The long room on the hundred and twelfth was fuller than Callie had ever seen it.

The Director sat at the long table with her spectacles on and her small book open. On her left sat the three from the household review, the same three as on the tenth day: the man in the long coat, tall and thin with his close grey beard, and the woman with the red face and the ledger, and the old man with the white moustache and the very pale eyes. On her right, in the chair by the window, Dessa sat with the big book on her knee.

Everybody else stood, or sat on the benches the gate crew had carried up the fourteen stairs, or leaned on the wall under the long window. The Home Line stood along the wall by the slate. The households stood wherever they could. Callie counted them without meaning to and then stopped, because she did not want to know the number. She knew some of the faces. The first yard lifter's wife, small and broad. The tall thin woman from the south row with her arms folded. A woman Callie did not know, holding the hand of the girl in mittens. A quiet man in a yard-office coat standing at the back, very still, with a careful face.

Tamsin Greave stood in the corner by the stove with her hat in her hand. She was not a household. The Director had said she could stand there and listen and say nothing, and she was doing exactly that.

The slate on its easel said *74*. The Director had chalked it that morning.

Her mother sat on the end of the front bench with her stick against her knee.

"I'll not make a speech," said the Director. "I said what this is on the hundred and sixth, at the plate. This is for you. Anybody with somebody on the defender sheet." She took her spectacles off. "Say your piece. Out loud. Dessa writes it. Nobody answers you today. Not me, not anybody. Today's for hearing."

Nobody moved for a long moment.

Then the first yard lifter's wife stood up.

---

She did not come to the table. She stood where she was, by the second bench, with her hands together in front of her and her husband beside her with his bandaged hand across his chest.

"He's on the sheet," she said. "Since the fifty-seventh. My husband." She did not look at him. "He's not going. To the hill. He's said. His hand's done and he'll not lift again and he's a jar now, the boy says, at the table." She stopped. "That's not what I want to say."

"Say what you want to say," said the Director.

"We're on twenty-eight days," said the woman. "Our household. Since the autumn. We came in off the plain with my mother sick, and she died, and we stayed, and we're on twenty-eight days." Her hands held each other. "If he doesn't go. To the hill. Does it go bad for us? At the review? Do we lose the hut?"

The long room went very quiet.

Callie looked at the three on the Director's left. The man in the long coat did not move. The woman with the ledger looked down at it. The old man with the white moustache looked at the first yard lifter's wife with his pale eyes and did not look away.

"Nobody answers you today," said the Director. "I said."

"I know you said."

"I'm going to break it," said the Director. "Once. For this. Because if I don't, you'll go home and lie awake, and so will everybody else in this room with the same question, and I'll not have that." She put her spectacles down on the table. "No household's standing turns on anybody going to that hill. Not on this sheet. Not on any sheet. Not ever, while I'm at this table." She looked at Dessa. "Write it."

Dessa wrote it.

"And while I'm breaking it," said the Director, "here's the rest. Every household with a person on the Home Line's sheet has done service to this station this winter. On the lip. At the lane. At the table. In the frost, on the seventy-ninth, when the Sag ran dry and nobody came through." She looked round the room. "That's service. Not the hill. That. So every one of those households has a year's standing from today. Whether their person goes to that hill or stays in this yard and holds a jar. A year. Reviewed next spring, not in twenty-eight days."

Somebody at the back of the room made a sound. It was not a word.

The first yard lifter's wife stood very still.

"A year," she said.

"A year," said the Director. "Write it, Dessa. Then I'll sign it. Then the panel will." She looked at the three on her left. The man in the long coat nodded once. The woman with the ledger was already writing. The old man with the white moustache did not nod. He took out a pen.

A woman stood up at the back. Callie did not know her. She was thin and grey in the face, in a coat that had been mended at both elbows.

"And us?" she said. "We've nobody on the sheet. My boy's eight. We're on twenty-eight days. We've been on twenty-eight days since the summer."

The Director looked at her for a long moment.

"You're still on twenty-eight days," she said. "I've not got a better answer for you today. I'll not pretend I have." She did not look away. "There are forty households on twenty-eight days in this station. Today some of them came off it, because of what somebody in them did on a lip. That's a start. It's not the end. I'm not going to stand here and tell you it is."

The woman looked at her. Then she sat down again, slowly.

In the corner by the stove, Tamsin Greave had not moved. But she was looking at the Director, not at anybody else, and she was looking at her the way she had looked at the box on the board.

---

They came after that, one at a time, and the room stopped being quiet.

The first yard lifter said his own piece, short. *I'll not go. I'm a jar. Here. That's mine.* His wife sat down beside him and put her hand on his good one.

The woman holding the girl in mittens stood up.

"She's twelve," she said. "She'll not go anywhere. Not past the berm." She held the girl's hand tighter. "She says she's at the table. She says she draws. A bit. When she's told to stop." She looked at the girl, and back at the Director. "She can do that here. If Miss Wren's at the table. If somebody's there who can say *off* to her."

"And if Miss Wren goes?" said the Director.

The woman did not know. She looked at the girl.

"Then whoever's there," said the girl in mittens, in a small clear voice, "who can say *off*." She looked at the floor. "That's what I'd want. That's my bit. Somebody at my shoulder."

Dessa wrote it.

The quiet man at the back in the yard-office coat stood up.

He did not come forward. He stood very still with his hands at his sides, and when he spoke it was so quietly that half the room leaned toward him to hear.

"Daven's my son," he said. "He's on the sheet. He'll want to go."

Daven, along the wall with the Home Line, went very still.

"He can go," said his father. "On one term." He looked down the room at his son. "He doesn't talk much. He never has. When he does, it's because there's something to say." He was quiet. "If he says a thing, and anybody talks over him, he comes home. That's my term. He's heard. Or he's here."

The room was silent.

"He's heard," said Dessa, from the window. She did not look up from the book. "I'll write it that way. *D. R.: heard, or home.*"

The quiet man nodded once and sat down. Daven did not look at him. But Callie saw his hands, at his sides, close and open.

The tall thin woman from the south row did not stand. She had said she would not, and she did not. She sat with her arms folded and looked at the Director, and when the Director looked back at her she shook her head, once, and the Director nodded, and wrote something in the small book herself.

Silas, along the wall, was red to the ears. He did not say anything either.

---

Then Dessa read Senna's.

Senna was not in the room. The fourteen stairs would have taken six orderlies, and Senna had said she would not be carried up a stair by six men to say twelve words. So she had said them to Dessa the night before, in the ward, and Dessa read them out now, in Senna's words, from the book.

"*My route's in this book,*" read Dessa. "*Every halt. Copy it twice. One goes to the hill. One stays in the long room with the atlas. The one that goes is carried by whoever's reading. Not by whoever's holding. Not by my grandson.*" She looked up. "*S. V.*"

Toren, along the wall, did not move. Callie looked at his hands. They were at his sides, both of them, the left one closing and opening.

"And Tonk," said the Director.

Everybody looked at Jab.

"He says ask him after the test," said Jab. "He says he wants to see me come back from the Sag first. In the day. Then he'll say." He stopped. "That's his term. It's on the paper. It's his."

The Director wrote it herself.

"And mine," said Dee.

She was by the slate, with her glass in her fist. She did not come forward.

"I'm not a household," she said. "I'm on the sheet. I'm the only other healer this station's got." She turned the glass. "I'll go to the hill if the table here has somebody who can draw and somebody who can say *off*. Both. Not one. Two." She looked across the room at the girl in mittens. "She can say *off*. She can draw a bit. She's twelve. She's not enough on her own and I'll not leave her to be."

"Then you'll stay," said the Director.

"Then I'll stay," said Dee. "Unless somebody comes up with a second."

In the corner by the stove, Tamsin Greave moved for the first time. She did not speak. She had said she would not, and she did not. But she turned her hat over in her hands, and looked at Dee, and then at the girl in mittens, and Callie saw her mouth go thin, as it had at the plate when she read the box.

---

Her mother stood up last.

She did it the long way, with the stick and her left hand on the end of the bench, the left leg first, then the right, late. It took a while. The room waited. Nobody helped her. Callie, along the wall, did not move, because her mother had not asked her to.

When she was up, she stood there by the front bench with her stick, and looked at the Director, and then round the room.

"I'm Tilda," she said. "From the weighbridge. The tally." Each word came with its space in front of it. "My daughter's on the sheet. She's been inside. At the hill. She's one of the six."

The room waited.

"I'll not tell you what she'll say," said her mother. "She'll say it herself. In the book. On the seventeenth." She paused. "I told her yesterday I'll not be the reason she stays. I'm telling you so it's said."

Callie felt the room look at her. She looked at the floor.

"So here's my term," said her mother. "If she goes. She writes me the halts. Every one. And the counts. On a paper. And whoever comes back first on the road brings it, and I'll read them on my stool." She shifted her weight on the stick. "And when that hill's ash comes in at this station's plate, whenever it comes, and whoever brings it, I'll be at the window. On the stool. And I'll keep the tally." She looked at the Director. "That's mine. Not hers. I'll weigh what she brings. That's my bit."

The Director looked at her for a long moment over her spectacles.

"Meridian keeps the tally," said a voice.

It was Tamsin Greave, in the corner. She had said she would not speak. She had spoken anyway, quietly, not to the room. To Callie's mother.

Her mother turned her head slowly and looked at her.

"The tally keeps itself," said her mother. "I write it." She did not look away. "You've seen my board. You read the box first."

Tamsin Greave looked back at her. Then she nodded, once, the way Jab nodded, and turned her hat over in her hands, and was quiet again.

---

They were nearly done. The Director had her spectacles off and her hands flat on the table, the way she sat when she was about to say that was all.

"One more," said Tamsin Greave.

The Director looked at her.

"I said I'd not speak," said Tamsin Greave. "I've broken it once. I'll break it again and then you can put me out." She came forward a step from the stove. "I'm not asking for a household. I'm asking a thing the river'll ask me when I get home." She looked along the wall at the Home Line. "Your reader. The girl with the hook. If she goes to the hill." She looked at Callie. "Who reads your Sag?"

Nobody answered for a moment.

"I do," said Elsie.

She was at the far end of the Home Line, by the door, with the old counter on her hip under its tape. She did not step forward. She said it from where she stood, in the voice she used for Duchess, not loud.

"I'm reading here," said Elsie. "On the lip. Every watch. When Callie's at the hill." She looked at Tamsin Greave straight on. "That's mine. Nobody told me to. I'm not going. I'm reading the Sag." She looked at the Director. "Write it as mine."

Dessa wrote it.

Callie stood along the wall with the hook and could not breathe properly. It was not her ribs.

*Who reads it when you can't?*

She had asked it on the sixty-first. She had answered it on the eightieth with a name in a column. She had thought it was finished. She had thought it was an answer to a question about winter, about six weeks, about a girl on a crate with cracked ribs.

It was not about six weeks. It was this. It was Elsie, standing by the door of the long room in front of the river and the households and the Director, saying *I'm reading here*. So that Callie did not have to.

She had not known that an answer could do that. That you could say *who reads when you can't*, and somebody could say *me*, and then you were free.

She looked along the wall at Elsie.

Elsie was not looking at her. She was looking at the slate, at *74*, as if checking whether it was right.

---

On the hundred and thirteenth Callie and Toren did the change in the east shed, with the sack.

It was Toren's idea, and Dee's, and Katori's. It had come out of the planning on the hundred and seventh, at the end, when Callie said *I can't do both and hold*, and Toren had looked at her across the chalk drawing and said nothing. That evening he had come to the door of fourteen and said: *your two. When you change. Somebody could hold it for you.*

So they did it at the shed door.

Callie laid the hook across the frame, the curve on the left post, and let the Ward go into the steel. It came from the well. Her body set behind it, back and belly and the place under her arms, and the place under the left arm said what it always said now.

Katori stood outside with the old sack on its rope.

Silas stood at the end of the long table, where he could see the door and the hook and Toren and the sack through the gap at the top of the door. He was the end. Dessa had said so. *If you're going to do it on the fifteenth, you're going to do it with an end.*

Toren stood at Callie's right side, a pace off, with his weight in his heels.

"Ward on the door," said Callie. "Holding."

"Sack," said Silas.

Katori let it go. It hit the door. The door did not move. The hook lay across it, still.

Callie counted under her breath, not breaths, just numbers, to keep her mind off the place under her arm. At twelve she felt it begin to grow. At fifteen it was bigger.

"Going," she said. "Change."

"*Taking*," said Toren.

He set. The strut came up out of the shed floor at its slant, soft behind, inside the door, its top against the planks just below where the hook lay. Callie felt it come. She felt the weight that came against the door each time the sack swung go half into her hook and half into his strut.

"*Yours*," said Callie.

"*Mine*."

"*Letting go*."

She let the Ward out of the hook.

One. The hook was steel. The door was held by Toren's strut, standing against it from inside, and nothing else.

Katori let the sack go. It hit the door. The door did not move. Toren breathed out through his teeth.

Two. Callie lit.

The light came up the inside of the curve and off the point and lay down along the sill, along the line where the shed floor met the frame. It bent round the post. It was short and white and it lay exactly where the floor changed.

"*Line's on*," said Callie.

"Clear?" said Toren. To Silas.

Silas looked at the door. He looked at the gap at the top of it, where he could see Katori and the sack. He looked down at the sill and the line. Two looks.

"*Clear*," said Silas.

Toren let the strut go.

The door stood. There was nothing against it but the latch, and the line lying along the sill, white. Katori did not swing the sack. That was the drill. Line on, strut off, and the line holding the floor.

Callie held it. She did not count. Silas counted for her. At sixteen he said, "*Sixteen. You're grey.*"

At eighteen she let it go.

She sat down on the bench with the hook across her knees. Her nose did not bleed. She put her hand up to check.

"Once," said Dee, from her stool. She had the glass. "That's one change. With somebody holding the two." She looked at Callie. "How's the arm?"

"Less," said Callie. She meant it. The place under her arm was there, but it had not grown as it grew when she changed on her own. "The two didn't cost. Not like it does."

"Because you weren't holding anything in the two," said Toren. He was breathing hard. "I was."

"You were holding the door."

"I was holding the door for eleven breaths," said Toren. "A door with a sack on it." He looked at his own hand. "It's cheaper than a pace of dark."

Silas, at the end of the table, was writing. He wrote on the card that Toren carried, the copy, at the bottom under everything else, in his big leaning hand.

*Ward to line, with a strut in the two: C. holds, T. takes, C. lets go, C. lights, end says clear, T. lets go. The strut pays for the two. Not her.*

He showed it to Callie.

"Is that right?" he said.

Callie read it.

"It's right," she said. "Two r's in Marr, though. You wrote one on the back."

Silas went red, and turned the card over, and looked, and fixed it.

---

The oldest of the four was at the weighbridge board that afternoon when Callie came back to the plate with the hook.

She saw his boots first. Road boots, with iron heel-plates, worn down on the outside edge. He was standing at the end of the plate with his hands in his pockets and his fur collar turned down now, because the wind was soft, and he was reading the board.

He was reading the new chalk. Callie watched him. He read it from the top, as he had on the sixty-first. *KATORI'S. HOME LINE.* The struck line. *The rest where it lies.* *SAG: 16, SPENT. 21 DAYS.* The box round *OWED*. And under it, newest, *RIVER (T. G.): 2 FLASKS, LENT. 1 THIN (C.'S CALL).*

He read that line twice.

Her mother was on the stool in the window with the tally board on her knee. The weighman was inside at the beam.

"You've a new name on your board," said the man.

"It's true," said her mother. "So it's on."

The man smiled a little.

"Miss Vale would like it known," he said, "that the oil was a gift."

"It's in Hallet's ledger," said her mother. "Paid. In full. On the ledge."

"Ledgers can be read two ways," said the man pleasantly.

"Not mine," said her mother.

The man looked at her through the window for a moment, with the same mild interest he gave to everything. Then he turned and saw Callie at the end of the plate.

"The reader," he said.

Callie did not say anything.

"One of the six," said the man. "Who's been inside." He looked at the hook in her hand, at the counter on her hip. "Your board says *C.'s call*. That's you. You called the river's ash thin." He looked back at the board. "Was it?"

"It was," said Callie.

"Then it's a good board," said the man. "Miss Vale values a good reader. She'd pay very well for one."

Callie looked at him. She thought about Tamsin Greave at the plate on the hundredth, tying off the canvas rope with a knot she did not know. *She pays well. That's the first thing she does.*

"She pays well first," said Callie. "I've heard."

The man looked at her.

For a moment his face did nothing. Then he laughed. It was the same short real laugh Toren had told her about, from the bench outside nineteen, as if somebody he liked had told him a good joke.

"You've been talking to the river," he said.

"I've been reading the board," said Callie.

He touched the edge of his collar, as if it were a hat, the way he had on the sixty-first, and turned, and went back across the south yard toward the south row with his hands in his pockets.

Her mother, in the window, was looking at Callie.

"*She pays well first*," said her mother slowly.

"Tamsin Greave said it."

"I know who said it. I heard her." Her mother picked up the pencil. "You said it to him." She wrote something on the corner of the tally board. Callie did not look to see what. "He'll remember you, now."

"He remembered me already," said Callie. "He watched me read on the sixty-first."

"No," said her mother. "He watched a girl read. Now he knows which one." She put the pencil down. "Be careful of being known."

---

On the night of the hundred and fourteenth, Callie sat on the step of fourteen with the hook across her knees and her hands in her lap.

Keel had looked at her ribs again that afternoon. *A door's worth. Standing. You've not been over it. I'd know.* She had not said anything about the line. Callie had not told her. It was not holding. It was the other thing.

The night was clear and dry and the stars were out over the berm. Rook had said it would be bright tomorrow. Toren had come to the door with it at the end of the evening, as if it were a thing he needed to put down somewhere, and Callie had said *ask Elsie in the morning*, and he had gone.

Down the row, on the step of eleven, there was nobody. Toren had gone in. Twelve was dark. Jab was in the ward, on half of the fifth bed, because Tonk had said half.

Her mother came out and sat beside her. She did it the long way.

They sat.

"You've got one," said her mother, after a while.

Callie looked at her.

"An answer," said her mother. "You've got one. I can see it on you. Since yesterday. Since the girl said *I'm reading here*." She did not look at Callie. She looked at the lamp at the end of the row, flat and white. "You've not said it."

"No."

"Don't say it to me," said her mother. "Not yet. Say it in the book. On the seventeenth. With everybody else." She was quiet. "But is it yours?"

Callie thought about it.

She thought about the corridor yesterday, and *don't you dare*. She thought about her grandfather reading the beam for forty years at a scale house for every farmer in the valley, and nobody knowing but the farmers. She thought about Elsie by the door of the long room. *That's mine. Write it as mine.*

She thought about the corridor at Caul Hill, going west under the hill toward a round wall, with ten doors on the left that nobody had opened. Ten rooms. Nobody had read them. Nobody had stood at the door of any of them and looked at the floor and said where it changed.

She wanted to.

That was all it was. Not because anybody needed her to. Because nobody had read those rooms, and she could, and she wanted to see the lines in them with her own eyes and lay them down in white where everybody else could see them too.

"It's mine," she said.

Her mother nodded slowly.

"Then that's all I needed," she said. She put her left hand out and found Callie's hand on the step and held it. "Now go to bed. You've a slope to read in the morning. And your nose has to last."

"It's not my nose," said Callie. "It's the change."

"It's all your nose," said her mother. "From where I sit."

Callie laughed. It hurt, under the arm. She did it anyway.

Beyond the berm, at the Sag, the upper lamps burned along the east track with winter oil in them that a woman called Vale had sent and the Director had paid for. The three river glasses on the lower line burned a little greener than the rest. And down in the ditch below the track, below post five, under the bank's shadow where the sun had not reached all day, the ice across the pipe's east mouth was a hand thick.

Elsie had looked at it at dusk. She had said so.

Callie went in.
