# Chapter 14 — The Shadow Side

Before the light, on the hundred and fifteenth, Elsie went down the made road alone with a bucket of whitewash and a brush, and painted the line.

Nobody had told her to go alone. Nobody had told her not to. She went at the first grey, when the frost was still white on the made road and the lamps along the east track were still burning, and she went along the top of the bank behind the upper lamps to the fifth, and down the ramp. It was frozen. It crunched under her boots, the gravel on top and the clay under it hard as a floor. At the bottom she stood on the track.

The track ran along the east side of the bowl below the lamps like a long stone shelf, three paces wide where it was widest. It went up behind her toward the south lip, toward the first lamp and the crates and the table, and down in front of her toward the bottom, where it narrowed and broke up and went under the water at post six. On her right the bank went up steep to the lamps. On her left the slope went down, grey with a winter of kills lying frozen on it, to the lower line and the ditch.

Post five stood at the edge of the track, white, with its number painted on it in black. The grey lay thickest round its foot.

She knelt on the stone beside the post and painted the line. She painted it straight across the track, from the foot of the bank to the edge of the slope, two hands wide, slowly, so that it would not run. Then she painted it again over the top, so that it would be white when the sun came, and nobody could say they had not seen it.

When she stood up she looked down the slope at the ditch.

The east mouth of the pipe was a stone arch in the ditch bank, as wide as a man's shoulders, thirty paces below her and a little further along toward the bottom. There was ice across it. She could see the ice from here, grey and thick, a lid on the dark. The ditch in front of it was frozen too, a long white ribbon at the bottom of the bank, with the shadow of the bank lying over it.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she picked up the bucket and went back up the ramp.

---

At the second hour, everybody was where the book said.

Dessa sat on her crate at the south lip with the book open on her knee and her eyes shut. Elsie was on the crate beside hers with the old counter, under its tape. Rook sat on a third crate on Dessa's other side, very straight, with his stick across his knees and nothing to do.

The table stood behind the first two lamps of the upper line, near the lip, two boards on trestles on the gravel. Jab was at it, with his right hand held out from his side and his knife in its sheath. Dee stood at his left shoulder with her glass. Otto stood behind the boards with the river's thin Flask, unsealed now, in his big hands, and the small jar beside it with the last of the clean. The first yard lifter sat on a stool at the end of the trestle with his bandaged hand in his lap and his right hand flat on the boards, waiting to be a jar. The girl in mittens sat on the canvas, with her mittens off.

Along the upper line, the lamps stood on their poles, not lit, their glasses catching the light. Katori stood at the top of the dark lane between the third and the fourth. Daven stood between the fourth and the fifth, with his blade dark on his fist. At the fifth, at the top of the ramp, Toren stood with his weight in his heels and his left arm out of its cuff, and Silas stood a pace behind him, where he could see the ramp and the track below it and the whole of the slope down to the ditch.

Below them, on the track, the three lifters were already working. The second yard lifter, big and quiet, with his old bitten calf. The east lifters' foreman, a long grey man with a face like a gatepost. His son, sixteen, as long as his father and half as wide. They knelt on the stone between post three and post five with their jars and their sacks, each with his palm flat on the grey.

And Callie walked along the track among them with the hook and her counter.

At the south end of the track, where it met the lip path by the first lamp, Wyck stood with his bar. Ines stood on the lip above him with her bow strung, where she could see the whole of the east slope from the side.

That was the line. That was every name in the book.

Behind it, at the head of the made road where it came over the rise from the yard gate, there was a rope. The gate crew had strung it between two posts that morning. Behind the rope the station stood and watched.

The Director stood at the rope with her spectacles round her neck. Tamsin Greave stood beside her with her hat on, and her two with their staves behind her. Hallet stood on the Director's other side with the ledger open on his arm and his pen on its string. Behind them were the households. Callie's mother was there, on a stool someone had carried down. The first yard lifter's wife was there. The tall thin woman from the south row stood with her arms folded. The quiet man in the yard-office coat stood very still. The girl in mittens's mother was there, holding on to the rope. Sowerby and Keel stood a little apart, by a stretcher on the ground, with two orderlies.

And on the berm, a long way back, where the made road left the yard gate, two men stood together against the sky. One had a fur collar. The other's hands were in good gloves.

The sun came up over the east lip into a sky with nothing in it at all.

"Read," said Dessa.

"Twenty at the lip," said Elsie. "On mine. Eighteen, true. Two high." She held the old counter out over the bowl. "Thirty on the slope. It's lying."

On the track, Callie held her own counter out over the grey by post four.

"Twenty-six," she said. "On the track. In the grey. It's lying."

"Lying," said Jab, from the table.

Dessa wrote it.

*115th. Second hour. Places. Lip 18 true. Track 26. Lying.*

---

On the track, Callie read ground.

It was the thing she was best at in the world, and she had not done it properly since the road. She walked along the track ahead of the lifters with the hook, slowly, and looked at the stone under the grey, and said what she saw.

"Stone," she said, at post four. "All the way across. Kneel anywhere."

"Stone," at the next pace. "Stone."

"Hollow," she said, a little further down. "There. Under the grey. There's a gap under the slab. It'll take a man kneeling. It'll not take a man kneeling and a full sack." She pointed with the hook. "Kneel to the side. Put the sack on the stone."

The foreman looked at her. Then he looked at the place she was pointing at. He moved his knee a hand to the left.

"Sharp," said his son, who had knocked with his knuckles on the slab beside it. "She's right. That one's sharp."

"She's always right," said the second yard lifter, from his jar. He did not look up. "Ask the boy at the ramp. She read him a road."

They lifted.

Callie had watched lifters all winter. She had never watched them from this close, kneeling on the same stone. She watched the foreman put his palm flat on the grey and not move. She watched his face go still and inward. Then the grey under his palm began to come, slowly, into the mouth of the jar he had worked down into it, and he did not haul. *It comes at the rate it comes.* When the jar was full he stoppered it, and laid it in the sack, and moved his knee a pace, and began again.

It was slow. It was the slowest work Callie had ever seen. And it was not dangerous at all, not on a bright morning with the fog lying white in the bottom and the sun on the east side and the line and the table and the whole station watching. She kept looking for the danger, out of habit, and could not find it.

"Twenty-eight," she said, at the third hour, with the counter over the slope. "Lying."

"Lying," said Jab.

At the third hour the son ran the first full sack up the track to the south path. He went at a jog, on the stone, with the sack over his shoulder and the jars clinking softly in it. Wyck took it off him at the top and carried it up onto the lip and set it down by the crates. The gate crew's boy, who was the only person allowed past the rope, came and counted the jars and ran back to Hallet with the number.

*Six*, Hallet wrote. *Clean, by the foreman's palm.*

---

At the table, Jab drew.

The lifters came up busy. They did not mean to. They were kneeling with their palms flat on dirty ground for an hour at a time, and the ground was a winter's kills, and it went into their hands and up their arms the way it always did. They came up the track to the south path one at a time when Callie or Dessa said, and up onto the lip, and stood in front of the table.

The second yard lifter came first, at the fourth hour.

"Busy," said Jab, before he got there. "In the arms. Both. It's in the hands most."

"I know where it is," said the second yard lifter. "I've had it since I was fourteen." He held out his arms.

"My line's a finger," said Jab. "I'm a hand and a bit. I'll stop at a hand."

"At a hand," said Dee.

"Jar," said Jab.

The first yard lifter put his right palm flat against the small jar on the boards and held it against Jab's left hand. He did not move. He was very good at not moving. He had knelt on the east track since he was fourteen.

Jab drew. It came up out of the second yard lifter's arms, warm and gritty, through him, into the jar.

"Busy," he said. "Less."

"The side's going," said the first yard lifter. "By your thumb."

Jab had not felt it. He felt it now, when the man said it. "Turn it."

The first yard lifter turned it.

At the rope, forty paces off, Tamsin Greave watched. She did not move. She watched Jab's right palm on the yard lifter's forearm, and the jar in the burned man's good hand, and Dee at Jab's left shoulder with the glass.

"Three," said Dee. "Turns."

"Clean," said Jab.

"Vent," said Dee.

He went to the dirty edge of the lip and put his palm on it and let it go. He stood up.

"A hand," he said. "Just over."

"A hand, just over," said Dee. She did not write it. She held out the pencil.

Jab was writing it when the girl in mittens said, from the canvas, very clearly:

"*Off.*"

He looked at her.

He had not been drawing anybody. He was standing at the table with a pencil. But she was looking at his right hand, held out from his side, and he looked down at it, and it was flat. The fingers were straight, all of them, the way they went on a man's chest.

He had not known. He curled them.

"Thank you," he said.

At the rope, Tamsin Greave turned her head and looked at the Director.

"She's twelve," she said.

"She is," said the Director.

"She said it to him. To the healer. Not to somebody under him."

"She did," said the Director. "Everybody here says it to everybody. That's the whole of it." She did not look away from the table. "Write that down, Mr Hallet."

Hallet wrote it.

---

By the fifth hour there were twenty-eight jars by the crates.

The son had run four sacks up the track. Wyck had carried them up onto the lip. The gate crew's boy had counted them, and Hallet had written each number, and somebody behind the rope had started saying the numbers out loud as Hallet wrote them, and then somebody else had joined in, so that by the fourth sack half the households were counting under their breath. *Twenty-two. Twenty-eight.*

Otto went and stood over the jars by the crates and put his palm flat an inch above the nearest sack, and then the next, and then the next, and did not move.

"Three Flasks clean," he said. "Near enough. And a Flask that's dirty. From the bottom of the patch, by post five." He took his hand away. "More than four Flasks, I said. It's four. With what's still down there, it'll be five."

On the crate, Dessa wrote it.

On the track, Callie was standing at post five, at the whitewash line, with the hook and her counter.

It was the thickest place. The grey lay round the foot of the post a hand deep, a long low drift of it, frozen in at the bottom and coming loose on top in the sun. It ran right up to the line and under the white paint and on past it, down the track toward six, where nobody was allowed to go.

The lifters had worked down to it. They had been working toward it all morning, from post three, a pace at a time. Now they were at it. The foreman knelt a pace above the line with his palm flat on the edge of the drift. His son knelt beside him. The second yard lifter knelt on the other side, at the foot of the bank.

Callie read the ground.

"Clay," she said. "Here. Under the stone. Right at the post." She tapped the track with the end of the hook, at the line. "The stone's laid on clay from here down. It's where the ramp comes down. The clay goes under the track." She stepped back a pace, up the track. "Stone from here up. Good stone. To post four and past." She tapped again. Sharp. "Here's where it changes."

"Is it holding?" said the foreman.

"It's frozen," said Callie. "It's holding."

She held the counter out over the drift.

"Thirty-four," she said. "In the grey. At the line. It's lying."

"Lying," said Jab, from the table.

The sun was high. It was hot on Callie's face, and on the back of her neck, and on the hook in her hand. It was the first real heat since the autumn. The frost had gone off the grey on the slope below the track, and off the stone, and the slope was dark and wet now where it had been white. There was a smell coming up off it that she did not like. It was not ash. It was wet ground, a great deal of it, all thawing at once.

---

At noon Elsie stood up off her crate.

"Noon," she said.

She said it to Dessa, but she was not looking at Dessa. She was looking down the east slope, past the lower line, toward the bottom.

"The sun's over," she said. "It's going west. The shade's started." She pointed. "Post six is in it. The bottom of the track's in it. It's at the lower line, there, at the far end. Coming up."

Everybody on the lip looked.

It was very slight. If Callie had not been told, she would not have seen it. But the sun had moved, and the bank above the east track was throwing its shadow down the slope now instead of up it. At the far end, at the bottom, the dark wet slope below the lower line had gone a shade darker. The line between the shade and the sun lay across the slope like a tide mark.

"That's noon," said Dessa. "Everybody up. Lifters off the track."

On the track, the foreman did not get up.

"One more row," he said.

Dessa did not open her eyes.

"One more row," said the foreman again. He said it up the bank, toward the crates, in a voice that carried. He did not sound frightened. He sounded like a man arguing with a weighman about a sack. "The grey under post five. By the line. The thickest bit. It's right here. It's a Flask on its own." He put his palm flat on the edge of the drift. "Half an hour and it's up. Tonight, if the sun's like this, it'll be running into the ditch. Tomorrow it'll be in the pipe." He looked up the bank. "It's the Sag's own. It's lying here. You said."

On the crate, Dessa sat with her eyes shut and the book on her knee.

Callie, standing on the track a pace above the line, watched her decide. She could see it from the track, forty paces off and up. She could see Dessa's hand go flat on the page and stay there.

"Elsie," said Dessa. "The pipe."

Elsie looked down the slope at the ditch.

"Iced," she said. "I can see the ice. The ditch is in the bank's shade still. The sun's not been on it."

"Callie. The ramp."

Callie turned and looked up at the ramp, six paces of gravel going up the bank from the track to the fifth lamp, where Toren stood.

"It faces west," she said. "A bit. The sun's been on it since the middle of the morning." She looked at it harder. The top was dark. The bottom, where it met the track, was still grey. "The top's wet. The bottom's dry. The clay's still frozen under the bottom. I think." She heard herself. "I can't read it from here. I'd have to knock."

"Then knock."

Callie went to the foot of the ramp and knocked with the end of the hook on the gravel. Sharp. She knocked again, a pace up. Sharp, but less. She could not reach higher without climbing.

"Sharp at the bottom," she said. "Less halfway. I can't reach the top."

Dessa was quiet for one breath.

"Rook," she said.

Rook did not move on his crate. He had not said anything all morning.

"You said you'd not say anything unless I asked," said Dessa. "I'm asking."

Rook looked down the slope at the shade line, and the ditch, and the pipe's mouth in its bank, iced, in the shadow. He looked at the ramp. He looked at the drift round post five, and the three lifters kneeling at it, and Callie standing a pace above the line.

"It's your book," he said. "I'd go up. I'd go up now and lose the Flask." He looked at Dessa. "But it's your book. And the man's not wrong. It's a Flask."

Dessa sat with her hand flat on the page.

"Half an hour," she said.

She opened her eyes. She did not often do that on the lip. She looked down at the track, at the foreman.

"Half an hour by Dee's glass," she said. "Thirty turns. The foreman, his son, the second yard lifter, named. Above the line. Not one hand below it. Callie stays on the track reading." She looked at Elsie. "You watch the pipe and the shade. Nothing else. Every turn, you say where the shade is." She looked at Toren, at the top of the ramp. "And you stay where you are."

"I'm where I am," said Toren.

Dessa looked down at the book. She wrote. Callie could not see what, from the track. But she saw Dessa's pencil go along the line and stop, and go on, and stop, and she knew what it said, because she knew Dessa.

*Noon. Half an hour more, for the post-five drift. D. C.'s call.*

"Dee," said Dessa. "Turn the glass."

At the table, Dee turned the glass.

---

"One," said Dee, when the sand ran through.

The lifters knelt at the drift by post five, a pace above the white line, with their palms flat.

"Shade's at the lower line," said Elsie. "At the far end. Half the far end's in it."

"Five," said Dee.

The foreman stoppered a jar and laid it in the sack and began another. His son did the same. The second yard lifter worked at the foot of the bank, slowly, with his face set.

"Shade's at the lower line all along," said Elsie. "It's over it at the far end."

"Ten," said Dee.

On the track, Callie held her counter out over the drift and counted ten breaths.

"Thirty-six," she said. "Lying."

"Lying," said Jab.

"Fifteen," said Dee.

Callie stood a pace above the line and looked down the slope.

The shade was coming up. She could see it now, without being told. It lay over the lower line and over the slope above it and was coming up toward the track, a pace at a time, slow as the hand of a clock. Below it the slope was dark and wet. Above it the slope was dark and wet. The only difference was the light.

*Hounds go for the dark.*

"Shade's ten paces below the track," said Elsie. "At post five. Less at six."

"Twenty," said Dee.

The foreman had six jars in the last sack. Six jars from the drift by post five. He was working the seventh down into the grey a hand above the white line. His son had stopped. He was sitting back on his heels, looking down the slope at the shade.

"Da," he said.

"Two more," said the foreman.

On the crate, Elsie had stopped saying where the shade was.

She was standing up. She was looking at the ditch. She had the old counter in her hand, not held out, hanging at her side, forgotten. She was looking at the stone arch in the ditch bank below post five, at the lid of ice across its mouth.

"The ice," she said.

"What about it?" said Dessa.

"It's dark," said Elsie. "It was grey. It's gone dark." She stared at it. "It's wet. On top. The sun's not on it. It's wet anyway." Her voice went up. "It's wet from *under*. There's water under it. Coming down the ditch from the top of the slope. Under the ice. Into the pipe."

"Twenty-two," said Dee.

At the table, Jab put his hand flat on the boards.

He did not decide to. It went flat on its own, the way his hand went flat on his coat at night, and his face changed.

"Gathering," he said.

Everybody on the lip went still.

"*Gathering.*" Jab's voice was not loud. It was very clear. "It's not the joints. It's not the middle. It's—" He stopped. He had his eyes shut. "It's under the track. It's under post five. It's in the *pipe*. It's moving in the pipe. Like water going down a drain." His eyes opened. "It's coming *up*."

There was a sound from the ditch.

It was not loud. It was a crack, sharp and flat, like a slab of ice going on a pond. And then a rushing, low, under everything, like a river behind a wall.

The ice across the pipe's east mouth broke outward.

---

On the track, at post five, Callie saw it.

The ice went out of the stone arch in the ditch bank in pieces. Water came after it, brown and fast, in a gout, and spread across the bottom of the ditch and ran. And behind the water, out of the dark of the pipe, came fog.

It did not come the way fog came out of the joints at night, heaving, slow. It came the way smoke comes out of a flue when somebody opens a door in a wind. It came out of the pipe's mouth grey and thick and fast, and it did not lie in the ditch. It came up the slope. It came up out of the ditch below post five, up through the shade, up the dark wet slope, straight at the track.

Straight at the white line. Straight at the drift. Straight at the three lifters kneeling a pace above it.

Callie held the counter out. She did not decide to. Her hand did it.

The needle went up off the stop and up, and up.

"*Ninety,*" she said. "*On the track. A hundred.*"

"*Everybody up!*" said Dessa. Loud. The loudest Callie had heard her since the seventy-ninth. "*Lifters up! The ramp! Now!*"

---

At the top of the ramp, Toren set.

He did not wait. The book said *the ramp*, and the ramp was his, and the lifters were at the foot of it, three of them, getting up off their knees with the fog coming up the slope behind them. The ramp was the quick way. Ten breaths up. Eleven, Silas had said.

"*Set!*" said Toren. "*Steps!*"

The strut came up out of the gravel at the foot of the ramp, flat, a step, off the right.

The son was first. He came up off his knees and went for the ramp at a run without his sack, the way his father shouted at him to, and put his foot on the first strut. It held. Toren paid for the breath. The son's other foot found the gravel above it.

"*Step,*" said Toren. He let the first go and set the second, a pace up.

The son came up onto it. It held. He came up the third. The fourth. He came up over the top of the ramp onto the gravel by the fifth lamp's pole, and Silas caught him by the coat and pulled him past.

"*One up,*" said Silas.

Below, on the track, the second yard lifter was at the foot of the ramp with a sack on his shoulder. Six jars. The last row. He had picked it up off the stone without thinking, because it was in front of him and it was full.

"*Leave the sack!*" said the foreman.

The second yard lifter did not leave it. He put his foot on the first strut, with the sack on his shoulder, and came up.

"*Step,*" said Toren. He set the second.

The yard lifter came up onto it. It held. He was a big man with a full sack. Toren felt the weight of him come onto the strut. He paid for it by the breath, and the breath was expensive.

"*Step.*"

He set the third, halfway up the ramp.

The yard lifter put his weight on it.

And the third strut moved.

Toren felt it go. He felt it before he saw it. It was exactly what had happened in the first lane on the ninety-ninth, in the wet cinders by the drain: the foot of the strut, where it came up out of the ground, went sideways. It did not break. It slid. Halfway up the ramp the gravel lay over the clay, and the sun had been on the clay since the middle of the morning, and the clay was not frozen any more. It was wet. It was soft as butter under the gravel, and the strut's foot went through the gravel into it and sideways like a boot on ice.

The strut leaned.

The second yard lifter went with it.

He went backward. He did not fall cleanly, the way Wyck taught on the sand heap. He had a sack of jars on his shoulder and his weight was all wrong, and he went backward off the leaning strut and down the ramp, sliding, and his left foot went under him as he went. Toren heard it. It was a small sound, a stick going.

The sack went off his shoulder. It burst on the gravel. Jars went down the ramp, rolling, and two of them broke, and grey went everywhere.

Toren set again. He did not decide to. He set a second strut, below the first, to catch the man. It was the old thing, the thing that came up out of him before he had decided anything, the thing Katori had seen four times on the fifty-eighth. Two struts at once. Twice the price.

He reached for it. He leaned out over the top of the ramp to set it lower, further, where the man was going.

The gravel at the top of the ramp went under his boots.

It was wet too. Everything was wet. He felt the lip of the ramp give under him like a step that was not there, and then he was going down it on his right side, sliding, with gravel going down beside him and his right leg folded under him wrong. Something in his right knee went with a hot, white feeling. Both struts went out. He did not let them go. They went because he was not standing on anything to hold them from.

He came to rest on the track at the foot of the ramp, on his side, beside the second yard lifter, in a spill of wet gravel and broken jars and grey.

He lay there.

Above him, at the top of the ramp, there was nobody. There was the fifth lamp's pole against the bright sky, and Silas's face, white, looking over the edge.

The ramp was a chute of wet gravel and clay. Six paces of it. Nothing would stand on it now. Nobody would go up it.

"*Ramp's gone,*" said Silas. His voice cracked in the middle and then steadied. "*Ramp's gone. Two down. Toren and the yard lifter. On the track. At the foot.*"

---

At the south lip, Dessa had her eyes open.

She sat on the crate with the book on her knee and looked down at the track. At the foot of the ramp. At two men lying in the gravel there, and the foreman standing over them, and Callie a pace above the white line with her hook and her counter held out. At the fog coming up the slope from the pipe, grey and fast, through the shade.

"*Read,*" she said.

Callie read. She held her counter out over the track and counted ten breaths, and it was the longest ten breaths Dessa had ever sat through.

"*A hundred and thirty,*" said Callie. Her voice came up the slope flat and thin. "*Forty.*" A pause. "*A hundred and fifty.*" Another. "*Red. On the track. It's red.*"

On the crate beside Dessa, Elsie had her own counter out over the bowl.

"*A hundred and thirty-two at the lip,*" she said. "*On mine. Two high. Not red here.*" She did not look away from the track. "*It's red down there. Under the fog. She's in it.*"

Dessa shut her eyes.

She did not do it slowly. She shut them the way you shut a book you have read the end of. Her hand lay flat on the page.

"*Red,*" she said, "*and bodies trapped.*"

The lip went silent.

It was not the silence of a watch at night. It was the silence of everybody on the south lip and at the rope and on the berm stopping what they were doing at once.

"*Everybody who isn't needed to get them out,*" said Dessa, "*goes out. Now.*" She did not raise her voice now. She did not need to. "*The rope goes back to the yard gate. All of it. The Director. The households. The river. Everybody behind the rope, up the made road to the gate. Now.*"

At the rope, nobody moved for one breath.

Then the Director turned round.

"You heard her," she said. To all of them. To the households, and the river, and Hallet with his ledger, and Sowerby by the stretcher. "Up the made road. To the gate. That's the red-call rule. It's signed. I signed it." She did not look back at the Sag. "Move."

They moved.

Tamsin Greave did not.

She stood at the rope with her hat on. She looked down at the track, at the two men at the foot of the ramp, at the fog.

"I'm a holder," she said. She said it to Dessa, across forty paces of gravel, not loud. "River tradition. I can lay a road. I've laid roads on the river for twenty years."

Dessa did not open her eyes.

"Have you stood on this ground?" she said.

Tamsin Greave was quiet.

"No," she said.

"Then no," said Dessa. "You don't know where it breaks. You don't know where our people stand. You'd lay a road across them." Her voice did not change. "Go back with the Director."

Tamsin Greave stood at the rope for one more breath. Callie would have seen her face, if she had been looking. She was not. Nobody was. Only Rook, on his crate, turned his head and looked, and saw the river woman's mouth go thin, and saw her turn, and go up the made road after the Director with her hat in her hand.

"*The fewest who can do it,*" said Dessa. "*Named. Out loud. As they go in.*"

She did not stop to think. She had thought already. She had thought on the hundred and seventh, in the shed, with the chalk on the boards, and it was in the book.

"*Wyck,*" she said. "*Down the track. The bar.*"

At the south path, Wyck was already moving.

"*Jab,*" said Dessa. "*Down the track. The fence. Then whatever's next.*"

At the table, Jab took his knife out of its sheath.

"*Dee. Down the track. His watch.*"

Dee picked up her glass.

"*Otto. Down the track. The jar. Take the small one and two out of the thin.*"

Otto put his palm over the thin Flask and took out two Handfuls with the horn spoon, fast, into the small jar, without spilling any.

"*Daven,*" said Dessa. "*Along the top. Then down the track behind Wyck. Light when you're called.*"

Between the fourth and fifth lamps, Daven turned.

"*Silas. The end. Stay on the line. Move along it to keep them in sight.*"

At the fifth lamp, Silas said, "*The end,*" and nothing else.

"*Katori. The line. Anything that comes up the bank toward the lamps.*"

"*The line,*" said Katori.

"*Ines. Where you are. Anything on the slope below the track.*"

On the lip above the south path, Ines nocked an arrow.

"*Elsie. Read. The bowl. The shade. Out loud.*"

"*Reading,*" said Elsie.

"*The girl,*" said Dessa. "*Out. With the Director.*"

The girl in mittens had stood up off the canvas. She stood there with her mittens in her hands.

"Who says *off*?" she said. "To Jab. If I'm not there."

"*I do,*" said Dee. She was already going, past the table, toward the south path, with her glass in her fist. "*Go.*"

The girl in mittens went. She went up the made road after the Director, running, with her mittens in her hand, and at the rope she stopped and looked back once, and then went on.

"*The first yard lifter,*" said Dessa. "*The table. The jar. You're what's left of the table when they come up.*"

"*The table,*" said the first yard lifter. He put his right palm flat on the thin Flask and did not move.

"*Rook,*" said Dessa.

Rook was already standing. He stood with his stick.

"*Out,*" said Dessa. "*You're not anything. You said.*"

Rook looked at her. Then he looked down at the track, at Toren lying at the foot of the ramp. His face did nothing at all.

"I said," he said.

He turned and went up the made road on his stick, slowly, in three movements every few paces, and did not look back. Dessa heard him go. She did not open her eyes.

"*That's the names,*" she said. She wrote them. She wrote them all, in the book, in order, as they went. "*Callie, Toren, the foreman, the yard lifter. On the track. In the red.*" She wrote that too.

Then she looked down at the track.

---

On the track, Callie stood a pace above the white line with the hook in her hands.

The fog had come up out of the ditch to the shade line, and through it, and it was coming up the last of the slope below the track. It did not heave. It ran. It came up the wet dark slope in a long grey sheet, low and fast, through the shadow of the bank, and there were things standing up in it.

Husks. She saw the first. Grey on grey, many-legged, the colour of an old wasp nest, standing up out of the running fog the way a husk had stood up out of the heap in a corner, once, a long time ago. And another, behind it. And another.

They came up the slope toward the track.

Toward the foreman, standing over the yard lifter at the foot of the ramp. Toward Toren, lying on his side in the gravel. Toward the six jars spilled and rolling on the stone at the foot of the ramp, and the grey from the two broken ones spread across the track. Toward the drift round post five, a winter's ash, worked and lying.

Toward Callie, with a counter on her hip that said red.

She looked at the slope. She looked at the stone under her feet, at the line where the stone ended and the clay began, a pace below her, at the post. She looked at Toren, on his side, with his right leg folded wrong. He was looking at her.

She knew what Keel had said. *Not over bodies.* She knew what Dessa had said. *Not holding unless named.*

She looked up the bank at the crate.

"*Ward,*" she said. "*Over the four. Named?*"

There was no pause at all.

"*Named,*" said Dessa. "*Callie. Ward. A door's worth. Say when.*"

Callie laid the hook down on the stone at the edge of the track, with its curve toward the slope, and its shaft along the edge, between the four of them and the fog.

She let the Ward go into it.
