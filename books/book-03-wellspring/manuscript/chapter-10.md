# Chapter 10 — Callie: The Line She Reads

Keel looked at her ribs on the ninety-seventh, six weeks from the fifty-fifth to the day, and did not say anything until she had finished.

She had Callie stand with her arms out, both of them this time, all the way. She unwound the bandage and dropped it in the basin. She pressed the side in three places, the same three as always, and then in a fourth Callie had not known was a place, low down under the arm, where Keel's thumb went in and found something and stayed.

"There," said Keel.

"Yes."

"How much?"

"Less than the first three."

Keel took her thumb away. She went round to Callie's back and pressed there too, both hands flat, and told her to breathe in as far as she could. Callie breathed in. It went further than it had gone since the forty-eighth. It stopped at the place under the arm, and she let it out again.

"Knit," said Keel. She went to the basin and began to wash her hands. "Not strong. There's a difference. Sowerby says that to everybody. I taught it him." She dried her hands. "You can read. You can walk without that hook if you like, though you'll not like. You can stand at a lip and you can stand at a plate. You can hold—" she held up one finger, "—a door's worth of whatever it is you do. Standing. On your own two feet. Not off a fall. Not a road. Not over bodies. Nothing you'd mind letting go of in a hurry."

"How long's a door's worth?"

"You tell me," said Keel. "I do the bones. You know what a door is." She hung the towel on its nail. "When it hurts there, under the arm, that's where you stop. Not when you're tired. Not when your nose goes. There. If I find you've held anything heavier than a door, I'll strap you back up, and you'll do another three weeks, and you'll do them in the ward where I can see you."

Callie put her shirt on. She did it with both arms. It was the first time since the forty-eighth that she had not done the left one last, slowly, with her elbow tucked.

"Thank you," she said.

Keel looked at her.

"Don't thank me. Thank your mother. She sent me a note every week." She opened the door. "Six words a week. *How are the ribs. Tell me.* Left-handed. I've kept them."

---

She went to the east shed, because that was where Dee was, and Dee had a glass.

She had not held anything since the forty-eighth. Forty-nine days. She had counted them without wanting to, the way she counted everything, and every one of them had sat in her like a stone in a boot. She had read from the crate. She had sat on her hands. She had filled lamps one-handed with a can. And every night on the lip, when the fog heaved, her body had gone hard under her to stand behind a wall that was not there.

Dee was at the long table with a cup of something hot and a book face down beside it. She looked up when Callie came in.

"Keel," she said.

"Knit. Not strong. A door's worth. Standing."

Dee put the cup down. She picked up her glass, the small one with the brass frame and the grey sand, and turned it once in her fingers so that the sand began to run.

"Show me a door's worth," she said.

Callie went to the shed door. It was shut. It was a good door, old planks and a cross-brace, and the cold came round its edges in thin lines. She took the hook in both hands and laid it across the frame at chest height, the curve against the left post, the long shaft against the right, the way she had laid it across the culvert and the stair and the arch and a dozen doors since the nineteenth.

She let the Ward go into the steel.

It came. That was the first thing, and it nearly made her cry, which she had not expected. It came out of her the way it always had, from the well, the water she could feel at the bottom of herself when she went still. It went down her arms and into the hook and stayed there. The hook stopped being a hook. It became a still thing across a door, simply there, the way a wall is there.

And her whole body went hard to stand behind it.

She felt it go. Her back. Her belly. The place under her arms. All of it, every muscle she owned, setting like mortar behind the Ward the way Keel had said on the sixty-first. *You think it's in your hands. It's in your back and your belly.* She had not believed her. She believed her now. It was like trying to stand up straight with a sack of grain tied to your ribs.

"Breathe," said Dee.

Callie breathed. It went in short.

"Where is it?"

"Under the arm," said Callie. "Already."

"Then that's where you stop. Not yet. When it's more."

Callie stood. The hook lay across the door, still. The cold stopped coming in round the edges where the hook lay; it came in only above and below. She counted her breaths without meaning to and then stopped counting, because counting made it worse. She looked at the grain of the planks instead.

The place under her arm got bigger. It did not get worse all at once. It grew, the way a puddle grows when rain falls into it, a little at a time, until it was all she could feel.

"Now," she said.

She let go.

The hook was a hook again. She took it off the door and stood holding it in both hands, breathing, and the cold came in round all the edges at once.

"Twenty-four," said Dee. She had not been watching the glass. She had been counting Callie's breaths. "Twenty-four, standing, a still hook on a door, with nothing pushing on it." She put the glass down. "You're grey. Sit."

Callie sat on the bench. She was grey. She could feel it, a coldness in her face, though she was not cold.

"It used to be minutes," she said. "On the culvert. On the arch."

"It used to be you with two ribs that weren't broken," said Dee. "It'll come back. Not this week." She took the stub of pencil out of her coat and held it out. "Write it. Your own book. Not Dessa's. Dessa's is for the line. This is for you."

Callie took the pencil. She did not have her own book. She wrote it on the back of her hand, which was what she had done at the scale house when she was six and there was no paper.

*97th. Door. 24 breaths. Stop: under the arm.*

Dee looked at it.

"That's a floor," she said. "Not your old one. Today's. Write tomorrow's tomorrow."

---

On the ninety-ninth, when the thaw came, Callie went to the south stable and found the weighbridge girl in Duchess's stall with a hoof pick.

"Toren wants your name," said Callie. "For his card. He asked me. I said I'd ask you."

The girl did not look up. She had Duchess's near fore between her knees and was working the pick round the frog, carefully, while the mare leaned on her and looked at the wall.

"Why didn't he ask me?"

"He did. On the ninety-fourth. You said *ask Callie*." Callie stood at the stall door. "So I'm asking."

The girl set the hoof down. Duchess shifted her weight onto it and blew.

"You can say it," said the girl. "It's not a secret. I signed it on the sheet. I told you at the stable. Nobody asked before, that's all." She straightened up, with the pick in her hand. "You've never said it."

"No."

"Not once. You wrote it. In the book. You said *it's hers* and shut it." She looked at Callie straight on. "Say it."

Callie felt her face go hot. She did not know why. It was only a name.

"Elsie," she said.

The girl nodded once.

"There," she said. "Now it's said." She came out of the stall and latched the door behind her. "Two r's in Marr. If it's going on a card, it's spelt right. And if anybody writes I saw something, they write what I saw." She hung the pick on its nail. "Not what they wanted me to see."

"Who's writing what you saw?"

Elsie looked at her.

"Nobody, yet," she said. "But they're saying it. At the pump." She wiped her hands on her coat. "*The weighbridge girl saw the hounds go west before anybody. Saw it in the fog like a seer.* A woman said that to me at the pump on the eighty-eighth. *Like a seer.*" Her mouth went thin. "I looked at where the dark was. That's all I did. Anybody could have."

"Nobody did," said Callie.

"You did."

"I was sitting on my hands," said Callie. "You said it. You stood up and said it. That's not the same."

Elsie looked at her for a long moment.

"No," she said. "It's not." She picked up the bucket. "So when it goes in the book, it goes in as *looked at the dark*. Not as *saw*. If you put *saw*, they'll want me to do it again. On purpose. Every night. Like it's a thing I've got." She went past Callie toward the water trough. "It isn't a thing I've got. It's a thing I did once."

Callie stood in the stable doorway with the wet south wind coming round her.

She thought about her grandfather saying *who asked you*, and the farmer paying, and the number going in the book in her hand. She thought about how good that had felt. She thought about how much she had wanted, ever since, to be the person who read it.

Elsie did not want to be that person. Elsie wanted what she saw written down properly, so that it belonged to the page and not to her.

Callie had not known that you could want that.

---

The river cart came to the weighbridge on the hundredth, in the afternoon, and Callie was at the plate because her mother was.

It came round the corner of the south yard from the gate with the woman in the waxed coat walking beside the mules' heads, and the man and the younger woman behind, with their staves. The weighman came out of his hut. He looked at the cart. He looked at the woman.

"River," he said.

"River," said the woman. "Second weir. Tamsin Greave." She did not offer her hand. "I've two Flasks to weigh. Lent, not sold. I'd like them on your board."

The weighman considered this for about as long as carrying a block.

"Lent to who?"

"Your Director. Against my stations. In my name." The woman looked past him at the board on the wall of the hut, and Callie watched her read it. She read it the same way the oldest of the four had read it on the sixty-first: from the top, all of it, as you read a notice nailed to a gate. But she did it faster. She stopped at *KATORI'S*, and at *HOME LINE*, and at the struck line, and at the box the weighman had ruled round *OWED*. She stopped longest at the box.

"Owed," she said.

"Owed," said the weighman.

"To who?"

"To the store," said Callie's mother, from the stool in the window.

The woman turned her head. Callie's mother sat in the window with the tally board on her knee and the pencil in her left hand. She looked back at the woman, slowly, in the way she looked at everything now, with all the time in the world.

"The Sag ran dry on the seventy-ninth," said her mother. "The Director brought a Flask down from under this plate. In the dark. That's the box. It's owed to the store. It'll be owed till something comes back."

"And who's *D. C.*?"

"A girl with a book."

"A girl."

"She's fifteen," said her mother. Each word had its space in front of it. "She's the command."

Tamsin Greave looked at the board a while longer. Callie could not tell what she thought. Her face was long and weathered and did not do much, like Katori's. But her eyes went back to *HOME LINE* once more, and then down to *OWED*, and then to the window.

"I had a cousin on your household sheet," she said to Callie's mother. "Six years ago. Twenty-eight days. She came in at your south gate with a boy who'd been near a drift. She went home in the spring owing your store a winter's ash and the boy's bed." She did not raise her voice. "She paid it off in three years. Off a river ferry."

"I'm on the sheet," said Callie's mother. "A year."

"Lucky you."

"No," said her mother. "I was reviewed." She put the pencil down on the tally board. "I sat at a table in a long room with my daughter beside me while three people read my folder out. I was lucky to be reviewed. I wasn't lucky to be on it." She looked at the woman. "Your cousin was on it too. I'm sorry for that."

Tamsin Greave did not answer for a moment.

Then she turned to the cart and pulled the canvas back.

---

They weighed the river ash on the plate in front of everybody who happened to be in the south yard, which by the end was quite a lot of people.

There were two Flasks, in the old jars, stoppered and wired. The man with the stave lifted them down off the cart one at a time and set them on the plate, and the weighman went inside to the beam and moved the little weight along it with one finger.

"Eight," said the weighman, for the first.

"Eight," said her mother, and wrote it.

"Eight," said the weighman, for the second.

"Read it," said her mother.

She did not say it to Callie. She said it to the plate in general, as she said everything at the plate. But Callie was standing at the end of it with the hook and her counter, and she knew who it was for.

She held the counter at a hand's width from the first jar and counted ten breaths. Then the second. She did not hurry. Tamsin Greave was watching her, and so was the man with the stave, and so was Elsie, who had come across from the stable and was standing at the corner of the hut with straw on her coat.

The first jar was clean. It sat on the needle where clean sat, and stayed.

The second was not dirty. It read the same at the start. But it went up off the stop a little slower, and sat a little lower when it settled, and when Callie held it longer it did not come up to where the first had come. It was not much. It was the difference between two buckets that weigh the same and one of them has been watered.

"Thin," she said.

Tamsin Greave looked at her.

"It weighs a Flask," said Callie. "It's got less in it. A tenth. About." She lowered the counter. "Call it seven."

"Who says?"

"I say," said Callie. "It's my call. It goes in the margin with my name on it. That's how this board does thin." She looked at the woman. "If you don't like it, you can read it yourself. The counter's there."

Tamsin Greave looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked at the man with the stave.

"Bar," she said.

The man came to the plate. He put his palm flat over the second jar, an inch from its mouth, and did not move. It was the thing Otto did. Callie had not known river people did it too.

"Thin," said the man, after a while. "She's right. It's off the bottom of the long drift. I said it'd be thin when we lifted it."

"You did," said Tamsin Greave. She looked back at Callie. "Seven. Write it."

Callie's mother wrote it. The weighman came out with the chalk and wrote it on the board, under *OWED*, where there was room.

*RIVER (T. G.): 2 FLASKS, LENT. 1 THIN (C.'S CALL).*

Tamsin Greave read it, and nodded.

"That's what I wanted," she said. "In chalk. Where everybody walks past it." She turned to Callie's mother. "When your Director forgets she owes the river, the board won't."

"She'll not forget," said her mother.

"Everybody forgets," said Tamsin Greave. "That's what a board's for."

The weighman, by the hut door with the chalk in his fingers, looked at her as if she had just said something he had been waiting thirty years for somebody else to say.

---

There was a third thing in the cart, under the straw at the back, and Tamsin Greave unwrapped it herself.

Glass. Three lamp glasses, tall, with the slight bulge in the middle, each one wrapped in sacking and then in straw and then in more sacking. She unwrapped the first and held it up in the grey light. It was greener than the station's glass. There was a bubble in it near the bottom, round and perfect as an eye.

"Your Director said three cracked," said Tamsin Greave. "Lower line. No glass that shape this side of the hills." She turned it in her hands. "There's a glasshouse at the weir. It's not that shape either. It's the nearest we make."

Elsie had come out from the corner of the hut. She stood beside the cart and looked at the glass, and then she held out her hands, and Tamsin Greave, after a moment, put it in them.

Elsie turned it over. She looked at the base, where the glass sat on the lamp's collar. She put two fingers inside the rim and felt round.

"It's a finger short," she said. "At the bottom. It'll sit loose on the collar." She looked up. "Felt. Round the base. Packed tight. Then it'll sit."

"Will it hold a flame in a wind?"

"If it's packed," said Elsie. "And if somebody looks at it every watch." She gave it back. "I'll look at it."

Tamsin Greave wrapped it up again.

"They're not a gift," she said. Not to Elsie. To the weighman, and Callie's mother in the window, and anybody else in the south yard who was listening, which by then was a good many. "They're glass. Your Director can pay for them or not. She can say what they're worth. But I'll not have anybody at this plate saying the river gave Meridian something for nothing." She looked at the board. "I hear there's been oil come up the east road for nothing."

Nobody said anything.

"I hear it's in your clerk's ledger as paid," said Tamsin Greave. "Good. Your clerk's got sense." She tucked the sacking round the last glass. "The river had a letter this winter too. Under a stamp. The same stamp as the oil, your clerk says, when he showed me." She put the glasses back in the straw. "Signed *N. Vale*. Offering to buy the river's ash. All of it. At the plate. At a fair price."

"What did the river say?" said Callie's mother.

"The river said nothing yet," said Tamsin Greave. "The river's very good at saying nothing." She pulled the canvas back over the cart. "But there's a ferry at the third weir. Two winters ago a woman bought it. She paid well. Better than it was worth. The ferryman's family were glad." She tied the canvas rope off with a knot Callie did not know. "Then the ferry started charging what she liked. And there's no other way across for eleven miles."

"Vale," said Callie.

"Vale," said Tamsin Greave. "Nessa Vale. That was the name on the deed." She looked at Callie. "She pays well. That's the first thing she does. Remember that, if you're the one who reads things here."

She clicked her tongue at the mules, and they leaned into their collars, and the cart went round the corner of the south yard toward the yard office.

Callie stood at the end of the plate with the counter in her hand.

Her mother, in the window, picked the pencil back up and wrote something on the corner of the tally board, small, left-handed. Callie could not see what. When she looked later, after her mother had gone in, it said: *100th. River. 2, lent. 1 thin. Glass, 3, to be paid. Ferry. N. Vale.* And under it, smaller: *She read the box first.*

---

On the hundred and second Callie tried to find her Edge and could not use it.

She had always had it. It was the second door, the one the register wrote down beside the first, the one that came into the hook with the Ward because it was the same hook. Katori had taught her to sweep it off the curve on the nineteenth day in the north yard. *You lean, and it comes. Then you finish the pull.* She had swept it at a hound on the road and at a husk at the culvert, a white curved slash off the inside of the hook that cut like a scythe.

She stood in the north yard by the sand heap with the hook in both hands and leaned.

The place under her arm said no.

It did not say it quietly. It said it the way it had said it on the morning of the sixty-first, with the boot, so loudly that she straightened up and stood there with her eyes shut and did not breathe for a while.

"That's the lean," said Katori.

She was standing by the shed wall with her cap pulled down and her hands in her coat, the way she stood now, like somebody who had come to watch. Jab was beside her, because Callie had asked him to be. He had his right hand held out from his side.

"I know it's the lean," said Callie.

"The sweep's all lean," said Katori. "It's the pull that does it. You lean into the curve and the light comes up the inside of it, and then you finish the pull and it goes. Off the end. Across whatever's in front of you." She did not take her hands out of her coat. "Your ribs'll not let you pull. Not this month."

"Then I've got no Edge."

"You've got the same Edge you always had," said Katori. "You've no sweep. That's not the same."

Callie looked at the hook.

"I could do what you do," she said to Jab. "Lie it down. Light it along the ground. Like at the far gap."

"No," said Jab.

She looked at him.

"Don't copy my knife," said Jab. He said it plainly, the way he said everything. "My light sticks at the belly. It always has. I don't know why. It's my knife and my door and that's where it sticks." He looked at the hook in her hands. "Yours'll stick somewhere else. If you try to make it do what mine does, you'll spend a month finding out it won't." He was quiet. "Toren tried to copy mine on the road. The heel. It took him nine days to find out his wasn't mine."

"Then what?"

Jab shrugged.

"What's the hook for?" said Katori.

Callie looked at her.

"Not what Rook said it's for," said Katori. "Not what I taught you to do with it. What do *you* use it for? Every day. When you're not holding anything and you're not cutting anything."

Callie looked down at the hook.

Four feet of conduit and a foot of curve. The grip, where it said *Eleven* when the technician washed it. The tag, *pen, 35th* and *Caul Hill, 55th*, with room under it. She had leaned on it for forty-nine days like a stick. She had laid it across her knees on the crate. She had pointed with it.

She had pointed with it.

At the scale house, at the culvert, on the road, on the lip. *There.* *Stone.* *Soft.* *That's where it broke.* *That's where the dark is.* She had stood with the hook and pointed at the line in the ground that nobody else could see, and said where it was, and then everybody had had to take her word for it.

"Reading," she said slowly. "I point. At where it changes."

"Then light that," said Katori.

---

She knelt on the cinders by the second post, where the old drain ran under the lane and the ground went soft. She knew where it changed. She had read it for Toren three days ago: soft here, stone there, at the frame. There was a line between them in the ground, a real line, as real as the edge of a road, though you could not see it with your eyes. She could see it. She had always been able to.

She laid the hook down on the cinders, flat, with the curve on the ground and the point toward the line.

She did not lean. She did not pull. She looked at the line where the soft met the stone, and she let the Edge come into the hook the way the Ward came, from the well, slowly, without any sweep in it at all.

It came up along the inside of the curve, white.

It always had. That part was the same. But she did not finish the pull. There was no pull to finish. The light came up the inside of the curve and reached the point and kept going.

It went off the point of the hook and along the ground.

It did not go straight. That was the first thing she saw, and it made her breath stop. Jab's light went straight out from his knife, along the ground, as straight as the blade. Hers did not. It went off the point and bent. It went along the cinders in a line, a thin white line a finger high, and it bent where the ground bent. It followed the place where the soft met the stone. It went along the edge of the old drain, curving where the drain curved, for two paces, two and a half, and then it stopped.

It stopped exactly where the soft ground ended under the frame's footing.

Callie knelt there with her hands on the hook and looked at it.

It was the line. Her line. The one she read. The one she had pointed at all her life and said *there*, and everybody had had to take her word for it. It was lying on the ground in front of her in white light, where anybody could see it.

"Oh," said Jab.

Katori did not say anything. She had taken her hands out of her coat.

Behind Callie, somebody came round the end of the shed and stopped. It was Silas, with a sack over his shoulder. He stood there and stared at the line of light lying on the cinders along the edge of the drain.

"What's that?" he said.

"That's where it's soft," said Callie. Her voice was not steady. "That's where the strut slid. Everything on that side of the light's soft. Everything on this side's stone." She swallowed. "Don't set on that side."

Silas looked at the line. Then he took two steps to the right, onto the stone side, very carefully, and stood there.

"I can see it," he said, in a voice of wonder. "I can see where it's soft."

The line went out.

It did not fade. It went, all at once, the way a lamp goes when the wick's dry. Callie felt it go. She felt where it had come from, too: not from the Ward's well, where the water was, but from somewhere next to it, a different place, a smaller one. The light had drunk that place down fast, faster than the Ward ever drank. She had held the line for perhaps fifteen breaths and the small place was nearly empty.

She sat back on her heels. The place under her arm had not said anything at all.

"No lean," said Katori. "No pull." She came across the cinders and stood over the hook. "It doesn't cost your ribs."

"It costs something else," said Callie. "Quick."

"Everything costs something else," said Katori. She looked at the place where the line had lain, at the dark edge of the drain in the cinders. "Can it turn a hound?"

"I don't know."

"It's worked light," said Jab. "Off a door. On a hook. It's the same as mine. Just—" he looked for a word, "—bent."

"It's the same if it's the same," said Katori. "We'll not know till something comes up a path and meets it." She put her hands back in her coat. "Don't tell Dessa it's a fence till it's been one."

---

Dee measured it that afternoon in the east shed with the glass.

She wanted the change measured, not just the line, because the change was what would matter on a lip. *You'll not light it from nothing. You'll be holding something, and something'll happen, and you'll have to put down the one and pick up the other.* So Callie stood at the shed door again and laid the hook across it, and let the Ward go into the steel, and held.

"Now change," said Dee.

The Ward and the Edge were two doors in the same hook, but they would not both be open at once. That was the rule. Rook had made her drill it a hundred times on the road. *Two to change. One, you let go. Two, you light. Nothing in between but you.* She had always done it fast, a quick count, the light coming up the curve almost before the Ward was gone.

"*Letting go*," said Callie.

She let the Ward out of the hook.

One.

The hook was just steel across a door. The cold came round all the edges.

At that moment Katori, who had been standing outside the shed with the old sack on its rope, let the sack go against the door.

It hit. The door shook on its hinges and the hook jumped in Callie's hands. It did not fall. But there was nothing in it, nothing holding, and the door moved inward a finger's width before the latch caught it.

Two.

She lit. The light came up the inside of the curve and off the point and lay along the bottom of the door, along the line of the sill, where the shed floor met the frame. It was a short line. It bent round the post.

"That was the gap," said Dee. She had the glass in her fist. "That was two. In the two, the door moved." She looked at the sill. "Katori."

"I know," said Katori, through the door. "That's why I did it."

Callie held the line. She held it for eighteen breaths, by Dee's count, and then it went.

"Again," said Dee.

They did it again. Ward on the door. *Letting go.* One, the gap. Two, the light. Eighteen breaths, near enough, and the small place drunk dry. Then a rest, while Dee watched her face, and then the Ward again, which came back from the well, the bigger place, untouched.

On the third change Callie's nose began to bleed.

She did not notice at first. She was looking at the line on the sill. Then something warm ran over her lip, and she put her hand up, and it came away red.

"Stop," said Dee.

Callie stopped. She let the light go and sat down on the bench with her head back and her hand over her nose, and Dee gave her a cloth.

"Third change," said Dee. She wrote it on the square of paper by the scale, not in Dessa's book. *102nd. C. Ward on a door to the line on the sill. Two to change. In the two, nothing. Line: 18 breaths. Third change: nose.* She looked at Callie. "So it's two. Two changes in a watch. Not three. On the third, your nose tells you, and by the time your nose tells you, you're under where you should be."

"It's not my floor," said Callie, through the cloth. "My floor's the arm. Keel said."

"The arm's the Ward's floor," said Dee. "The nose is the change's. They're two things. You've got two doors. You've got two floors." She put the pencil down. "Nobody said you'd only have to watch one."

Callie sat with her head back and looked at the ceiling of the shed, where the drip ran down the beam to the bucket and went *tick*.

Two doors. Two floors. And in between them, every time, a count of two where she held nothing and anything could come through.

She wrote it on the back of her hand, under the other.

*102nd. Line: 18. Change: 2, nothing in it. Third change: nose. Stop at two.*

---

The technician brought the old counter back on the hundred and third.

He came to the weighbridge with it in his good hand, the left, and held it out to Elsie, who was at the end of the plate. He had mended the needle. He had not been able to mend the glass. There was a strip of clear tape across the cracked face, from one side to the other, holding the two halves together, and the yellowed numbers showed through it a little blurred.

"It'll read," he said. "Not as it did. The needle's new. It sits different on the pin." He looked at it. "Test it against hers."

They tested it at the plate against the watch's jars, the way they had on the sixty-second, with Callie's mother in the window and the weighman inside at the beam. Elsie read with the old one. Callie read with hers.

"Sixteen," said Elsie.

"Fourteen," said Callie.

They did the next jar. Two apart. And the next.

"Two," said Elsie. "It's two high now. Not one."

"Write it," said Callie's mother.

Elsie wrote it on the corner of the tally board, under the old line, which she did not rub out. *E. shed counter: 1 high.* And under it: *Mended 103rd. Now 2 high.*

Then she unhooked Callie's counter from her belt, in its bag, and held it out on her flat palm.

"Yours," she said.

Callie took it. It was warm from Elsie's hip. She hooked it onto her own belt, where it had hung every day from the nineteenth to the seventy-ninth, and it sat there as if it had never been away.

"Now we've one each again," said Elsie.

"And we know they're two apart."

"And we say which," said Elsie. She hooked the old counter on her own belt, with its tape. "That's not *one* each. That's two of us."

---

That night, on the second watch, a hound came up the lip path.

It was the first watch since the frost with winter oil in every lamp. Elsie had filled them at dusk from the new cans, and they burned tall and yellow along the upper line and the lower, and the three river glasses on the lower line, packed with felt at the collar, burned a little greener than the rest. Elsie had looked at all three before she sat down. She had said they were holding.

Callie was on the crate beside Dessa with her own counter. Elsie was on the other crate with the old one. They read in turn. Callie said hers, Elsie said hers, and Dessa wrote whichever she needed and sometimes both.

It was a small night. Three pushes. Husks at the lamps, and the line meeting them. Then, at the fifth hour, Ines on the west rim called across the bowl, flat and thin as voices go over water.

"*Path. One. Coming your way. I can't get an angle.*"

Callie was on her feet before Dessa spoke. She did not decide to be. She had the hook in her hand.

"Callie," said Dessa. Her eyes were shut. "Named. Top of the path. *Not holding.*"

"Not holding."

"And not your nose."

"Not my nose," said Callie, and went.

The first south lamp stood at the top of the lip path, where the rope began on its first iron stake. Beyond it the path went round the west rim into the dark, a pace wide, the rope on one side and the slope on the other. There was no lantern tonight. There was no girl in mittens. There was the lamp, and the path going into the dark, and somewhere along it something coming.

Callie could not see it. She could hear it, faintly, the scrabble of something low on wet gravel.

She knelt at the top of the path, at the foot of the lamp pole, and laid the hook down.

She did not lay it along the path. She laid it across. And she looked for the line.

There was always a line. That was the thing she knew that nobody else did. There was a line here between the gravel of the south lip, which had been laid and rolled and packed hard by forty years of boots, and the beaten earth of the path, which had been walked into the rim by people going round. It ran across the top of the path from the rope to the edge of the slope, crooked, a hand's width wide. She could see it. Everybody else would see a path.

She lit.

The light came up the inside of the curve and off the point, and lay down along that line, across the top of the path, from the rope's stake to the edge of the slope. A pace. A pace and a bit. Bent where the line was bent, white, a finger high, unsteady in the wet air.

The hound came round the bend of the path into the lamplight.

It came low and fast, the colour of the dark, with its two green points, the way they always came. It came along the path toward the lamp, toward the light, toward Callie kneeling at the top of the path with her hands on a hook.

It met the line.

It turned.

It turned the way Toren had told her they turned at the far gap, the way a dog turns off a hot stove. All at once, sideways, its whole body swinging away from the white line on the ground as if the line were a wall it had walked into. It went off the edge of the path onto the slope, scrabbling, sliding on the wet, below the rope.

"*Off the path,*" said Callie. Loud. The voice for a number. "*Slope, below the first stake, going down.*"

Ines's arrow came out of the dark from the west rim, a long way off, and the hound came apart on the slope, and its grey slid down toward the ditch.

Callie let the line go.

She knelt there at the foot of the lamp pole with her hands on the hook and her breath going in and out. It had held. It had been eleven breaths, perhaps twelve. The small place was not empty. It was not full either.

Something warm ran over her lip.

She put her hand up. It came away red.

"*Down,*" said Dessa, from the crate. Then, not loud, only to Callie, across the lip: "That's one."

"That's one," said Callie.

"Not two tonight."

"Not two tonight." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "It wasn't the change. I didn't change. I wasn't holding anything." She looked at her hand. "It was the line. On its own."

"Then that's another floor," said Dessa. "Write it."

Callie got up. She did it without the hook first and then remembered, and took it. She went back to the crate and sat down beside Elsie and held the cloth Dee had given her that afternoon against her nose.

Elsie did not look at her. She was looking at the path, at the place where the line had been.

"It turned off it," said Elsie.

"It turned off it."

"Like Jab's."

"Like Jab's," said Callie. "Bent."

Elsie was quiet for a while. She held the old counter out over the bowl and counted ten breaths and said a number, two high, to Dessa. Then she said, still not looking at Callie:

"In the book. When you write it." She lowered the counter. "Write that it went where the ground changed. Not where you wanted it to go." She looked at Callie then. "That's the thing. That's what's yours. It's not a light. It's where it lies."

Callie looked at her over the cloth.

"Yes," she said. "That's what it is."

---

Her mother was on the step of fourteen when Callie came in off the watch. She was sitting with her hands in her lap and her stick against the door frame, in the dark, with the wet wind coming over the berm.

Callie sat down beside her. It hurt to get down, under the arm. Less than it had.

"Your nose," said her mother.

"Once. It stopped."

"Keel will know."

"It wasn't holding. It was the other thing."

Her mother did not ask what the other thing was. She sat with her hands in her lap. After a while she said, slowly, each word with its space in front of it:

"That river woman read my board."

"She read the box first."

"She did." Her mother turned her head a little. "She doesn't trust us."

"No."

"Good," said her mother. "Then she'll read it properly." She looked at the dark row, at the lamp at the end on its pole, flat and white. "The ones who trust you read it fast. They read what they think is there."

They sat. The gutter at the end of eleven ran into its barrel. Down the row, on the step of eleven, Toren was sitting with something on his knee, not writing. Twelve was dark.

"The Director's going to ask," said Callie. "In the spring. *Will you come.* Each of us."

"I know. You've said."

"I've not said what I'll say."

"No," said her mother. "You haven't."

Callie waited. Her mother did not say anything else. She sat with her hands in her lap, the left one over the right, where she could see them both.

"Will you—" said Callie.

"Don't ask me yet," said her mother.

Callie stopped.

"Don't ask me what I think," said her mother. "Not yet. If you ask me now, you'll do what I say. Either way." She was quiet. "Ask me when you've asked yourself. When you've got your own answer. Then I'll tell you mine, and we'll see if they're the same."

"And if they're not?"

"Then we'll have two," said her mother. "And we'll say which."

She put her left hand out and found Callie's hand on the step, and held it, the way she had held Katori's. She could not hold with the right. She did not try.

Callie sat with her hand in her mother's and did not count anything. On the back of the other hand, in pencil, smudged now, it said *Stop at two.*
