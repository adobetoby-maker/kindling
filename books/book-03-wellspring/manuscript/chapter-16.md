# Chapter 16 — The Spring Review

On the morning of the hundred and seventeenth Elsie read the board.

The Director had said the board would be read at the plate first, every line, before anybody was asked anything. She had not said who would read it. On the hundred and sixteenth Callie's mother had sent the weighman up the fourteen stairs with six words in her left hand on the back of a tally slip, and the Director had read them and sent the weighman back down with three.

*The one who reads it.*

So it was Elsie.

She stood at the end of the plate with her plait over her shoulder and the old counter on her hip under its tape. The weighman had put the test weights on and taken them off. Callie's mother sat in the window on the stool with the tally board on her knee. And the south yard was full again, the half-ring of the hundred and sixth, only more of them this time, standing on the dry concrete in the first real warmth of the spring.

Callie stood at the end of the Home Line with the hook and a strip of cloth in her pocket in case. Keel had said *a week, nothing*. She was not holding anything. She was standing.

Elsie looked at the board. She did not hurry. She looked at it from the top, all of it, as she looked at everything, and then she began.

"*Katori's*," she read. "The east lifters. The old north crew, that were before. In white. They're in the ground."

The yard was quiet.

"*Home Line*," read Elsie. "In white. Under *Katori's*. *D. C.* beside it, in chalk."

She read the struck line from the fifty-eighth, the Director's order, every word of it. *Lent. Caul Hill.* The ruled line through it. *Back. Home 3, not touched. Fence 11. Thirty-five clean.* She read the spend lines under it, and the chalk the weighman had put up on the fifty-eighth, under the Director's paper.

"*The hill's: twenty-eight clean, lifted, spent,*" read Elsie. "*Four dirty. The rest where it lies.*"

She read it once. She did not read it twice. Callie, at the end of the line, knew that she had thought about it, and decided once was what it was.

"*Sag: sixteen, spent. Lanes, W.: four, spent. Twenty-one days,*" read Elsie. "*Store: one Flask, lent, to the Sag, by the Director, at night. Two and a quarter spent. Five and three quarters left. Against D. C. Owed.*" She stopped. "In a box. With a ruler. And a line through the box, corner to corner. And under it, new." She read it. "*Repaid, a hundred and fifteenth. From the Sag's own slope. By the Home Line.*"

At the back of the half-ring, somebody let out a breath that was almost a word.

"*River, T. G.: two Flasks, lent. One thin. C.'s call,*" read Elsie. "Not rubbed out. Still lent."

At the corner of the hut, with her hat in her hand, Tamsin Greave did not move.

"*Store: eight, marked,*" read Elsie. "*The hill's.*" She looked at it for a moment. "That's new too. From the hundred and fifteenth."

She stood at the end of the plate and looked at the board a while longer. Then she looked at the Director.

"That's all of it," she said. "Every line. There's room under."

"Thank you," said the Director.

Elsie nodded once and went and stood at the end of the Home Line, beside Callie.

She did not look at Callie. But she said, very low, so that nobody else could hear: "I read *thin* as yours. Not mine. It's your call."

"I know," said Callie. "You read it right."

---

At the back of the half-ring, where the concrete met the cinders of the path to the south row, the two men from nineteen stood a little apart from everybody else, as they had on the hundred and sixth.

Callie saw them because she had been looking for them. The oldest had his fur collar turned down and his hands in his pockets. The gloved man stood beside him with his gloves on.

The oldest was looking at the board. He had heard every line read out loud by a girl of fifteen at the end of a weighbridge in front of the station. He had stood there and listened to all of it, and his face had done nothing at all, the whole time.

Except once. When Elsie read *Repaid*, and the line through the box, and *By the Home Line*, Callie had seen his eyes go to the board and stay there, and his mouth move at one corner. It was not a smile. It was the look of a man adding up a column and finding the total was not what he had written at the bottom.

---

The long room was fuller than it had been on the hundred and twelfth.

The Director sat at the long table with her spectacles on and her small book open. The three from the household review sat on her left. Dessa sat in the chair by the window with the big book on her knee, open at a page near the back that had seven columns ruled on it, all of them empty except the one on the right. And on that page, at the top, in Dessa's small neat hand, it said:

*The hill. Whether.*

Toren stood along the wall by the slate with his right knee strapped and a stick under his right hand that Pell had sent across the ward with the word *Concur* on a paper tied to it, the same as Tonk's. He was learning to stand on it. He was not good at it yet.

The slate said *74*. The Director had not chalked a new number. It had not changed since the hundred and twelfth.

Everybody on the defender sheet was there who could get up the fourteen stairs. The ones who could not had sent their words by somebody who could. The second yard lifter had sent his by Otto, from the bed by the door of the ward. Pell had sent his by Toren. Senna had sent hers by Dessa, and it was in the book already.

Tamsin Greave stood in the corner by the stove with her two behind her. She had been asked, this time. The Director had sent Hallet to the river cart on the hundred and sixteenth with a paper: *The river will sit at the table on the hundred and seventeenth if it wishes. It will be heard. It will not be asked to answer for Meridian.* Tamsin Greave had come. She had not sat. She stood in the corner with her hat in her hand, as she had on the hundred and twelfth.

And in the doorway, at the top of the fourteen stairs, with the door open behind them, the two men from nineteen stood and listened. The Director had said they could. *They can stand in the doorway and listen, if they like.* They had.

"Bodies first," said the Director. "Before anybody's asked anything. Doctor Sowerby."

---

Sowerby read them from a paper. He read them as he read everything, as if it were bad news, even when it was not.

"The second yard lifter," he said. "Left ankle. Broken on the ramp, the hundred and fifteenth. Set. Six weeks in plaster, then we'll see. Drawn on the track by Jab, the rest at the table by Wren. He'll keep the ankle. He'll not go anywhere this spring." He turned the paper. "Toren Voss. Right knee. Wrenched on the same ramp. Not torn. Strapped. A fortnight on a stick, then he'll walk on it like an old man for a month, and then like a young one." He did not look up. "He set off the left on the hundred and fifteenth. He told me before he did it." He paused. "He told the sky. The whole long building heard. I'm counting it."

Somebody in the long room laughed, and stopped.

"Callie," said Sowerby. "Keel's. Ribs held. Not cracked again. She held over bodies, a bit at a time. A week of nothing. Then a door's worth, standing. The other thing, the light, Keel's still deciding. Nose bled once, on the track." He turned the paper. "Daven Rusk. Busy, deep, from the light and the red. Drawn at the table. Sick two days. He's up today. He's standing at the back of this room, I can see him, and he should be sitting."

At the back, Daven did not sit.

"The foreman," said Sowerby. "His back. He carried a man seventy paces over his shoulder on a stone shelf. He'll be stiff a week and he'll tell everybody it was nothing." He turned the paper. "Mr Marsh."

Rook, on the chair Otto had carried up, did not move.

"Cleared to ride a cart," said Sowerby. "Not to walk a road. Not to lift. Not to hold anything, not a road, not a door, not a cup if he can help it, until I say. And I'll not say for a while." He looked at Rook over the paper. "If he goes anywhere, he goes sitting down."

"I'll go sitting down," said Rook.

"Senna Voss. Next course the hundred and twentieth. In tune. Her hands are where they were." Sowerby turned the paper. "Tonk. Frame off, the hundred and fifth. A Little short. Standing on a crutch. He's asked me eleven times since yesterday if he can walk to the yard gate. I've said no eleven times. I'll say no tomorrow." He paused. "I'll say yes one day. I've told him that too."

He folded the paper.

"That's the bodies," he said. "Everybody who went into the red on the hundred and fifteenth came out of it. Some of them came out worse than they went in. None of them came out under." He looked at the Director. "That's what I've got."

"Thank you," said the Director.

---

Then she picked up the sealed paper from nineteen.

It had been in her cupboard since the eighty-second, with the grey wax broken and the stamp pressed into it. She did not read it out again. She held it up so everybody in the long room could see it, and then she put it down on the table in front of her, flat.

"On the eighty-second," she said, "four men in nineteen offered to clear the plant at Caul Hill under Meridian's paper. With their wagons and their holders and their medical hand. For one part in three of the ash, paid at my plate. And all fittings, boxes, and papers." She did not look at the doorway. "They asked for an answer before this review. I said they'd have it at this review, in public. Here it is."

She put her hand flat on the paper.

"No," she said.

Nobody in the long room moved.

"Nobody from this station goes to that hill under your paper," said the Director. Now she did look at the doorway. "Not the road. Not one person who has been inside. Not for a third. Not for half." She took her hand off the paper. "That's the answer. It's written. Mr Hallet will put it in his ledger beside your offer and your brass and your oil."

In the doorway, the oldest of the four stood with his hands in his pockets. He did not look surprised.

"The hill's not yours to refuse," he said.

He said it pleasantly. He did not raise his voice. It carried into the long room anyway, as if the room had been built to carry it.

"No," said the Director. "It's not." She took her spectacles off. "It's nobody's. That's why, whoever goes, it'll be weighed. In front of everybody. At a plate. With a board." She looked at him. "Yours can be weighed too, if you go. I'd be glad to see it on the board. Any board."

The man looked at her for a long moment.

"Miss Vale will be sorry," he said.

"Miss Vale paid well first," said the Director. "I've heard."

At the end of the Home Line, by the window, Callie did not move at all. But Toren, beside her, felt something go through her, and saw her mouth go thin at the corner. It was not a smile. It was very close.

The oldest of the four inclined his head to the Director, as if she had paid him a small compliment. Then he turned and went down the fourteen stairs, not hurrying. The gloved man stood a moment longer in the doorway. He looked along the Home Line until he found Jab. He looked at Jab's right hand.

Then he went down the stairs after the other.

---

"Now," said the Director. "The answers."

She looked at Dessa.

"Each person on the sheet," she said. "Their own answer. In their own hand. In Dessa's book, on that page. And they say it out loud as they write it, so it's said." She folded her hands on the table. "*Will you come.* To that hill. In the spring. With whoever else will come. Bounded. That's all I'm asking. I'll say what the station does after."

Dessa held the book out.

Nobody moved first. Toren felt it, all along the wall. It was like the lip before a push, when everybody knows it is coming and nobody wants to be the one who moves.

Then Pell's paper came first, because Toren had it, and he went to the window and gave it to Dessa, and she read it out and wrote it in.

"*Pell. Concur. With whatever they say. I'll be here with the atlas.*"

Then Dessa wrote her own.

She did not get up. She could not, easily, and she did not try. She wrote it on her knee, in the column for reasons, and read it out as she wrote.

"*Dessa Cole. Yes. To keep the book. Not to command it on my own.*" She looked up. "That's mine."

Katori came to the window. She took the pencil from Dessa and wrote, slowly, and read it out flat.

"*Katori. Yes. I saw the page for one breath. I'll see the rooms.*"

Ines came behind her. She did not take the pencil. She held out her hand for it after Katori, and wrote two words, and read them.

"*Where Katori goes.*"

"That's not a reason," said Dessa.

"It's mine," said Ines.

Dessa looked at her, and then wrote under it, in her own hand, *I.: her reason.*

Otto went red before he got to the window. He wrote his very carefully, in big square letters, and read it with his whole face.

"*Otto. Yes. The jar.*"

Wyck wrote one word. "*Yes.*" Then he looked at it, and added under it, left-handed: "*Somebody has to be the door.*"

The first yard lifter came up from the bench with his bandaged hand across his chest, and wrote with his right, slowly.

"*No. I'm a jar here.*"

His wife, on the bench, did not move. Nobody in the room said anything about it. The Director wrote something in her small book, and Toren saw what it was, upside down. *Standing: a year. As said.*

Then Otto read the second yard lifter's, from the paper the man had given him in the ward.

"*No. Ankle. Next time.*"

---

Silas went to the window red to the ears.

His mother was on the second bench, with her arms folded. She had not said anything on the hundred and twelfth, and she did not say anything now. She watched her son take the pencil from Dessa.

He stood at the window a long time with the pencil over the page. Toren watched him. He watched him want to write *yes*. He watched him want it so badly that his hand shook. He watched him look at the page, and then down the room at his mother, and then back at the page.

He wrote.

He read it out. His voice cracked in the middle, and then steadied.

"*Silas Tolland. Not this time. Mine to say next time.*"

He gave the pencil back. He did not look at his mother. But Toren, along the wall, did. And he saw her arms come unfolded, slowly, as if she had not meant them to, and her hands go into her lap.

Daven came next.

He took the pencil from Silas without a word. He wrote fast and small and neat. When he read it out, he did not say it quietly. He said it so that it carried to the back of the room, where his father stood in his yard-office coat, very still.

"*Daven Rusk. Yes. Heard, or home.*"

His father did not move. But he nodded, once.

Elsie came after Daven. She took the pencil and did not stop to think at all.

"*Elsie Marr. No. I'm reading here. Two r's.*"

Somebody laughed. It was Silas. He stopped.

Then the girl in mittens came to the window.

She was not on the sheet. She was twelve. The Director had not asked her. But she came anyway, with her mittens in her hand, and stood in front of Dessa's chair, and looked at the Director across the long room.

"Can I write?" she said. "I'm not going. I know I'm not. I'm not asking to go." She held out her hand. "I want to write where I'll be."

The Director looked at her over her spectacles for a long moment.

"Write it," she said.

The girl wrote it, slowly, in a round careful hand. She read it out.

"*The table. Here. With somebody at my shoulder.*"

---

Then Dee.

She came to the window with her glass in her fist, and she did not take the pencil at once.

"I said on the hundred and twelfth," she said. "I'll go if the table here has somebody who can draw, and somebody who can say *off*. Two. Not one." She looked at the girl in mittens, going back to her mother's bench. "She can say *off*. She can draw a bit. She's not enough on her own and I'll not leave her to be."

The long room was quiet.

"The river'll leave one," said Tamsin Greave.

Everybody looked at her. She was in the corner by the stove, with her hat in her hand. She had not been asked to speak. She did not wait to be.

"My niece," she said. She did not turn round. The younger of the two with staves, behind her, stood very straight. "She draws. River way. On her own, mostly, because that's how the river does it. Six years." She looked at Dee. "She'll stand at your table while you're gone. With your girl. With your jar-man. She'll say *off* to your girl and your girl will say *off* to her." She paused. "And when the hill's done, Meridian sends one of yours down the river. To stand at one of ours. For a season. So the river learns your three people. That's the trade."

Dee looked at her for a long time. Then she looked at the younger woman behind her.

"Can you stop," said Dee, "when somebody says *off*?"

The younger woman looked at her aunt. Then back at Dee.

"I've never had anybody to say it," she said. Her voice was the river, slower than Dee's. "I don't know."

"Then that's what you'll learn here," said Dee. She took the pencil. She wrote, and read it out.

"*Dee Wren. Yes. The river's healer at the table here, and the girl, and the jar. Two who can say off. And one of ours to the river, after.*"

---

Rook did not get up off his chair. Dessa held the book out to him, and he took it on his knees, and wrote, slowly, with the pencil gripped hard because his back would not let him lean.

He read it out.

"*Elias Marsh. Yes. Behind. Not first. Sitting down.*"

Tamsin Greave moved in the corner.

"Not on the river's paper," she said, "as anything but a man who's been inside."

Rook looked at her.

"That's all I am," he said.

"You're a road name," said Tamsin Greave. "The river knows your road name. Where you go, that company goes. Or you go after it. Either way there's a fight in somebody's yard." She did not raise her voice. "I'll not have it be the hill's yard, with river people standing in it."

The long room was very quiet.

"Then write it," said Rook. "On the river's paper. *E. M.: holds no command. Leads no party. Goes where the book says, behind.*" He held out the book to her, across the room. "Write it in your own hand. I'll sign under it."

Tamsin Greave looked at the book in his hands for a long moment.

She did not come and take it. But she nodded, once.

"When there's a river paper," she said. "I'll write it."

"When there's a river paper," said Rook, and gave the book back to Dessa.

---

Callie went to the window with the hook.

She did not look at her mother. Her mother was not in the long room. Her mother was at the weighbridge, on the stool, where she had said she would be on every day that mattered, and this was one. Callie had told her, at breakfast, *I'll tell you what I wrote*. Her mother had said, *I know what you'll write. Write it anyway.*

She took the pencil from Dessa and wrote, small and square.

She read it out flat, like a number.

"*Callie. Yes. To read the rooms. Because nobody has.*"

She gave the pencil back. Dessa looked at the page, and then at her, and did not say anything, and wrote nothing under it.

Jab came after her.

He held his right hand out from his side, and he took the pencil in it, and wrote with the white place on the heel of it pressed against the page.

"*Jab. Yes. Tonk said go. I'd have wanted to anyway.*" He stopped. He looked at the page. Then he wrote one more line, under it, and read that too. "*Tonk's term: a stone from the river by the hill. This long.*" He held up his thumb.

The long room did not laugh. But something went through it, from one end to the other, and Toren felt it, along the wall, the way he felt a strut take weight.

Then it was Toren.

He went to the window on Pell's stick, slowly, with his right knee strapped and stiff. He took the pencil from Jab. He stood over the page.

He had had it ready since the eighty-second. *Not mine to say. Mine to put in the book.* It was on his paper, in his coat, at the bottom, under *Nobody else had a strut.* He had thought he would write that.

He looked at the page. At all of them. *Yes. To keep the book. Where Katori goes. The jar. Somebody has to be the door. No, I'm a jar here. Not this time. Heard, or home. Two r's. With somebody at my shoulder. Behind. Not first. Because nobody has. Tonk said go.*

Every line in a different hand.

He wrote.

"*Toren Voss. Yes. Not to lead. To strut. And to put it down when the end says.*"

He read it out. His voice did not do anything strange. It came out the way he said a count.

He gave the pencil back to Dessa, and went back to the wall, and stood there on Pell's stick.

---

The Director did not say anything for a while after the last answer.

She sat at the long table with her hands folded and looked at the slate. *74*. Then she looked at the paper from nineteen, lying flat on the table with its broken wax. Then she looked along the wall at the Home Line, one face at a time.

"On the hundred and fifteenth," she said, "I stood at a rope at the head of the made road and watched a test go wrong."

Nobody moved.

"I watched it go wrong at noon," said the Director. "When a man asked for half an hour, and was given it, and the person who gave it wrote down that it was her call. In front of me. In her own book." She did not look at Dessa. "I watched the ice go from under. I watched a ramp that held one man not hold the next. I watched two people go down it." She was quiet. "And then I watched a girl of thirteen read red on a stone shelf, and a girl of fifteen say *bodies trapped*, and send me back up the made road with my own station behind me. And I went. Because I'd signed it."

She took her spectacles off.

"I've had people lift things for this station for eleven years," she said. "I've had them lift well and lift badly. I've had them come back in carts. I've never once had a crew do what the Home Line did on the hundred and fifteenth. Get it wrong, on purpose, in writing, and then get four people out of the red on a stone shelf in front of everybody by handing things to each other that I didn't know could be handed." She put the spectacles on the table. "A Ward to a strut. A strut to a bar. A light to a light. And a boy at the top of a bank saying when."

She looked at Silas. Silas did not go red. He went white.

"That's what I'll say yes to," said the Director. "Not a party. Not a name. That."

---

She said what the station would do. She said it slowly, so Dessa could write it, and Hallet in the doorway, who had come up the stairs after the two men had gone down them, could write it too.

Meridian would go back to Caul Hill.

Not on its own. Not as the owner of it. Meridian would *join* a return. The river had said it would stand with Meridian to look, on the hundredth, and Tamsin Greave had said, in the corner, on the hundred and seventeenth, that it would stand with Meridian to go. If the plain answered, it could join. If the south road answered, it could join. Nobody yet had.

There would be one paper, written by both. Not Meridian's paper with the river's name on it. Both.

There would be two books. Dessa's, and one the river would keep. Both would be written every halt.

There would be no sole command. Dessa would keep Meridian's line and Meridian's book. Tamsin Greave, or whoever the river sent, would keep the river's. When the two of them did not agree, whoever could see both would say one name. And if they still did not agree, everybody out.

"That's on the card," said Toren, from the wall, before he could stop himself.

"It's on the card," said the Director. "I've read the card. Dessa showed me. I'm stealing it."

There would be an abort line. *Two Flasks inside: everybody out.* There would be the red-call rule, as signed, unchanged. There would be the reasons column.

No room at Caul Hill would be opened unless both books had written, first, that it would be, and why.

Every Handful lifted would be weighed at Meridian's plate, in public, with a river weigher standing beside the weighman, and Tilda on the stool keeping the tally.

"And the shares," said Tamsin Greave, from the corner.

The Director looked at her.

"The river wants half," said Tamsin Greave.

"I know the river wants half," said the Director. "Meridian's got the road and the plate and the people who've been inside. The river's got the healers and the holders and a ferry it can't use." She folded her hands. "I'll not settle shares in a long room before there's anything to share. We'll settle them at the plate, in public, when there's something on it."

Tamsin Greave looked at her for a long moment.

"That's not an answer," she said.

"No," said the Director. "It's a promise to have the argument where everybody can hear it. That's the best I've got today." She did not look away. "Write that down, Dessa. *Shares: not settled. To be argued at the plate.*"

Dessa wrote it.

"And when?" said Katori.

"When the road's good," said the Director. "Mr Hallet will tell me when the new water's down past the siding. When Doctor Sowerby says the people going can go. When the river's paper is written." She looked at Toren's knee, and Callie's sleeve, and the bed by the door of the ward where the second yard lifter was. "Not tomorrow. Not this week. When it's ready." She paused. "And not after the company. If they go first, they go first. Nobody from this station runs up that road to beat them there. That's how people come back in carts."

She stood up.

"That's what the station does," she said. "Bounded. With a line on it, and an abort, and a red-call rule, and two books." She looked at Dessa. "Is it in?"

"It's in," said Dessa.

"Then that's all."

---

Dessa did not shut the book at once.

She sat in the chair by the window with it open on her knee at the page near the back, and looked at it, and Toren, standing on Pell's stick at the wall, watched her look. He watched her read every answer, top to bottom, every hand. Then he watched her pick up the pencil and look at the heading.

*The hill. Whether.*

She drew a line through *Whether*. One line, straight, so that it could still be read.

Beside it, she wrote one word.

*How.*

Then she shut the book.

---

The two men from nineteen left by the south gate at first light on the hundred and eighteenth.

Hallet wrote it. He wrote the time and the gate and the direction. He wrote that they had paid their hut to the end of the week in the station's coin, and that they had not asked for the envelope with *HELD. N. VALE* on it, and had not been offered it. He wrote that they had gone east, on foot, with packs, and that a wagon had met them at the first bend of the east road, a long flat wagon with a canvas over it and two mules.

Ines was on the berm. Nobody had asked her to be. She stood above the south gate with her bow unstrung and her eyes on the east road, and she watched the wagon go along it until it was small.

Then she came down the berm path and stood at Hallet's window.

"They turned north," she said. "At the first fork. Past the old milestone."

Hallet looked at her.

"North," he said.

"North," said Ines. Flat. "Not east. North and west, after. Toward the old line." She looked at the ledger. "Write it. I counted the wheel-ruts. It's the same wagon as brought the oil."

Hallet wrote it.

---

That afternoon Sowerby said yes.

Not to the yard gate. To the front step of the long building, which was nine paces from the door of the ward and down a corridor and past Mrs Lask's desk. He said it at noon, after Tonk had asked him for the twelfth time, and he said it in the voice he used for bad news, and Tonk did not care at all.

Jab walked beside him. Not holding him. Beside him.

Right foot. Crutch. The thin foot touching. Right foot. Crutch. Touch. Down the ward between the beds, with Senna counting from the fourth bed, and Rook sitting up against the wall in the second, and the second yard lifter in the bed by the door turning his head on the pillow to watch. Past Mrs Lask's desk. Mrs Lask looked at Tonk over the top of her ledger and said nothing at all, very loudly.

Through the doors.

Onto the step.

Tonk stood on the front step of the long building on his crutch, in the sun, and looked out.

He had not been outside since the twenty-fifth. Ninety-three days. He stood there with the cord round his fist on the crosspiece and the warm wind coming across the yard into his face, and he did not say anything for a long time.

"It's *big*," he said at last.

"It's big," said Jab.

Tonk looked across the yard. He looked at the yard office with its steel stair up the outside. He looked at the weighbridge, small and far, with its hut, and a figure on a stool in the window. He looked north, past the corner of the long building, to where the north yard was, and the east shed, and the lanes.

"There," he said. "That's your table."

"That's the shed. The table's inside."

"That's your table," said Tonk. He did not want to argue about it. "And that's the lane. With the crooked posts." He looked at it. "There's somebody on the end."

Jab looked.

It was Silas. He was standing at the end of the first lane by the frame with the brackets that did not match, with the card in his hand, and Daven was on his knees by the first post with his blade lit and laid flat along the cinders, a thin white line. Otto was at the second post with the bar. And at the head of the lane, sitting on a crate because his right knee would not let him stand there, with Pell's stick across his lap and his hands empty, was Toren.

He was not holding anything. He was watching.

"*Going,*" said Daven's voice, very small and very clear across the yard. "*Five.*"

Katori, behind him, lit.

On the crate outside the east shed, beside Dessa's barrow, Callie sat with the hook across her knees and her hands in her lap. She was not holding anything either. Keel had said a week. Beside her, on the other crate, Elsie was talking, and Callie was listening, and Callie's mouth was shut.

And inside the shed door, which was open, Jab could see the long table. The girl in mittens was on the end of the bench, with her mittens laid side by side on the boards. The first yard lifter was beside her with a jar against his right palm. And standing at the girl's left shoulder, with her hands at her sides, was a young woman in a waxed river coat, very straight, listening to something the girl was saying.

"Who's that?" said Tonk.

"The river's healer," said Jab. "She's learning to stop."

Tonk looked at her for a long time.

"Is she good at it?"

"Not yet," said Jab.

"Then the girl'll tell her," said Tonk. "She's good at it. She told *you*." He shifted his weight on the crutch. His thin foot touched the step. "I want to go there. To the table. Not today."

"Not today."

"When I can walk it."

"When you can walk it," said Jab.

Tonk stood on the step and looked at the north yard. At the lane with its crooked posts, and Silas on the end, and Daven's light going out after Katori's had come on, and Toren on the crate at the head of it with his hands empty. At the shed, and the table inside it, and three people at it who were not his brother. At the crate outside, and a girl with a hook sitting on her hands.

"They're all doing it," he said. "Without you."

Jab looked at the yard.

"Yes," he said.

Tonk looked up at him. He looked at him for a long moment, from the step, on his crutch, with the cord round his fist. Then he looked back at the yard.

"Good," said Tonk.

---

On the steps that night, the three of them did not sit together.

Callie sat on the step of fourteen with her mother, with her hand in her mother's left hand, not counting anything. Toren sat on the step of eleven with his strapped knee out in front of him and Pell's stick across his lap and the paper on his knee. He did not write on it. Jab was not on the step of twelve. He was on half of the fifth bed on the right, in the ward, because he wanted to be.

Down in the east shed, Dessa's lamp was still lit, because it always was.

She sat in the barrow by the stove with the big book open on her knee, at the page near the back. *The hill.* The struck word. *How.* And under it, every answer, top to bottom, each in its own hand, or in the hand of whoever had carried it up the stairs. And under the answers, the Director's terms, in Dessa's own. *One paper. Two books. No sole command. Whoever can see both. Two Flasks inside: everybody out. Red-call as signed. No room opened unless both books write it first. Weighed at the plate. Shares: not settled. To be argued at the plate.*

And under that, the seven columns, ruled to the bottom of the page, and every one of them empty.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then she turned back, past the spring and the hundred and fifteenth and the frost and the fifty-fifth, all the way to the front. To the first page. To the line in a boy's hand that was older than any of it.

*Say it out loud. Even when it's awful. — M. F.*

She read it. She did not say it out loud. There was nobody in the shed to say it to.

She turned back to *How*, and left the book open there, and put out the lamp.
