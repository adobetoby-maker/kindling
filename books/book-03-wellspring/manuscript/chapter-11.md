# Chapter 11 — Jab: Not Both

The quiet boy came to the table on the hundred and second, and the first thing he asked was whether a warm hand meant he could draw.

Jab had ash on the table again. That was new, and it still felt strange. The Director had sent the river's clean Flask down to the Sag on the hundred and first, against *D. C.* and the river both, and Dessa had sent a Handful of it across to the east shed in the horn spoon, in a small jar, for the table days. *For teaching. Not for the watch. Write every pinch.* The small jar stood at the end of the long table by the brass scale. The thin Flask stood in the frame against the wall, sealed with wax, and nobody touched it. The Director had said it was held. She had not said for what.

So there was ash, and Otto on the bench, and the girl in mittens at the far end of it with her mittens laid side by side on the boards. Dee was on her stool. The boy from the plain road who had come in on the eighty-first was there too, with his eyes shut, finding where Dee was.

And the quiet boy from the lane was standing inside the door.

"Daven," said Jab. He had learned the name off Toren's card two days ago. It still felt like using something that was not his.

"Daven," said the boy.

He did not come further in. He stood with his back to the door and his hands at his sides, and looked at the table, and at the jar, and at Jab's right hand held out from his side with the dressing on its heel. He looked at all of it carefully, the way he had looked at Toren's card, as if he wanted to know what was already there before he put anything of his own down.

"When it's lit," he said, "my hand goes warm."

Jab waited.

"My blade hand," said Daven. "When the light's on it. On the lip, at the far gap, on the seventy-ninth, I had it lit for two hours near enough. And my hand was warm the whole time. Not hot. Warm. All the way up." He looked at Jab's palm. "You said busy's warm. On the lip. To the yard lifters. *It's warm, it's in the arms.*" He stopped. "Is that busy? In me? Is that the same thing?"

"No," said the girl in mittens, before Jab could speak.

They both looked at her. She went pink, but she did not stop.

"I felt him," she said. "On the eightieth. At the plate. He was standing next to me and he'd been lit all night and I put my hand on his arm because I wanted to know." She looked at her mittens. "It was warm. It was his. All of it. It wasn't busy. Busy's different. Busy's like grit." She looked at Jab. "I didn't tell him. I didn't know if I was allowed to feel people without asking."

"You're not," said Dee.

The girl went pinker.

"But you did, and you were right," said Dee. "So say it next time. And ask first."

Daven looked at the girl, and then at Jab.

"Then what was it?"

"You," said Jab. "When you're lit, you're warm. Because it's coming out of you. It's yours." He thought about how to say the rest. "It's like your hand's warm when you've been holding a cup. The warm's not in the cup. It's the cup being in your hand."

Daven thought about this for a while. He did not hurry it.

"Then I can't draw," he said.

"I don't know if you can draw," said Jab. "Nobody's found out. That's not the same." He looked at the table. "But you can't draw *lit*. Nobody can. Not me. Not Dee. Not anybody below what Rook is."

"Why?"

Jab looked at the frame against the wall. At the jar on the table. At his own right hand.

"I'll show you," he said.

---

There was a woman on the bench outside the shed who had come off the first watch at noon with her arms aching and a little grey in her face. She had sat down outside the door because she had heard there was a table today, and had not come in, because she did not know if she was allowed. Otto went and asked her. She came in, wiping her hands on her coat, and sat on the end of the bench nearest the stove.

"A bit," she said. "In the arms. From the slope. I'll not take much."

"You'll take what you've got," said Dee. "Not more."

Jab went round the table. He knelt on the boards in front of the woman, where he could reach her arm.

"Watch," he said to Daven, without looking round.

He brought the fish knife onto his left fist. It came the way it always came, heavy, fat in the belly, hooked at the end. He did not light it yet. He laid his right palm flat on the woman's forearm, through her sleeve, and felt.

Busy. A little. In the arm, low, gritty, warm in the way that was not hers.

"There," he said. "That's busy. I can feel it. I've not drawn yet." He looked at Otto. "Jar."

Otto took the small jar off the end of the table and held it against Jab's left palm, in his big fingers, round the knife's grip.

"Now," said Jab. "Lit."

He lit the knife.

The light came along the edge the way it always came, along the hook, and stuck at the belly. White, unsteady, a hand of it. He held it there on his left fist, with Otto's jar against the same palm, and his right palm flat on the woman's arm.

He drew.

Nothing came.

It was not that it came slowly. It did not come at all. He could feel the busy in her arm, warm and gritty, a hand's breadth under his palm. He could feel the clean in the jar against his other palm. Between them there was nothing. Where the busy should have come up into his palm and through him, there was only the light, going out of him along the knife, and all of him was busy with that. It was like trying to drink with your mouth already full. It was like trying to pick something up with a hand that was already a fist.

"Nothing," he said. "Daven. Look at me. I'm drawing. As hard as I know how."

Daven looked at him.

"Nothing's coming," said Jab. "Because the door's in the knife. It's open there. It's going out there." He looked at the light on the belly of the knife. "It can't be in my hand as well. It's one door. It goes one way at a time."

He let the knife go dark.

It went out. He let it go off his fist.

And the busy came. At once. As if it had been waiting at a gate. It came up out of the woman's arm into his palm, warm and gritty, and through him, down his left arm into the jar against his palm, and it went still in the clean.

"Busy," he said. "Less."

"One," said Dee.

"Clean," said Jab.

He took his hand off. The woman looked at her arm, and opened and shut her fingers.

"Better," she said, surprised. "That's it?"

"That's it," said Dee. "A quarter of a turn. Not even." She held out the pencil. "Write it, Jab. In the pinch book."

Jab wrote it. *102nd. Shed. A woman off the first watch, the arms, a little. From the small jar, a pinch. J. drew. D. W. watched. To show D. R.: lit, nothing comes. Dark, it comes.*

He put the pencil down and looked at Daven.

Daven had not moved from the door. He was looking at the knife, lying on the table now, dark.

"So *lit* and *drawing*," he said slowly, "never in one person. At one time."

"Never."

"Not even a bit. Not even if you're good."

"Not below what Rook is," said Jab. "Rook can hold a road and light his blade. Maybe. I've not seen it. He says it's a different thing. He says you don't get there by trying to do two things. You get there some other way." He shrugged. "I'm not there. Nobody here is."

Daven nodded slowly.

"Then on the lip," he said. "At the far gap. You were the fence. Lit. For two hours." He looked at Jab. "If somebody'd been going under in front of you. In the gap. While you were lit."

"I'd have had to go dark first."

"And then there's no fence."

"Then there's no fence," said Jab. "Unless somebody else lights first. Before I go dark." He looked at Daven. "Like you did. At the sixth lamp. At the end."

Daven was quiet for a long time.

"So the healer," he said, "can't be the fence. Not at the same time. If the healer's the fence, there's no healer."

"Yes."

"And if there's no fence, the healer can't kneel down to draw anybody."

"Yes."

"So you need two people," said Daven. "At least. One lit. One not." He frowned. "And they have to change. Lit before dark."

"Yes," said Jab.

Daven looked at the table. At the jar, and the brass scale, and the square of paper with the pinch on it.

"I wanted to be at the table," he said. "Not on the line." He said it quietly, as if it were not something he had meant to say. "On the line, everybody looks at you. At the light. They say *that's the quiet one, that's the one with the light*. At the pump, they say it." He looked at his hands. "Nobody looks at the table."

Dee laughed. It was short and surprised, and she put her hand over her mouth after.

"Everybody looks at the table," said Jab.

Daven looked at him.

"Everybody," said Jab. "That's the whole of the table. Everybody comes and sits there and watches you and wants you to make them better. And when you can't, they look at you harder." He picked up his knife and put it in its sheath. "If you want nobody to look at you, don't come to the table. Go and be a jar."

"I didn't move," said Otto, from the bench, as if somebody had asked. "That's all I do. Nobody looks at me."

"Everybody looks at you," said the girl in mittens. "You go red."

---

On the hundred and third Jab went to the lane to watch Daven hand his light on.

Toren had asked him. *He's quick at lighting and he's slow at saying. Come and see.* So Jab stood at the side of the first lane by the shed wall, near Katori, with his hands at his sides, and watched the far-gap drill from the card.

Otto had the bar at the second post. Silas was on the end. Elsie was the killer. Toren set the strut off the right at the dry place by the frame, not at the drain, because Callie had read it and it was on the card now: *Before you set.* And Daven knelt by the first post with his blade lit and laid flat along the cinders, a thin white line, unsteady, three paces of it.

Katori was his relief. She stood a pace behind him with her grey blade dark on her fist.

"When you're going," Toren had said, "say *going*. She lights. Then you go dark. Lit before dark."

They had done it four times. It had worked four times. Daven said *going*, quietly, and Katori's blade came up lit and lay down beside his, and Daven let his go out, and there was never a moment when the lane had no light in it.

On the fifth, Daven did not say it.

Jab felt him go. He was ten paces off and he felt it, the way he felt anybody he stood near long enough. The light was coming out of Daven, steady, and then it was coming out of him less, and then he was holding it on by something that was not the light, by his teeth. Jab saw his shoulders go up. He saw Daven's face go still and set, the way a man's face goes when he has decided to carry something one more step.

He did not say *going*.

"Daven," said Jab.

Daven's light went out.

It did not fade. It went. One moment there was a white line on the cinders by the first post, and the next there was nothing but the wet dark cinders, and Katori standing behind him with her blade still dark on her fist because nobody had said.

"*Through!*" said Silas, from the end, too late.

The sack came off the frame and swung through the place where the light had been. Elsie came in and knocked it off its swing with the flat of her blade. It thudded on the cinders.

Nobody said anything. Daven knelt by the first post with his hands on his knees and his head down, breathing hard.

"Why didn't you say?" said Katori.

Daven did not answer.

"You were going," said Katori. "I could see you going. Jab could feel you going from over there. Why didn't you say?"

Daven still did not answer. Jab watched him. He had his head down and his face hidden, and Jab could feel him, the whole of him, small and tight and hot with something that was not busy.

"Because then everybody looks," said Daven at last, to the cinders.

Nobody said anything.

"If I say *going*," said Daven, "everybody looks at me. Everybody hears it. *The quiet one's going. The quiet one can't hold it.*" He lifted his head. His face was red now, for the first time Jab had ever seen. "I wanted to get to the end. So nobody had to hear me say it."

"The end of what?" said Katori.

Daven opened his mouth and shut it.

"There isn't an end," said Toren. "Not on a lip. Not at the far gap. It goes on till the fog lies down." He looked at the place where the light had been. "If you hold it on till it goes, it goes when it goes. Not when we know. And nobody's lit."

Daven looked at the cinders.

Jab took a step forward. He did not mean to say anything. It was Toren's lane, and Katori's. But he had been on the lip on the seventy-ninth at the sixth lamp with his own light lying on the ground for two hours, and he knew what it felt like to be holding a thing on by your teeth.

"On the lip," he said, "if nobody hears you, somebody dies of it."

They all looked at him. He felt his face go hot. He did not stop.

"That's all," he said. "It's not about you. Nobody's looking at you when you say *going*. They're looking at the gap. They're looking for the next light." He looked at Daven. "If you don't say it, they look at you after. All of them. Because there was a hole and something came through it."

Daven looked at him for a long moment.

Then he looked at Katori.

"Five," he said.

"What?"

"I'll say *going*," said Daven. "Then a number. When I've got five breaths left. *Going. Five.*" He was speaking slowly, carefully, as if he were laying each word down on the cinders where he could see it. "Then four. Three. So whoever's next knows how long. Not just that I'm going. How long." He looked at Toren. "Write it. On the card. *Going* and a count. Not just *going*."

Toren looked at him. Then he took the card out of his coat, though the card was in Dessa's book now and this was the copy he carried, and wrote it.

*When a light's going: say* going *and the breaths left.* Going. Five.

"Again," said Katori.

They did it again. Daven lit and lay his light down by the first post. Toren set. Otto held. Silas looked below and looked along. And when Jab felt Daven begin to go, at the edges, the way frost comes across a puddle, he did not have to wait for Daven's teeth.

"*Going,*" said Daven. Clear. Not loud, but clear, so it carried down the lane. "*Five.*"

Katori lit.

"*Four.*"

Her blade came down beside his on the cinders.

"*Three,*" said Daven, and let his go.

There was never a moment with no light.

Daven sat back on his heels by the first post. He did not look round to see who was looking at him. Jab watched him not look round. He watched him decide not to.

The river woman came to the table that afternoon.

Jab felt her before she came in. Not much. A person standing outside the door who was not busy and was not careful, either, the way the gloved man was careful. She was something else. She was weighing.

She came in without knocking. She stood inside the door in her waxed coat with her hat in her hand, and looked at everything: the long table, the brass scale, the small jar, the girl in mittens on the end of the bench with her bare hands in her lap. The first yard lifter, who was sitting on the bench with his bandaged left hand on his knee, waiting for his salt water. Dee on her stool.

She looked at Dee for a long time.

"Wren," she said.

Dee did not get up.

"Greave," she said. "Second weir."

"Circuit, the lower river. Seven years." The river woman did not move from the door. "You went under at the ferry house at the bend. Twice."

"Three times," said Dee.

"They said twice."

"They didn't see the third." Dee turned her glass over in her fingers. "I was in their barn. With the youngest. Nobody saw. I woke up in the straw at dawn and walked home." She did not look away. "I didn't tell anybody. I'm telling you. So it's said."

The river woman looked at her for a moment longer.

"And then you came south to Meridian," she said.

"I came south because I was tired," said Dee. "And because a name came down the river that I'd been waiting eleven years to hear."

The river woman glanced at the door, toward the long building and the ward, and then back. She did not say the name.

"Sit," said Dee. "If you're watching. Or go out. Don't stand in the door. It lets the cold in."

The river woman sat on the end of the bench nearest the door, with her hat on her knee. She did not take her coat off.

"Show me," she said. "Your Director wrote that you've a way. That nobody goes under. I've come up a hundred and forty miles of river road to see if it's true."

Jab looked at Dee. Dee looked at the first yard lifter.

"Salt water first," said Dee. "Then whatever she likes."

---

He did the first yard lifter's hand. He did it the way the nurse had taught him on the eightieth: the bandage off, the cloth in the warm salt water, laid on, gently, round the edges where the burn had begun to close. The man sat very still on the bench and looked at the wall. The burn ran from the back of his hand round the thumb and up the inside of the wrist under his sleeve. It was red and shiny and in places it had gone white and tight, like Jab's own palm.

"It's done," said Jab. "Most of it. Sowerby says."

"I know it's done," said the man. "I can't feel it in the middle." He looked at Jab's right hand. "Same as yours."

"Same as mine."

The man watched him wrap it again. Then he looked at the small jar at the end of the table.

"I can't lift with that," he said. He meant his left hand. "Not a bar. Not a sack. Sowerby says not ever, the way it'll heal. Tight." He was quiet. "I've lifted at the Sag since I was fourteen. On the east track. Before the lamps. Before the girl."

Jab did not know what to say to that.

"I can still feel ash," said the man. "With the other one." He held up his right hand. It was big and scarred across the knuckles and the fingers were thick. "That's lifting. You don't need a door. You put your hand flat and you don't move and it tells you." He looked at Otto, at the end of the bench. "Same as him."

Jab looked at Otto. Otto looked back, and went red, and then understood, and looked at the first yard lifter with his whole face changing.

"Jar," said Otto.

"Jar," said Jab.

He took the small jar off the end of the table and held it out. The first yard lifter took it in his right hand. He held it the way Otto held it, his palm flat against its side, his fingers round it, very still.

"It's full near enough," he said, after a while. "A pinch out. The side by the scale's a bit warmer. The sun's been on it through the window." He looked up. "That's not used. That's just warm."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's warm all through that side. Not in a patch." He set the jar down. "When it's used it goes in a patch. Where your hand is."

Otto stared at him.

"I didn't know that," said Otto. "About the sun."

"You've not been lifting forty years," said the first yard lifter. He looked at his bandaged hand on his knee. "I'm a jar, then."

"You're a jar," said Jab.

The man nodded, slowly, and something in his shoulders went down. It was not happiness. It was the look of a man who has been told where to stand.

By the door, the river woman had not moved. She was watching the first yard lifter's right hand round the jar.

---

He showed her the rotation after that.

It was not a thing he could show at a table on a quiet afternoon with nobody busy. So he told it. He stood at the end of the long table with Dee on her stool and the girl in mittens on the bench and Otto and the first yard lifter beside her. He told it the way Toren had told the card, as plain as he could make it.

"Somebody draws," he said. "Somebody watches. Somebody holds the jar. That's three. Not one person doing three things."

The river woman listened.

"The one who watches says *off*. Not the one drawing. The one drawing always wants one more. Dee wrote that. *Because whoever's drawing will want one more.*" He looked at Dee. "So somebody else says it. And the one drawing stops. Not after the breath. Before."

"And when the one watching's the one who's tired?"

"Then they change," said Jab. "The *off* goes round. Whoever's at the left shoulder. It doesn't matter who's drawing." He thought. "And nobody draws who's lit. Nobody's the fence who's drawing. Nobody draws without a watch. Ever." He stopped. "That's it. That's the whole of it."

The river woman looked at him.

"That's the whole of it," she said.

"Yes."

"On the river," she said, "there's one of us. For forty miles. Wren knows." She did not look at Dee. "There's one healer, and she goes up and down the river road with a bag, and people come out of their houses when they see her coming. Nobody stands at her shoulder and says *off*. There's nobody to stand there." She turned her hat over on her knee. "When she goes under, she goes under on her own, in a barn, and wakes up in the straw. If she wakes up."

Nobody said anything. The bucket under the drip went *tick*.

"So your way," said the river woman, "needs three people. Four. To do what one does on the river."

"Yes," said Jab.

"That's expensive."

"It's cheaper than one going under," said Dee. "Every winter. On her own. In a barn."

The river woman looked at her. Then she looked at the girl in mittens, at the end of the bench, with her bare hands in her lap.

"How old are you?"

"Twelve," said the girl.

"Do you draw?"

"A bit. When somebody says *off*." The girl looked at Jab. "I've drawn four. I've stopped every time I was told." She was quiet. "I've not got a floor yet. Dee's finding it."

The river woman looked at her for a long time.

"On the river," she said, "you'd be drawing on your own by now. At twelve. Because somebody'd have to." She put her hat on. "Write me that. Your three people. Your *off*. On a paper. I want to take it down the river."

"It's in Dessa's book," said Jab.

"Then copy it out of Dessa's book."

"It's not mine to copy," said Jab. "It's Dessa's book."

The river woman stood up. She looked down at him from under the brim of the hat.

"Then I'll ask Dessa," she said. "And she'll say it's not hers either, I expect. It's everybody's." She went to the door. "That's what your board says. *Home Line. Not anybody's.*" She opened the door, and the wet wind came in. "On the river we've a word for a thing that's everybody's. It's *nobody's*. And nobody's things get left out in the rain."

She went out. The door shut.

The first yard lifter was still holding the jar.

"She's not wrong," he said, after a while.

"No," said Dee. "She's not. That's why she's here."

Sowerby took the frame off Tonk's leg on the hundred and fifth.

He did it in the morning, before the trays, with Mrs Lask at the head of the bed and an orderly at the foot and the curtain pulled round. Jab sat in the chair. Senna was in the fourth bed on the right, with the curtain open on her side, because Tonk had said she was to watch and Sowerby had said, *of course she is, the whole ward is, they've been waiting a month*.

The pins came out one at a time.

Sowerby did the top one first. He did it with a tool like a key, turning it backward, slowly, and Tonk bit the cord and did not make a sound. He did the second. Tonk bit harder. He did the third, and Tonk's left fist hit the blanket once, hard, and Mrs Lask put her hand flat on his shoulder and kept it there.

He did the lowest one, the bad one, the one that had wept since the fiftieth. It came out with a sound Jab would remember for a long time. Tonk took the cord out of his mouth and said a word Jab had not known he knew.

"Yes," said Sowerby, without looking up. "That one does that." He laid the last pin in the dish. "That's the frame. Hold still."

He lifted it off. The bar came away. The trapeze hung over the bed on its chain with nothing to do.

And there was Tonk's leg.

It was thin. It was thinner than the right by a lot, and pale, and the skin was dry and peeling and the hair on it had gone dark and flat. There were four red holes in it, two above the knee and two below, where the pins had been. It lay on the sheet very still, as if it did not know it was allowed to move.

Tonk looked at it.

"That's mine," he said.

"That's yours," said Sowerby.

"It's thin."

"It's been in a frame since the twenty-fifth. Eighty days. It's forgotten how to be a leg." Sowerby wiped his hands. "It'll remember. Not this week."

Tonk lifted his head off the pillow to see it better. He looked at it for a long time. He did not touch it. Jab watched him not touch it, and kept his own hands behind his back, flat against the chair, the way he had kept them on the fifty-sixth.

"Now the picture," said Sowerby.

---

They took him down the long slope to the machine in the afternoon, in the chair, with Jab walking beside it and Senna following in hers, pushed by an orderly. It was the first time Tonk had been out of the ward since the twenty-fifth.

He did not say anything all the way down. He looked at everything. The corridor, and the doors, and the long slope with its camber. *Mind the camber*, said Senna, behind, and Tonk said *I know* without looking round, though he had never been on it in his life.

At the yellow line the technician lifted him onto the narrow bed with his good hand and an orderly's. The machine warmed. It sang its long note and held it, and the lights went green along the rows, every one. Tonk lay under the great pale ring with his eyes wide open and the cord in his fist.

It did not take long. It was a picture, Sowerby said, not a course. The machine took it the way you take a breath, and let it go.

Sowerby looked at the picture on the technician's glass for a long time. He looked at it closer than he looked at most things. Then he looked at Jab, and Jab knew, from his face, that he was about to tell it like bad news even if it was not, because that was what Sowerby did.

He did not tell Jab.

He went round the narrow bed to where Tonk was lying, and bent down so his face was level with Tonk's, and spoke to him.

"Do you want the number?"

"Yes," said Tonk.

"You know what a number is?"

"Senna reads it me," said Tonk. "That's what I said. You tell me. She reads it. Not Jab."

Sowerby looked across at Senna, in her chair by the yellow line. Senna nodded.

So Sowerby told him.

He told him plainly, slowly, the whole of it, in words a four-year-old could carry. The bone was clean and knit. That was good. That was the end of the frame. But the growing place at the knee end of the bone, the plate, was what it was. On the inside of the leg it had shut. On the outside it was still open.

"So it'll grow," said Sowerby. "But only on the one side. And when a thing grows only on one side, it leans." He held up his two hands side by side, and then tipped one. "Like that. Out. And it'll be short. Not as long as the other."

"How short?"

"Today," said Sowerby, "a thumb. About." He held up his thumb. "If nobody does anything, by the time you're grown, two hands. And leaning out at the knee."

"And if somebody does something?"

"Then maybe less. Maybe straighter. Maybe not." Sowerby did not look away from him. "I'll not cut now. I'll not cut this year. I want to watch it grow first, so I know how it's going. Next spring, another picture. The spring after that, maybe. Then we'll talk about cutting. You and me. With Senna reading."

Tonk lay under the ring and looked at the ceiling.

"Say it in a number," he said.

Sowerby looked at Senna.

"A thumb now," said Senna, from her chair. She said it the way Callie said numbers, flat and clear. "Two hands when you're grown, if nobody cuts. Leaning out. The next picture's next spring." She paused. "That's the number, Tonk. That's all of it."

Tonk was quiet for a while.

"A thumb," he said. "Now."

"A thumb."

"Two hands. When I'm grown."

"If nobody cuts."

"Next spring, another one."

"Another one."

Tonk turned his head on the narrow bed and looked at Jab. Jab was standing at the yellow line with his hands behind his back, flat, and his face doing whatever it was doing. He did not know what it was doing. He could not feel his own face.

"Jab," said Tonk.

"What."

"It's a *thumb*," said Tonk. He held up his own thumb, the left one, the one not wrapped in the cord. He looked at it. "That's not much. That's *little*."

Jab could not say anything.

"Little's a thumb," said Tonk. He meant the fish. He meant the smoked-black wooden fish on the pillow in the fifth bed, as long as Jab's thumb, with one fin gone. "Little's a thumb long. My leg's a Little short."

Senna, by the yellow line, made a sound. It was not a laugh. It was the sound Callie's mother had made at the weighbridge, the sound of somebody being surprised by a thing that was true.

"Write it," she said to Sowerby. "Write *a Little short*. In his notes. In those words."

Sowerby looked at her. Then he took out his pen and wrote it.

---

Tonk stood on the hundred and sixth.

Pell sent the crutch across the ward that morning. He had made it in the bed with the frame on his own ankle, over eleven days, out of a length of ash pole the orderly had brought him from the stable and a crosspiece he had shaped with a knife. The top was wrapped in a strip of blanket, wound tight. It was exactly the right height, which meant Pell had measured Tonk from his bed across the ward with his eye, as he measured distances on the road.

There was a paper tied to it with string. On it, in Pell's square hand, one word.

*Concur.*

"With what?" said Tonk.

"With whatever you do with it," said Jab.

Sowerby let him sit on the edge of the bed first, with his feet over the side, for a long time. Then the orderly stood on one side of him and Jab on the other, and Mrs Lask stood at the end of the bed with her arms folded, and Tonk put the crutch under his right arm the way the orderly showed him and the cord round his fist on the crosspiece.

"Right leg," said Sowerby. "Then the stick. Then the other one. Don't put your weight on the other one. Just let it touch."

Tonk stood up.

He did it on the right leg, all at once, the way he did everything. He came up off the bed and stood there, swaying, with the crutch under his arm and his right foot flat on the boards and his left foot, the thin one, the one a Little short, just touching the floor beside it with its toes.

He stood.

His face went white. Then it went red. He held on to the crutch with both hands and the cord and stood on the boards of the ward for the first time since the twenty-fifth, and looked down at his own feet.

"I'm *up*," he said.

"You're up," said Sowerby.

"I'm taller than the bed."

"You were always taller than the bed. You just weren't standing on the right side of it."

Jab stood beside him with his hands behind his back. He had them flat against each other, the right palm with its white place pressed into the left. He did not put them out. He wanted to more than he had wanted anything since the rope by the door. He wanted to put his hand under Tonk's elbow and hold him up. He kept them where they were.

"The window," said Tonk.

"It's four steps," said Sowerby. "You've done one. Sitting to standing is a step."

"I want the window."

Sowerby looked at him. Then he looked at Mrs Lask. Mrs Lask looked back at him over her folded arms, and her mouth did something at one corner.

"Four steps," said Sowerby. "Right, crutch, touch. Right, crutch, touch. If you wobble, the orderly's there. Not your brother. The orderly." He looked at Jab. "Your brother's going to stand at the window and wait for you."

Jab looked at him.

"Go on," said Sowerby. "Stand at the window. That's your job."

Jab went and stood at the window. It was the one over the fifth bed, with the grey light coming through it and the north yard beyond, where the lanes were, and the shed, and somebody on the sand heap falling off it like a sack.

He turned round.

Tonk came.

Right foot. Crutch. The thin foot touching. Right foot. Crutch. Touch. He came slowly, with his whole face set and his tongue between his teeth, the way he had done the chair on the fifty-sixth, and the orderly walked beside him a hand's width off and did not touch him. Senna counted from the fourth bed. *One. Two.* Mrs Lask said nothing. The whole ward said nothing. Rook, in the second bed on the left, had sat up against the wall and was watching with his stick across his knees.

*Three.*

*Four.*

Tonk stood at the window beside him, swaying, with the crutch under his arm.

He did not look at Jab. He looked out of the window, at the north yard, at the lanes and the shed and the sand heap, at Wyck on the top of it and two boys falling off.

"That's where you go," he said. "In the day."

"That's where I go."

"It's not far."

"It's not far," said Jab.

Tonk looked at it for a long time.

"I want to go there," he said. "Not today. Sowerby'll say no." He swayed, and the orderly's hand came up, and did not touch, and went down again. "When I can walk it. I want to go and see your table."

Jab could not answer. He stood at the window beside his brother and looked at the north yard, and his eyes stung, and he let them.

---

The gloved man came to the ward that afternoon, at visiting hours, with a letter.

Jab felt him in the corridor. Careful, all the way through. Nothing busy. Nothing in him that was not on purpose. He came in past Mrs Lask's desk because it was the afternoon and visiting was in the afternoons, and Mrs Lask, who had never once enjoyed it, looked at him over the top of her ledger and let him pass.

He came down the ward between the beds and stopped at the end of the fifth on the right.

Tonk was sitting on the edge of it with the crutch beside him. He had not wanted to lie down again. He had sat on the edge all afternoon with his feet over the side, the thin one beside the other, touching the floor.

The gloved man looked at the leg. He looked at it a long time. Then he took off his gloves, finger by finger, and took a letter out of his coat.

"I heard the frame was off," he said. To Jab. Pleasantly. "I said I'd mention the man on the east road. Who sets growth plates." He held out the letter. "He's written. He's seen children like your brother. He can come up the road in the spring, with my people, and look."

Jab did not take the letter.

He did not say anything. He had his answer ready. He had had it ready since the seventy-first. *Supper's first. I wrote mine.* He opened his mouth to say it.

"I've got my number," said Tonk.

The gloved man turned his head and looked at him.

Tonk was sitting on the edge of the bed with the cord round his fist and Little in his other hand. He looked up at the man without any fear in his face at all. Jab had never seen him look at an adult like that. He looked at him the way he looked at a pin site he had decided to do himself.

"Sowerby told me," said Tonk. "Senna read it. It's a Little short." He held up the fish. "That's this long. Now. And two hands when I'm grown. And it leans." He put Little back in his lap. "Next spring there's another picture. Then we see. Sowerby and me."

The gloved man looked at him.

Jab felt it. It went through the man very fast, faster than anything had gone through him at the table on the seventy-first. It was not careful. For one breath it was not on purpose at all. It was surprise. It was the feeling of a man who has walked up to a gate he knew was locked and found a child standing in front of it with the key.

Then it was gone, and he was careful again.

"Numbers change," said the gloved man gently.

"Then Sowerby'll tell me," said Tonk. "Not you."

"Visiting's over," said Mrs Lask.

She said it from the end of the ward. It was not the end of visiting. It was the middle of it. She said it in the voice she kept, the orderlies said, for people who would not leave.

The gloved man looked at her. Then he put the letter back in his coat, and his gloves back on, finger by finger.

"Of course," he said. He looked at Tonk once more. "You're a very clear young man."

"I'm *four*," said Tonk.

The gloved man went back down the ward between the beds and out past Mrs Lask's desk. Jab felt him go, down the corridor and out of the doors and across the yard, careful again, all the way.

Across the ward, Rook was sitting against the wall with his stick across his knees. He had not moved the whole time. He did not move now. But he looked at Tonk on the edge of the fifth bed, and he lifted one hand off the stick, a little, and put it down again.

---

That night Jab did not sit in the chair.

He stood by the fifth bed at the end of supper with his tray in his hands and did not sit down. Tonk was lying on his back with the cord round his fist and Little on the pillow. The bed looked strange without the frame. It looked bigger. There was the whole of the blanket where the bar had been, flat, and Tonk's two legs under it side by side.

"Rook says there's a test," said Tonk. "At the Sag. In the day. With everybody watching."

"The Director said it this morning. At the plate."

"Are you in it?"

"I'm in it."

"Are you going to the hill? After?"

Jab put his tray down on the locker.

He had thought about how to say it for two days. Since the river woman. Since Daven on the cinders, saying *five*. He had thought about it in the chair, and on the steps, and at the window with his eyes stinging. He had thought about saying *because of them*, the way he had on the seventy-first, because it was true. He had thought about saying *if you say I can*.

He did not say either.

"I want to," he said.

Tonk looked at him.

"I want to see what's in the rooms," said Jab. "The ones we didn't open. I want to know what *second* is." He looked at his hands. "And there's ash there. More than anybody's ever seen. And it's finite, here. It runs out. Every winter it'll run out." He was quiet. "And I'm good at it, Tonk. At the table. At the fence. I'm good at it and I want to be good at it somewhere that isn't just a table."

Tonk did not say anything for a long time.

"You want to," he said at last.

"I want to."

"Not because of them."

"Because of them too. But I want to."

Tonk looked at the ceiling. He turned Little over on the pillow with his left hand.

"Ask me after the test," he said.

Jab looked at him.

"I want to see you come back from the Sag first," said Tonk. "In the day. With everybody watching." He did not look away from the ceiling. "If you come back with your hand the same, ask me then. I'll say."

"What'll you say?"

"I'll say what I say," said Tonk. "It's my bit." He turned his head and looked at Jab. "You said it. On the paper. *Tonk's term.* It's mine."

"It's yours."

"Then ask me after."

Jab nodded. He picked up his tray, and then put it down again, because there was nowhere to take it.

"Can I stay?" he said.

Tonk looked at him.

"Not because of the leg," said Jab. "There's no frame. You don't need anybody." He felt his face go hot. "Because I want to."

Tonk looked at the bed. At the whole flat blanket where the bar had been.

"There's more room now," he said. He moved over, carefully, on his good side, toward the window, and the thin leg came with him under the blanket, slowly, because it did not know yet that it was allowed. "You can have half. Not the edge. Half."

Jab lay down on half of the fifth bed on the right with his knees up and his back to the ward. He did not lie on the edge. He lay on half. Tonk lay on the other half with the cord round his fist and Little between them on the pillow, and after a while Tonk's thin foot, under the blanket, came across and touched Jab's leg, the way it had touched the floor that morning. Just touching.

"That's my leg," said Tonk, into the dark.

"I know."

"It's a Little short."

"I know," said Jab.

Across the ward Rook was not asleep. In the fourth bed Senna was not asleep either. Jab could feel them both, lying still on purpose in the dark, listening, and he did not mind.
