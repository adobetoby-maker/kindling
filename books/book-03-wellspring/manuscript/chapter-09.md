# Chapter 9 — Toren: Names on the Card

The store's Flask went dry on the ninety-third, in the last hour of the second watch, with nobody busy standing in front of the table.

Otto found it. He had the Flask in his big hands behind the trestle, as he held it at the end of every watch, and he put his palm flat an inch over its mouth to say what was left. Toren was at the top of the dark lane with Katori. He heard Otto stop breathing for a moment, and then start again.

"There's nothing cold in it," said Otto. "Any side."

Dessa did not open her eyes. She sat on the crate with the book on her knee and the pencil in her fingers, and she wrote before she spoke.

"That's the store's," she said. "That's the one the Director walked down. Five and three quarters on the eightieth. It's the ninety-third." She turned a page she did not need to turn. "Thirteen days."

Nobody on the line answered her. There was nothing to answer. Everybody on the south lip could do that sum, and everybody had been doing it every night since the frost, a little at a time, the way you watch a candle burn down and pretend you are not watching it.

Jab stood at the table with his right hand held out from his side. Dee was at his left shoulder with her glass. There was nobody on the canvas. It had been a small watch, two pushes and a handful of husks at the lamps, and the line had come up off it tired but clean.

"Then it's dry," said Jab.

"It's dry," said Dessa. "Put it in the frame. Empty. Don't take it up to the shed and put it on a shelf as if it's something." She wrote. "And tomorrow night, if anybody comes up busy, they go up the made road to the ward. Not to the table. Not to the fifth bed at supper. To Sowerby. And nobody draws into themselves." She turned her face toward Dee, though her eyes stayed shut. "Nobody."

"I heard you the first time," said Dee. "On the seventy-ninth."

"I'm saying it so it's said."

Toren stood at the top of the lane with Katori a pace off at his right shoulder and the dark running down from his feet toward the ditch. His left arm was in its cuff against his chest. He set nothing. Nothing was coming. The fog lay in the bottom of the Sag, white in the lamplight, and breathed a little over the joints and settled again.

He did not do the sum. He felt it there, the ifs lined up one under another, and he left them where they were.

The next night, two of the second watch went up the made road busy, grey in the face, and sat on the bench outside Sowerby's door with their heads in their hands until he came. Sowerby looked at them, gave them a basin each and a bed each, and said a word to the orderly that Toren did not catch. Then he came out into the corridor where Toren was standing.

"I've basins," said Sowerby. "I've beds. I've a medicine for the sick that does about as much good as a warm brick." He looked at Toren's cuff. "I've not got ash. Nobody's got ash. Tell Dessa I said so, so she's heard it from somebody who isn't her."

On the slate in the long room, under *66*, the Director chalked *68*.

---

On the ninety-fourth Dessa wanted the lane written down.

She said it at the head of the first lane in the morning, from the barrow, with the book shut on her knee. The frost was still on the north side of the shed wall. The sack hung still on its rope at the far frame.

"Not the reasons," she said. "I've got the reasons. Senna's done those, and she's done them better than you would. I want the lane." She tapped the book. "If you're on the lip, and the tall one's in the ward, and Katori's sick, somebody has to be able to open this book and run your lane from it. Every word. In order. Who says what, and when, and why it's that word and not another one."

"It's not my lane."

"Then it'll be easy for you to write it so it isn't," said Dessa. "That's the point."

Toren looked down the lane.

He had been running it since the fifty-eighth, under Katori, with whoever came. He knew it as he knew the stairs in the long building, without counting them. *Set. Holding. Mine. Letting go.* The end, saying one name. The killer coming in. He had never written any of it down. It had come out of his mouth in the lane, and other people had heard it and said it back, and that had been enough.

"How long?" he said.

"As long as it is." Dessa held out a square of card. It was the back of an old station form, stiff, cut to the size of her palm. "Write it on that first. If it doesn't fit on that, it's too long for anybody to remember on a lip at night. Then we'll copy it in."

He sat on the crate by the shed wall with the card on his knee and the pencil in his right hand, and he wrote the first line.

*The holder sets. Says* holding.

He looked at it. Then he wrote the second.

*The end looks. Says* clear *or* hold.

He stopped. He sat with the pencil over the card and looked down the lane to the end of it, where the tall boy from the south huts stood by the frame with the brackets that did not match. The tall boy was waiting for somebody to say something. He always stood like that, a little forward on his toes, as if he meant to be first to whatever it was.

*The end.* That was not a person. That was a place you stood.

If the card was going to be read by somebody who had never stood in this lane, it needed people on it. It needed to say *this one, at the end*. It needed to say who had stood there before, so they could be found and asked.

Toren realized he did not know the tall boy's name.

He had stood beside him on the lip on the seventy-ninth for six hours. He had done what the tall boy told him, *clear* and *hold*, with a strut off his bad arm and his own going down by the breath. He had said *the tall one* to Jab, and *the tall boy* to Dessa, and *you* to the boy himself. He had never once asked.

He got up off the crate and went down the lane.

The tall boy watched him come. So did the quiet one, who was standing at the side by the second post with his blade dark on his fist. So did the weighbridge girl, who had come up from the stable with straw on her coat and was leaning on the frame.

"I'm writing the lane down," said Toren. "For Dessa's book. For whoever reads it."

"All right," said the tall boy.

"It needs who's stood where." Toren looked at him. "I don't know your name."

The tall boy stared at him. Then he went red, slowly, from the collar up, all the way to his ears.

"Silas," he said. "Silas Tolland."

"Silas Tolland," said Toren, and wrote it on the back of the card, where there was room.

"You never asked," said Silas. It did not come out angry. It came out surprised, as if he had kept it ready since the fifty-eighth and had stopped expecting anybody to want it. "Nobody asks. They say *you, there*. Or *the tall one*." He looked at the card. "My mam says Silas. My sister says worse."

Toren looked at the quiet boy.

The quiet boy looked back. He was smaller than Silas and narrower, with a careful face and hair cut short by somebody who had used a bowl. He did not go red. He looked at the card in Toren's hand as if checking what was already on it before he added anything.

"Daven," he said. "Rusk."

"Rusk," said Silas. "Like the biscuit."

"Like the biscuit," said Daven, in the voice of somebody who had heard it every day of his life and had decided long ago not to mind. "Both s's. One k."

Toren wrote it. *Daven Rusk.* Then, because the weighbridge girl was standing there watching him write, he looked at her too.

"I know yours isn't mine to write," he said.

"No," said the girl. "Callie's got it. Ask her." She did not seem to mind. "She'll tell you, if I say."

"Do you say?"

The girl thought about it.

"Not today," she said. "Today I'm mucking out."

---

He wrote the rest of the card on the crate with the three of them looking over his shoulder, which he had not planned.

He had planned to write it and give it to Dessa. But Silas came and stood at his left side to read it, and Daven came and stood at his right, and after a while the weighbridge girl gave up on the stable altogether and stood behind them both. Katori stayed by the shed wall with her hands in her coat and did not come at all.

*The holder sets. Says* holding.
*The end looks. Says* clear *or* hold.
*On* clear, *the holder lets go and the killer comes in.*
*When two say* mine, *the end says one name.*
*Handing a load: the one letting go says* letting go. *The one coming in says* taking. *Then* yours. *Then* mine.

"That's wrong," said Silas.

Toren looked up.

Silas had gone red again, but he was pointing at the last line with one long finger, and he did not take it back.

"You've got *letting go* first," he said. "Then *taking*. That's how you did it at the barrow, Otto said. With Wyck. You said *letting go* and Wyck said *taking*." He moved his finger along. "But on the lip, on the seventy-ninth, at the lane, Wyck came up behind you and said *taking* first. Then you said *yours*. Then he said *mine*. And then you said *letting go*, last, and let it go." He looked at Toren. "Which is it?"

Toren looked at the card.

He thought about the barrow on the sixty-seventh, the sand heap, Wyck at his right side with the bar. He had said *letting go* first because he had not wanted to say it at all, and he had wanted the word out before he changed his mind. Wyck had said *taking* after it.

He thought about the lip. The top of the dark lane at the third hour, Wyck coming along behind the lamps with his bar, saying *taking*, and putting the bar down against the end of the strut before Toren had said anything. Then *yours*. Then *mine*. Then *letting go*, and the strut going out, and the bar still there.

"The second one," he said slowly. "The lip."

"Why?"

"Because at the barrow nothing was coming." Toren looked at the line he had written. "It was sand. I could let go, and if he'd not got it, it would roll a foot, and Otto would swear. On the lip, if I let go first and he's not got it—" He stopped.

"Then it's a hole," said Daven quietly, from the other side. "And the thing comes through the hole."

"Then it's a hole," said Toren.

He crossed the line out. He wrote it again underneath, in the order Wyck had used on the lip.

*Handing a load: the one coming in says* taking. *The one holding says* yours. *The one coming in says* mine. *Then the one holding says* letting go. *Never let go first.*

Silas read it, moving his lips.

"And when there's nobody to take it?" he said. "Like me. With the sack. On the fifty-eighth."

"Then you say *letting go* and you get out of its road." Toren wrote it. "That's not handing. That's a different thing. That's just going."

"Two words that sound the same," said Silas, "doing two different jobs."

"Then put them on two lines," said Daven. "So nobody reads one and does the other."

Toren put them on two lines. He looked at Daven.

"What else?"

Daven was looking at the second line. *The end looks. Says* clear *or* hold.

"Write that the end only says those," he said. "*Clear*. *Hold*. A name. *Through*. Nothing else. Not *yes*. Not *go on*. Not *now*." He did not raise his voice. "People say *yes* to everything. If somebody says *yes* at the end and somebody else hears it, they'll do whatever they were already going to do."

Toren wrote it. *The end only says* clear, hold, *a name, or* through. *Not* yes.

He held the card out at arm's length and looked at it. It was full. It went up the right-hand margin and round the bottom. There was a line crossed out in the middle with another under it, and Silas's finger had smudged the pencil.

It did not look like something Toren had written. It looked like something four people had argued about.

"Right," he said. "Now I go away."

They looked at him.

"Dessa wants to know if somebody can run it from the card," said Toren. "If I'm standing here, I'll run it. I'll not mean to. I'll just do it." He got up off the crate. "So I'm going to the sand heap. And Katori's watching. And you three and Otto do it. From the card. Silas on the end."

"Without you," said Silas.

"Without me."

He gave Silas the card. Silas held it in both hands, carefully, as if it were the book itself and not the back of an old form.

Katori, by the shed wall, still said nothing. But she took one hand out of her coat and pointed at the sand heap. Toren went.

---

Wyck was on the heap with two boys from the south row, teaching them to fall. Toren stood at the foot of it with his right hand in his pocket and his back to the lane.

He could hear it behind him. He could not help hearing it. Otto said *set*, and the bar went down. Somebody said *holding*. Then nothing, which was the end looking, and then Silas's voice, too loud, cracked in the middle: "*Clear!*"

The sack thumped. The weighbridge girl's blade knocked it off its swing.

"Again," said Katori.

They did it again, and again. Toren stood at the foot of the sand heap and counted. Four times. Five. On the sixth Katori said something he did not hear, and then a second rope creaked, and he understood that she had hung the old second sack on the other bracket. She had not told them. She was going to give them two.

He kept his back to the lane.

"*Holding*," said Otto.

"*Clear!*" said Silas.

The first sack thumped. There was a second sound, heavier, a sack hitting a person instead of a bar, and Daven said a short word Toren had never heard him use, and then Otto said "*Oof*," with dignity.

Toren took his hand out of his pocket. His whole body turned toward the lane, exactly as it had on the fifty-eighth with the sack coming at Silas from the side.

"Don't," said Wyck, from the top of the heap.

Toren stopped.

"Your feet," said Wyck. He did not look round. He was watching a boy go off the heap like a sack, chin in, arms out. "Your weight's on your toes. You're going to go and put it right." He watched the boy get up. "Good. Again." Then, to Toren: "Stand there. They've got a card."

Toren put his weight back in his heels and stood there.

Behind him the lane had gone quiet. He heard Katori's voice, low. Then Silas, not loud now.

"I'd seen it," said Silas.

"You'd seen one," said Katori.

"There was only one."

"There were two," said Katori. "You looked at the bar. You saw it was on the bar. You said *clear*." A pause. "What's *clear* for?"

There was a long silence.

"It means nothing else is coming," said Silas.

"Did you look?"

"I looked at the bar."

"Then you said *clear* about the bar," said Katori. "The bar was fine. The bar's always fine. Otto's on it." Another pause. "*Clear* isn't about the thing that's there. It's about the thing that isn't there yet."

Toren stood at the foot of the sand heap and did not turn round.

He wanted to. He wanted to go and stand at the end of the lane beside Silas and say *look below, then look along, then say it*. He had the words already. They were sitting in his mouth like a number in Callie's.

He stayed where he was.

"Why'd you say it so quick?" said the weighbridge girl. It was not unkind. It was the voice she used for Duchess.

Silas did not answer for a while.

"Because if I'm quick," he said at last, "nobody can say I'm slow. And if nobody can say I'm slow, nobody can say *go back to the south row, you're not ready*." His voice went up and came down again. "My mam says I'm not ready. For anything. She's said it since I was twelve. I wanted to be quicker than ready."

The sack creaked on its rope.

"Being quick's not the end," said Daven quietly. "Being right's the end."

"I know that *now*."

"Then write it," said Katori.

Toren heard the pencil. He heard Silas breathing hard through his nose while he wrote, the way people breathe when they are trying not to be embarrassed in front of others. He waited until the pencil stopped.

Then he went back.

The card was on the crate. Silas stood beside it, red to the ears, with the pencil still in his hand. Under the line about the end, in a big untidy hand that leaned forward, there was a new line.

*Look below. Look along. Then say it. Two looks. — S. T.*

Toren read it twice.

"That's right," he said.

"I know it's right," said Silas. "I got hit for it. Well. Otto got hit for it."

"I didn't move," said Otto, from the frame, rubbing his shoulder. "That's all I said I'd do."

---

The oil came on the ninety-sixth, up the east road, in a wagon nobody had asked for.

Toren was at Hallet's window because Rook was. Rook walked to the south gate every morning now, on his own, with his stick. He went slowly, stopped when he needed to, and did not ask anybody to count. But Toren met him at the corner of the long building most mornings anyway and walked beside him, and Rook let him, and neither of them said why.

They saw it from the south yard: two mules and a long flat wagon with a canvas over it, coming in at the south gate at a walk. The young man driving looked as if he had been on the road for a week and slept on the wagon for most of it. Hallet came out of his door, which he did not do for most things.

The young man got down. He gave Hallet a folded paper and went round to the tail of the wagon and pulled the canvas back.

Cans. Iron lamp-oil cans with spouts and screw caps, standing in straw in rows, the same as the station's. Toren counted them without deciding to. Forty.

Hallet read the paper twice. Then he held it out, not to the young man and not to Rook, but to Toren, who was nearest.

"Read it out," said Hallet. "I'd like to hear it in somebody else's voice. I'm not sure I believe mine."

Toren read it out.

"*For the lamps at the Sag, Meridian Station. Winter oil, forty cans. No charge. With the compliments of N. Vale, who regrets that it comes late.*"

For a moment nobody spoke. The mules shifted. One of them blew out through its nose.

"I wrote four letters for winter oil," said Hallet. "To the east road. To the oil men at the second junction, who've sold us winter oil every autumn for thirty years and not this one. Not one of them answered." He looked at the cans. "I didn't write to anybody called Vale."

"No," said Rook. "You didn't."

"Then how does a person I didn't write to know what I asked for?"

Rook did not answer. He was looking past the wagon, across the south yard, toward the end of the south row. Toren looked too.

The oldest of the four was coming across the yard toward the gate. He walked easily, with his hands in the pockets of his coat and his fur collar turned up, though it was not as cold as it had been. He came to the wagon and looked at the cans and nodded to the young man, who nodded back. He did not look surprised. He looked like a man meeting a cart he had been expecting for some days.

"Miss Vale's compliments," he said to Hallet. "She heard you were short."

"From whom?" said Rook.

The man turned his head and looked at him. It was the same look as on the bench outside nineteen, pleasant and long, as if Rook's face were a page he meant to finish.

"From your clerk's letters, Mr Marsh," he said. "The east road's a long road. Letters go along it. People read them."

Toren felt it go through Rook. He was standing at Rook's left side, a little behind, as he always walked, and he felt the arm beside him go hard all the way up, like a strut taking weight. Toren counted. One breath. Two. Three.

On the fourth, it was an arm again.

"Who's Miss Vale?" said Rook.

"The person who pays my wages," said the man. "And his." He nodded at the young driver. "And a good many people's, on the east road, this winter." He looked at the cans again. "She doesn't like to see a station in the dark. It's bad for everybody."

"That's kind of her," said Hallet. His voice was very precise.

"She'd say it's sense," said the man. "Kindness is when it costs you something."

---

The Director came down from the long room herself. Hallet sent the gate crew's boy for her, and she came across the south yard in her long coat with her spectacles hanging round her neck on their cord. She stood at the tail of the wagon and looked at the cans for a while without speaking.

Then she put her spectacles on and read the paper, and took them off again.

"Is it winter oil?" she said.

The weighbridge girl was there by then. Toren did not know who had fetched her. Perhaps nobody had. She came round the side of the wagon with straw on her coat, unscrewed the cap of the nearest can without asking anybody, and tipped it. The oil came out of the spout thin and quick and clear in the cold, as no oil at Meridian had come since the forty-fifth.

"It's winter oil," said the girl.

"Then we'll take it," said the Director.

The oldest of the four inclined his head very slightly, as if he had been paid a small compliment.

"And we'll pay for it," said the Director.

The young driver looked at the oldest man. The oldest man looked at nobody.

"I've no instructions to take coin," said the driver.

"Then you'll have to leave it somewhere," said the Director. "Mr Hallet. What does winter oil cost? At the river price. Not the east road's. Not this winter's. A fair year's."

Hallet told her. He did not have to look it up.

"Then that, for forty," said the Director. "Out of the station's box. Counted on the ledge, where everybody can see it." She looked at the driver. "If you'll not take it, you can leave it on the ledge. Your friend left his brass there."

Hallet went inside and came back with the station's cash box. He counted the coins out on the window ledge one at a time, out loud, with the same care the weighman gave the test weights. It took a while. A crowd had begun to gather, as crowds did at the south gate, two and three at a time: a woman with a basket, an orderly, two lifters from the east crew on their way to the yard office. They watched the coins go down on the ledge in a row.

When he had finished, the coins lay there. The driver did not touch them. He looked at the oldest man again, and the oldest man looked at the coins with mild interest, as he had looked at the brass on Rook's palm.

"Miss Vale will be glad you're warm," he said.

"Tell her," said the Director, "that Meridian bought forty cans of winter oil on the ninety-sixth, at the river price, and paid for them on the ledge in front of witnesses." She let her spectacles fall back on their cord. "Tell her it's in the ledger. Mr Hallet will write it."

"I'll write it," said Hallet.

He wrote it. Toren watched him through the glass. He wrote it in full, as he wrote everything, in his small upright hand.

*96th. Winter oil, forty cans, by the east road, unasked. Sent as a gift with the compliments of N. Vale. Bought by the Director at the river price, paid in full on the ledge. Coin not taken. Held for N. Vale.*

And under it, after a moment, in capitals, so it stood out on the page:

*PAID. NOT OWED.*

The oldest of the four had already turned away. He went back across the south yard toward the south row with his hands in his pockets, not hurrying. The driver climbed back onto the wagon and sat there, looking at nothing.

"Unload it," said the Director to the gate crew. "To the lamp store. The girl will say where." She looked at the weighbridge girl, who nodded once. Then she looked at Rook. "Mr Marsh."

"Director."

"He called you by your name."

"He did."

"Nobody here's said it to him."

"No," said Rook. "Nobody here has."

The Director looked at him a moment longer. Then she went back across the south yard toward the yard office, her long coat moving round her ankles, and she did not hurry either.

---

Rook stood at the window a while after the wagon had gone round to the lamp store. Toren stood with him. Hallet had gone back inside and was writing something else, not the oil. Toren could see him through the glass, with an envelope beside him. He had swept the coins into it off the ledge with a ruler, without touching them, and written on its front in capitals: *HELD. N. VALE.*

"She's paying for the road," said Rook.

"Who is?"

"Vale. Whoever she is." Rook leaned on his stick. "She's not paying for oil. She doesn't care about the oil. She's paying so that when she comes up that road in the spring with her wagons, everybody at this gate remembers whose oil was in the lamps." He looked at the envelope through the glass. "The Director knew. That's why the coin."

"Will it work?"

"Paying for it?" Rook thought. "It'll work in the ledger. It'll not work at the pump. At the pump they'll say *Vale's oil*, tomorrow and every day after." He breathed out. "That's what she bought. Not the oil. The word."

Toren felt the sum come up.

It came as it always came, from the same place the strut came from. *If she's at the end of the east road. If she's read all four of Hallet's letters. If she reads the Director's letters too, to the river, to the plain. If she knows what they asked for. If she knows who's answered. If—*

He let it come. He looked at every line of it.

Then he put it down. It was like letting a strut go at the end of a push, when the end had said *clear* and nothing was on it any more. He did not know where it went. It went out of his hands, and he stood there without it.

"You're not doing a sum," said Rook.

"I did one. I put it down."

Rook looked at him sideways.

"Good," he said. "Somebody'll have to pick it up. But it doesn't have to be you, at a gate, on an ordinary morning." He pushed himself off the window ledge with his stick. "Walk me back. I'm tired. Don't count."

Toren did not count. He walked beside him to the corner of the long building with his left arm in its cuff, and at the corner Rook went in, and Toren stood in the south yard and looked at the empty place on the ledge where the coins had been.

*N. Vale.* It was the first name that had come up the east road attached to anything.

He did not write it on his paper. Hallet had written it already, in the right place.

---

The thaw started on the ninety-ninth, in the night, with a wind from the south that smelled of wet ground.

Toren woke in eleven to the sound of the roof dripping, which it had not done since the fifty-eighth. By morning the frost had gone off everything but the north side of the berm, and the concrete of the row was black and wet. The made road had puddles in it. The cinders in the first lane had gone from grey to dark, and when he walked down the lane they gave under his boots a little, like bread.

Nobody had told him that cinders did that. He had only ever stood in this lane in the frost.

"Again," said Katori.

They were doing the far-gap drill, the long one, from the card. Otto had the bar at the second post. Daven was the light, on his knees by the first post, his thin white light laid flat along the cinders. Silas was on the end. The weighbridge girl was the killer. Toren had the strut, because the card said *the holder sets*, and it meant a strut, and the strut was his.

He set off the right. The strut came up out of the dark wet cinders at its slant, soft behind, and stood.

"*Holding*," said Toren.

Otto let the sack go off the frame.

It swung in at the strut and hit, and the weight came on.

And the strut moved.

It did not break. It did not go out. It slid. Its foot, where it came up out of the ground, went sideways through the wet cinders like a boot on ice, and the whole strut leaned. The sack came down off it and swung on, low, and hit Silas in the knees, and Silas sat down.

Toren stood there with the strut still up and nothing on it, leaning at a new angle, its foot a hand's width from where he had set it. He could feel the cinders round its foot. He could feel, for the first time, how little there was under it.

He let it go.

"It slid," he said.

"It slid," said Wyck.

He had come over from the sand heap without anybody seeing. He stood at the side of the lane with his bar in his left hand and looked at the place where the strut's foot had been. There was a dark smear in the cinders, a hand long.

"It's never done that," said Toren.

"It's never been anything but frost," said Wyck. "All winter. Everything you've set on has been stone. The lip's gravel froze solid. The lane froze solid. The sand heap froze so hard I had the boys falling off it onto iron." He put the end of the bar down on the cinders where the strut had been and leaned on it a little, and it went in. Not far. A finger. "This isn't stone. Not today."

"It holds on the gravel."

"It held on the gravel in the frost." Wyck lifted the bar out. There was wet on the end of it. "A strut's only as good as what it's standing on. Same as a man. You can have the best legs on the station, and you put them on ice, and you're on your back." He looked at Toren. "You never had to think about it. Now you do."

Silas was getting up, wet at the knees.

"Then how do you know?" he said. "Before. If the ground's going to go."

Wyck turned the bar in his hand and knocked the end of it on the cinders, twice, hard, near the first post. It made a dull sound. He moved two paces and knocked again by the frame, where the old timber was sunk in. That sound was sharper.

"You knock," he said. "Before you need it. Dull's soft. Sharp's something under." He looked down the lane. "Or you ask somebody who can read it."

Toren looked at the lane, and the wet dark cinders going away down it between the crooked posts. He could not read ground. He never had. On the road it had been Callie, calling *stone*, *soft*, *hollow*, a step ahead of every wheel.

He went to find her.

---

She was on the crate outside the east shed, beside Dessa's barrow, with the hook across her knees. Keel had looked at her ribs on the ninety-seventh and said a number of things, most of which Callie had not repeated. But she was sitting straighter than she had sat all winter, and when Toren told her about the strut she got up off the crate without taking the hook for a stick first. Then she remembered, and took it.

She went down the lane slowly with the hook, looking at the ground. She did not stop at every pace. She stopped where she stopped, looked, and went on.

"Soft by the second post," she said. "That's where the old drain went, under. It's always been soft there. It was just frozen." She went on. "Stone under the frame. They put footings in. A hand down." She stopped by the shed wall. "Clay along here. Clay's worse than soft. Soft gives. Clay holds till it doesn't, and then it all goes at once." She looked at Toren. "Where did you set?"

He showed her.

"That's the drain," said Callie.

Toren looked at the place. Then he took the card out of his coat, where he had carried it since Dessa gave it back to him to finish, and wrote a new line at the top, above everything, above *The holder sets*.

*Before you set: what's it standing on? Knock, or ask the reader.*

Callie read it over his shoulder.

"That's mine," she said. "The asking."

"It's everybody's," said Toren. "It's on the card."

She looked at him for a moment, as she looked at a number she was not sure about. Then something in her face changed at the corner of the mouth.

"All right," she said. "It's everybody's. But I'm the one they'll ask."

---

On the hundredth a cart came in at the south gate from the river road, and Toren saw it from the berm.

He was walking the inside of the berm with Rook. They did that now in the afternoons, slowly, because Sowerby said Rook was to walk further every day, and Rook said the berm was the only place in Meridian where nobody stopped him to ask how his back was. They were on the south side, above the gate, when the cart came round the bend in the river road.

It was not like the east-road wagon. It was flat-bottomed and low and painted a dark green gone grey at the edges, with a canvas over the back held down by ropes, and two mules pulling it. A woman drove. She sat very straight on the board in a long waxed coat with the collar up and a broad hat. Two people walked beside the cart, one on each side, a man and a younger woman, both in the same waxed coats, both carrying staves.

"River," said Rook.

"How do you know?"

"The cart. They build them flat so they'll float if the road goes under. And the coats." He watched it come. "The Director wrote to the river on the seventy-eighth. Twenty-two days. That's about the length of a letter and an argument."

The cart stopped at the south gate. The woman got down and went to Hallet's window, and Hallet came out of his door for the second time in four days, which was more than he had come out all winter.

She talked to Hallet for some time. Then she turned and looked up at the berm.

She looked straight at them. She had a long weathered face under the hat, about forty, with a mouth that did not look as if it smiled for nothing. She looked at Toren, and then at Rook. She looked at Rook for longer.

Then she came up the berm path.

She came up it quickly, in good boots, and stopped at the top, three paces off. She did not offer a hand. She looked at Rook's stick, and his coat, and his face.

"Your clerk says you're the road name," she said.

"I'm Rook."

"I know who you are. Everybody on the river knows who you are." Her voice was low and even, with the river in it, like Dee's but slower. "Tamsin Greave. Second weir. I'm here for the river stations, because your Director wrote and asked who'd stand with her. I'm here to see if we will." She looked at Rook steadily. "I'm not here for you."

"I didn't think you were," said Rook.

"Good. Then we've understood each other." She looked at Toren. "And you're the boy on the board. The one the crew's not named after."

Toren did not know what to say to that. He said, "Yes."

"I read it at the gate," said Tamsin Greave. "Your clerk showed me. *Home Line.* Not anybody's." She looked down at the south yard, at the cart, at the two people standing beside it with their staves. "We'll see."

She went back down the berm path as quickly as she had come up. At the bottom she turned toward the yard office and the stair, and the two with the staves stayed by the cart.

Rook watched her go.

"She doesn't like you," said Toren.

"No," said Rook. "She's got reasons. They're probably good ones." He leaned on his stick. "Where I go, that company goes. Or I go after it. Either way there's a fight in somebody's yard, and it's never mine." He was quiet. "The river's had a few of those yards."

"Was it you?"

"Some of it," said Rook. "Not all. It doesn't matter much, to the yard."

---

Sowerby took the cuff off on the hundred and first, three weeks to the day.

He did it in the small room off the east corridor. He undid the straps one at a time and lifted it off, and Toren's arm came out of it. It was thinner than the right, pale, and smelled of leather. But it was not as thin as it had been on the sixty-fifth, and when Sowerby said *close your hand*, the last two fingers went most of the way and stayed there.

Sowerby pressed the ridge with his thumb. Toren did not make a sound. This time he did not have to make himself not make one.

"Straight," said Sowerby. "The green-stick's gone out of it." He sat back. "You waited the whole three weeks."

"Yes."

"Did it hurt? Waiting?"

Toren thought about it.

"Yes," he said.

"Good," said Sowerby. "It's supposed to." He put the cuff on the shelf, and this time he did not look at it as if he were thinking of keeping it. "A week of nothing off the left. Then whatever you like, within reason. And if you're going to set off it in a hurry, you're to tell me before. Not after. I'm tired of being told after."

"I'll tell you before."

"You'll try," said Sowerby. "That's more than you did last time." He picked up his pen. "Go and grease it. It's a lizard again."

---

That night Toren sat on the step of eleven with the card on his knee.

It was not cold. That was the strange thing. It was the first night since he had come to Meridian that he could sit on the step without his breath standing in the air in front of him. The wind was from the south and everything was dripping, and somewhere down the row a gutter ran into a barrel with a sound like somebody pouring tea.

The card was full. It went round all four edges. It had Silas's line on it in his big leaning hand, *Two looks*, and Daven's line in Toren's hand because Daven had not wanted to write it himself, and Callie's line at the top, *Before you set*. It had two words that sounded the same doing two jobs on two different lines. It had a line crossed out in the middle where Toren had got it wrong.

On the back it had three names. *Silas Tolland. Daven Rusk.* And under them, in Callie's small square hand, a third. Toren had asked her that afternoon, and she had asked the weighbridge girl, and the weighbridge girl had said *all right, but she writes it*.

*Elsie Marr.*

He looked at the names for a while.

He had stood on the lip with these people. He had done what they told him. He had given Silas a strut's worth of trust at the fifth lamp, with his own going down by the breath, and Silas had paid it back at the right moment, *clear*, every time but one. He had not known their names.

Now the card had them. Anybody who opened Dessa's book would know who had stood where, and could go and find them, and ask.

He looked down the row. There was light in fourteen, the stove, not the lamp. There was no light in twelve, because Jab was on the edge of the fifth bed tonight, where Tonk had put him.

He did not count. He got up and went across the wet concrete to the east shed, where Dessa's lamp was still lit, because Dessa was always the last to put her lamp out.

She was in the barrow by the stove with the book on her knee. She looked up when he came in.

"It's done," he said, and held out the card.

Dessa took it. She read all of it, front and back, slowly, turning it in the lamplight. She read the crossed-out line. She read Silas's line twice. When she came to the names she said nothing, but she rested her thumb on them for a moment.

"It's not yours," she said at last.

"No."

"Good." She opened the book to a new page near the front of the second half, laid the card flat against it, and began to copy it in, line by line, in her small neat hand. She copied the crossed-out line too, and drew the line through it again, so that whoever read it would see that it had been got wrong once and put right.

Toren stood by the stove and watched her do it.

It was the first page in Dessa's book with his handwriting behind it and somebody else's crossing it out. He found he did not mind at all.
