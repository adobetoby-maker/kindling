# Chapter 15 — Lit Before Dark

Jab went down the track from the south path at a run, which nobody ever did on the lip, and he did not care.

The stone was dry under his boots up here, in the sun. It went down ahead of him along the east side of the bowl like a shelf, with the bank going up on his left to the lamps and the slope going down on his right toward the bottom. Wyck was ahead of him with the bar, running heavily, the way a big man runs when he has decided to. Behind him came Dee, fast and light in her good boots, with her glass in her fist, and Otto behind her with the small jar held against his chest in both hands like an egg.

He could feel them. He could feel all of it.

He could feel the track ahead, seventy paces of it, and at the bottom of it, at post five, four people. He knew them. He would have known them at a mile. Callie, standing, hard all through, the way she went hard behind a Ward. The foreman, big and frightened and busy in the arms. Toren, on the ground, hurting in the right leg, and something else in him, low and tight, that Jab knew was the sum.

And the second yard lifter. Lying at the foot of the ramp. Busy. Not in the arms now. In the chest and the belly and all through, deep, gritty, warm, the way the first yard lifter had been on the fifty-fifth, on the canvas behind the lamps, with his face like porridge.

Worse than that. It was coming in faster than anybody could put it out. Jab could feel it come. The counter on Callie's hip said red, and the yard lifter was lying in the red with a broken ankle and a morning of dirty ground in his hands already, and every breath he took, more came in.

And below them all, under the track, under the slope, coming up out of the pipe in the shadow of the bank, the thing that was not a person. It moved like water going down a drain backward. It gathered. It pushed. It had no count.

"*He's going,*" said Jab. He said it to Dee, over his shoulder, running. "*The yard lifter. He's going under. Not now. Soon.*"

"*How soon?*"

"*I don't know. Soon.*"

"*Then that's next,*" said Dee.

---

On the track at post five, Callie held.

The Ward stood off the outside of the hook's curve like a wall leaning out of the ground. It went along the downslope edge of the track from the curve, pale, the way it always went, a little longer than the hook. Three paces of it, perhaps. It stood between the four of them and the slope, and the slope was full of fog.

The first husk hit it.

She felt it hit. She felt it in her back and her belly and the place under her arms, all at once, the way a man feels a door he is leaning on when somebody kicks it from the other side. The husk scrabbled at the Ward with its many legs and could not get through and went sideways along it, looking for the end.

"*End's at the post,*" said Callie. Flat. The voice for a number. "*Mind the end. By the post.*"

The foreman was standing over the yard lifter. He looked at the end of the Ward, at the post, at the white line, at the grey drift lying across the line and on down the track.

The husk came round the end of the Ward.

It did not come at them. It went past the end of the Ward and onto the drift, onto the grey lying round the foot of post five, and stopped there, and its legs went into the grey, and it stood over the ash the way a dog stands over a bone.

"*They're going for the ash,*" said the foreman.

A second husk came round the end. It went to the drift too. It went past the drift to the spilled jars at the foot of the ramp, the four that had not broken, rolling on the stone, and the grey from the two that had. It stood over them.

Callie looked at it.

Worked things. Husks went at worked things. Lamps, and lit blades, and Sustain, and a winter's ash lying frozen on a slope. And six jars of the best of it, from the thickest place, worked into the jars by a man's palm all morning.

"*The jars,*" she said.

The foreman looked at the jars.

He did not say anything. He looked at them for one breath. They were the last row. They were his. He had argued for them at noon, up the bank, in a voice that carried. *It's a Flask on its own.*

Then he bent down and picked up the burst sack by its corner, with the four whole jars still in it, and swung it. He swung it the way you swing a bucket at a dog, round and out, low, past the end of the Ward and over the white line onto the clay below post five. It landed in the drift and the jars spilled out of it and rolled away down the track toward six.

"*Let them have it,*" he said.

The husks went after it. Both of them. And a third, that had come up the slope behind them. They went past the end of the Ward and over the line, down the track, after the jars, and stood over them in the grey below post five, and did not come back.

"*That's a Flask,*" said the foreman. His voice did not change. "*Where it lies.*"

The place under Callie's arm grew.

It did not get worse all at once. It grew the way it had at the shed door, the way a puddle grows when rain falls into it. She was holding still over bodies. Keel had said not to. Dessa had said *a door's worth*. A door's worth was twenty-four breaths on a shed door with nothing pushing on it. This had husks on it.

She had been counting without meaning to. She was at fourteen.

"*Going,*" she said. "*Toren.*"

---

At the foot of the ramp, Toren got onto his left knee.

His right leg would not do anything. It lay on the stone straight out in front of him, and when he tried to bend it something in the knee said no in a hot white voice. He did not argue with it. He got onto the left knee and his right hand and knelt there in the gravel and the spilled grey, beside the yard lifter, and looked at the Ward standing along the edge of the track.

A husk was coming up the slope at it. Another. They came at the Ward because the Ward was worked, and Callie was behind it, and the four of them were behind her.

"*Taking,*" said Toren.

He did not have to think about the order. It was on the card. It was in Dessa's book. Silas had crossed out the wrong one.

He set off the right. The strut came up out of the stone at the edge of the track, inside the Ward's line, upright, soft behind, leaning out toward the slope. It was stone here. Callie had said so. He felt it stand. He felt how much was under it.

"*Yours,*" said Callie.

"*Mine.*"

"*Letting go.*"

The Ward went out.

The husk hit the strut. It hit where the Ward had been and found the strut there instead, and scrabbled at it, and the weight came on, and Toren paid for it.

He paid by the breath. Every breath the husk stood against the strut, a little went out of his own. He had paid for six steps on the ramp. He had paid for two struts at once when he reached for the yard lifter. He did not know how much was left. A turn. Less.

Callie sat down on the stone beside him with the hook across her knees and her face grey and her breath going in short.

"*The arm,*" she said. "*I stopped at the arm.*"

"*You stopped,*" said Toren.

The second husk came up the slope beside the first. It came at the end of the strut, where there was no strut, only the edge of the track and the slope. The strut was a strut. It was as long as he was tall. It did not cover three paces.

"*Silas,*" said Toren.

He did not look up. He could not take his eyes off the strut. But he said it up the bank, toward the lamps, and from the upper line, from somewhere along between the fourth lamp and the fifth, Silas's voice came down.

"*Hold,*" said Silas. "*There's two. One's on you. One's coming round the end. There's more on the slope.*" A pause. Two looks. "*Hold. Wyck's coming. Twenty paces.*"

Toren held.

He held while the second husk came round the end of the strut and onto the track. It came at the nearest thing, which was the foreman. The foreman had nothing in his hands. He stood over the yard lifter and put his boot out and kicked it, hard, the way you kick a dog off a step, and it went over and came back.

"*Ten paces,*" said Silas.

Toren held. His own went down. He could feel it go, every breath, like water out of a cracked jar. He did not let go. He did not set another strut for the one at the foreman. He wanted to. He felt it come up in him, the old thing, *two struts, twice the price*, the thing that had put him on his side at the foot of the ramp in the first place.

He did not set it.

"*Hold,*" said Silas. "*Hold. Five.*"

"*Taking,*" said Wyck.

---

Wyck came down the last of the track with the bar in his left hand and did not stop. He put the end of the bar down against the strut, low, at the foot of it, where the husk was, between the husk and the stone. He planted it. He put his weight in his heels.

"*Yours,*" said Toren.

"*Mine.*"

"*Letting go.*"

The strut went out. The bar stayed. The husk was on the bar now, scrabbling, and Wyck stood behind it, settled, the way he had stood behind the barrow of sand on the sixty-seventh, and did not look round.

"*Clear?*" said Wyck. Up the bank.

"*Hold,*" said Silas. "*One's on the foreman.*"

Daven came down the track behind Wyck. He came fast and quiet, with his blade dark on his fist, and he did not wait for anybody to say his name. He went past Wyck to the foreman and the husk the foreman was kicking, and as he went past his blade lit, thin and white and flickering, and he put it down across the husk's back, and it came apart over the foreman's boots in a burst of grey.

"*One,*" said Silas. "*Clear. The bar's clear. Daven.*"

Daven turned. He put his lit blade down across the husk on Wyck's bar. It came apart.

"*Two,*" said Silas. "*Clear.*" A pause. "*Daven, dark. They'll come at your light.*"

Daven let his blade go dark.

It was the first time Toren had ever heard Silas say anything that was not on the card. *They'll come at your light.* He had said it anyway. He had said it because it was true, and there was nobody else to say it.

Toren knelt on the stone with nothing in his hands and his own down to almost nothing. He looked at the bar, and Wyck behind it. He looked at Daven, dark. He looked at Callie, sitting beside him, grey. He looked at the yard lifter.

The yard lifter was not looking at anything. His eyes were open and his face was the colour of porridge and his lips were blue at the edges.

"*Jab,*" said Toren.

"*Here,*" said Jab.

---

Jab knelt on the stone beside the second yard lifter and put his right palm flat on the man's chest, through his coat, and did not draw.

He felt first. That was the first thing. Rook had said it on the sixty-third. *Where first. Then how.*

The man was big, and his own, and hurting badly in the left ankle, and that was done. That was a break. That was Sowerby's. And all through the rest of him, in the chest and the belly and the arms and the back of the neck, there was the other thing. Warm. Gritty. Busy. More of it than Jab had felt in anybody since the fifty-fifth. And it was still coming in.

"*He's deep,*" said Jab. "*He's going. If I don't, he'll go under here. On the track.*"

"*Then draw,*" said Dee. She was at his left shoulder. He had not heard her kneel. She had the glass in her fist. "*Say your line.*"

"*My line's a finger,*" said Jab. "*I'm a hand and a bit. I'll stop at a hand. Not at a finger.*" He looked at the yard lifter's face. "*Even if he's still busy.*"

"*Even if he's still busy,*" said Dee.

"*Jar.*"

Otto knelt at his left side and put the small jar against Jab's left palm, in his big fingers, two Handfuls of the river's thin in it, and did not move.

Jab drew.

It came. It came faster than it had ever come, because there was so much of it, and it came hot, hotter than busy usually was, and it came up out of the man's chest into his palm and through him and down his left arm into the jar. He did not haul. *It comes at the rate it comes.* He let it come at the rate it came, which was very fast.

"*Busy,*" he said. "*Busy. Less.*"

"*Side's going,*" said Otto. "*Your thumb.*"

"*Turn it.*"

Otto turned it.

"*Two,*" said Dee. Turns.

Jab drew. He felt his own bucket going down. He felt it the way he always felt it, from the inside, a line on the bucket's side going lower. He had come down the track at a hand and a bit. He was at a hand and a bit less. A hand and less than that.

He felt the yard lifter's face change under his other hand, the one on the man's coat. Not much. The blue went out of the lips.

"*Three,*" said Dee.

"*Busy,*" said Jab. "*Less.*" He was at a hand. He could feel it. He was at a hand and the yard lifter was still busy. Less, much less, but busy all through, and still coming in, a little, because they were all still lying in the red.

One more breath and he would have him nearly clean.

"*Off,*" said Dee.

Jab's hand stayed where it was.

He felt it stay. He felt himself want to take the breath. It was the thing Dee had written. *Because whoever's drawing will want one more.* He wanted one more. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything since the rope by the door.

"*Off,*" said Dee. Louder. "*Jab. You said loud.*"

His hand came off the yard lifter's chest.

He knelt there on the stone with his right palm held up, and looked at it. The white place on the heel was where it had been. The pink round it was where it had been. It was the same hand.

"*A hand,*" said Dee. She was not looking at the glass. She was looking at him. "*You stopped at a hand.*"

"*He's still busy.*"

"*He's not going under,*" said Dee. "*He's busy. He'll be sick. He'll be sick for three days and Sowerby will set his ankle and swear at him. He's not going under on this track.*" She put the glass away in her coat. "*The rest's the table's. Not yours.*"

"*Vent,*" said Otto.

Jab put his palm flat on the stone at the edge of the track, on the dirty grey where the husks had come apart, and let it go.

---

"*Shade's on the track,*" said Elsie.

Her voice came down from the south lip, forty paces off and up, thin and flat. Everybody on the track heard it.

"*At post five. On the white line. Coming up.*"

Callie looked down the track.

The shade was there. It lay across the stone at the white line, at the foot of post five, a long soft edge. Below it the track and the drift and the spilled jars lay in shadow. Above it, where the four of them were, and Wyck, and Jab, and Dee, and Otto, and Daven, the stone was still in the sun.

The fog from the pipe was in the shadow. It had come up the slope and over the drift and was lying on the track below the line, low and grey and moving. The husks were in it, over the jars.

And something else was in it.

Callie saw the green points before she saw what they were in. Two. Low down, in the shadow below the white line, in the fog, where the track went down toward six. Then two more, on the slope below the track's edge, in the shade.

"*Hounds,*" said Daven.

He said it loud. He said it before anybody else, and he said it so it carried up the bank to the lamps and across to the lip, and he did not look round to see who had heard.

"*Hounds. In the shade. Below the line. Two on the track. Two on the slope.*"

"*Heard,*" said Dessa, from the crate. Her voice came down the slope flat and steady. "*Move. Up the track. Now. Stay in the sun.*"

---

They moved.

The foreman picked up the second yard lifter. He did it the way a lifter picks up a sack: he crouched and got his shoulder under the man's middle and his arm round his legs, and stood, and the yard lifter went over his shoulder with his head down his back and his broken ankle hanging, and made a sound, and was quiet. The foreman was a long grey man with a face like a gatepost. He stood with the yard lifter over his shoulder and did not stagger at all.

"*Up,*" he said.

Otto got his shoulder under Toren's left arm. Toren came up onto his left leg, and stood, with his right leg hanging, and the knee said what it said.

"*Fence,*" said Jab.

He did not wait to be named. He was already named. *The fence. Then whatever's next.* He brought the knife onto his left fist, dark. He looked at the slope below the edge of the track, where the shade was coming up, a pace at a time.

He lit.

The light came along the hook and stuck at the belly, white, the old ugly light. He knelt at the edge of the track and laid the knife down flat on the stone with the lit edge facing down the slope, and the light lay along the stone, five paces of it, straight.

On the slope below the track, in the shade, the two green points came up toward the group. They came up the wet dark slope low and fast. They reached the line of white along the track's edge.

They turned.

They turned the way they always turned. All at once, sideways, as if the light were a stove they had walked into. They went along the slope below the light, sideways, looking for the end of it.

"*Jab's on,*" said Silas, from above. "*Five paces. The slope side. They're going along it.*"

"*The track,*" said Callie.

She was standing. She had got up off the stone with the hook. She did not remember doing it.

She looked down the track at the white line, at post five, at the shade lying across the stone there, and the two green points in the fog below it, coming up the track. Not up the slope. Up the track. Along the shelf, the way people walked it. Toward them.

The track below them had no fence. It had nothing. It had the white line Elsie had painted, and that was paint.

She knew where the stone changed.

She had read it at noon. *Clay from here down. Stone from here up. Here's where it changes.* A pace above the post. A pace above the white line. A real line, across the track, from the foot of the bank to the edge of the slope, where the good stone ended and the stone laid on clay began. Everybody else would see a track. She could see it.

She knelt and laid the hook down across the track on the stone side of it, the curve on the ground, the point toward the bank.

"*Line,*" she said. "*Across the track. Below us.*"

She lit.

---

It was not a change. She was not holding anything. It was the line on its own, the way it had been at the top of the lip path, and it came the same way. The light came up the inside of the curve and off the point and lay down along the ground, white, a finger high.

It did not go straight. It went where the stone changed. It went across the track from the foot of the bank to the edge of the slope, crooked, bending where the seam bent, a pace and a half of it, two paces, lying on the line between the good stone and the bad that nobody but Callie could see.

Now everybody could see it.

The hounds on the track came up out of the shade toward it. Two. Low and fast, with their green points, up the stone from the white paint.

They met Callie's line.

They turned.

Both of them, all at once, sideways, the way they turned off Jab's light at the far gap, the way the one on the lip path had turned. They went sideways off the track onto the slope, into the shade below it, scrabbling on the wet. And there they met Jab's light, lying along the slope edge, and turned again, and went down.

"*Callie's on,*" said Silas. "*Across the track. They turned off it. Both.*" His voice went up and came down. "*Two fences. Slope and track. The front's open. It's sun all the way up.*"

"*Move,*" said Dessa.

"*Callie's line's eighteen breaths,*" said Dee. She was standing by the foreman with her glass. "*She's at—*"

"*Twelve,*" said Callie. Her nose had not bled. She could feel the small place going down fast.

"*Move,*" said Dessa again. "*Up the track. In the sun. Jab, Daven: lit before dark. Silas, call it.*"

---

They went up the track in the sun.

The foreman went first, with the yard lifter over his shoulder, walking. Wyck went beside him on the slope side with the bar. Otto came behind with Toren's arm over his shoulder, Toren hopping on his left leg, his face white, setting nothing. Dee walked beside them with her glass. Callie walked at the back of the group, with the hook, not lit, because her line was lying behind them across the track, and she had let it go at seventeen, and it had gone out.

And along the slope side of the track, the fence moved.

It moved because it could not. Jab's light lay along the ground from his knife, five paces of it, and it went only where his knife was. If he picked the knife up and walked, the light went off the ground and was a lit knife in a boy's fist, not a fence. If he left it lying, the group walked away from it.

"*Daven,*" said Silas, from above. "*Ahead of Jab. On the slope edge. Light.*"

Daven was already there. He knelt at the edge of the track four paces up from Jab, ahead of the group, in the sun, and laid his blade down flat, and lit. The thin white flickering light lay along the stone.

"*Daven's on,*" said Silas. Two looks. "*Jab, dark.*"

Jab let his go dark. He picked up his knife and went past Daven at a run, five paces, six, up the track, and knelt, and laid it down, and lit.

"*Jab's on,*" said Silas. "*Daven, dark.*"

Daven let his go dark, and got up, and went past Jab.

It was the far gap. It was the far gap on the seventy-ninth, lit before dark, never dark before lit. But it was moving. It was two boys leapfrogging up a stone shelf in the sun, each one lighting before the other went dark, so there was never a moment when the slope side had nothing on it. And a boy on the upper line saying when, because he could see both.

Below them, on the slope, in the shade, the hounds went along the line of light, and turned, and went along it, looking for the end. Every time they found the end, there was a new light ahead of it.

At the south lip, on the crate, Elsie said: "*Shade's at post four and a half. Coming up. Slow.*"

They were past post four.

"*Going,*" said Daven.

He was kneeling at the slope edge with his light down. He said it loud. Not cracked. Clear.

"*Going. Five.*"

Jab was behind him, dark, coming up.

"*Jab lights,*" said Silas. "*Ahead of Daven. Two paces.*"

"*Four,*" said Daven.

Jab went past him and knelt and laid the knife down and lit.

"*Three,*" said Daven, and let his go.

There was never a moment with no light.

Daven sat back on his heels at the edge of the track, grey, his blade dark, his hands shaking. Wyck came past him with the bar and put his free hand under Daven's arm and stood him up without a word, and Daven walked.

"*Daven's out,*" said Silas. "*Jab's the fence. Katori—*"

"*Here,*" said Katori.

She was on the upper line above them, at the third lamp, at the top of the dark lane, on her knees on the gravel at the edge of the bank, with her grey blade on her fist. She had been there all the time. She had killed four husks that had come up the bank toward the lamps, one after another, and Callie had not seen her do any of it.

"*I can light from up here,*" said Katori. "*Down the bank. Not on the track. It'll not lie on the slope edge.*"

"*Then not you,*" said Silas. Two looks. "*Jab holds. It's twenty paces to the path. Jab holds it.*"

"*I'll hold it,*" said Jab.

---

At post three there was a hound on the track behind them.

Callie saw it first, because she was at the back. It came up out of the shade below post four, along the stone, low and fast, where her line had been and was not any more. It came up the track toward the back of the group. Toward her.

It was in the sun. It should not have been in the sun. Hounds went for the dark. But it had come up the track along the edge of the shade, and the shade was only a pace behind it, and it was coming.

"*Behind,*" said Callie. "*On the track. One.*"

"*Toren,*" said Silas.

Toren, hopping on his left leg with his arm over Otto's shoulder, turned his head.

He looked at the hound coming up the track. He looked at the stone between it and Callie. He looked at his own right hand, and then, for the first time, at his left.

"*Sowerby,*" he said, out loud, to nobody, to the sky. "*I'm setting off the left. Before.*"

He set off the left.

The strut came up out of the stone across the track behind Callie, at its slant, soft behind, leaning toward the hound. His left arm went to it. It did not scream the way it had on the seventy-ninth. It ached. It held.

The hound hit the strut and scrabbled, and Toren paid for it, out of the last of his own.

"*Holding,*" he said. His voice was a thread. "*Wyck.*"

"*Taking,*" said Wyck, from behind him, and came back down the track past Callie with the bar, and put it down against the strut's foot, under the hound.

"*Yours.*"

"*Mine.*"

"*Letting go.*"

The strut went out. Toren sagged against Otto's shoulder and Otto held him and did not move.

The hound was on the bar. Nobody was lit. Daven was out. Jab was the fence. Katori was on the bank, twenty paces up, and could not reach.

"*Clear?*" said Wyck.

"*Hold,*" said Silas. Two looks. "*There's another. Behind it. Coming up out of the shade.*"

Callie looked at the stone.

At post three the track had been mended once, a long time ago, before the lamps. She had read it in the morning. *Stone. Stone. Different stone.* The old slabs were grey and the mended ones were browner, from somewhere else, laid in a row across the track like a seam in a coat. There was a line where the old met the new. Nobody else would see a line. They would see a track.

She had used one change. That was what Dee had said. *Two changes in a watch.* She had held the Ward and let it go, and lit the line once on its own. She did not know if that was one or two. It did not matter. Her nose had not bled.

She knelt behind Wyck's bar on the brown stone and laid the hook down across the track along the seam.

"*Line,*" she said. "*Behind the bar.*"

She lit.

The light lay down along the seam across the track, crooked, where the brown stone met the grey. A pace and a half. Behind Wyck. Between the hound on his bar and the one coming up behind it.

The second hound came up out of the shade and met it and turned. It went off the track sideways, down the slope, into the sun on the slope below the track, which it did not want, and it hesitated there in the light.

Ines killed it from the south lip.

Callie heard the arrow. A thin hard sound from above and behind, a long way off. The hound came apart on the slope in the sun.

"*Clear,*" said Silas. "*Behind's clear. Wyck. The one on the bar.*"

Wyck turned the bar. He did not hold it. He hit it, a short hard swing, the way you knock a husk down a slope, and the hound went off the track sideways and down the slope into the sun. Ines killed it.

Something warm ran over Callie's lip.

She put her hand up. It came away red.

"*That's two,*" she said.

"*That's two,*" said Dessa, from the crate. "*Let it go. Walk.*"

Callie let it go. She got up. She did it without the hook first, and then remembered, and took it, and walked.

---

They came up onto the south lip at the first lamp, all of them, one at a time, off the end of the track by the south path, past Wyck standing at the top of it with his bar turned to face back down.

The foreman first, with the yard lifter over his shoulder. He walked to the canvas behind the table and knelt and put the yard lifter down on it, carefully, the way you put down a sack you do not want to split, and then sat down on the gravel beside him, all at once, as if somebody had cut a rope.

Otto, with Toren. He put Toren down on the third crate, beside Dessa's, and stood behind him with his hand on Toren's shoulder and did not move.

Dee. Daven, grey and shaking. Jab, last but one, with his knife dark and his right hand held out from his side.

Callie, last, with the hook and her sleeve red to the elbow where she had wiped her nose.

Wyck came up behind her and stood at the top of the south path with the bar, facing down the track, and did not move.

Dessa counted.

She did it from the crate, with the book open on her knee, out loud, one name at a time, with her eyes open.

"The foreman," she said. "The yard lifter. On the canvas." She looked at the crate beside her. "Toren." At the table. "Jab. Dee. Otto." At the edge of the lip. "Daven. Callie." At the top of the path. "Wyck." Up the bank, along the line. "Katori. Silas." Above the path. "Ines." At the crate beside her. "Elsie." At the table. "And the first yard lifter. The jar."

She stopped.

"Out," she said. "Everybody out of the red. Everybody named."

She wrote it.

Then she shut her eyes, and sat very still on the crate with her hand flat on the page, and Callie, standing at the edge of the lip with her nose still bleeding, saw that her hand was shaking.

"The table," said Dessa. "Now. Dee."

---

Dee drew them at the table, one after another, with the first yard lifter at the jar and Jab at her left shoulder with her glass.

She drew the second yard lifter first, the rest of what Jab had left. *The rest's the table's.* It took two turns. She drew the foreman. She drew Daven, who sat on the gravel with his back against the trestle and his eyes shut and did not say anything at all. She drew Toren, and Callie, and Wyck, and Otto, because they had all been in the red. She said *off* to herself, twice, and Jab said it after her, for the book.

The first yard lifter held the jar against her left palm with his right hand and did not move.

"Side's going," he said, every time. "Turn."

He turned it.

At the end of it he put his palm flat over the thin Flask and held it there a long time.

"One," he said. "Left. Of the eight and a half." He looked at Dessa. "The morning was two and a half. The track was a Handful and a half out of the small one. This was three and a half." He took his hand away. "One left."

"One left," said Dessa, and wrote it. "Below the line. We were below the line before we were up." She looked at the page. "Everybody was up."

Down in the bowl the fog from the pipe lay over the bottom and the slope below the track, grey in the shadow. It had stopped running. The water from the pipe's mouth ran across the bottom of the ditch, brown, and away. The husks stood over the jars below post five, over the drift, and then, one by one, they came apart on their own, or went back down into the joints, and nobody on the lip could tell which.

At the third hour after noon the fog lay down.

"Read," said Dessa.

"Forty at the lip," said Elsie. "On mine. Thirty-eight, true. Sixty on the slope." She held the old counter out over the bowl a long time. "It's lying."

"Down," said Dessa. "Red's off."

She wrote it. *115th. Third hour after noon. The fog lay down. Red off. Everybody up.*

Then, under it, smaller, in the column for what went wrong:

*Noon. Half an hour more, for the post-five drift. D. C.'s call. The ice went from under. The ramp went at the clay. Two down on the track. Six jars left on the drift below post five, where they lie.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she wrote one more line, in the right-hand column, for reasons.

*Why noon means noon: because the sun doesn't read the book. D. C.*

---

The Director came back down the made road at the fourth hour.

She came with the rope behind her, and the households behind it, and Tamsin Greave beside her with her hat in her hand. Sowerby came with the stretcher, and Keel. Rook came on his stick, slowly, at the back.

Sowerby went to the canvas. He knelt by the second yard lifter and looked at his ankle and said a word, and then looked at his face, and said a different word, less bad. Keel went to Toren on the crate and put her hand on his right knee and he said *yes* very loudly, and she nodded, as if he had told her something useful.

Then Keel went to Callie.

She looked at Callie's sleeve, red to the elbow. She looked at Callie's face. She did not touch her ribs.

"You held over bodies," said Keel.

"In bits," said Callie. "A door at a time. Fourteen breaths. Then I let go."

"That's still over bodies."

"Yes," said Callie.

Keel looked at her for a long moment.

"A week," she said. "Nothing. Not a door. Not a line, whatever that is." She looked at Callie's nose. "And I want to see you tomorrow, and you'll tell me what the line is, and I'll decide if it's mine." She went back to Toren.

Tamsin Greave stood at the head of the made road with her hat in her hand and did not come any further. She looked at the table. At the canvas. At the foreman sitting on the gravel beside the man he had carried seventy paces. At Dessa on the crate with the book shut on her knee.

She did not say anything to anybody. But Callie, standing at the edge of the lip with the cloth Dee had given her, saw her look at the book on Dessa's knee for a long time. Then she put her hat on.

At the rope, the girl in mittens came running down past everybody, without her mittens, straight to the table, and stopped at Jab's left shoulder.

"Did you stop?" she said.

"I stopped," said Jab. "At a hand. Dee said."

The girl looked at his right hand. At the white place on the heel.

"It's the same," she said.

"It's the same."

---

They weighed it at the plate in the evening, in front of everybody, because the Director said so.

Twenty-eight jars. The gate crew carried them up the made road in sacks and set them on the steel, a sack at a time. The weighman put the test weights on first, slowly, in front of all of them, as he did every morning, and went inside to the beam.

"Fifty," said Callie's mother, from the stool.

"Fifty," said the weighman.

Callie read. She stood at the end of the plate with her counter and a cloth stuffed up her left nostril and read every jar, ten breaths each, and said it, and her mother wrote it. She did not hurry. Nobody asked her to.

"Clean," she said. "Clean. Clean." Twenty-one times. Then, for the last seven, from the bottom of the drift by post five: "Dirty. Grit. It'll burn wrong."

The weighman weighed them.

"Clean, three Flasks," he said, when he had finished. "Twenty-four. Dirty, a Flask. Eight." He came out with the chalk.

The Director stood at the end of the plate with her spectacles on.

"Eight of the clean under the plate," she said. "To the store. Now. Tonight." She looked at the board. At the box round *OWED*. "That's the Flask I brought down the made road on the seventy-ninth."

The weighman looked at the box.

He took the ruler off its nail. He drew a line through *OWED*, through the box, from corner to corner, slowly, so that everybody standing at the plate could see it. He did not rub it out. He wrote under it, in chalk:

*REPAID 115TH. FROM THE SAG'S OWN SLOPE. BY THE HOME LINE.*

Somebody at the back of the crowd began to clap, and then stopped, because nobody else did, and the weighman looked at them.

"Eight to the Sag table," said the Director. "For the spring watches." She looked at Dessa. "Against *D. C.*"

"Against *D. C.*," said Dessa.

"And eight to the store," said the Director. "Marked. Not for the machine. Not for the watch. Not for anything." She looked at the board, at the struck line from the fifty-eighth, at *The rest where it lies.* "For the hill. If there's a hill. If anybody goes." She did not look at anybody. "Write it, Mr Hallet. *The hill's eight.*"

Hallet wrote it.

"And the river's," said the Director. She turned to Tamsin Greave, who was standing by the hut with her two behind her. "Two Flasks lent. Both spent. The clean on the watches, the thin today." She held out her hand toward the plate. "There's sixteen clean on this plate still. I'll repay you now. In kind. On the plate."

Tamsin Greave looked at the jars.

"No," she said.

The Director waited.

"Repay the river when the hill's weighed," said Tamsin Greave. "Not out of this. This is your Sag's. Your own slope." She looked at the board. "Leave it on there. *Lent.* I like it there." She looked at Callie's mother, in the window. "Where everybody walks past it."

The weighman, by the board, did not rub out the river's line.

---

Jab went to the ward at supper with his right hand held out from his side.

Tonk was sitting on the edge of the fifth bed with the crutch beside him and the tray on his knee. He had not lain down all day, Mrs Lask said. He had sat on the edge of the bed since the second hour of the morning with his feet over the side, facing the window, and when the orderlies came in from the yard gate after noon with the news he had made them tell him twice.

He looked at Jab coming down the ward.

He did not say anything. He looked at Jab's right hand.

Jab held it out to him, palm up.

Tonk put his tray down on the bed. He took Jab's hand in both of his, the left and the right with the cord round it, and turned it over, and looked at the white place on the heel, and the pink round it. He looked at it very closely, the way he looked at his pin sites. He pressed the white place with his thumb.

"Does that hurt?"

"I can't feel it."

"I know you can't *feel* it." He pressed round the edge. "That?"

"Round the outside."

"Same as before?"

"Same as before."

Tonk let go of the hand.

"It's the same," he said.

"It's the same."

Tonk looked at him for a long time. He had the cord round his right fist and Little in his lap, and his two legs hanging over the side of the bed, the thin one a Little short.

"Ask me now," he said.

Jab looked at him.

He did not know how to say it. He had thought about it all the way up the made road and across the yard and down the corridor. He had thought about saying *will you let me*, and *can I*, and *is it all right*. None of them were right. They were all asking Tonk to be the reason.

"I want to go to the hill," said Jab. "In the spring. With the Home Line. I'm asking what you say."

"Go," said Tonk.

He said it at once. He did not have to think about it. He had had it ready all day.

Jab stood by the bed.

"Go," said Tonk again. "You came back. With the hand the same. You stopped when Dee said." He looked at Jab. "Rook said. He came in after and told me. He said you stopped and the man was still busy and you stopped anyway." He picked up Little. "That's what I wanted to know. If you'd stop."

"I stopped."

"Then go," said Tonk.

He turned Little over in his fingers.

"And bring me something," he said. "From the hill."

"Not ash."

"Not *ash*," said Tonk, scornfully. "Everybody brings ash. A stone." He held up Little. "From the river. By the hill. Rook says there's a river. With a bridge. Three planks." He looked at the fish. "A stone this long. Out of the river. That's been in the water."

"A stone this long," said Jab.

"And when you go," said Tonk, "I'll walk to the gate." He looked at the crutch beside him on the bed. "On this. To the yard gate. Not in a barrow. Sowerby'll say no. I'll ask him every day till he says yes." He looked at Jab. "That's my bit. You go. I walk to the gate."

"That's a long way."

"It's not far," said Tonk. "I've seen it. Out of the window." He put Little down on the pillow. "Now eat your supper. It's on the paper."

Jab sat down in the chair. He picked up his tray. It was porridge. It was cold. He ate all of it.

Across the ward, in the second bed on the left, Rook was sitting up against the wall with his stick across his knees, and he was not asleep. In the fourth bed on the right, Senna was not asleep either. And at the far end of the ward, in the bed by the door, where the orderlies had put him at the fifth hour with his ankle in a splint and his face grey, the second yard lifter turned his head on the pillow and looked down the length of the ward at the fifth bed, at the boy eating cold porridge with his right hand held out a little from his side.

He did not say anything. He was too tired. But Jab felt him look, and felt what was in it, and it was not busy at all.
