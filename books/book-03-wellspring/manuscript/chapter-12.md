# Chapter 12 — Toren: The Assumed Line

The Director said it at the plate on the hundred and sixth, in the morning, to everybody who would stand in the south yard and listen.

That was a great many people. Toren had not known there were so many people at Meridian who did not work on the lip. They came out of the south row and the long building and the yard office, and from the huts behind the stable, and stood on the wet concrete in front of the weighbridge in a loose half-ring. Women with baskets. Men from the yard office in their long coats. Lifters off the east crew with their hands in their pockets. Children. Toren saw the first yard lifter's wife, small and broad in her grey coat, with her husband beside her and his bandaged hand across his chest. He saw a tall thin woman in a south-row apron standing very straight with her arms folded. He saw the girl in mittens with a woman who must have been her mother, holding her hand.

The Home Line stood at the end of the plate, because the Director had asked them to. Tamsin Greave stood at the corner of the weighbridge hut with her hat on, and her two with their staves behind her. And at the back, at the edge of the south yard, where the concrete met the cinders of the path to the south row, two men stood a little apart from everybody else. One had a fur collar turned up. The other wore good gloves.

The weighman put the test weights on, as he did every morning. He did it slowly, in front of all of them, one block and then the other, and went inside to the beam.

"Fifty," said Callie's mother, from the stool.

"Fifty," said the weighman.

He took the blocks off. Then the Director stepped up onto the end of the plate, onto the steel, so that she stood a hand higher than everybody, and put her spectacles on, and took them off again.

"I said on the eighty-second," she said, "that the spring review would ask one thing. Whether this station goes back to Caul Hill. Bounded. With a line on it, and an abort, and a red-call rule, and a book." Her voice was not loud. It carried anyway, the way Dessa's did. "I said I'd ask every person on the defender sheet for their own answer. And I said I'd ask it in public, with this board read out first."

Nobody said anything. A child somewhere at the back asked a question and was hushed.

"I'm going to tell you how I'll ask," said the Director. "So it's not a surprise to anybody. So nobody can say afterward that it was done in a room with the door shut."

She held up one finger.

"On the hundred and twelfth, in the long room, any household with somebody on the defender sheet can come and say what it thinks. Out loud. Before anybody's asked anything. Whether they want their person to go, and on what terms, and what they'd want instead." She looked round the half-ring. "Any household. Not just the ones I'd like to hear from."

She held up a second finger.

"On the hundred and fifteenth, there'll be a field test. At the Sag. By day." She turned and looked north, over the wet roofs of the yard office, toward the berm and the yard gate and the made road going down to the south lip. "There's a winter lying on the Sag's east slopes. Every kill since the first frost came apart where it fell, and nobody's knelt below the lamp-line to lift it. Because that was the rule. Because the rule's written in blood." She turned back. "When the thaw's done, it'll go into the ditch. Then into the pipe. Then nowhere. That's the Sag's own ash, and it's lying there, and it's going."

Toren felt the Home Line beside him go still. He felt Dessa, in the barrow, put both her hands flat on the book.

"I'm asking the Home Line to lift it," said the Director. "Below the lamp-line. By day. Bounded. Under its own command, with its own line, and its own abort, and its own red-call rule, and its own book. And I'm asking it to do it in front of everybody. In front of this station, and the river, and anybody else who'd like to stand on the berm and watch." She did not look at the back of the yard. She did not have to. "If they can't lift the Sag's own slope by daylight, they'll not lift a hill a hundred miles off. And I'd rather know that here."

She held up a third finger.

"On the hundred and seventeenth, in the long room, I'll read the board. Then I'll ask. Each person. *Will you come.* And their answer goes in Dessa's book in their own hand. Then I'll say what the station does." She put her hand down. "That's all. That's the whole of it. I'll not change it after."

She stepped down off the plate.

The half-ring broke up slowly. People talked. Toren heard it go round the south yard like water finding its level. *Below the lamp-line. By day. In front of everybody.* He looked at the back of the yard.

The two men had not moved. The oldest was looking at the Director with the same mild interest he had given the coins on the ledge. The gloved man was looking at the end of the plate. At the Home Line. At Jab.

Then they both turned and went back along the cinders toward the south row, not hurrying.

"She's using it for both," said Callie, beside him, low.

"Both what?"

"The ash and the proof," said Callie. "If we lift it, there's ash. Enough to take the box off the board. And if we lift it right, in front of everybody, she can say *they can*." She was looking at the board on the wall of the hut, at *OWED* in its ruled box. "She's not wasting anything. Not even the test."

---

They planned it in the east shed on the hundred and seventh, with the door shut and the stove going and everybody who would be on the Sag that day sitting or standing round Dee's long table.

Dessa did not let anybody else run it. She sat in the barrow at the end of the table with the book open on her knee, and when anybody started to say a thing before it was their turn, she said their name, and they stopped.

"Ground first," she said. "Callie."

Callie stood. She had the hook. She did not lean on it. She went to the long table, which had been cleared of everything but the brass scale, and took a lump of chalk from Dee's tin and drew the Sag on the boards.

She drew it the way she read it. Not as a map. As where things changed.

The bowl first, a long oval, three hundred paces lip to lip. The made road down the middle of it, breaking into slabs at the bottom, with the black joints between them. The south lip at the near end, with the crates and the table and the four south lamps round them. The north lip at the far end, toward the culvert. The ditches down both sides.

Then the east side, which was what mattered.

"The track," said Callie. She drew it, a line along the east slope, a little below the upper edge. "It's stone. It was always stone. It's why the lifters used it before the lamps. It's three paces wide where it's widest and two where it's not." She drew the upper lamp-line above it, along its upper edge, and put seven dots on it. "Lamps. One by the south lip. Seven out toward the north. The dark lane's between three and four." She drew a short line down from between the third and fourth dots. "Here."

She drew the lower line, below the track, nearer the bottom: five dots. Then the posts down the east side, painted white, with their numbers.

"Posts," she said. "Every fifty feet. One by the south lip. Six at the bottom, flat in the water." She put a small cross on the slope below the track, about level with the fifth lamp. "And here's the pipe. The east mouth. In the ditch bank. Under the track, a long way down. Below post five."

Everybody looked at the cross.

"The grey's lying here," said Callie. She drew a long shaded patch along the track and the slope below it, from about the third lamp to the fifth. "Thickest between post three and post five. That's where the far gap was, the seventy-ninth. That's where the most came up. That's where the most came apart." She put the chalk down. "A winter of kills on the slope and the track. Frozen in. Coming out now."

"How much?" said Dessa.

Callie looked at Otto.

"I went along the top of the bank yesterday," said Otto. He went red. "By the lamps. And I lay down on my belly at the third and put my hand flat over the edge. Where it was lying." He looked at the table. "A lot. I can't say. I've never felt that much on one slope. It's thin in places. It's thick in others." He was quiet. "More than four Flasks. Less than ten."

"More than four, less than ten," said Dessa, and wrote it.

---

"Exits," said Dessa. "Toren."

Toren went to the table and stood beside Callie's drawing.

He saw it as weight, and where it went. He saw the track running along the east slope like a shelf, with the steep bank going up above it to the lamps and the slope going down below it toward the bottom. He saw a man kneeling on that shelf with a jar, lifting, and the shelf going away on both sides of him, a long way, and the only ways up off it.

"Two," he said.

He put his finger on the south end of the track, by the first lamp.

"Here. The south path. Where the track meets the lip path by the first lamp. It's flat. It's stone. You walk off the track onto the south lip. That's the long way, from post five. Seventy paces, near enough. At walking pace with a full sack, a long time."

He moved his finger to the fifth lamp.

"Here. The ramp. There's a scramble up the bank by the fifth lamp's pole. Gravel. Six paces up, steep. The lifters used to use it when the track was the only way down." He looked at Callie. "You read it yesterday."

"Gravel over clay," said Callie. "Frozen. A hand down there's clay, and the clay's frozen hard. While it's frozen, it'll hold. It's the quick way off from post five. Ten breaths up."

"And when it thaws?"

"Then it's clay," said Callie. "And clay holds till it doesn't."

Toren looked at the ramp on the drawing.

"I'll be at the top of it," he said. "With struts. If anybody's coming up it in a hurry with a sack, I'll set steps. A strut a step. Each one holds you for a breath." He looked round. "We did it on the road. At the wash. It works on stone."

"It works on frozen," said Wyck, from the stove. "You found that out on the ninety-ninth."

"It works on frozen," said Toren. "So it works till noon."

"Why till noon?" said Dessa.

Toren looked at Elsie.

---

Elsie was sitting on the end of the bench by the door with the old counter on her hip, under its tape. She had not said anything yet. She stood up when Toren looked at her, and came to the table, and stood looking at Callie's drawing for a while before she spoke.

"The dark moves," she said. "The day holder said. With the sun."

She put her finger on the east slope.

"In the morning the sun's in the east. Behind us. Over the east lip. It's on the west side of the bowl. The east side's in the shade of its own bank, a bit, low down. Only at the bottom." She moved her finger. "As the sun goes over, the shade goes down. By the middle of the day there's none. The whole east side's lit. Track and all." She moved her finger again, slowly, back up the slope. "After the middle, the sun's in the west. And the east side's the dark side. The shade comes up out of the bottom. Up the slope. Over the lower line. Up to the track." She stopped. "By the middle of the afternoon the whole east slope's in shadow. And the hounds go for the dark."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"I went and asked her," said Elsie. "The day holder. On the hundred and fourth. I asked her about the thaw too." She looked at Dessa. "She said the thaw's worse than the frost. She said in the frost everything's held, and in the thaw everything moves at once." She looked at the cross on the slope, below post five. "She said the pipe runs. When the ditch thaws, the water goes into the pipe, and things come out of it like rats out of a drain when the water comes."

Toren looked at the cross.

"Both mouths," said Rook.

He was sitting on a chair by the stove that Otto had carried across from the long building. He had not said anything all morning. He sat with his stick across his knees and his back very straight.

"It's in the book," said Rook. "The day holder told the girls on the sixty-sixth. First at six, then the pipe, both mouths. Dessa read it me." He looked at the cross on the drawing. "The east mouth's under your track."

"It's iced," said Elsie. "Now. I went and looked. Both mouths. There's ice across the east one a hand thick, and the ditch in front of it's still frozen at the bottom." She looked at the drawing. "The sun's not on that ditch till the middle of the day. It's in the bank's shade in the morning. That's why it's still iced."

"So it holds till noon," said Dessa.

"It holds till the sun's been on it," said Elsie. "That's noon. Or after."

Dessa wrote. She wrote for a while.

"So," she said. "We start at the second hour. In the morning. With the east side lit and the ramp frozen and the pipe iced." She looked at the drawing with her eyes open, which she did not often do. "And we're off the slope by noon. All of us. Every jar up. Nobody on the track after the sun's gone over." She put the pencil down. "That's the line. That's the time line."

"And the ground line?" said Toren.

Dessa looked at Elsie.

Elsie took the chalk from the table and drew a short heavy line across the track, at post five.

"Whitewash," she said. "Across the track. At the fifth post. Nobody below it." She put the chalk down. "Below post five's the bottom. The flat slab at six. The pipe. The water. Above it's the track, and the grey, and the lifters." She looked round. "It's a line you can see. If it's painted, nobody can say they didn't know where it was."

"The assumed line," said Rook quietly.

Everybody looked at him.

"That's what Vell would have called it," said Rook. "At Homura. She'd draw a line on a plan and write *assumed* beside it. Not *safe*. *Assumed.*" He looked at the drawing. "So you remember it's a guess."

Dessa looked at him for a long moment.

Then she picked up the pencil and wrote it in the book, under the line about whitewash. Toren saw it, upside down. *Post 5: the assumed line. Nobody below it.*

---

"Ash," said Dessa. "Dee."

Dee did not get up. She sat on her stool with her glass in her fist and spoke from there.

"The table's got a Handful and a half of the river's clean, left from the watches. That's what's on the shelf." She nodded at the frame against the wall, at the thin Flask sealed with wax. "And the river's thin. Eight by weight. Callie called it seven. The Director says it's for this."

"Eight and a half," said Callie. "Working."

"Eight and a half," said Dee. "That's all. For everybody on the slope, all morning, and whatever comes. Lifters lifting dirty ground get busy. Not much. But all morning, it adds." She turned the glass over. "Two drawers. Jab and me. The girl to watch, and to draw small if it's small. Otto at the jar, and him." She nodded at the first yard lifter, who was sitting at the end of the bench with his bandaged hand on his knee. "The *off* goes round."

"And the line?"

"Two Handfuls left on the table," said Dee. "Everybody up. Off the slope. Whether it's noon or not."

"Two Handfuls left," said Dessa, writing. "Everybody up."

"And red," said Dee.

The shed went quiet.

"The red-call rule stands," said Dee. She said it the way she had written it on the thirty-eighth, at another table, a long way off. "*Red, and a body trapped: everybody who isn't needed to get that body out goes out at once. The fewest who can do it stay, and the watch says their names out loud as they go in.*" She looked round the table. "*Red, and nobody trapped: everybody out at once.* That's on the paper. The Director signed it. It's not changing because it's daylight and there's people watching."

"Who reads red?" said Toren.

"Me," said Elsie. "From the crate. With this." She put her hand on the old counter. "It's two high. So red on mine's a hundred and fifty-two. I'll say it as I read it and say *two high* after."

"And me," said Callie. "On the track. With mine." She looked at Dessa. "Somebody should be reading on the track. Down there. Not just the crate. The crate's forty paces off and up."

"You'll be on the track," said Dessa.

"I'll be reading ground for the lifters. Where it's soft. Where the stone's gone." Callie looked at the drawing. "And reading the count. Both. I can do both. I can't do both and hold."

"Then don't hold," said Dessa.

"Keel says a door's worth."

"Then a door's worth," said Dessa, "and only if I say. Named." She wrote it. "Callie: on the track. Reading ground and count. Not holding unless named."

---

They did the roles after that. It took a long time. Dessa went round the table one name at a time, and every name said where they would be and what they would do and what they would not do, out loud, and Dessa wrote it.

The second yard lifter would lift. So would the east lifters' foreman, a long grey man who had come to the shed because the Director had asked the east crew for two, and his son, who was sixteen and had lifted on the east track with his father before the lamps. The three of them on the track, between post three and post five, with jars and sacks, working down toward the whitewash line and no further.

Wyck would be at the south path, where the track met the lip by the first lamp, with his bar. *If anything comes along the track toward the lip, I'm the door.* Katori would be on the upper line at the dark lane, with her blade, as she had been all winter, and relief light if anybody's light went. Ines would not be on the west rim. She would be on the south lip by the first lamp, above Wyck, where she could see the whole of the east slope from the side. *The west side's not where we're working. The east is. I'll be where I can see the east.*

Silas would be the end. At the fifth lamp, at the top of the ramp, beside Toren. *Where I can see the ramp and the track and the slope.*

"And the line below the track," said Silas. "The whitewash. I can see that from the fifth lamp."

"Two looks," said Toren.

"Two looks," said Silas. He did not go red.

Daven would be a light. On the upper line between the fourth and fifth lamps, lit only if he was called. *Husks go at lit things,* said Katori. *In the day as much as the night. If you light for nothing, you'll bring them up the slope to you. Light when you're called. Say* going *and the count.*

"*Going*, and the count," said Daven.

"And me?" said Jab.

Dessa looked at him.

"You're the table," she said. "With Dee. Drawer first. Lifters come up busy, you draw them. And if I call a fence, you're the first fence, and Dee has the table, and somebody else is lit before you go dark." She looked at Daven. "That's you. Or Katori."

"Not both," said Jab.

"Not both," said Dessa. "Never both. Write it, Jab. In your own hand. On the table page."

Jab wrote it.

"And me," said Toren.

"You're the ramp," said Dessa. "You're at the top of it with a strut. If anybody needs up it in a hurry, you set steps. If anything comes up it, you hold it on a strut and the end calls, and Daven or Katori or the yard lifter's bar takes it." She did not look away from him. "You're not at post five. You're not on the track. You're not at the whitewash line. You're at the top of a ramp, six paces up, and that's all you are."

Toren felt it come up in him. He knew it was coming. It came from the same place the strut came from, and it said: *the hardest place is post five. The pipe's under post five. Put yourself there.*

He let it come up. He looked at it.

"I'd rather be at post five," he said.

"I know you would," said Dessa.

"That's where it's worst."

"That's why you're not there," said Dessa. "If you're at post five, you'll hold everything that comes. You'll not mean to. You'll just do it." She tapped the book. "It's on the card. Your card. *Never let go first.* You can't let go of a thing you're standing in the middle of."

Toren looked at the drawing. At the ramp. At the top of it, six paces above the track, where the fifth lamp stood.

"The ramp," he said.

"The ramp," said Dessa, and wrote it.

---

"And me," said Rook.

They all looked at him.

He had not been on Dessa's list. Sowerby had said he could stand at a lip and watch. He had said he was not to hold anything, or lift anything heavier than his stick, or go below the lamp-line on any account. *If I see you below that line I'll leave you there.*

"I'll be at the crate," said Rook. "Beside Dessa. Not holding. Not anything." He turned his stick over on his knees. "I'll be the one who isn't anything."

"Why?" said Dessa.

"Because somebody has to be," said Rook. "Somebody who's not on the line and not at the table and not reading. Who can see all of it and isn't busy with any of it." He looked at Toren. "Whoever can see both. That's Silas, at the ramp. I'll be it for the whole bowl. If you want me." He paused. "And if you don't, I'll stand on the berm with the river woman and the gentlemen from nineteen."

Dessa looked at him for a long time.

"You'll not say anything," she said. "Unless I ask."

"I'll not say anything unless you ask."

"You'll not *do* anything."

"I can't do anything," said Rook. "Sowerby's seen to that."

Dessa wrote it. *R.: at the crate. Not on the line. Sees. Says only if asked.*

Then she shut the book and looked round the table at all of them.

"That's it," she said. "Read it back, Callie."

Callie read it back. Every name. Every place. Every *not*. It took a long time. When she got to *Post 5: the assumed line*, she read it twice.

Toren stood at the end of the table and looked at the chalk drawing on the boards while she read. The bowl, and the track, and the posts, and the lamps, and the cross under post five where the pipe was. The heavy line across the track at five. Dessa's line. Elsie's line. The guess, painted white so nobody could say they did not know where it was.

He did not do the sum. But he looked at the cross under the line for a long time.

---

Silas's mother came to the lane on the hundred and eighth.

Toren knew her before she spoke. She was the tall thin woman in the south-row apron who had stood at the plate with her arms folded. She came across the north yard at the end of the morning, straight, not hurrying and not slow, and stopped at the head of the first lane where Toren was standing.

Silas was at the end. He saw her and went red, all the way, and then white.

"You're Toren," said the woman.

"Yes."

"You're the one who wrote his name on a card." She did not look at Silas. "He told me. He came home and told me a boy had written his name on a card and put it in a book. He'd not stopped talking about it in three days."

Toren did not know what to say.

"I'm his mother," said the woman. "I've come to say what I think. Before the hundred and twelfth. Because I'll not stand up in that long room in front of the river and the south row and say it, so I'm saying it here." She folded her arms. "He can do the Sag. On the fifteenth. In the day."

Silas, at the end of the lane, let out a breath.

"He's stood on that lip all winter," said his mother. "He came home on the eightieth grey to the lips and slept two days and got up and went back. I didn't stop him. The Sag's home. It's half a mile from my door." She looked at Toren. "He can stand at a lamp in the daylight with you and do whatever it is he does. *The end.* I don't know what it means. He's told me. I still don't know." She stopped. "He can do that."

"And the hill?" said Silas, from the end. Loud. Too loud.

His mother turned her head and looked at him for the first time.

"No," she said.

"Mam—"

"That hill took the old north crew," said his mother. "Every one. They're in the ground. Their names are on the weighbridge board in white paint and they're in the ground." Her voice did not rise. "That hill's not home. It's a hundred miles off and it's got rooms nobody's opened and a company of men wanting it, and you're fifteen."

"I'm *nearly*—"

"You're fifteen," said his mother. "I was there. I know exactly how old you are." She looked back at Toren. "That's what I came to say. The Sag, yes. The hill, no. Not this time." She paused. "Not this year."

Silas looked at Toren.

Toren saw it. He saw what Silas wanted, all of it, in his face. He wanted Toren to say *he's good*. He wanted Toren to say *he's the end, he's the best end we've got, he said* clear *at the fifth lamp all night on the seventy-ninth and was wrong once*. He wanted somebody who had stood on the lip with him to stand at the head of the lane and argue with his mother for him.

Toren opened his mouth.

He shut it again.

"It's not mine to say," he said.

Silas's face went still.

His mother looked at Toren for a long moment. Something in her face changed. It was not softer. It was more careful, as if she had been ready to fight and had found nobody in front of her.

"No," she said. "It's not." She unfolded her arms. "He's on your lane till the fifteenth. Then he's mine." She looked down the lane at her son. "Supper's at the second sitting. Don't be late."

She went back across the north yard the way she had come.

---

Silas did not come to the head of the lane after. He did the drill. He did it right. He looked below and looked along and said *clear* and *hold* in the right places, and never once too quick. But when Katori said *that'll do*, he put the sack on the frame and walked off toward the sand heap without looking at anybody.

Toren found him there later, sitting at the foot of the heap with his back against it and his long legs out on the wet cinders.

"You could've said I'm good," said Silas, without looking up.

"You are good," said Toren.

"Then why didn't you *say*?"

Toren sat down on the cinders beside him. It was wet. He sat anyway.

"Because that's not what she asked," he said. "She didn't ask if you're good. She knows you're good. She came to say she doesn't want you to go to the hill." He looked at the sand heap. "If I'd said you're good, she'd have had to argue with me. And then it's not hers, what she decides. It's hers against mine."

Silas did not say anything.

"Gran says my father would have carried the whole party," said Toren. "If you'd let him. Garrick. She says he couldn't give a thing away to save his life. Not a spade. Not a job." He was quiet. "He carried it to the gate, in the end. That's how she says it. *To the gate.*"

Silas looked at him sideways.

"He's dead?"

"He's dead," said Toren. "He carried the whole party. And there's nobody to ask him what he'd have done different." He looked at his left hand. He closed it and opened it. "I've been trying not to be him all winter. I'm not very good at it. That's why I didn't say anything to your mam." He looked at Silas. "It wasn't mine to carry."

Silas sat with his back against the sand heap.

"I'll say it myself," he said at last. "In the book. On the hundred and seventeenth. When they ask." He picked up a handful of wet cinders and let them run out of his fist. "Whatever I say. It'll be mine."

"It'll be yours," said Toren.

"Even if it's *not this time*."

"Even then."

Silas threw the last of the cinders at the heap. They spattered on the sand and stuck.

"Two looks," he said. "I'll do two looks on the fifteenth. At the ramp. All day." He looked at Toren. "And then she'll have seen. And then next year it's mine."

---

Gran's course was on the hundred and ninth, in the afternoon.

Toren walked beside the chair down the long slope with the orderly pushing. He said *camber* at the place where the slope tipped, before she could. She did not say anything about it this time. She had stopped saying anything about it on the eighty-seventh.

"You'll go," she said, halfway down.

"If they ask."

"They'll ask." She looked ahead down the slope at the doors at the bottom, and the shed beyond them, and the machine. "And you'll say yes. You've had it in your face since the eighty-second." She was quiet. "I'm not going to say no."

Toren walked beside the chair.

"I can't go," said Gran. "You know that. I'll be at that yellow line every eleven days till they carry me out. I was at that hill when I was fifty-three. It took my hands and my feet and twenty years." She turned her hands over in her lap. They lay there, the fingers not quite closed. "But I had the route. I had all of it. Every halt. Every stone. Which way the gate opens. I've given it to Dessa, a bit at a time, all winter." She looked up at him. "You're not to carry it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were," said Gran. "You've had your hand on your shirt every time anybody's said *road* since the fifty-fifth. You'd carry it if I let you." She looked ahead. "So here's my bit. On the sheet, or wherever the Director wants it. Copy my route. Twice. One goes to the hill. One stays here, in the long room, with the atlas. And the one that goes, you don't carry. You give it to whoever's reading. Callie. Or the river woman's reader, if she's got one." She was quiet. "Not you. Never the one who's holding."

"Why?"

"Because the one who's holding's busy," said Gran. "Katori said it. You wrote it on your card." She almost smiled. "*Not the one holding.* I can read, Toren. Dessa reads me the book every night."

At the yellow line they lifted her onto the narrow bed. The technician warmed the machine with his left hand. It sang its long note and held it, and the lights went green along the rows, every one.

Toren stood at the yellow line. He looked, without deciding to, at the end of the first row, at the round seat in the steel with its nine fine ribs, bright, the only thing on the rack without the grey film on it.

His hand did not go to his shirt.

He did not make it stay at his side. He noticed, after a while, that it was simply there, at his side, and had been the whole time.

"Steady," said the technician.

"It's in tune," said Gran, to the ceiling. Then, still to the ceiling: "And come back up that slope, Toren. Count the elevens. I'll be here for every one of them."

---

On the hundred and eleventh they tried the ramp.

It was early, before the first watch went down, with the frost still on the north side of everything and the sky going pale over the east lip. Wyck went down first, along the track from the south path, slowly, knocking with his bar every few paces. *Sharp. Sharp. Sharp.* Stone, all the way. Callie went after him with the hook, reading. Toren stood at the top of the ramp by the fifth lamp's pole and looked down it.

Six paces of gravel, steep, going down the bank from the foot of the pole to the track below. It was grey and loose on top. It had frost on it.

Wyck knocked at the bottom of it. Then halfway up. Then near the top.

"Sharp," he said. "All the way."

"It's clay under," said Callie, from the track. She was standing at the foot of the ramp with the hook, looking at it the way she looked at everything she read, as if she could see through it. "Frozen. It's hard as a floor now. When the sun's on it, it'll go." She looked up the bank at the sky. "It faces west. A bit. The sun gets on it after the middle of the day. Not before."

"Then before," said Toren.

Otto came down the track with a sack on his shoulder. It was a sack of sand off the heap, the weight of a full sack of jars, near enough. He stopped at the foot of the ramp and looked up at Toren.

"Set," said Toren.

He set off the right. The strut came up out of the frozen gravel at the bottom of the ramp, flat, a step. Otto put his foot on it. It held his weight. He came up onto it.

"Step," said Toren, and let the first go and set the second, a pace higher.

It was the thing they had done at the wash, on the road, a long time ago. A strut a step. Each one holding a man's weight for a breath, while he got his other foot on the next. It cost by the breath. Every breath there was a man on a strut, Toren paid for it out of his own. But it was a breath each, no more.

Otto came up the ramp on six struts with the sack on his shoulder, one breath to each, and stood at the top beside the fifth lamp's pole, breathing hard and red in the face.

"Six," said Silas. He had been counting, from the end. "Six struts. Six breaths. Eleven breaths, bottom to top, with the steps between."

"Ten breaths up," said Callie, from the track. "I said."

"Eleven," said Silas. "I counted."

"Then write eleven," said Callie.

Toren stood at the top of the ramp with his own a little lower than it had been. It had held. Every strut had stood on the frozen gravel and not slid. He looked down the ramp at the place where the first one had stood, at the bottom, where the gravel lay over the clay.

"Before noon," he said.

"Before noon," said Dessa, from the crate on the south lip, forty paces off, where she had been listening with her eyes shut. "That's what's in the book."

Elsie, at the crate beside her, was not looking at the ramp. She was looking down the slope, past the lower line, at the ditch. At the cross in the ditch bank where the pipe's east mouth was.

"Iced," she said, before anybody asked. "Still."

---

On the night of the hundred and fourteenth Toren sat on the step of eleven and did not take the card out.

It was in Dessa's book. All of it. The card, and the table page in Jab's hand, and Callie's drawing copied in from the chalk, and every name and every place and every *not*. Dessa had read it all back to them at the end of the evening in the shed, one more time, and then shut the book and said *Sleep*, as if it were an order. Perhaps it was.

The row was quiet. The gutter at the end of eleven had stopped running. The wind had gone round in the evening and was coming from the west now, dry, and the sky over the berm was clear all the way up, with stars in it.

Rook came along the row on his stick, slowly, from the long building. He did not usually come to the row. He stopped at the foot of the step of eleven and looked up at the sky.

"It'll be warm tomorrow," he said. "Bright. The first real one."

"Is that good?"

Rook thought about it.

"It's good for lifting," he said. "The grey'll come out of the slope easy. It's good for seeing." He looked at the stars. "It's bad for anything that was waiting for the sun to come and let it out."

Toren looked at him.

"I'm not saying anything," said Rook. "I said I'd not say anything unless I'm asked. I'm practising." He leaned on his stick. "I'm saying the weather."

"Elsie said the pipe holds till noon."

"Elsie said the pipe holds till the sun's been on it," said Rook. "That's not the same. She said it right. You heard it wrong." He turned round, slowly, on the stick, in three movements. "Noon's when Dessa says. The sun doesn't read Dessa's book."

He went back along the row toward the long building.

Toren sat on the step.

He let the sum come up. He did not try to stop it. *If it's warm. If it's bright. If the sun's on the ditch early. If the ramp's soft before noon. If we're late. If the lifters want one more row. If.* He let every line of it come up and he looked at all of them.

Then he did a thing he had not done before. He did not put it down. He did not carry it either. He got up and went across the row to fourteen and knocked on the door.

Callie opened it. She was in her coat. She had not been asleep.

"Rook says it'll be bright," said Toren.

"I know," said Callie. "I can feel it. The air's gone dry." She looked past him at the sky. "The sun'll be on the ditch sooner."

"How much sooner?"

Callie looked at the sky for a long time.

"I don't know," she said. "I can read ground. I can't read the sun." She looked at him. "Elsie can. Ask her in the morning. At the crate. Not now." She was quiet. "Don't do it on your own."

"I wasn't doing it on my own," said Toren. "I came here."

Callie looked at him. Then something went across her face, at the corner of the mouth.

"So you did," she said. "Go to bed, Toren."

He went back across the row and in at the door of eleven, and lay down, and did not count anything. He had not put the sum down and he had not carried it. He had taken it across the row and knocked on a door with it, and left it there, and come back.

He did not know if that was the same as putting it down. He thought it might be better.
