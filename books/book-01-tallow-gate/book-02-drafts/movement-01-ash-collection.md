He came down off the roadbed alone with the sack of jars across his shoulder, and above and behind him the noise of the others went on being ordinary — Wyck saying something twice, and Dessa not answering it, and then the flat knock of a tailboard going up.

The two of them had come apart about forty feet from each other, which was the whole of the difficulty.

"Flask," he said, of the drift up on the cart track, and did not break stride. "Flask and a fair one. Handful in the ditch and it's a dirty Handful, and you'll be lucky." He came down onto the stone. "Four minutes. Same four minutes for the both of you, and I've got one pair of hands, so one of you is going into the ground."

He set the sack on the dry stone and took two jars out of it and worked their bottoms into the grit until they stood on their own, and drew both stoppers and laid them in the mouth of the sack where they would not roll.

Then he went to the good one first, because that is the order.

He put his hand flat on the track in the middle of it, the whole palm, heel down first and the fingers laid after it the way a man sets a plank, and pressed until the ash packed up under him and there was nothing at all between his skin and it.

"Flat," he said. "All of it flat. Fingers too. There."

Under his hand the grey dulled and went on dulling, out from the heel of it in a circle that widened and slowed and stopped, and he lifted the palm and moved it a span and set it down again and the circle started over.

"Don't haul," he said. "It isn't rope. It comes at the rate it comes, and pulling at it doesn't get you more of it, it gets you less — no. No. There. There, that's it."

He worked along the drift that way, on one knee, a span at a time, and what he lifted he held, and he held it low and open at his side with the fingers apart, and did not close them.

"High," he said. "Hold it high. It'll go down through you if you let it, and then you've got the ache tomorrow and the road's got the money." And after a moment, further along, in the same flat voice: "Nothing in it to want anything. That's the whole of why you can carry it at all. Nobody home, and you're the better hole."

Behind him the ash in the ditch went on going into the ditch.

He did not look at it.

When the track was done he took his palm off the stone, and where he had been there was a patch of it soft-edged all the way round like a puddle drying, and what was still lying in it had gone colorless, and the wind that had not been able to shift any of it an hour ago came up the cut and took that.

He came up with the hand low and took the first jar and tipped its mouth against his palm and held it there, and it went down out of him into the glass and was grey again by the time it lay in the bottom.

He put a finger into it and took it out and looked at what was on the finger, and rubbed it off between finger and thumb, slowly, and wiped the hand on his coat.

"Hand before lamp," he said. "Old saying and a true one, and nobody standing at a scale has ever once believed it." He turned the jar a quarter and did not hold it up to anything. "Clean. That's clean, and that's a fair Flask, and I'll say so in front of whoever's weighing."

He worked the stopper in with the heel of his palm, and stopped with his hand still on it.

"Thinner, though."

He said it to the jar.

"That's thinner than it ought to come off a thing that size, and it was thinner at the weighbridge, and I said nothing then either." He set it down against the sack. "Or I'm old and my hand's going. I'd sooner it was me."

The ditch had had most of the rest. What was left lay along the black shallow in a smear with the light still crawling through it, and the smear was short now, and going.

He went down the bank into the water anyway and put his knee in it and his hand on the bottom.

It dragged.

"There it is," he said. "That's silt. That's four feet of ditch bottom coming up with it, and it'll weigh dirty, and whoever's on the scale will write it dirty and be right to." He moved the hand two inches over and it dragged again. "Come on. Come *on*."

He had his head turned toward the bank when the last of it went, because something had moved up there or had not, and he was a moment late with it, and what he had not got yet went out from under his palm and down into the mud.

"No," he said.

He looked at the place where it had gone in.

"Thirty years, and you turned your head."

He came up the bank and put what he had into the second jar, and it was not much, and he stoppered it and set it beside the first. Then he sat down on the dry stone with his forearms across his knees and his hands hanging off the ends of them.

They were not steady. He did not put them away. There was nobody down there to put them away from.

Up on the road Wyck laughed at something, once, and stopped.

After a while Rook got up and packed the jars into the sack with a fold of canvas between them, one and then the other.

"Flask and a fair one," he said, to nobody at all. "And a dirty Handful, less what the ditch got."

He went up.
