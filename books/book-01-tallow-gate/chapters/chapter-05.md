# Chapter 5 — The Waypoint

Tallow Gate had been a fuel depot once.

You could see the bones of it from a mile out on the loop road: four enormous rust-red cylinders standing in a row inside a ring of earth berm, and a fifth one lying on its side across the only break in the berm, cut open at both ends, so that the way in and out of the settlement was a tunnel through a fallen tank about eighty feet long. Somebody had hung two doors in it. Somebody else had spent a great deal of effort painting a number on the outside of the tank and then given up halfway through the second digit.

Toren had never seen a wall before. Coldwater had a fence.

"Don't stare," said Rook.

"I'm not staring."

"You're all staring. Every one of you, you look like a delegation of owls." He did not slow down. "It's a hole in the ground with a lid on it. Straighten up. Walk like people who've been somewhere."

They straightened up. It made a difference that Toren found faintly insulting.

It had taken them two and a half days, not two, because Senna Voss on a litter went at the speed she went and no faster, and because on the second afternoon there had been a thing in the culvert under the loop road that Rook had gone down and dealt with alone, and had come back up from with ash in his hair and a set to his mouth, and had said nothing about.

Milo had asked. Of course. "How many?"

"One," Rook had said. "One is plenty. One is how everybody dies. Nobody ever gets killed by a hundred of them; they get killed by one they didn't count."

Milo had turned that over for about a mile.

"Is that a lesson?"

"It's a fact," Rook said. "Lessons are the ones you have to pay for."

---

Warden Ottoline Braid was a small square woman of about sixty with reading glasses pushed up into a great deal of grey hair, and she received them in an office built into the curve of a standing tank, at a desk with three ledgers open on it, and she was, Toren decided within about ninety seconds, the most reasonable person he had ever met.

That turned out to be the problem.

She wrote it all down. That was the first thing she did, before she offered them anything, before she asked Rook a single question about himself: she took a fresh page and she wrote down Coldwater and the date and the milepost, and she wrote down Odo Prentice, guide, twenty-two crossings, and then Nell Tanner and Ilsa Cole and Garrick Voss, in that order, with their ages where anybody knew them.

She asked Dessa how to spell Cole. Dessa spelled it.

"That's in the ledger now," Braid said, "and the ledger goes to Meridian twice a year and it doesn't get lost. Whatever else happens today, that's done and it's permanent. I want you to have that."

Dessa nodded and could not speak.

"Right." Braid put her glasses down. "Now the unpleasant half."

---

"I can't take the old woman."

"She's got a splinted leg and eight days of road left in her," Rook said.

"She's got twenty years of reactor sickness and a leg, and I'd take the leg." The warden said it without any cruelty at all, which made it much harder to argue with. "I've got eleven beds and I've got the loop road, and the loop road gives me road-injured all year and about forty of them a season, and my charter says I hold a bed for the next one. A chronic case takes a bed and doesn't give it back. If I put her in one, I'm gambling somebody I haven't met yet against somebody I have, and I'm not allowed to do that. It's not that I don't want to. It's that I signed something."

"Then read me the part where it says you can't."

"I'll get it out for you if you like," said Braid, "but you already know I can, or you wouldn't have asked me that way."

Rook was quiet for a moment.

"The four," he said. "Take the four."

"The four I can do." Braid pulled the second ledger toward her. "Bond intake. Seven years, board and keep, and at the end of it they're free with a trade and a letter. That's the standard and I don't negotiate it because the moment I negotiate it once I'll be doing it for everybody." She began ruling a line down the page. "Two Kindled with Embers go on the wall rotation — that's not punishment, that's where the value is, and it's where I can actually keep an eye on them. The rift-touched girl goes to the infirmary; if she can close a cut she's worth more there than anywhere else in this station. The boy with the light —"

"Milo," said Milo.

"— goes to the kitchen and the stores, and don't make a face at me, young man, I started in a kitchen." She kept ruling the line. "They'd be split across three rosters. They'd see each other at meals. I'm telling you that now rather than letting you find it out in a month, because I've had that conversation before and it goes better this way."

"Seven years," said Wyck.

"Seven years, and you'd be alive at the end of them," said Warden Braid, "which is the honest comparison, and I'd rather make the honest one." She looked up. "Understand me. I'm not doing you a kindness. I'm buying seven years of labor off four orphans at a price I set, and I sleep at night because the alternative on this road is worse and because I keep my end. But I'm not going to stand here and pretend it's charity, and if anybody wants to tell me I'm a hard woman I'll agree with them and we can get on."

She turned the ledger around and pushed it across the desk, and set the pen on top of it, and looked at Rook.

"You've got a claim on them or you haven't," she said. "If you have, sign, and I'll take them today. If you haven't, they're vagrant minors on my road and I'll take them anyway in about four days when the food runs out, and it'll be exactly the same page and exactly the same seven years, except that you'll have made them walk to it."

The office was very quiet.

Toren looked at the ledger. He looked at his grandmother, propped in a chair by the door with her splinted leg out and her hands folded, watching all of this with an expression he could not read at all. He looked at Dessa's face, and at Milo's, and at Wyck's, and Wyck was staring at the desk with his jaw set and something in his eyes that was not fear.

He looked at Rook.

Rook had picked up the pen. He was holding it the way you hold a tool you have used ten thousand times, loosely, between two fingers, and he was looking at the ruled line, and his hand was perfectly steady, and he did not move.

Ten seconds. Toren counted them.

"No," Rook said.

He put the pen down on the desk, squarely, alongside the ledger.

"That's a decision," said Braid.

"It is."

"You want to think it over? I'll leave the page open till the morning."

"Leave it open," Rook said. "I'm not signing it in the morning either."

---

Outside, in the yard between the second and third tanks, with the light going and the whole station smelling of woodsmoke and rendered fat, Rook stood with his back to the four of them for a long time.

Then he said: "Get in a line."

Nobody moved.

"You heard the woman," he said. "Four days, and then she takes you, and she'll be right to. Four days is what I've bought you and it cost me the only clean way out of this that I had, so you're going to spend all four of them doing what I say. Get in a line."

They got in a line.

"Tanner. Ember out."

Wyck lit it. Three feet of white, clean, steady, and he held it, and Toren watched him hold it — and Wyck got past forty and kept going, jaw locked, and got to somewhere around fifty-five before the light guttered and he shook his hand out.

"That's good," said Rook.

Wyck's head came up.

"I mean it. That's two years of somebody making himself do a boring thing every day when nobody was watching, and that's most of what this is. Don't get proud about it. Get proud about it later." Rook was already moving down the line. "Cole. Thirty feet?"

"About."

"Through a wall?"

Dessa blinked. "I've — never tried it through a wall."

"Try it through a wall tonight and tell me tomorrow. Faust."

Milo held up his hand and made the light. It sat in his palm, pale, about the size of a plum, entirely useless.

Rook looked at it for a while.

"How long?"

"Uh — as long as I want, really. It's not hard. It's just—"

"How long."

"An hour? More. I fell asleep with it on once."

"Huh," said Rook, and did not say anything else, and moved on, and Milo stood there with his mouth slightly open, holding a light nobody had ever said *huh* about before.

"Voss."

Toren got the Ember out and lit it, and it lit, and it stayed a rod.

"Edge."

"I can't always—"

"I didn't ask what you can always. Edge."

Toren thought about a blade with everything he had, and got nothing, and felt his face going hot, and stopped thinking about a blade and let the thing roll loose in his fingers and thought *please*, and got four inches of white light for about two seconds.

"There," he said, breathing hard, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

"Again."

He did it again. Three inches. Then nothing, four times running. Then five inches, twice, and then nothing again for so long that his hands started to cramp.

"It only does it sometimes," he said at last.

"Does it."

"It — yes. It does it when it wants to. On the road, it did the same thing. It wouldn't do anything when I needed it, and then it just —" Toren heard himself and pushed on anyway, because it was the only explanation that fit and he had been building it for three days. "It decided. That's what it feels like. Like it's got its own opinion and sometimes you're lucky."

He waited for Rook to correct him.

He was certain, standing there in the yard with a dead rod in his hand, that this was the moment where the man was going to tell him the real thing — that there was a trick to it, a grip, a word, a way of holding your breath, the actual answer that every Ember-user knew and that nobody had gotten around to telling Toren Voss because his father had died fourteen hours after handing him the thing.

Rook looked at him for a long moment in the failing light.

"Fine," he said. "Again."

---

They drilled until it was full dark and then they drilled in the dark.

Not fighting. There was almost nothing in it that Toren would have called fighting. Rook walked them up and down the yard between the tanks in a loose diamond — Wyck at the front, Dessa at the left, Toren at the right, Milo in the middle where the light was — and made them stop, and turn, and change which way the diamond faced, over and over, until Milo was staggering.

"Again."

"We've done it eleven times—"

"Then it'll be easy." Rook stood in the middle of the yard with his arms folded. "You four walked down a road three days ago in a clump with a cart in the middle and a man out front who was thinking about fords. When it started, every one of you ran a different direction and two of you ran uphill into open grass, and one of you lit up a beacon on a bare slope with nothing behind him but sky." He let that sit exactly long enough. "Nobody's blaming anybody. I'm telling you what I saw so you know what we're fixing. Again."

They went again.

At some point past midnight, with the four of them sitting against the curve of a cold tank and Milo actually asleep, Toren said, "Why do they come at the light?"

Rook was on the other side of the yard, doing something to his boot.

"They come at worked power," he said. "Ember, magic, doesn't matter which. Anything anybody's *doing* on purpose. So the rule is: don't light unless you mean it, and if you light, be standing somewhere you chose to be standing."

"Why?"

"Because that's the rule."

"No — why do they come at it?"

Rook finished with his boot and put it back on.

"Go to sleep," he said.

Toren lay awake for a long time with his back against seventy-year-old steel and the disk warm in his coat pocket, and thought about a pen set down squarely alongside a ledger, and about a piece of bread on a rock in the rain, and about the fact that nobody in the yard that night had said out loud what they were drilling *for*, and that all five of them knew, and that not one of them was going to be the one to say it.
