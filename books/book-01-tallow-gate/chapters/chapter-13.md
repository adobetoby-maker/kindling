# Chapter 13 — The Day Before

"You're not getting hit today," Rook said.

Toren had his coat off and his sleeves pushed up and had spent the walk down off the bank preparing himself, in the private way you prepare for a thing you have already survived eleven times, and he stood in the wet grass with all of that preparation going nowhere.

"Why not?"

"Because hitting you worked and it's finished working." Rook set the ash shaft down against the bank, where Toren could see it, which was not an accident. "Light up."

They were four days north-west of the birch country and the grade had come down out of the hills into flat ground with standing water in the fields on both sides and a wind off it that did not stop at any point in the day. It was the twenty-eighth morning since Tallow Gate. Toren's eyebrow had gone from red to white and had settled into a thin seam that Milo said made him look like a person who had been in a fight with a person, which was, Milo maintained, better than the truth. His right hand worked. It did not close all the way and the two middle fingers ached when the weather turned, and he had stopped noticing either of those things somewhere in the last week.

He lit up.

The room was there. It was always there now, which was the strange part — the thing that had taken eleven hits and a split eyebrow to find was, at eight in the morning in an empty field with nothing happening, no trouble at all. He went to the back of the house and stood in it and the Ember put out light, and the light came up and kept coming and steadied out at about the length of his forearm.

"Twenty inches," Rook said.

"Is that—"

"Hold it. Don't talk."

He held it. The wind came across the field and he stood in it with his arm out and a length of white light burning steadily out of his fist, and nothing in him strained at all. He had time, standing there, to think about how little it cost, and how much that frightened him.

"Now walk to the water and back."

Toren walked to the water and the light went out at the third step.

---

"Again."

He got four steps.

"Again."

He got two, which was worse than the first time, and he said so, and Rook said *again* without any change in his voice whatsoever and Toren went again.

By the middle of the morning, he had worked out what was happening and he had worked out a way around it, and he was very pleased with himself for about an hour.

What was happening was this: when he walked, he had to look at the ground. When he looked at the ground he came out of the room to look, the same way he had come out of the room to look at a stick swinging toward his head, and the light went with him. So he stopped looking at the ground. He picked a line of flat grass with nothing in it, and he put his eyes on a fence post two hundred paces off, and he shut the whole of the rest of the field out of his head, and he walked.

He got to the water with twenty inches of Edge still burning.

"Again," said Rook.

He did it again and it worked. He did it a third time with Milo walking beside him talking without pause about a bird he had seen, and it worked, and Milo's entire commentary went past him like weather.

"That's it," Toren said. He was breathing hard, not from effort — from something else. "That's the thing. You stop letting anything in. You just—"

"Cole," Rook said. "Give him a distance."

Dessa, on the tailboard of the barrow with her boots off, did not open her eyes. "Eighty feet, off the left of the grade, coming up on the flat."

The light went out before Toren's feet had stopped.

He stood in the grass with a dead rod in his hand and his heart going and his head whipping round to the left of the grade, where there was nothing, where there had obviously been nothing, where Dessa Cole was sitting on a tailboard with her eyes shut and had said a true thing about an empty field because a man had asked her to.

"There's nothing there," he said.

"No."

"Then why did you—"

"Because you've spent the morning teaching yourself to be deaf," said Rook, "and I'd rather you found out now than at eighty feet."

---

They stopped at noon and ate standing up.

Toren could not sit down. He walked the length of the barrow twice and came back and stood at the end of it with his bread in his hand and did not eat any of it, and Senna Voss watched him do all of that from inside the cart and said nothing at all, which from her was a considerable act of restraint.

"I don't know what you want," he said at last.

"Yes you do."

"I *don't*. You told me to get in the room and I got in the room. You told me to hold it and I held it. You told me to hold it walking and I found a way to hold it walking and then you took it off me for finding a way." He heard his own voice going up and did not stop it. "What's the version where I don't do it wrong?"

Rook finished his bread and wiped his hands on his coat.

"You're not trying to stop noticing," he said. "You're trying to notice from inside."

"That's not a—"

"It isn't an instruction. I know." Rook picked up the near shaft of the barrow and looked down the grade north-west, at the flat, at the line of it going away. "There isn't an instruction. There's a thing that's true and there's you being wrong about it in a slightly different place every day until you aren't. This morning you were wrong about the ground. Now you're wrong about the girl. That's a whole step and it took you four hours and I'd have given it a week."

Toren stood there with the bread in his hand.

"Is that good," he said.

"That's good," said Rook, and put the shafts on his shoulders.

---

Dessa told him about her hands that afternoon, walking, in the tone of somebody mentioning that a strap needed replacing.

"I closed Toren's eyebrow in three minutes on the ballast."

"I know. I watched you."

"I didn't lie down after." She had her hands in her pockets and her eyes on the middle distance and her voice was absolutely level, which Toren, twelve feet ahead on the near shaft, had learned to read as the sound of Dessa Cole being frightened of something. "In Coldwater I did a boy who went through a window and it took me an afternoon and then I was in the dark for four hours and my mother made everyone be quiet. On the ballast it took three minutes and I got up and went back to work and I've done two more since and I don't go down anymore. At all."

"When did you stop?"

"I don't know. That's what I'm telling you. There wasn't a day." She kicked a stone off the crown of the grade. "I noticed on the ballast and I didn't say anything, and then I noticed again about ten minutes later and I still didn't say anything, and that was eight days ago."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

Dessa was quiet for about twenty paces.

"Because if I said it out loud and I was wrong," she said, "then it would have been a thing I wanted."

Rook walked on for a while with the barrow creaking and Senna's blanket shifting in it.

"That's the same as the rest of it," he said. "You stopped throwing it. You've been filling instead of throwing for about a fortnight and you can't feel it happening because nobody can. It'll keep going."

"How far?"

"Don't ask that."

"You say that to him," said Dessa, "and he keeps asking."

"He does," Rook agreed, "and I keep telling him don't, and neither of us has stopped, and that's probably how it should go."

---

Toren went back to it at four in the afternoon, and the fourth hour was the one.

He did not get there by trying. That was the thing he would think about later. He had tried all morning and most of the afternoon, and had thrown himself at the problem from every angle he had, and at some point around the middle of the day he had simply run out of ways to be clever and had started walking the line with twenty inches of light and no plan at all.

And then Wyck said, from six feet behind him, "Your right foot's turned out."

Toren said, "What?"

"Your right foot. Every fourth or fifth step you turn the toe out and it drops your hip and then you've got to catch yourself." Wyck came up alongside at his own speed, the stick going down and taking weight and coming up. "You've done it since the pumphouse. It's the slope. You learned to walk on a road with a camber on it."

And Toren turned his head and looked at him, and answered him, and thought about his own foot, and looked down at it, and turned it in — and the light did not go out.

He got another eighty paces before he noticed.

When he did notice, it went out at once, and he stopped dead in the middle of the grade and said a word that his grandmother would have had opinions about.

"How long," he said. "How long was that."

"You don't want to know," said Wyck.

"How long?"

"About a minute and a half." Wyck leaned on the stick. "You talked to me and looked at your own boot and went on up the grade like a man going to fetch a bucket. I've never seen you do that. I've never seen *anybody* do that except him."

---

Rook came down the grade and stood in front of him and put his hand out flat, and Toren lit up before he was told to, and got two feet of it, clean, and held it.

"Walk."

He walked. It held.

"Look at me."

He looked. It held.

"Tell me what's in the field on your left."

"Water. Two birds in it. A post with — there's a post with wire on it." He heard himself saying it and heard the light still burning and something in his chest went up like a bird out of grass. "It's still on. It's still—"

Rook moved his right hand.

It was not a strike. It was six inches of motion, a man shifting his fingers at his side, and Toren's whole body went to it, and the room emptied, and twenty-four inches of white light stopped existing.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Right," Rook said. "That's the shape of it. Sit down."

---

They sat on the bank with the wind coming off the flooded fields and the light going long.

"There's a word for where you were between about four in the afternoon and a minute ago," Rook said. "It's Flame. You know it. Your grandmother sang half of it at a fire a month ago and Tanner's had it since before the rain."

Toren's throat had gone tight.

"Am I—"

"No." Rook said it without any weight at all. "Flame is when it holds while something's happening. What you did this afternoon is it holds while you're *busy*, and busy isn't happening, and the difference between those two things is a boy's leg, and you know that better than anyone on this grade."

"Then what am I?"

"You're the day before Flame," said Rook. "And you can be the day before Flame for a year, and men have. That's not me being hard on you. That's the actual size of the step and I'd be lying to you about it if I said a number."

"How long?" Toren said.

"Don't ask that."

"How long?"

Rook looked out across the water.

"Sooner than I'd have said a fortnight ago," he said.

It was not much. Toren carried it up the grade to the camp and it was still there when he lay down, and it was more than he had been given in a month.

---

Milo's trouble came at dusk, and it came the way Milo's things came, which was pleasantly and by accident.

He had got his light an inch off his palm on the ballast and had been working at it every evening since, and what he had arrived at, that evening, in the grass above the camp, was setting it down.

He put it on a flat stone at the top of the bank and took his hand away and it stayed.

"Look at that," he said. "Look at — Dessa. *Dessa.* Look at it."

It sat on the stone about the size of a plum, pale and steady and entirely unsupported, and it did not gutter and it did not drift, and Milo Faust walked in a circle around it with both hands in his hair.

"How long will it stay?" Toren said.

"I don't know! I've never — it's just there, it doesn't need me, I could go and—"

"Faust," said Rook.

He had come up out of the camp without any of them hearing him and he was standing eight feet off with his hands in his pockets, and he was looking at the stone.

"That's good," he said. "That's very good and I want you to hear me say it, because in about four seconds I'm going to tell you to put it out and you're going to think the two things cancel."

Milo's face fell about a foot. "Why?"

"What comes at worked power?"

"Everything, but—"

"Everything. And a light you're holding is a light you can drop, and a light sitting on a stone at the top of a camp is a fire on a hill with nobody's hand on it." Rook crouched down by the stone and looked at it from a few inches away, for longer than he needed to. "You've made a thing nobody I've ever met can make and the first true thing about it is that it's dangerous. Both of those are permanent. Put it out."

Milo put it out.

"Tomorrow," Rook said, standing up, "you're going to put it on a stone two hundred paces down the grade in the dark and come back and sit with us, and Cole's going to hold, and we're going to find out what walks up to it."

Milo stared at him.

"That's *horrible*," he said, with enormous admiration.

---

Toren woke once in the night and the fire was down and there was nobody at it.

Rook was up on the crown of the grade, forty feet off, standing with his hands in his pockets and his back to the camp, looking north-west.

There was nothing up there. Toren raised himself on one elbow and looked along the line of it, the way he had learned to look at country in the last month, and there was flat ground and standing water and a sky going grey at the bottom edge, and the grade running away straight into all of it, and nothing at all to see.

The man stood there for a long time and did not move and did not go any further, and after a while Toren lay back down.
