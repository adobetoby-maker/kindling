# Chapter 6 — What He Saw

On the third morning Warden Braid put a mug of something hot into Rook's hand and mentioned, in the manner of a woman mentioning the weather, that she had lost two people at the quarry pumphouse since the spring.

"Water's fine. The water's the only reason this station exists. But there's something living in the pump shed and it's cost me a fitter and a boy, and I can spare four men to go and look at it, and if I send four men I get four men back or I don't, and either way I've got no fitter." She blew across her own mug. "It's half a mile. You could see it from the berm if the fog lifted."

"You're not asking me."

"I'm certainly not asking you," said Braid. "The page is still open on my desk. I mention the pumphouse the way I'd mention there's a hole in the road."

Rook drank his drink and looked out at the fog and did not answer her, and an hour later he told the four of them to eat properly and get their boots on.

---

The quarry was a grey oval bitten out of the hillside with sixty feet of flat green water standing in the bottom of it, and the pumphouse sat on the lip: a squat concrete box the size of a barn, roof half fallen in, with a sluice channel running out of its lower wall and away down the slope in a straight cut lined with stone.

"Diamond," Rook said. "In through the upper door, sweep left, out through the lower. Voss at point."

Toren's stomach dropped about a foot.

"Point," he said.

"Point."

"Why him?"

It came out of Wyck flat and fast, and everybody heard exactly how long it had been sitting behind his teeth.

Rook turned around.

"Say the rest of it," he said.

"There's nothing to—" Wyck stopped. He was fifteen, and he had been up before all of them every morning for three days doing the boring thing where nobody was watching, and Toren could see him deciding, in real time, that he was going to say it. "I've had an Ember two years. I can hold a full Edge for the better part of a minute. He can hold four inches when it feels like being nice to him. If you want somebody at point who can actually *cut* something, that's me, and you know it's me, because you said so yourself on the first night."

"I did."

"Then—"

"You're on left," Rook said. "Cole, right. Faust, centre, and don't light until somebody tells you. Voss, point."

Wyck stood there with his jaw working.

"Is there a reason," he said, "or is that just how it is."

"That's just how it is," said Rook.

He said it perfectly evenly and he did not offer a single word more, and Toren stood at the front of the diamond with his father's Ember in his fist and his face burning, because he did not have the reason either, and would have given a great deal for it, and knew exactly how it looked.

---

They went in at a little before ten in the morning.

The upper door was a rectangle of dark in a wall streaked with seventy years of rust-run. Inside, the floor went down in three broad steps toward the sluice wall, and the bottom step and everything past it was under water — shin-deep at the near edge, knee-deep at the far, still and green-black and absolutely opaque. Two rows of pump housings stood up out of it like the stumps of teeth. Overhead, the collapsed section of roof let in a hard white slab of daylight that fell across the middle of the room and made everything outside it worse.

The far wall had the lower door in it, and beside the lower door the sluice channel came out through a gap the height of a man, with a rusted catwalk running over the top of it.

There was a click in the dark on the left.

Then, unhurried, three more, from three different places.

"Seven," said Dessa. Her voice was very tight and very controlled. "Maybe eight. They're spread all around the low end."

"Spread how?"

"Even. Like they've been waiting."

"They have," Rook said, from somewhere behind Toren's right shoulder, and did not move up.

Toren looked back once.

The man was standing three steps up in the doorway with his arms folded and his Ember still in his coat, and the look on his face was not encouragement and it was not indifference either. It was attention. It was the flat total attention of somebody watching a thing that has already started and cannot now be stopped, and Toren understood, with a cold lurch, that they were going to do this themselves.

"Down the steps," Rook said. "Diamond. Go."

---

Toren had thought about the water. He had not thought about it hard enough.

He went down the three broad steps and into it and the water took his legs the way deep sand does, and every stride became a thing he had to plan, and the noise of it — the great sloshing racket four people make wading — filled the room and made hearing anything else impossible.

The first one came out of the dark on the left at the height of the second pump housing.

It did not wade. That was the entire lesson and it arrived in about a second and a half. It came across the flooded floor in that long unhurried fold-and-unfold, all its extra joints working, and the water barely marked its passage, and it crossed twenty feet while Toren was crossing six.

Wyck lit up and took it across the throat-joint before it reached the diamond at all, and it went to ash, and warm grey came down across the black water and floated.

"*Left*," Dessa said. "Two more left — Wyck, two—"

"I see them."

Toren got his own Ember lit and it stayed a rod, and he thought about a blade so hard his ears rang, and it stayed a rod, and something came at his right side out from behind a pump housing.

He did the loose thing.

He had been doing the loose thing for three days on Rook's orders, over and over in the yard, poorly. He rolled the shaft in his fingers, and stopped picturing anything at all, and thought *please* — and got a foot of white light, and it stayed.

It stayed. He put it through the thing's foreleg fold and the creature came apart and the light was *still there*, burning steady out of the end of the shaft, twelve inches of it, and he had time to see it and to feel the enormous stupid joy of it go through him from his heels up.

Eight seconds. Longer than he'd ever held it before.

Then it guttered and went out and he nearly fell over.

"*Toren!*"

Dessa. Right side of the diamond, and the right side of the diamond had gone, because Toren had walked forward out of it in the eight seconds he had spent being delighted with himself, and Dessa was eleven feet behind him and to the right with two of them between her and the group, backed up against a pump housing in water to the knee with no Ember and nothing in her hands but a bill-hook.

"Hold *on*—"

He could not get to her. He worked that out instantly. Eleven feet of knee-deep water was four seconds and four seconds was too many, and he had one thing and one thing only, and he had known what it was since the road.

*They come at the light. The biggest light.*

He had watched it happen. He had watched an entire hillside of them turn as one toward three feet of white in Wyck Tanner's fist and go straight past Milo Faust standing in the open with a palm-light. He had watched every living thing on that roadbed swing toward a man in a grey coat the instant he lit up seven feet of spear. Twice. He had seen it twice with his own eyes and it was the only piece of the world he was certain of.

So Toren Voss planted his feet in the water in the middle of the white slab of daylight, and did the loose thing, and gave it everything he had.

The Ember put out light.

Not four inches. Not a foot. Two feet of it, hot white, brighter than anything in the room, and it hurt his hand and his forearm cramped instantly and he held it up over his head like a man holding a lantern in a storm and he *roared* at them.

And every head in the pumphouse came round.

He saw it. He saw it happen exactly the way he had known it would — six or seven long grey heads turning toward him across the black water, unhurried and simultaneous, the seams down their chests all brightening at once as if a wind had gone through a row of coals.

For about two seconds nothing in that room moved except him.

It was the two seconds Dessa needed. She got off the pump housing and behind it and around, and the pair that had been closing on her lost the line and had to come around the housing the long way, and by then Wyck had pushed right and Milo had shoved himself in front of her, and the right side of the diamond stitched itself back together out of nothing.

It worked. It worked, and Toren felt it work, and in the wash of it he did not notice that not one single Riftspawn in that building had actually come toward him.

They had all looked.

Then every one of them had gone for whatever was closest.

---

Wyck was closest to the sluice gap.

He was on the left, where Rook had put him, and he had pushed right to cover Dessa the way Toren's plan had needed somebody to, and that had put him with his back to the wall between the lower door and the sluice channel, in a corner about four feet wide, with a stone-lined drop at his heels and nowhere at all to give ground. There were two of them in front of him and one of them had come out of the sluice gap behind, and Wyck's Edge was lit, three clean feet of it, and Wyck Tanner was the nearest worked light in that corner of the room by a distance of about six feet.

Toren was thirty feet away with a burning arm and a lantern held over his head.

He got there in nine seconds. He would take those nine seconds apart, over and over, for a very long time.

By the time he got there, Rook had crossed the whole room.

Toren did not see him come off the steps. He saw the end of it: a hard blue-white frame burning around a man's legs, and a spear-point at half length coming down the wall line, and the thing at the sluice gap going to ash and the drift of it blowing out through the gap and down the channel; and then the two in front of Wyck, one and then the other, in less time than it takes to describe them.

And then Wyck was sitting down in the water with his back to the wall and his Ember gone dark in his hand and his left leg out in front of him at a bad angle, and there was a great deal of red in the black water and it was moving away from him in a slow spreading fan.

---

Dessa got both hands on it and got nothing.

"I can't — it's too big, I can only do—" She was working. Toren could see her working, her whole face straining with it, and nothing was happening. "It's too *big*, I can close a cut, this isn't a cut—"

"Move your hands." Rook was already down in the water beside her. "Faust. Light. Right here, right where I'm looking, and hold it."

Milo put his hand out and made the little useless plum of pale light and held it eight inches over Wyck's leg, and it did not waver at all, and he held it there without moving for the next eleven minutes.

Rook took the Ember out of his coat, rolled it once against his palm, and laid it flat along the length of the wound with both hands over it.

He did not put a hand in any ash. There was ash on the water all around them, a slick grey film of it going soft and coming apart, and he did not touch it. Toren — kneeling in the water with his own arm still cramping and his heart going like a hammer — noticed that and filed it, the way he had filed *he doesn't have the Ember* on the road, in the place where things go that you cannot use yet.

At the end of it Wyck's leg had stopped bleeding and was closed along most of its length and was still, unmistakably, a leg that had been opened from above the knee to the hip.

"That's the ends together," Rook said. His hands had started shaking about four minutes in and had not stopped. "The rest is you. Six weeks. You'll walk on it in three if you're an idiot and in five if you're not, and it will hurt every single day, and there is nothing else and nobody has anything else. Do you understand me?"

Wyck's face was grey. "Yeah."

"Say it back."

"Six weeks. Nothing else. Nobody has anything else."

"Good."

---

They did the debrief in the lower doorway, because Rook would not do it inside and would not do it walking.

He sat Wyck against the frame with the leg out straight, and put the other three in a row where Wyck could see all of them, and he said:

"Voss. Tell me what you did."

"I made myself the biggest light in the room," Toren said. His voice came out wrong. "To pull them off Dessa."

"Did it pull them off Dessa?"

"...It bought her two seconds."

"It did. That's real and I'm not taking it off you." Rook's face did not change. "Now tell me why it only bought two."

Toren opened his mouth and had absolutely nothing in it.

"They looked," Rook said. "Every one of them looked at you. You saw them look and you thought that meant they were coming, because on the road you saw them look and then you saw them come, and you drew a line between the two. That's a fair line. I'd have drawn it at thirteen." He leaned forward slightly. "They don't come at the biggest. They come at the *nearest*. Looking is free. Moving isn't. On the road, Tanner's blade was the nearest worked thing on that hillside, and I was the nearest worked thing on that road because I came straight up the middle of them. You got both of those right and you got the reason wrong, and the reason is the only part that transfers."

Nobody said anything.

"So." Rook sat back. "You lit up the middle of a room and gave every one of them a free look, and while they were looking, each one of them went for whoever was standing six feet from it. Cole was covered by then. Faust was behind Cole. You were thirty feet from anything. And Tanner was in a four-foot corner with a drop behind him and a lit Ember in his hand, and he was there because your plan needed somebody to be, and nobody went to him, because you'd sent yourself to the middle of the room instead."

Toren could not look at anyone.

"That's the lesson and it cost a leg," Rook said. "That's the price and it's already paid, so use it. Nearest, not biggest. Say it."

"Nearest, not biggest."

"All four of you."

They all said it.

"Why didn't you *tell* us?" Milo said. It was not a challenge. It was almost a plea. "You knew. You could have just said it on the first night—"

"I could have."

"Then—"

"Because you'd have known it," Rook said, "and knowing it is worth nothing." He got up, slowly, in the way of a man whose hands are still shaking. "You'll never make that mistake again as long as you live. Every one of you just got that for the rest of your lives and none of you paid for it except him." He looked down at Wyck. "That's the arithmetic and it's ugly and I'd do it again, and you're allowed to hate me for it."

"I don't hate you," said Wyck.

He was looking at Toren.

He said it in an absolutely reasonable voice, from the ground, with his leg out straight in front of him, and he did not raise it at all.

"You made a call," he said. "Nobody gave you the call. He put you at point and you took it like it was yours and you made a call and it went through my leg. And tomorrow he's going to put you at point again, isn't he. And you know what the worst part is? You're going to let him."

Toren said, "Wyck—"

"Don't," said Wyck Tanner, and shut his eyes, and that was the end of that for the day and not remotely the end of it.

---

Toren noticed the disk was gone at the top of the slope.

He put his hand in his pocket the way he had put his hand in that pocket four hundred times in six days, and the pocket had a corner torn out of it, and there was nothing in it, and the world tipped.

He was back down the hill and into the water before anyone could say his name.

He found the pocket-corner. He did not find the disk. He was on his hands and knees in the black water going along the base of the sluice wall with both arms in to the shoulder, sobbing in a hard ugly way he could not stop and had no attention to spare for, and he had been at it for some minutes before he registered that there was somebody in the water beside him.

Rook came up out of the sluice channel with it in his hand.

He was crouched at the head of the cut with the water running past his wrists and the disk flat on his open palm, and he did not hand it over.

Toren stopped.

He was afterward never able to say what it was. Rook's face did not do anything. That was the point; that was the whole thing; the man knelt there in a foot of moving water with a small dark object on his hand and his expression did not alter by any amount that Toren could have described to another person — and every single thing about him was different. His shoulders. The set of his neck. The way he was breathing, which he had stopped doing.

He turned it over once with his thumb, and the nine channels came around into the light, and the light went down them.

His hands began to shake.

They had been steady all the way through the pumphouse until four minutes into Wyck's leg. They had been steady coming up the hill. They started now, holding a warm dark disk in a quarry sluice with nothing in front of him at all, and they got worse for about five seconds and then Rook noticed Toren watching and put a stop to it in the way you stop a shiver, by force.

"Where did you get this," he said.

"My father."

"And him?"

"His father." Toren's own voice sounded a long way off. "Five generations. It's a Voss thing. There's a story that goes with it."

"I'll bet there is," said Rook.

He held it a moment longer.

Then he reached out and put it into Toren's hands and closed both of the boy's fists over it, one finger at a time, with a care that Toren had felt exactly once before in his life, four nights ago, in wet grass under a broken overpass.

"Sew your pocket," Rook said. "Tonight. Two rows of stitches, not one. And don't show that to another living soul on this road, or at Tallow Gate, or anywhere else, and don't tell anyone you have it, and if somebody's already seen it you tell me who."

"Why?"

"Sew your pocket."

"You're not going to tell me anything, are you," Toren said. "Not what it is, not why you looked at it like that, not why you put me at point when Wyck's better than me. Not one thing."

Rook stood up out of the water.

He looked down the sluice channel toward the quarry and the flat green water and the fog that had never once lifted all day, and he was quiet for long enough that Toren thought that was going to be the whole answer.

"We're not going back to that ledger," Rook said at last. "Tell the others. We leave at first light and we're not going east, and your grandmother's coming, and everything gets harder starting tomorrow — the drilling, the hours, all of it. You'll wish it was today by the end of the week."

"Because of the disk."

Rook did not answer that.

He went up the slope in the fog with the water running off his coat, and Toren Voss stood in the sluice with both fists closed around a thing that felt like a room he had lived in, and understood that he had just been given the answer to a question and no part at all of what it meant.
