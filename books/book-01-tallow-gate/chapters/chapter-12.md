# Chapter 12 — Point

Wyck Tanner walked up to the head of the cart at first light with his stick in his hand and his coat already on, and stood there until Rook looked at him.

"I'm walking today."

"You've been walking half of every day since the rain."

"I'm walking *all* of it." He had the leg braced and his weight mostly off it and he was standing anyway, which was the argument. "Ten days. It's closed. It's stiff and it's ugly and it hurts and it's going to hurt in five weeks too. I can hold a full Edge for five minutes on it sitting down and I can do three standing, and I did three standing on that ballast while you were hitting me with a cart-pole."

Rook looked at him for a while.

"All right," he said.

"And I'm at point."

The yard went quiet in the way a yard does when four people who were doing things have all stopped doing them.

Rook finished what he was doing with the strap. Then he straightened up, and said, without any particular weight on it:

"Voss at point."

---

It was the worst possible version, Toren thought afterward — not the refusal, but the flatness. If the man had hesitated for one second it would have been survivable. If he had said *not yet* it would have been something a person could work with.

"Give me a reason," Wyck said.

"I gave you one at the pumphouse."

"You gave me *that's just how it is*, which isn't one, and I've thought about nothing else for ten days." Wyck did not raise his voice. He had not raised his voice about any of it, not once, which was somehow the most alarming thing about him. "I'll take a bad reason. I'd honestly rather have a bad one. Tell me it's because I lit up on a hillside and got my aunt killed, and I'll take it, and I'll stand where you put me for the rest of my life."

Senna Voss, in the cart, said nothing at all and did not pretend to be asleep.

"It isn't that," said Rook.

"Then what."

"Because you'd be good at it."

Wyck stood there.

"That's a reason to put me there," he said.

"It's a reason to put him there," said Rook, and picked up the shafts, and the cart came up out of the mud of the yard, and that was the end of it.

---

They went north-west up the grade all that day with the burned wayhouse falling behind them, and it was a good day, and Toren hated how good it was.

The sky came clear about ten in the morning for the first time in a week. The grade ran up through open birch country where the trees had got into the ballast on both sides and made a green tunnel of the cutting, and the walking was easy, and the country did not have a single thing wrong with it.

And they were good at it. That was the thing he could not stop noticing.

Milo came down the line at eleven with the water and gave it out in his order — Senna, then Wyck, then whoever was on watch, then everybody else — and nobody had told him to do that and nobody had noticed him starting and now it was simply the order in which water happened.

Dessa walked in the middle with her hands in her pockets and said "clear" about every ten minutes, not loudly, to nobody in particular, and had been doing it for two days, and the strange thing was the effect it had on Toren's shoulders. He had not known his shoulders were up. He had been walking at point with his shoulders somewhere around his ears since the loop road, and Dessa Cole saying *clear* in a bored voice took two inches off them every time she did it.

At the top of the long pitch where the grade climbed out of the birch, where the ballast was loose and the cart began to slide, all four of them got on it without a word being said. Milo and Toren on the near side. Dessa on the off. Wyck on the tail with his shoulder into it and his bad leg braced straight out behind him, pushing with the good one, breathing through his teeth. Rook in the traces, leaning.

Nobody thanked anybody. At the top they came off it and went back to where they had been walking and Milo said something about birch bark and the day went on.

---

At noon Wyck rewrapped Toren's hand.

He did it without being asked and largely without looking at him. Toren had been trying to get the cloth round the palm one-handed with his teeth for about four minutes, and Wyck watched him fail at it twice from six feet away, and then came over and took the end of the cloth out of his mouth and did it properly.

"You've got it too tight at the wrist and too loose where it matters," he said. "Open your fingers. Not that far. There."

"Thanks."

"Yeah."

He wound it, and tucked the end, and pressed the tuck flat with his thumb, and sat back on his heels, and his face had nothing friendly in it at all.

"That's not me being all right about it," he said.

"I know."

"I want to be clear, because Faust is going to see this and get ideas." Wyck got his stick under him and stood up off the bad leg in the careful two-part way he had learned. "You'd have made a mess of your own hand and then you'd have been no good at point tomorrow, and I'm the one who'd be standing where you fell over. That's the whole of it."

"I know," Toren said again.

"Good."

He limped off up the grade, and Toren looked at his own hand, wrapped better than he could have wrapped it, by somebody who had spent ten days learning how a splinted limb wants to be handled, and did not know what to do with any part of that.

---

That evening Rook drilled the four of them together for the first time since the pumphouse.

It was full dark and the moon was down. He took them off the grade into the birch below the bank, and put them in the diamond — Toren at point, Wyck left, Dessa right, Milo centre — and told them to sweep a line of trees three hundred paces long and come back, and then he walked away and sat on the bank and did not come with them.

"He's not coming," Milo said.

"No."

"He's *not coming*."

"Faust," said Dessa, "walk."

They walked it.

It took forty minutes and nothing happened and Toren would remember it for years.

Nothing happened, and that was what was extraordinary. They went into standing birch in the dark, four children, one of them with a stick and a bad leg, one of them with a light he had never once used for anything, and they moved through it like a thing with four legs. When Toren turned, the diamond turned, because Dessa felt him turn before she saw him. When Wyck fell behind on a bad bit of ground, Milo shortened up without being told and the shape held. Twice Dessa said a distance and a direction in a flat voice and the two of them nearest went and looked and it was a fox and it was a stump, and neither time did anybody light anything.

At the far end Toren stopped the diamond at a place he did not like, a low fold in the ground with the trees thick above it, and held them there while Dessa put it down and stood in it for nearly a minute.

"Clear," she said. "It's clear. It's a bad-looking hole with nothing in it."

"All right," Toren said. "Back."

And they came back, and Rook was sitting on the bank exactly where they had left him, and he watched them come up out of the trees in formation and break out of it at the top without being told, and he said:

"Good."

That was all. That was the entire debrief. Milo looked around at the others to check that it had actually happened.

---

Later, at the fire, Milo Faust broke the first pan-bread off the stone, tore a piece off the corner of it, put the piece on a flat rock at the edge of the coals, and set it aside.

Then he handed the rest round in his order.

He did it without any ceremony whatsoever. He did it the way you put the lid back on a crock. Toren was almost certain Milo had no idea he was doing anything at all — that four days of watching a man do a thing had simply put the thing into his hands the way it puts a whistled tune into your head, and that if you had stopped him and asked him he would have gone red and had no answer.

Across the fire, Rook looked at the piece of bread on the rock.

He looked at it for about three seconds, which was two and a half seconds longer than a piece of bread requires, and his face did not change, and then he looked away and ate his own and said nothing.

Dessa saw it. Toren saw her see it. Neither of them said anything, then or afterward, and the piece of bread sat on the rock all night the way it had sat on a rock above a road eleven days before.

---

Senna waited until the others were down and then told Toren to go and get her the water can, which he did, and then to go away, which he also did, and he went about fifteen feet and sat down behind the cart wheel instead of lying down, and was not proud of it.

"You're going to break that boy," Senna said.

"No," said Rook.

"You put the one who can't hold a light in front of the one who can, in the dark, every day, and you won't say why, and when he asks you for a reason you tell him he'd be good at it." Her voice was very dry. "I ran a house with three sons in it. I know exactly what you're doing and I'd like to hear you say it out loud."

"Standing where you're already good is worth nothing. He'd be safe at point. Voss isn't, and he's the one who needs the hour."

"That's true," said Senna.

"It is."

"It's true and it isn't the whole of it, and you know that, because if it were the whole of it you'd have said it in the yard this morning and saved yourself the week you're about to have." The old woman resettled the blanket. "You could give him something. Not all of it. Anything. You've got four of them and three of them would follow you into a river and the fourth one is doing arithmetic in the dark every night, and he's not stupid, and he's going to get an answer whether or not it's the right one."

For a while there was nothing but the fire.

"I know," Rook said.

"Then why—"

"Because the answer isn't mine to hand out," he said, "and because the day I do hand it out, it stops being something he's angry about and starts being something he has to carry, and he's fifteen, and I'd like him to have a while longer."

Senna Voss did not say anything to that for long enough that Toren thought she had gone to sleep.

"That's the first honest thing you've said since the ledger," she said.

---

Wyck came and found him at the change of the watch, near two in the morning, and sat down on the ballast beside him, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"I've worked something out," Wyck said.

"Wyck—"

"No, it's all right. It's not a fight. I'm too tired and my leg hurts and I genuinely haven't got one in me." He had his stick across his knees. "I've been going at this wrong for ten days. I kept asking why he picks you and I kept answering it with things about me — I'm better, I'm older, I've done the work, he must not rate me. That's all I had. And it's rubbish, because he *does* rate me, he said so on the ballast in front of everyone and he meant it, and he made me make it four feet because he wanted to see where the end of me was."

Toren's stomach had gone cold.

"So it was never about which of us is better," Wyck said. "It was never about us at all. He's not picking the better one. He's picking *you*, specifically, and he's been doing it since the first afternoon at the Gate, before he'd ever seen either of us do anything."

The fire had burned right down. Out beyond it the grade ran away north-west in a straight black line under a sky with no moon in it.

"At the pumphouse," Wyck said, "I was sitting in that lower doorway with my leg open to the hip and I was watching you go up the slope, because there wasn't anything else to look at and I couldn't stand up. And you got to the top and you put your hand in your pocket and you went straight back down that hill into a flooded building on the worst day of my life, for something. And he let you. He'd spent four days telling us nobody goes anywhere alone and he let you go, and then he went in after you, and he came out, and neither of you has ever said one word about it to anybody. Not one word. In ten days."

Toren's right hand, wrapped that afternoon by the boy sitting next to him, was flat against his coat pocket. He did not remember putting it there. Under the cloth and under his palm was a double row of stitches he had sewn himself by firelight in a yard at Tallow Gate, two rows, not one, because he had been told two.

"I'm not going to ask you what it is," Wyck said.

"Wyck—"

"I've thought about it all week and I'm not going to, and I want you to understand why, because it's the only part of this I'm actually proud of." He got his stick under him and stood up, the careful way, in two parts. "If I ask you, you'll lie to me. You'll have to. And then I'll have that as well, and I've got enough."

He went off toward the cart, and stopped, and did not turn around.

"Just so you know what it's like from here," Wyck Tanner said. "You've got something. He knows what it is and you know what it is and the three of us don't, and every morning he puts you in front of us in the dark and won't say why, and every single one of us walks along behind you anyway."

He walked back to the fire.

Toren Voss sat on seventy-year-old broken stone with his hand flat over two rows of stitches, and did not go after him, and did not take his hand away.
