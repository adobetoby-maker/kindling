# Chapter 15 — A Mile Short

They did not move that day.

Rook came down off the bank at seven in the morning and said they were staying, and did not give a reason, and then he did the thing that made it worse: he gave them all work.

Dessa went on holding, in shifts, out to two hundred feet and then out past it. Milo got sent down the grade at dusk with his light on a stone, which he had been looking forward to for a day and a half, and came back thrilled and empty-handed because nothing at all had walked up to it. Wyck was put on the leg — standing holds, sitting holds, the leg first and the light second and then the other way round — and got through it without complaint and sat down afterward with his face grey and his jaw set. Toren was sent up the grade two hundred paces to sit on the crown and watch the country, which was the job he had been given on the second day out of Tallow Gate and had not been given since.

It was a full, sensible, well-run day, and every single one of them knew exactly what it was for.

"He's keeping us busy," Milo said at noon, in the tone of a man who has discovered something and is not enjoying it.

"Yes," said Dessa.

"That's — he's never done that. He gives us things because they're the thing. He doesn't give us things so we've got something to do."

"No."

"So why's he—"

"Faust," said Wyck, from the grass, with his eyes shut, "you are going to say every part of this out loud over the next four hours and it will not help, and I'm asking you as a friend to do it further away from me."

Milo went and did it further away from him, which was the closest thing to a kindness anybody managed all morning.

---

Senna got hold of Toren in the middle of the afternoon by the simple method of telling him to bring her the water can and then not letting go of his wrist.

"Sit down."

"I'm supposed to be—"

"You're supposed to be on a bank looking at an empty field and you've been doing it since seven and the field is still empty. Sit down."

He sat down on the tailboard.

His grandmother had her leg out straight along the boards and the blanket up to her chest and her hands folded on top of it, and she looked, in the flat afternoon light, worse than she had at Tallow Gate. That was a thing Toren had been carefully not looking at for about two weeks. She had got thinner in the face in a way that made her eyes bigger and she coughed the same small dry three-beat cough she had coughed his whole life, and he had started, recently, to hear it at night and lie still and wait for the fourth beat.

"How long has he been standing up there?" she said.

"Since yesterday evening."

"Mm." Senna looked out at the junction. "You know what I keep thinking about? The pen."

"What pen?"

"At the Gate. That woman put a ledger on a desk and put a pen on top of it and he picked the pen up." She resettled her hands. "He held it for a good while. He held it long enough that I had time to work out what it would mean for me if he signed it, which is that I'd have been left in that yard, and four days after that I'd have been left on the road outside it, and I made my peace with that in about the time it takes to boil water, because I'm eighty-one years old and I've had practice."

Toren's throat closed.

"Gran—"

"Don't. I'm not asking you for anything, I'm telling you something." She turned her head and looked at him. "He put the pen down. He didn't sign it and he didn't explain it and he's never once mentioned it since, and I have thought about that pen every single day for a month. Whatever's going on up on that bank, boy — whatever it is — it's the same thing as the pen. I'd put money on it if I had any."

---

Toren went to him at dusk.

He had been building himself up to it since the middle of the afternoon and by the time he actually went he had stopped building and had simply started walking, which was, he thought afterward, the only way he ever managed anything.

Rook was sitting on the crown of the grade with his forearms on his knees. He had a stone in one hand and he was turning it over, and he did not look up.

Toren took the disk out of his pocket and held it out.

"Take it," he said.

The stone stopped turning.

"Put that away."

"No." Toren's hand was shaking, which made him furious. "You take it. I've thought about it all day and it's the only thing in any of this that's actually mine to do, so take it."

"Voss—"

"You wouldn't sign the ledger and you wouldn't say why. You put me at point every day and you won't say why. You burned a board off a wall and you won't say why. You've been standing on this bank for a day and a night and everybody down there is pretending they can't see you." He was not shouting. He had expected to shout and he was not, and his voice had gone somewhere thin and flat instead. "And every single one of those things started at a sluice with this in your hand. So take it. You carry it. Wyck's had a hole in his life for a month because of it and he doesn't even know that's what it is, and I'm not — I can't—"

He stopped, because his voice had gone.

Rook had not moved.

"And if you won't take it," Toren said, "then there's a river at the bottom of the east line. Dessa saw the willows. I'll walk down there tomorrow and I'll put it in, and that's what my father told me to do with it, and I'll have done one thing he asked."

---

The wind came across the flat and moved the grass on the bank and went on.

"When did he tell you that," Rook said.

"The night I was Kindled. The same night he gave me the Ember." Toren's arm was still out. He could not seem to put it down. "He said it isn't a gift, it's a debt. He said somebody handed it to us and nobody's ever been able to put it down. And he said if it ever came to a choice between that and me, I should put it in a river and not tell anybody where."

"And what did you say?"

"I said yes."

"Were you lying?"

"Yes," said Toren. "He knew. Neither of us said so."

Rook set the stone down on the ballast beside him, carefully, as though it mattered where it went.

"There was a river three miles up the loop road," he said. "The morning after we buried them. You'd have been on the plank bridge with the litter poles."

Toren's whole chest went cold.

"I stood on it," he said. "The others went past me. I stood there with my hand in my pocket and I didn't take it out, and then I walked on, and I've felt sick about that since the day it happened, and I've never told anybody, and I don't know which half I feel sick about. Whether it's that I thought about it. Or that I didn't."

"You were nine days into being an orphan and you stood on a bridge and had a hard thought and then you walked," said Rook. "There's no version of that you owe anybody an apology for."

"He asked me for one thing—"

"He asked you for one thing and he asked you for it four hours after you'd got the thing in your hand, and he'd had it his whole life and never done it either, and neither did his father, and neither did hers." Rook stood up. "Five of them, Voss. Five generations of your people have stood on a bridge with that in their pocket. Every single one of them walked on. You're not the one who's weak. You're the one who's *fifth*."

---

Toren stood on the bank with his hand out and the disk on his palm and the wind coming across it, and understood, about two seconds after the man had stopped talking, that he had just been told something.

"You know what it is," he said.

"No."

"You just said—"

"I said I know what it does to people who carry it." Rook put his hands in his pockets. "I don't know what it is. Nobody does. I've spent a long time around the only people who ever seriously tried to find out and I can tell you exactly what they had at the end of it, which was less than your father had."

"Then tell me that."

"No."

"Then take it—"

"I can't take it." Something came into the man's voice that had not been in it before, and it was not anger, and it was gone again almost at once. "Put it away. Put it in the pocket with two rows of stitches in it that you sewed by a fire in a yard because I told you to, and don't ever offer it to another living soul the way you just offered it to me, because I'm the only one on this road who'd say no."

Toren put it away.

His hand was still shaking. He got it into the pocket and got his hand out of the pocket and stood there with both arms at his sides and nothing at all left in him.

"I'm going to ask you one thing," he said, "and then I'll go down and I won't ask again."

"Ask it."

"Is it my fault?"

Rook turned his head.

"Is *what* your fault."

"All of it." Toren's voice cracked in the middle and he pushed through the crack. "You were crossing a road. You said so. You weren't on it, you were crossing it, and you'd have walked us to a wall and left us there and gone back to whatever you were doing, and you *tried*, you had the pen in your hand — and then you pulled that out of a sluice and you didn't go. You've had four of us round your neck for a month and a woman in a barrow and a boy with a leg, and you were supposed to be somewhere, and you're standing on a bank in the dark looking at a fork in a railway, and I'm the reason. Aren't I."

---

The light had gone out of the west entirely. Down in the corner between the hedge and the bank there was the small red eye of the fire, and three shapes around it, and one of them was standing up.

Rook looked down at them for a long moment.

"Get your coat," he said.

"What?"

"Get your coat. It'll be cold out there and we'll be a while." He came down off the crown of the grade past Toren without stopping. "And tell them we're walking up to the junction and we'll be back before the middle of the night, and tell them in those words, and don't make anything of it, because they'll make enough on their own."

---

They made a great deal of it, and they did it almost entirely without speaking, which was worse.

Milo said, "Now?" and then, "It's dark," and then nothing else at all, and went and sat down on the far side of the stove with his back to both of them.

Dessa said, "How long?"

"He said before the middle of the night."

"Right." She had her arms crossed. She looked at Toren and then past him at the shape of a man waiting up on the crown of the grade, and then back. "Do you know what it's about?"

"No."

"Do you know what it's *about*, Toren."

"No," he said, and it was true, and he could hear exactly how it sounded, and there was nothing whatsoever he could do about that.

Dessa nodded once and went and sat down by Senna and did not look at him again.

Wyck was at the top of the camp with his stick, standing.

Toren went past him because there was no way out of the corner except along the grade and Wyck was on it, and he had got two steps past when Wyck said, quietly, "Voss."

He stopped.

"I'm not asking," Wyck said.

"I know."

"I want you to know that I know it's tonight. Whatever it is. He hasn't slept, and he's kept us busy all day like a man getting a house ready, and now he's standing up there with his coat on waiting for you." His voice was completely level. "So I know. And I'm still not asking. And I'd like that on the record, because in about three hours you're going to come back down here knowing something, and I'd rather have said it before than after."

Toren could not think of one single thing to say to him.

"Go on," said Wyck. "He's waiting."

---

They walked the mile out to the junction in the dark and neither of them said anything the whole way.

The triangle of raised ground was pale under a sky with no moon in it. Rook went straight across the middle of it to the brick box with no roof and put his hand on the wall and went round to the lee side, out of the wind, where there was a step and a fall of old brick to sit on, and sat down.

Toren sat down at the other end of the step.

From there he could see both of the banks going out. The left one ran away north-west, flat and straight and pale, until it stopped being visible. The right one curved east through a long shallow bend and went behind the shoulder of the hill.

Rook took his Ember out of his coat and rolled it once against his palm, which he did when he was about to use it, and then did not light it. He held it in both hands with his forearms on his knees and turned it over slowly, twice, and then held it still.

"You asked me if it was your fault," he said.

"Yes."

"It isn't. And you're going to think it is anyway for a while, because I'm about to hand you a reason to, and I can't help that." He looked out at the left-hand line. "The rest of them are going to know I told you something tonight and they're never going to know what. That's going to cost you with them. I'd take it off you if there was a way and there isn't."

"I know."

"You don't. You will." Rook turned the Ember over once more in his hands. "Before the Fall there were places that did research. Universities, laboratories, whatever you want to call them. Almost all of it went in the first ten years, either burned or taken apart for the copper. A few of them didn't. A few of them were built in the wrong century, out of too much concrete, in a hole in the ground, and those came through, and people found them again, and one or two of them got started up a second time by people who'd decided the question was worth it."

He stopped.

Toren waited.

"There was one of those," Rook said, "in a bowl of hills about four hundred miles north of where you're sitting, and they ran it for eleven years, and I was there for six of them, and the place was called Homura."
