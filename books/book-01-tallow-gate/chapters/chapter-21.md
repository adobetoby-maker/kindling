# Chapter 21 — The Weir

"Say the failure," Rook said.

They were on the south apron in the dark with the weir going white eight feet away and the noise of it sitting on top of everything.

"We get pushed off this end," said Wyck.

"That's it. That's the whole of it and there's nothing underneath it." Rook had his coat off and folded on the sluice-house step, which none of them had ever seen him do at night. "The barrow does not move fast and there is a door with your grandmother behind it, and if any of them get past this end of that walkway then everything after that happens in a stone room with one way out. You hold the south end or nothing else you do tonight means anything. Say it back."

"Hold the south end," said Toren.

"Hold the south end," said Milo.

"Good. Cole."

"Three hundred," said Dessa.

---

She gave them the whole of the approach.

She could not hear a thing. That was the reason she was there and Toren had worked it out about four seconds after Rook put her against the buttress: the weir took hearing off every person on that riverbank equally, and there was exactly one of them for whom that did not matter, and she had been sitting in a field at noon with her eyes open for six weeks learning how not to mind.

"Two hundred and eighty. Still two groups. Five in front, four behind, and the four behind are further off the water than the five."

"How much further?"

"Sixty feet up the bank. Maybe more." Her head had not moved at all. "They've stopped."

"They've seen it," said Rook.

"They've—"

"They've seen Faust's light."

Milo made a small sound.

"Now they'll send two," Rook said. "Nobody in that company is going up a stone chute in the dark without leaving somebody at the bottom of it. Cole, tell me when it's two."

Forty seconds.

"It's two," said Dessa.

Rook let out a breath that Toren had not known he was holding, which was in itself the single most alarming thing that had happened all night.

"Nineteen minutes," he said. "That's what he's just bought us. Sit down, all of you, and drink something, because you won't want to in a quarter of an hour."

---

They came to the north end of the walkway at about one in the morning.

Toren saw them arrive as a change in the shape of the dark and then, when one of them put up a hooded lamp, as seven men and women standing in a loose arc on the north apron sixty feet away with the whole white sheet of the weir between.

"Look at them now," Rook said, without turning his head. "You won't get another chance and you'll want to have done it.

"Brown coat is Torch. He's been Torch longer than any of you have been alive and he'll have held it at three feet since before your mothers were born, and he's not going to try for three feet tonight, because a yard of concrete doesn't want three feet. The narrow one at the back is Flame and it's magic-side and it's all sensing, and he's the reason they came down the bank in two groups instead of four. The tall one with the cord is Flame and doesn't know it, and nobody's ever told her, and I'm not going to get the chance to.

"The rest are men with tools, and men with tools have killed more people on this continent than everybody on that ladder put together, so don't get clever about it."

"And you?" said Wyck.

"Further up," said Rook.

He walked out onto the walkway about fifteen feet and stopped, where the lamp could reach him.

"Far enough," he said.

He did not shout it and it carried anyway, which was a thing Toren had seen him do twice before and had never understood.

The lamp came forward. Behind it, in the edge of its light, there was a man in a brown oilskin coat, broad through the shoulders, somewhere past fifty, with a beard cut short and square and a heavy short blade already lit in his right fist — not three feet of white but eighteen inches of it, thick, held low and easy at his hip like a tool he had been carrying all day.

"Hale Orsk," he said. "I run this detachment and the contract's in my name. I'd like it said out loud that I came down here to talk, because I'd like it said afterward."

"Say it, then."

"Whitlow says you're the one that's been on us five years." Orsk's voice was reasonable and carried the way a man's voice carries when he has spent thirty years making himself heard across open ground. "I'll tell you what I told him. I came into this company six years ago. Whatever you've got, you've got it with four men, and two of those are dead of ordinary things and one's in Undern with no legs. I've got seven people here and a paper on a tear and eleven houses depending on it, and I will take all of that up that valley in the morning and never come back down this water, and you can walk east, and that's the end of it."

"No," said Rook.

"I've not finished."

"You have. You came down here to find out what I am." Rook put his hands in his pockets. "You've found out. Go on back up the bank."

Orsk stood on the north apron for a moment.

"Whitlow," he said.

The narrow older man came into the lamplight with his hands loose at his sides and his head slightly forward.

He looked across sixty feet of moving water at the man standing on the walkway, and he did not say anything at all, and then he turned to Orsk and nodded once, slowly, the way a man agrees to something he has been arguing against.

"Right," said Hale Orsk. "That's a pity."

---

Toren had thought he knew what the next part would look like.

He had six weeks of ballast and birch and a flooded pump house, and under all of that he had a road on the fifth of the month, and he had built out of the whole of it a picture of what happened when a fight began: that it began, that there was a moment when the thing turned over and everybody started.

There was no moment. The lamp went out and four people came down the walkway at a run.

---

The walkway was a yard wide.

That was the entire tactical shape of the next two minutes and Rook had seen it from two hundred paces away that afternoon. A yard of concrete with the sheet of the weir pouring over on the upstream side and a fourteen-foot drop into the white of the tailwater pool on the other, and nowhere on any part of it for two people to stand abreast.

The first man came into Rook's range and Rook was not there.

He had gone low and left, one hand down on the curb, the hard blue-white frame coming up around his legs so fast that Toren's eyes printed it as a single bar of light — and the spear came out of the Ember already at half length because there was no room for seven feet of it, and the shortening was the strike, and the man went off the walkway into the pool.

The second one Rook put down without straightening up.

The third got a hand on him. Toren saw it happen. The man was big and had a mattock haft and he came in under the spear and got his shoulder into Rook's chest, and for one second it was two people on a yard of wet concrete, ordinary and physical and almost stupid, the way the biggest moments turn out to be.

And then there were two short blades where there had been one long one, one in each hand. Toren still did not know where the second one came from. The man went off the downstream side.

The fourth one stopped.

He stopped at about thirty feet with his boots skidding and he went back up the walkway faster than he had come down it, and Rook did not follow him.

That was the part Toren could not stop thinking about afterward. Sixty feet of open concrete and a man running away up it, and Rook stood where he was, in the middle, with the frame still burning around his legs, and let him go.

"He doesn't leave the end," Wyck said, at Toren's shoulder. He was breathing hard and he had not moved and neither had his Edge. "Look at him. He won't go past the middle."

"Why not?"

"Because we're at this end," said Wyck.

---

The rifle came into it eleven minutes later.

There was no warning of any kind. There was a hard flat crack from somewhere high up the north bank, arriving a long time after it should have, and a piece of the buttress six inches above Dessa's head came off it and went past Toren's ear with a sound like a wasp.

Nobody on the south apron understood what had happened.

"*Down*," Rook said, from thirty feet out on the walkway, and he was already coming back. "Everybody down. Behind the sluice-house wall, now, move—"

The second one took a splinter of concrete out of the curb where his foot had been.

"That's a rifle," said Wyck. He said it in the flat curious voice of a person whose mind has gone somewhere else entirely. "That's a—"

"Get behind the *wall*."

They got behind the wall.

Toren went down on his knees in the wet with his back against eighteen inches of mill stone and his heart going and his father's Ember still lit in his fist, and he heard, through everything, Rook saying one short sentence to himself that was not for any of them, and the sentence was: *she's in the winding-house*.

---

"Listen to me," Rook said. "All four. Fast.

"She's a hundred and ten feet away across moving water and she is up in the stone of that winding-house and she will not come down and she does not need to. Nothing I have reaches her from here. Nothing any of you have ever *will*. There is one thing on this river that gets to her and it's me and it takes about nine seconds, and for those nine seconds there is nobody on this end of the walkway but you.

"So here's the order and you don't get a second one. Stay behind stone. Do not go out onto that apron for any reason at all. If they come across, Tanner holds the mouth and the rest of you stay where you are and let him.

"Nine seconds. Cole, tell them."

And he went.

He did not go along the walkway.

He went over the crest of the weir, upstream side, straight across seventy feet of brown water sliding over concrete two inches deep and going faster than a man runs, with the light around his legs turning the whole sheet of it white from underneath — and Toren, with his face against the mill stone, saw it in three separate pictures, like lightning, and understood in the third one that the man had not been running on the weir at all for the last twenty feet of it.

---

Two of them came up out of the tailwater on the south side.

Toren never found out how they got there and Rook told him later that it was the obvious thing and that he had expected it half an hour earlier — that the pool below a weir is shallow along its edges, and that any two men with a rope and no particular objection to being cold can be across a river in four minutes if nobody is looking at them.

They came up over the lip of the apron thirty feet behind the sluice-house wall, in the dark, on the side where there was nothing.

And the four of them did the thing they had been built to do.

It took no time at all. Toren came off the wall and out and to the front because point was his, and Wyck went left because left was his, and Dessa went right, and they made the shape they had made in a yard between two fuel tanks and on a hillside in the birch and in forty minutes of standing black timber where nothing had happened, and they made it fast and clean and with nobody saying one word, and it worked.

It worked. The first man came at the front of it and got three feet of Wyck Tanner's Edge across the forearm from the left side where he was not looking, and went down. The second one turned to face that and Toren was already inside of him with two feet of white light and put it through the shaft of the thing in his hands, and the man ran.

Six seconds. It was the best six seconds of Toren Voss's life.

And Milo Faust was standing in the middle of it, eleven feet out on the open apron, where the middle goes.

---

The third shot came from a hundred and ten feet away, across moving water, out of a stone winding-house, from a woman Toren never saw at any point that night or afterward.

Milo sat down.

That was all Toren's eyes gave him and it was all they ever gave him. There was a sound that was not the weir, and Milo Faust sat down on the wet concrete with his legs out in front of him like somebody stopping to fix a bootlace, and put one hand flat on the ground beside his own hip, and did not get up.

Toren was seven feet away.

He got there. He got there and he had both hands on him and Milo was looking at him with his eyebrows up in an expression of enormous, uncomplicated surprise, and there was a dark place high on the left side of his coat, and Toren put his palm over it because there was nothing else in the world to do with a hand.

"Dessa," he said. And then, much louder: "*Dessa*—"

---

Up on the north bank there was a noise.

It was not loud and it did not last. It was over before Toren's head came round, and afterward he was never able to say whether he had actually heard anything at all or had simply seen the light go out of a stone window, and he did not ask.

Nine seconds, Rook had said, and it had been fourteen, because he had had to go up the outside of the winding-house wall.

---

Hale Orsk came down the walkway while he was still on the far side.

He came at a heavy unhurried run with eighteen inches of white in his fist, and Toren, kneeling on an apron with both hands on his friend, saw him arrive at the mouth of the walkway and did not have anything left to do about it.

Wyck Tanner sat down.

He did it deliberately. He went the last four feet to the mouth of the walkway on his stick and then he put his back against the buttress and lowered himself onto the concrete with his bad leg straight out across the gap and the good one folded under him, the careful way, in two parts — and the white light came out of his fist and kept going and did not stop at three feet.

It stopped at five.

"Five sitting," said Dessa, from somewhere behind him, in a voice with nothing in it. "You've got five sitting. He's at forty feet."

"How long?"

"Twelve seconds."

"Tell me every three."

Toren had his hands on Milo and could not turn his head and heard all of it.

He heard Hale Orsk come into the mouth of that walkway, thirty years and a company and a contract and a Torch's worth of one door lit all the way up, against a boy of fifteen sitting on the ground in his way with five feet of light across a yard of wet concrete and a drop on both sides and nothing behind him but an old woman in a stone room.

He heard the first exchange. He heard the second.

"Nine," said Dessa.

He heard Wyck make a sound he had made once before, on a hillside above a road, on the fifth of the month.

"Six."

Wyck held.

---

Rook came back across the weir crest and up onto the south apron and did not stop moving, and he put his left hand out.

Nothing came out of it. That was what Toren's eye insisted on, and his eye was wrong.

Fifty feet of walkway went hard and thick and wrong. The sheet of water coming over the crest beside it stood up in a line, all at once, as though a wind had come from directly above it, and Hale Orsk stopped with his forward boot off the ground like a man walking into a door in the dark.

Three seconds.

In the third second Rook's arm began to shake.

Then the pressure let go and the water came down and Rook went past Wyck Tanner into the mouth of the walkway, and it was finished in about as long as it takes to say so, and Toren did not watch any of it, because he was holding a coat shut with both hands.

---

Dessa got there at a run and put her hands where Toren's were and Toren took his away, and she worked.

He watched her work. He had watched her work on a hillside of broken ballast with her tongue between her teeth and on a boy who had gone through a window in Coldwater when she was fourteen, and he knew exactly what her face did when it was going, and he knew within about four seconds that it was not.

"Move your hands," said Rook.

He was already down beside them. His own hands were shaking so much that he had to put the Ember into the crook of his arm to get the coat open, and he did it anyway, and laid the thing flat and put both palms over it and held.

The weir went on making its noise.

Toren knelt on the wet concrete of an apron on the south bank of the Reave and watched a man who had put the ends of a leg together in a flooded pump house for eleven minutes, and a splintered shin in a road on the fifth of the month, hold Vera's Ember flat against Milo Faust's chest for a long time.

"That's the ends together," Rook said at last.

His voice had nothing in it whatsoever.

"There has to be somebody to do the rest."

---

The Riftspawn came out of the water at about half past two.

There were four of them in the pool below the weir and then six, folding and unfolding up over the lip of the apron with the seams down their chests lit like a row of coals, and there were more of them on the north bank in the dark where Cinder Company was.

Eleven people had been using worked power for an hour and a half on both sides of a river four miles below an open tear.

Nobody had to say any of that. Dessa said, "*Left*," in a flat voice, and Wyck got up off the concrete on his stick, and Rook took his hands off Milo's chest and stood, and after that it was work.

It took about nine minutes and Toren did almost none of it and could not afterward put any of it in order. He had two feet of white light out of his father's Ember and he had it the whole way through, and he had it while he was moving and while he was shouting and while he was doing a thing with his hands that he never told anybody about, and it did not go out one single time, and he did not notice that until two days later.

On the north bank there was a lamp, and then there was not, and the sound of Cinder Company going up that valley in the dark was audible for a long while afterward and nobody on the south apron went after it.

"Flask," said Rook, of the drift nearest him on the concrete, because that was what he said, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has said ten thousand times. "Flask. Handful. That's a Flask and a bad one."

He did not draw any of it up.

He put his palm flat on the apron for a moment, out of thirty years of habit, and then he took it off the stone and wiped it on his coat and went back and sat down on the wet ground beside Milo Faust and put a hand under the boy's head so it was not on concrete.

---

A quarter of a mile up the north bank, on the top of the stone chute above the winding-house, one small pale light was sitting on a stone.

It had been there for four hours. Nothing had put it out and nothing was going to. It was the only made thing in eleven miles of dark, and it did not gutter and it did not drift, and it did not need anybody at all.
