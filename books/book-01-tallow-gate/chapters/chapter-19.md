# Chapter 19 — Greenmill

There was a fourth beat in his grandmother's cough now.

Toren had heard it for the first time two nights before the junction and had said nothing, and had spent every night since lying on his side in the grass with his eyes open, waiting for it. It came at some point in every hour. Three small dry sounds and then a pause long enough to believe in, and then a fourth, lower, coming up from somewhere further down.

He had not told anybody. He had worked out, somewhere in the last week, that this was a thing he was going to be doing a great deal of.

"Stop listening to me," Senna said, without opening her eyes.

"I'm not."

"You're walking on the wrong side of this barrow." She pulled the blanket up. "You've been on the off shaft since the Gate. You're on the near one because that's the ear."

Toren went round to the off shaft, and Milo, on the near one, gave him a look and did not say anything, which from Milo Faust was worth more than most people's speeches.

---

The grade ended at the river at about eleven in the morning on the second day east.

It did not taper off or run down into the ground. It went straight out over the water and stopped, in the way a sentence stops when the man saying it has been shot. Forty feet of embankment, and then a squared stone abutment with iron stubs sheared off flush across the top of it. Then the Reave, brown and wide and moving. Then two stone piers standing up out of the middle of it with nothing on them, and the same abutment again on the far bank with grass growing out of the joints.

Toren stood on the end of the world they had been walking along for six weeks and looked at the gap.

"They took the bridge as well," said Milo.

"They didn't take it," Rook said. "It went. Iron holds a bridge up. Everything else is just heavy."

He set the shafts down and rolled his shoulders once and looked downstream, east, where the north bank ran on in a long flat shelf of willow and sedge with the water beside it.

"Line's finished," he said. "From here the road's the river, and in two days the river turns and there's a road that's still a road. Everybody drink something. We're going down the bank."

---

They went downstream all afternoon with the water on their right hand.

It was not like the grade and Toren felt the difference in his shoulders before he could say what it was. The grade had been a made thing — flat, straight, indifferent, running through the country without belonging to any of it. The riverbank belonged to the river. It bent when the water bent. It went soft where the water had been at it and hard where it hadn't, and twice they had to take the barrow up into the field above because a cut bank had fallen in, and both times it cost them a quarter hour and Rook said nothing about it.

Toren drilled while he walked, because he could now.

That was the other thing about the afternoon, and he did not trust it. His Edge had been gone for four days after the junction, entirely, not a flicker, exactly as Rook had said it would be and exactly as Toren had been unable to believe. And then on the first morning east it had come back, two feet of steady white out of his father's Ember, and it had not gone anywhere since. He walked eight miles down the bank of the Reave with the light burning in his fist and his eyes on the water and his mind on his grandmother's cough, and the light stayed on.

Rook, forty feet ahead in the traces, did not turn round and did not mention it.

---

Greenmill announced itself by smell, which Toren did not identify for a full minute and then did, and the identification went through him like cold water.

Bread.

"Oh," said Milo, in a completely different voice. "Oh, that's—"

"Don't run," said Rook.

"I'm not running."

"You're about to."

There was a mill on a race and eleven houses behind it and a smoke-house at the end of a row of drying frames, and a track came down out of the fields and ended at the water, and there were people in it. Not many. A man on the mill step with his sleeves rolled. Two women doing something to a frame of split fish. Three children on the bank throwing things at the river, who stopped throwing things at the river and stood up.

It was the first settlement any of them had seen since a woman in reading glasses had walked them to the outside of a pair of doors in a fallen fuel tank and told them the page was closed but not torn out.

Toren looked at Dessa. Dessa was looking at the drying frames with an expression he recognized from a great distance, because it was the one she had worn in the tunnel at Tallow Gate while a warden weighed jars, and it was the face of somebody watching a whole ordinary world go on being ordinary.

"Straighten up," said Rook. "All of you. Walk like people who've been somewhere."

"We *have* been somewhere," said Wyck.

"Then it shouldn't be hard."

---

The miller was called Pell Hoy and he came down off his step and looked at the barrow before he looked at any of them, which Toren thought was probably the correct order.

"That's a road cart," he said.

"It's a quarry barrow."

"It's a quarry barrow with a woman in it." Hoy put his hands on his hips. "How far?"

"Coldwater," Rook said. "Six weeks, near enough."

Hoy whistled one note and did not ask any of the four questions Toren could see arriving behind his face, which was, Toren decided, the second correct thing he had done.

They had a bed for Senna. That was all they had and they gave it without any negotiating at all — a corner of the mill's grain loft with a real mattress on a real frame, and a woman called Marda Wain who had been the nearest thing to a wound-woman in Greenmill for thirty years and who came and knelt by the barrow before anybody had asked her to.

She got Senna's wrist in her fingers and put her head down near the old woman's chest and stayed there, listening, for a long time.

"Father's word," she said at last, "look at her."

"I'm looking at me all day," said Senna. "Say it out."

"Reactor."

"Twenty years ago, and I've had it said to me by better than you, no offense. Can you do anything?"

"No," said Marda Wain.

"Good. Say that first next time, it saves everybody's afternoon."

The woman laughed, which surprised all of them, and then sat back on her heels and looked at Senna Voss for a while with something on her face that was not pity.

"Meridian," she said.

"Meridian."

"It's seventeen days on from here if the fords are down." She got up. "You'll want the honey. I'll not argue about it, so don't start."

---

They ate in the mill yard at dusk with about half of Greenmill sitting round the edges of it watching them do it, which was uncomfortable for approximately four minutes and then stopped being uncomfortable at all, because Milo Faust happened to them.

Toren could not afterward reconstruct how it went. It was not that Milo said anything clever. He asked the children what they had been throwing at the river and they told him, and then he asked whether it worked, and it turned out that it did not work. And then he was down on the bank with the three of them and a fourth who had come from somewhere, showing them how to make a flat stone go across water instead of into it, which he could not do either. About twenty minutes later one of the children did it four times and made a sound like a kettle, and the whole yard turned round.

"He's going to want to stay," Dessa said.

"He's not."

"I know he's not. I'm telling you he's going to want to." She had her arms round her knees and her bread untouched in her hands. "So am I, a bit. You can say it. Nobody's listening."

Toren looked across the yard at the mill and the drying frames and the smoke coming flat off the smoke-house roof, and at the loft window where his grandmother was lying on a mattress on a frame for the first time in six weeks.

"A bit," he said.

---

The company came up in the talk at about the time the light went.

It came up the way weather comes up, as the thing everybody had already been not-quite-saying, and it came out of Pell Hoy, who had drunk two mugs of something and had got comfortable.

"You'll not go up past the quarry," he said. "Not the north side. Not for a fortnight yet."

"Why's that?" said Rook.

"Tear." Hoy said it the way a man says *rats*. "Opened in the spring up in the old cut, and we had a bad summer off it — lost two off the top field in the sixth month and near lost the whole of the hay. So we put in for a company."

"You put in."

"All of us. Eleven houses and the mill and the two farms up the hill, and it took the lot of us to make the fee, and I'll tell you plainly it took the lot of us and then some." He turned the mug round. "Three weeks they've been up there. They know the work. They've had two casks of Flask off it already and one of them was the best weighing anybody's seen out of this valley, and when the tear's shut we get the half of the ash that's in the contract, and we've a paper on it, and the paper's good."

"Who weighs it?"

"They do."

"Who buys it?"

"They do that as well." Hoy looked at him. "That's how it's done."

"It is," Rook agreed. "Where do they winter?"

Toren had his bread in his hand and did not move any part of himself.

He had been listening to this man ask questions for six weeks. He had listened to him ask a warden about a charter and a wheelwright about a road and a woman at a ford about the depth of it, and every single one of those questions had come out in exactly the same shape: flat, short, in the middle of a sentence about something else, so that the person answering never once noticed they had been asked anything.

This one came out the same way. It was the third in a row and it was the only one he had actually wanted.

"Undern," said Pell Hoy. "Same as all of them. Weighing goes on till the ground freezes."

"It does," said Rook, and drank his water, and asked about the fords.

---

Wyck found Toren at the woodpile afterward.

"Don't," Toren said.

"I'm not going to." Wyck leaned his stick against the stack and started handing him splits, one at a time, so that Toren could stack them with the hand that worked. "I'm going to say one thing and then I'm going to go to bed, because there's a roof and I'd like to lie under one."

"All right."

"Three questions," said Wyck. "Weighs, buys, winters. And he wanted the third one and he put two in front of it so it wouldn't sit on its own." He handed over another split. "I've watched him do that eleven times since the Gate and I've never once seen him do it about something that didn't matter."

Toren stacked the wood.

"I know," he said.

"Good," said Wyck Tanner, and took his stick, and went to lie under a roof.

---

Dessa came and got him an hour later with the fire down and most of Greenmill asleep.

"Come up the bank with me."

"Now?"

"I've been holding since we came in," she said, "and there's a thing at the top of the field that I've had my hand on for two hours, and I'd rather look at it with somebody than by myself."

They went up the track past the last house, and past a gate, and out onto the shoulder of the hill where the grass was cropped short by something and the whole valley lay below them in the dark with the river going through it pale.

The graves were under the wall at the top corner.

There were three of them.

Toren stood in the wet grass and felt six weeks come round and click into place the way a wheel drops into a rut, and he did not have to be told and did not have to ask, and neither of them said anything for a long moment.

The earth was fresh. Not this week — the grass had started — but this season. Somebody had dug them to a decent depth in hard ground. Somebody had squared the corners. Somebody had set a flat stone upright at the head of each one and packed it round with smaller stones so it would stand through a winter.

"They're not written on," Dessa said.
