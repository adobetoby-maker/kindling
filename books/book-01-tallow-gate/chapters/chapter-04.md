# Chapter 4 — After

The ash did not blow away.

That was the first thing Toren noticed once there was room in him to notice anything. It lay on the roadbed in long soft drifts where the things had come apart, warm grey with a slow crawl of light still going through it, and the wind came up the valley and moved the grass on both banks and did not move the ash at all. It sat there. It was going somewhere else, slowly, downward, the way water goes into sand.

"Don't stand in it," Rook said.

Dessa was standing in it. She had been standing in it for some time with her boots buried past the laces, and when he spoke she came back from wherever she had been and looked down.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's not warm. You're feeling it because you're rift-touched and it's going into the ground under you. It'll make your head ache tomorrow and you'll think it's grief." He crouched by Senna and did not look up. "Go and stand on clean road."

Dessa did not move.

"Cole," Rook said, and it was not sharp, and it worked. She stepped out of the drift and stood on the gravel with her arms around her ribs. There was ash on her coat that had nothing to do with the road. She had not brushed it off. She was not going to.

---

Senna Voss's left leg was broken below the knee and again above the ankle, and she had a boot-print of a bruise coming up across her whole left side from the cart's rail, and she was, by any accounting Toren had ever heard of, an old woman who had been thrown forty feet down a hillside inside a wooden box.

She was also entirely awake and had opinions.

"You can put it in a splint," she said. "I've had a splint before."

"Splint's for after." Rook had her boot off and both hands on her shin, one above the break and one below. "Hold still and don't help."

"I'm not helping."

"You're talking."

Toren knelt where he could see. It seemed important to see. He had a vague, half-formed notion that he was supposed to be watching over her, that somebody now had to, and that in the absence of any better applicant it was going to be him, and he was thirteen years old and had been Kindled for eighteen hours.

Rook put one hand flat on the road, in the ash.

Toren saw it happen and never once, in all the months after, worked out what he had actually seen. The drift under the man's palm went dim. Not scattered — *dim*, the crawling light in it dulling out from his hand in a slow spreading circle a foot across, and everything that had been in it going somewhere, and the somewhere was Rook's arm. And then his other hand, the one on Senna's shin, got faintly warm-looking around the edges the way air over a fire does, and Senna Voss said one flat syllable through her teeth and gripped the road with both hands.

It took a long time. The ash under Rook's left hand went from grey to a colorless nothing that the wind, finally, picked up and took.

"That's the ends together," Rook said, sitting back. "The rest is you and it's going to take six weeks and it's going to hurt every day of it. Do you understand me?"

"I've been dying for twenty years," said Senna. "I know how long things take."

Rook looked at her properly for the first time.

"Reactor?"

"Scavenger run. Eighty miles north of here, before this one's father could walk." She nodded at Garrick and then, without any change of expression at all, looked at the sky instead. "Meridian's got a machine that can see the whole of me at once. That's where we were going."

"I know where you were going. It's the only place anybody's going on this road." He stood up. His hands were shaking again and he put them away again. "Boy. Come here."

Toren came.

"Hands."

He put them out. The palms had come off on the gravel bank and had been bleeding and had stopped and started twice, and he had not thought about them once.

Rook did not put his hand in the ash this time. He took his Ember out of his coat, rolled it once against his palm the way he had on the road, and laid it flat across Toren's two upturned hands, and held it there.

It was warm — the deep steady warmth of the Kindling fire, not the awful crawling heat of the drift — and it went in through the torn skin and out the backs of his wrists, and the raw scoured feeling in both palms banked down into a dull ache and then into nothing much at all. Rook took the Ember away. The cuts were still there. They had stopped mattering.

"That's a different thing," Toren said. "What you did for her — that was different."

"It was."

"Which one's the real one?"

Rook looked at him for about two seconds longer than the question deserved.

"Get the shovel off the cart," he said. "If the cart's got a shovel."

---

There was a shovel. It was the only thing in the wreck that came out unbroken.

They buried three people in the long grass above the road because the roadbed was too hard and the scrub was full of thorn, and it took the rest of the day and most of what any of them had left. Rook dug. He did not ask any of them to and he did not stop any of them when they took turns, and Wyck took a turn that went on far too long, and nobody said anything about that either.

Odo Prentice they never found. Rook went into the scrub twice, the second time for the better part of an hour, and came back out and shook his head once, and set the man's staff upright in the ground at the end of the row and drove it in with a stone. Eleven notches, and the fresh gouge across all of them.

Toren's father went in the first grave. His grandmother sat on the ground beside it the whole time with her splinted leg out in front of her and her hand flat on the turned earth, and she did not cry, and Toren, who was crying so hard he could not see, understood dimly that this was a thing she was doing on purpose and would pay for later.

Ilsa Cole went in the second. Dessa said the words. She got most of the way through them.

Nell Tanner went in the third, and Wyck said nothing at all, and when Milo put a hand on his shoulder he shrugged it off so hard he nearly went over.

Patience they did not bury. Rook went down into the thornbrush before he did anything else that afternoon, alone, and Toren heard nothing from down there, and Rook came back up wiping his hands on the grass and said the mule was seen to. He said it in a way that closed the subject. Milo asked anyway, because Milo always asked, and Rook told him the truth in one short sentence, and Milo went and sat by himself on the concrete for twenty minutes.

Toren thought that was cruel until much later, when he understood that every other adult he had ever known would have lied.

---

They ate at dusk, sitting in a rough circle in the grass above the road with a fire that Rook built too small and would not let them build up.

There was food. That was almost the strangest part of the whole day. The cart had gone over the edge, but the cart had been packed by Ilsa Cole, and Ilsa Cole had packed it in six separate bundles distributed around the bed on the theory that you never put everything in one place, and four of the bundles had survived the fall. There was flour and dried apple and a great deal of salt fish nobody wanted and a sack of the mill's own coarse meal.

Rook made a flat pan-bread out of the meal and a little water and cooked it on a stone.

And then, before he gave any of it out, he broke a piece off the first one, put it on a flat rock at the edge of the fire, and set it aside.

He did not say anything about it. He did not look at it. He handed the rest around, one piece each, and started on his own, and the piece on the rock stayed there.

Toren watched it the whole meal.

"Is that for something?" Milo asked. Of course he did.

"No," said Rook.

"It's just — you put it—"

"I know what I did."

Milo, who was thirteen and had a light in his hands that did nothing and had spent two months learning to laugh about it, said, "Right," and ate his bread, and let it go, and Toren found that he liked Milo Faust a great deal more than he had liked him yesterday.

The piece of bread was still on the rock in the morning. Toren looked for it when he woke. It had been rained on.

---

"I'm not taking you east," Rook said.

They were all looking at him. He was standing with his back to them, feeding the fire down to nothing, and he had clearly been building up to this for some hours and had decided that the only tolerable way to do it was to get it out in one piece.

"There's a relay station two days north-east of here on the loop road. Tallow Gate. Walled, garrisoned, they take road-traffic and they've got a charter and a warden and a duty of care, and they're two days from here at the speed your grandmother's going to manage, which is slow." He put the last stick on. "I'll walk you to the gate. I'll say what happened. And then that's where I stop."

Nobody spoke.

"I'm not going to Meridian," Rook said. "I'm not on that road. I was never on that road, I was crossing it, and I was a mile and a half out when I heard your mule, and I came because I was close enough to." He turned around. His face had nothing on it at all. "That's the whole of it. I want you to have it plain now instead of getting it in pieces over two days and thinking I'm working up to something."

"You're leaving us at a wall," said Wyck.

"I'm leaving you at a *garrison*, with a warden, with your grandmother's name and your dead written down in a ledger, which is more than you'd have got out of anybody else on this road today." Rook did not raise his voice. It was somehow worse. "You want me to pretend it's better than that? It isn't. It's the best thing available and it's still bad. You're going to be alive at the end of it. That's the only part of this I can actually do anything about."

"You could take us."

"No."

"You could—"

"*No*," Rook said, and there was a fractional thing in it — not anger, something under anger, something Toren did not have a name for and would spend a long time trying to name — and Wyck stopped.

Senna Voss said, from the ground, "How far can you carry an old woman before it starts costing you?"

Rook looked at her.

"About two days," he said.

"Then we'd better start."

---

They started a little after seven in the morning, in a grey light with the rain still deciding.

They had one shovel, four bundles, a splinted grandmother, a bill-hook, a splitting axe, three Embers between five people and only two people who could do anything with one, and a man in a re-sewn grey coat walking forty feet out in front of them and never once looking back to see if they were keeping up, which they were, because he had set the pace to the slowest of them without ever saying that was what he was doing.

Toren walked at the head of the litter poles with his father's Ember in his coat pocket and his hand around it.

He had worked something out during the burying and he had not said it to anyone and was not going to.

His father had been an Ember-user for twenty-six years. His father had stood on a wall at Coldwater with three feet of light in his fist. And two nights ago, at a fire under a broken overpass, on a road, five days out, with eleven days still to go, his father had knelt down in the dirt in front of him and given it away.

*It's a debt*, Garrick Voss had said, about a different object entirely, and Toren had not understood him at all.

He understood him now.

He put his other hand in his other pocket, where the disk sat, nine channels warm against his knuckles, feeling exactly like a room he had lived in.

There was a river three miles up the loop road. Rook had mentioned it at first light, in the tone of a man listing obstacles. Toren thought about it for all three miles.

He did not put the disk in it. He did not get anywhere close to putting the disk in it. He stood on the plank bridge with the water going grey-brown underneath and his hand in his pocket for a count of ten while the others went past him, and then he walked on, and the shame of that stayed with him longer than most of the rest of it.
