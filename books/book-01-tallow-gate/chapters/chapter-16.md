# Chapter 16 — Homura

"Say it back," Rook said.

"Homura."

"That's it. It's not a word from around here. It came with the people who named it and they got it from a long way further back than that, and I'm not going to pretend I know more about it than I do, which is that it means a flame." He turned the Ember over in his hands. "Somebody there thought it was clever. It was. It wore off."

Toren sat on the brick step with his coat pulled round him and did not move.

He had been prepared, walking out along the grade, for a great many things. He had been prepared for a secret and for a warning and for the thing about the disk. He had not been prepared for the man to sound the way he sounded, which was not solemn at all — which was the flat, slightly worn voice of somebody giving directions to a place he has been asked about too often.

"What was it?"

"It was a research station."

"Researching what?"

"Us," said Rook.

---

He told it in a particular order, and the order was not the order of the story, and it took Toren most of the night to understand why: the man was going around it. He would start somewhere at the edge, in the practical, in the plumbing and the roofs and the hours, and work inward as far as he could get, and then stop and go back out to the edge and start again from a different side.

He started with the buildings.

"Four of them in a bowl of hills with a ridge on three sides, so you couldn't see it from anywhere except directly above. Two above ground and two under. The two above ground were offices and sleeping quarters and a kitchen and they'd been rebuilt about four times, because they were made of the sort of thing people make buildings out of when they're in a hurry. The two underneath were the reason anybody went to the trouble. Concrete a yard thick and no windows and a generator hall with its own water off the hill, and everything in them still worked, because whoever put them there in the first place had been frightened of exactly what happened."

"They had power?"

"They had power. Not much and not all the time. They ran the instruments in the morning because that's when the head was highest in the tank."

"What instruments?"

"Machines that look at the inside of a head," Rook said. "There were three that worked. You lay on your back on a table and they ran you into a tube about so wide" — he put his hands up, a little wider than his own shoulders — "and you stayed there for the better part of an hour and it was very loud and very cold and nothing hurt and nothing happened, and then you got up and went and had your breakfast, and somewhere on the other side of the wall there was a picture of the inside of you with something lit up in it."

Toren sat with that.

"That's it?"

"That's it. That's what it was, nearly all of the time. It was the dullest place I have ever lived in my life." Rook shifted on the step. "People hear *research station* and they see something out of a story. It was sixty people in a hole in a hill being bored and cold and hungry and doing the same thing every morning for years, and the whole of what it was for happened in a room they weren't allowed into, on paper."

---

"How did you get there?"

"They came and got me. They had four people whose entire job was walking around the country asking after anyone who could do anything, and one of them turned up at my village when I was nineteen."

"And you went?"

"I went for the money," said Rook, "except there wasn't any money, so I went for the thing they gave instead of money."

"What did they give?"

"A charter." He said it the way Warden Braid had said *I signed something*. "Protection and medicine for the place you came from, for as long as you stayed and for five years after you left. Written down, witnessed, and they honored it, which not everybody would have. They had a physician out on a circuit and four men with rifles who were not for show, and my village got both of those for six years because I lay in a cold tube every morning and let them take a picture of the inside of my head."

Toren did not say anything.

He was thirteen years old and he had been on a road for five weeks with a dying woman in a cart, and the shape of what he had just been handed arrived in him whole and did not need any explaining at all: a family at the far end of something, and a person who had gone and done a thing they did not want to do, so that somebody at that far end could have a doctor.

"That's the same," he said.

Rook did not answer.

"That's the same thing we were doing. That's exactly the — my father and Odo and all of us, we were eleven days out from Meridian because there's a machine there that can see the whole of her at once. That's the same thing."

"Yes."

"Is that why you—"

"No," Rook said. "Don't do that. It's not that tidy and I won't have you making it tidy tonight, because you'll be stuck with whatever you make." He turned the Ember over. "It's the same shape. Half the people on this continent are somewhere inside that shape. It doesn't mean anything about you and me."

"But you noticed it."

"I noticed it on the loop road inside the first hour," said Rook, "and I did not enjoy it."

---

"What did they find?"

"One thing," Rook said. "They found one thing and they were certain of it and they spent eleven years trying to get anybody to care."

He looked out at the pale line of the left-hand bank running away north-west, and then he did the thing with his hands: he stopped turning the Ember over and held it still, in both of them, the way you hold something you have decided not to fidget with.

"Put an Ember-user in the tube," he said. "You get a picture. There's a place at the back and underneath, about the size of a walnut, and it's lit, and the rest is dark. Take him out. Put a magic-user in — somebody like Cole, somebody like Faust, somebody who's never touched a device in their life and thinks the rift reached through a hole in the sky and handed them a present."

"And it's a different place."

"It's the same place," said Rook. "It's the same place down to the last thing the machine can tell apart. Not similar. Not near. The same."

Toren had known that. He had been told it under a stone arch in the rain four weeks ago by this same man, flat, once, and he had believed it inside three sentences and had gone out at dusk and stood in the wet grass and been unable to do a single thing with it.

He had not known that somebody had photographed it.

"They had it in a *picture*," he said.

"They had it in about nine thousand pictures. They had it in men and women and children, in people from three regions who'd never met each other, in an old man who'd been Ember forty years and a girl of fourteen who lit a candle with her hand, and it came out the same every time for eleven years, and there is no argument anywhere in the world that beats that." Rook's voice had not changed at all. "It wasn't a theory. That's what I want you to have. Everything else they had was a theory. That one wasn't."

"What else did they have?"

"Theories. Some of them good." He shrugged one shoulder. "They had a decent one about why the ash burns clean. You know how it works — you can't take power off a person and put it in a lamp, because it's already somebody's, and it has to be lent and it has to go back, and it always costs more going back than it did coming out. Rift-things aren't anybody's. Nobody's home in them. That's the whole of the difference and it's why hunting is what everybody does for a living out here instead of just what kills them." He paused. "They had more than that. They had the beginning of a thing about how much of a person is lit and what it buys. I'm not giving you that one tonight."

"Why not?"

"Because it's four hours and you've got about two in you," said Rook, "and because I'd rather teach it to you in a field than hand it to you on a step."

---

The wind came round the corner of the brick box and Toren pulled his coat in, and out on the flat something small went through the grass and stopped.

"Was it you?" he said.

"Was what me."

"You said they found one thing and they were certain of it. And you said you were there six years, and you're the only person I've ever met who can — " He stopped. He tried again. "You do both of them. Everybody says one at a time. You came down a hill with light round your legs and a spear in your hand and I've been thinking about that since the day it happened, and nobody I've ever asked has heard of it. Was that them? Did they do that to you?"

Rook laughed.

It was a short, entirely unmusical sound, one breath out through the nose, and it was the first time Toren had heard anything like it from him.

"No," he said. "Nothing like it. They couldn't do anything to anybody. They were sixty people with three machines and a generator and no money, and half of what they did all day was keeping the roof on. Nobody at Homura ever made a single person able to do a single thing."

"Then how—"

"They measured," said Rook. "That's all they did. They were very good at it and they were honest about it and they never once said more than they had, and it was the best work anybody's done in this country since the Fall, and it was measuring." He turned the Ember over. "What I can do, I learned. It took years and most of it was miserable and none of it came out of a machine."

"Who taught you?"

The wind went over the top of the brick box.

"That's the other half of it," Rook said.

---

"There were sixty of us. About a third were magic, about a third Ember, and about a third were the other kind — people with no device and no rift in them at all who'd sat down at nineteen and started working on the inside of their own head and had got somewhere by being extremely stubborn for a decade. They're rarer than either of the other two and they're the ones nobody ever talks about, and Homura had eleven of them, which as far as I know is the most that have ever been in one building."

"Which were you?"

"Magic. My mother's side. Everybody in that valley was." He said it without any particular emphasis. "I'd never held an Ember in my life. I thought they were a superstition. I'd have told you that to your face at nineteen and been slightly pleased with myself about it, and about a third of the station thought the same about me from the other direction, and we all ate in the same room twice a day and were perfectly civil and thought each other fools."

"That's stupid," said Toren.

"It is. It's also what the whole world does, and the whole world has been doing it for seventy years, and it is the entire reason anybody bothered to build that place." Rook shifted. "And it is very hard to keep doing at close quarters, because you run out of ways to explain the other lot, and eventually somebody says something at a table and you have to actually look at them."

"Who said something?"

"She did," said Rook.

---

He did not say the name for a while.

He put both elbows on his knees and held the Ember out in front of him in both hands and looked at it, and Toren sat at the other end of the step and did not breathe very much.

"There was a boy there," Rook said. "Sixteen, Ember, from the east coast. Good with it — better than Tanner at the same age and Tanner's good. And they had him in the tube one morning, and they brought his picture out, and one of my lot — one of the magic side, a man I liked — looked at it and said, out loud, in the kitchen, that it was a shame the machine couldn't show the difference between a man's own power and a stick's.

"And a woman at the next table put her cup down and said, without looking up: *It can. It shows there isn't one. That's what the picture is.*"

He stopped.

"And the man said, what's that supposed to mean. And she said, it means you've read the same nine thousand pictures I have and you've decided you didn't. And the man laughed and it turned into an argument, and the argument was the usual one and it went round the usual six corners and got nowhere, and I sat there through the whole of it not saying anything, because I had also read them, and I had also decided I hadn't."

The wind moved the grass out on the flat.

"Her name was Vera," Rook said.

---

Toren did not say anything. There did not seem to be a place to put anything.

"She'd been there two years longer than me. Ember. Her people were a clan off the northern coast — they'd come over three or four generations before the Fall and they'd kept nearly all of it, the language in pieces and the way they laid out a table and about two hundred words nobody else on that continent had, and then the Fall came and they kept all of that too, which is harder than keeping a town. They were the best Ember-users I have ever seen. I don't say that because of her. I say it because they trained children on the same three movements for eleven years before anybody was allowed a fourth, and it showed."

"Did she teach you?"

"She taught me the Ember." Rook turned it over once. "Poorly at first, because she'd never taught anybody who hadn't started at six, and she kept telling me things that only make sense if you've already got eleven years of it. I was twenty-two and I could throw a wall of air across fifty feet of hillside and I could not light a piece of metal in my own hand, and it took me four months, and she found the whole thing extremely funny."

"What was she like?"

He was quiet for long enough that Toren thought he had asked the wrong thing.

"She corrected people," Rook said. "All the time. Everybody. Not unkindly and never once for the pleasure of it — she just could not sit in a room and let a wrong thing stand, and she'd been like it since she was a child, and it had cost her. She talked with her hands and then she'd notice she was doing it and put them under the table. She was frightened of deep water and would not say so. She ate the crust first." He stopped, and then went on in exactly the same voice. "She was about as tall as Cole. She had a scar on the back of her left hand from a stove when she was nine and she'd tell you the story if you asked and it wasn't a good story, and she'd tell it anyway.

"And she was the only person in that building who'd worked the whole thing out, and she'd worked it out years before I got there, and she never once got any credit for it, because she was a subject and not staff and the paper had other names on it."

"Worked what out?"

"That it goes both ways," said Rook. "Everybody at Homura had the picture. The picture says the two traditions light the same place. Everybody in that building agreed with that, out loud, at meals, for eleven years. And every last one of them still went out in the afternoon and practiced the one thing they'd been doing since they were thirteen, because agreeing with something is free." He looked at the Ember in his hands. "She was the first person I ever met who tried the other door. Just — tried it. Went and sat in a field for two years and tried it, because the picture said the door was in the same house and nobody had gone and looked."

"Did it work?"

"Eventually," Rook said. "For her and then for me and for nobody else there, and it took both of us the better part of four years, and every single person in that station could have done it and not one of them did, and I have thought about *that* more than I have thought about anything else in my life."

---

They sat for a while. The brick was cold through Toren's coat. Out past the triangle of raised ground the two banks went away in their two directions and neither of them looked like anything in the dark.

"Can I ask something that isn't about any of it?" Toren said.

"Ask."

"The bread."

Rook's hands went still on the Ember.

"You put a piece of the first one on a rock," Toren said. "Every night since the road. Milo asked you the first time and you said it wasn't for anything, and then you said *I know what I did*, and nobody's asked you since. And Milo does it now. He's been doing it for about ten days and I'm fairly sure he doesn't know he started."

"I know he does."

"Is it a thing you're supposed to do?"

"It's a thing *she* did," said Rook.

He said it perfectly evenly, and he did not look up from his hands, and Toren watched a piece of the last five weeks come apart and put itself back together in a completely different shape.

"Her clan's. Every meal, the first piece off, set aside, and you don't eat it and you don't throw it away and nobody explains it to you. I asked her once what it was for. She said her grandmother had never told her either and that she'd stopped asking at about eleven." Rook turned the Ember over. "I've done it every night for nine years. I couldn't tell you what it means. I'm not sure it means anything. I know what happens if I stop."

Toren sat in the cold with his arms round his knees.

"There's more of that," he said. "Isn't there. Things you do that we all think are just how you are."

"Most of it," said Rook.
