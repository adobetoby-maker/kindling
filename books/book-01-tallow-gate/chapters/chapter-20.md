# Chapter 20 — The Contract

Rook went up to the top field at first light and looked at the graves for about as long as it takes to fill a can, and came back down, and said they were leaving after breakfast.

"She's had one night," said Dessa.

"She's had one night."

"She could have two. He offered. He said—"

"I know what he offered." Rook was rolling the canvas. "Get her down the stair with Faust on the other side and don't let her do the last three steps herself; she'll try."

That was all he said about it. Toren, who had watched this man refuse a warden and burn a slate that would not burn, went and got the honey jar off the loft shelf and did not ask. He had learned about four weeks ago that there was a particular flatness the man used when a thing was already decided, and a different one he used when it wasn't. This was not the second one.

---

The company came down into Greenmill at nine in the morning.

There were three of them and they came along the north track out of the west with the light behind them, and the first thing Toren registered was that Pell Hoy was pleased to see them.

That was the thing he could not get past, afterward. The miller came off his step wiping his hands and went out to meet them in the middle of his own yard, and one of the women left the drying frames and went and stood where she could hear, and two of the children came round the end of the smoke-house at a run and then stopped and hung back at a distance that was not fear, exactly. It was the distance you keep from something big and useful. It was how Toren had stood near his father's forge bellows at six.

They were not frightening to look at. That was the second thing.

One was a heavy young man with a mattock over his shoulder and a bad sunburn across the back of his neck. One was a woman of about thirty with a coil of cord round her body from shoulder to hip and a canvas roll on her back, and she carried it the way Odo Prentice had carried his staff, which is to say as a part of herself she had stopped noticing.

The third was older. He was perhaps sixty, narrow, grey-stubbled, in a good coat that had been mended in five places by somebody who could sew, and he walked with his hands loose at his sides and his head slightly forward, and he looked at the mill and the frames and the barrow and the six strangers in the yard in one unhurried pass, the way you look at a room you have come into before.

"Flour," he said to Hoy. "Salt if you've salt. And we'll want the hay off your top field after all, if the offer's still in it."

"It's in it."

"Then that's settled and I'll not haggle you." He got a purse out. "Four days more on the cut and then it's shut, and you'll have your paper honored inside the week."

"Four days," said Hoy, and something came out of him all at once that he had clearly been holding for three weeks, and Toren looked away from it because it was private.

---

Rook was at the barrow.

He had the shafts up on his shoulders. He had put them there four minutes earlier because they had been about to leave, and now he was standing in the corner of the mill yard with two lengths of ash across his back and both hands up on them, entirely still, facing half away.

He did not put them down.

That was all it was. A man stood in a yard holding a cart he was not pulling, and did not set it down, and Toren Voss, thirteen years old, with a piece of bread in his hand that he had been about to eat, understood the whole of it before one single word had been said by anybody.

He did not look at the three people in the middle of the yard. He looked at Dessa.

Dessa had gone the color of the mill wall.

Her eyes were not on the company either. They were on Rook, and her mouth was slightly open, and Toren watched her hand come up and take hold of Wyck's sleeve without any part of her noticing she had done it, and Wyck — who had been watching the older man for some seconds by then — did not shake it off.

---

The older man bought his flour.

He paid it into Pell Hoy's hand in coin and in a small folded packet of something that Hoy put away fast. He asked after a man called Tully who had a boat, and was told where Tully was. He said something to the woman with the cord about the weather coming round, and she said something back that made the heavy young man laugh.

And then he turned to go, and his eyes went across the yard, and they went over the barrow and past it, and came back.

He stopped walking.

The mill race went on making the noise a mill race makes. Somewhere behind the houses a dog was going at something. The woman with the cord got four paces before she noticed she was alone and turned round.

Nobody in that yard said anything at all.

Rook stood with the shafts on his shoulders and his hands up on them and looked back, and his face had nothing on it, and nothing in his body moved, and Toren, eleven feet away, could feel his own heart going in his ears.

It lasted perhaps three seconds.

Then the older man's mouth did something at one corner. It was not a smile. It was the thing a man's mouth does when he has just put a weight down on a table to see how heavy it is.

He turned his head forward again and walked on out of the yard the way he had come, and the other two came after him, and the heavy young man looked back once and had no idea what he was looking at.

"Milo," said Rook, in an entirely ordinary voice. "Get the stove."

---

They were out of Greenmill in six minutes and going east along the water at a pace that had Wyck's jaw locked inside the first quarter mile.

Nobody asked. That was the part Toren thought about later — that four children and an old woman were walked out of the first friendly place they had seen in six weeks at a speed that nearly broke one of them, and not one of them opened their mouth, because all five of them had been in that yard.

At about a mile Rook took the barrow up off the bank into the field, where the ground was better, and slowed, and said:

"Right. Here's the whole of it."

---

"That man was at Homura nine years ago on the sixteenth of the eighth month in the afternoon. I don't know his name. I have known his face for nine years and I'd know it in a fire.

"He knows mine. He's known it since about half past three that afternoon, and he's had five years of being told by people at weighings and in yards that there's a man on the road asking after his company, and now he's stood in a yard and looked at me, and he is going to walk up that valley and tell the man who pays him.

"The man who pays him has nine people, a contract he's four days off finishing, an open tear at his back, and a paper with a farmer's name on it. He is not going to have a known enemy sitting between him and his road out. He's going to come and settle it while he knows where I am, and he's going to do it tonight or tomorrow, because tomorrow I could be anywhere."

"Then we go back," said Dessa. "Greenmill. There's forty people—"

"No."

"There's *forty people*, there's a mill, there's a wall on the—"

"There are eleven houses and three children who throw stones at a river," Rook said, "and those people hired that company with everything they had, and if I stand in the middle of Greenmill tonight then whatever happens, happens in Greenmill." He did not raise his voice at any point. "I did that once. I wasn't the one who brought it and it happened in my mother's kitchen anyway. We're not going back."

Nobody said anything.

"We're not going north either," he said. "I want that said out loud so none of you spends today wondering. Undern's four days up that line and there'll be thirty of them in it by frost, and I'm not going, and I wasn't going at the junction and I'm not going now. We're on the east road. We're going to stay on the east road, and the only thing that's changed is that the east road now has men on it behind us, and we're going to deal with them and then we're going to keep walking."

"Can we outrun them?" said Milo.

"With a barrow. No."

"Then—"

"Then I pick the ground," said Rook, "the way I always pick the ground, and I picked it an hour ago, and it's two miles ahead of us and it's called a weir."

---

The old weir came up in the middle of the afternoon.

It was a low concrete dam thrown across the whole width of the Reave, seventy or eighty feet of it, with the water going over the crest in a smooth brown sheet and coming down white into a pool below that was loud enough from two hundred paces to change how people talked. On the top of it, on the downstream edge, there was a walkway — a concrete curb about a yard wide with a handrail down one side that had been iron and was now a row of stubs.

On the north bank, where they were, there was the wreck of a winding-house and a stone chute going up the hill.

On the south bank there was a mill ruin, roofless, with a sluice-house beside it that still had most of a roof and a door.

And past the sluice-house the track went on east, up over the shoulder, toward the place where the Reave turned south.

"That's the crossing," Rook said. "It's the only one for eleven miles in either direction and it's why there's a mill on both ends of it and it's why the road's here. Everybody look at it properly, because from now on it's the thing all of you are standing on."

---

He put Senna in the sluice-house on the south bank with the barrow backed into the corner and the door on its hinge, and he put the second axe across her knees, and she took it and laid it flat and did not make one single remark, which frightened Toren more than anything that had happened in the yard that morning.

Then he got the four of them out on the south apron in the last of the light and taught them, for twenty minutes, as fast as he had ever taught them anything.

"Men aren't rift-things. Say it."

They said it.

"Now here's what that means and there's four parts and you're going to get all four tonight whether you can hold them or not.

"One. A rift-thing comes at the nearest worked power. A man comes at what he can see and what he thinks is going to hurt him, and those are two different things, and a clever man goes for the second one first. Nothing you do with a light will pull a man off a line he's chosen. It'll only tell him where you are.

"Two. A rift-thing has to close. That's the gap we've been living in for six weeks — looking is free and moving costs them time, and every drill I've put you through since the Gate is built on buying that time. A man does not have to close. A man can hurt you from where he is standing, and one of those nine can hurt you from further away than you have ever in your life been hurt from, and there is no answer to it. There's no guard. There's no light. There's ground, and there's being behind something, and that's the entire list.

"Three. A rift-thing does not change its mind. A man does. If the man in the brown coat decides at midnight that the arithmetic's gone wrong, he will turn round and go back up that valley and finish his contract and we'll never see him again, and that is a thing you can actually work toward and it is the only kind of winning available to us tonight.

"Four." He stopped for a moment. "The diamond is for rift-things. It was built for a thing that comes at the nearest light, and it puts the one who can't fight in the middle where the nearest light is furthest from the outside, and it has kept all four of you alive for six weeks and it is the best shape I know. Tonight it is the worst place any of you could stand. There is no middle against men. The middle is the open. Spread and get behind something and stay behind it."

"Say it back," said Dessa, before he could.

"Spread and get behind something," said Wyck.

"Spread," said Milo. "Behind something."

"Spread and behind something," said Toren.

---

Rook looked at the four of them in the last grey light with the weir going white behind them.

"You'll do it anyway," he said.

Milo said, "Do what?"

"The diamond. Something will happen and the three of you will go to your corners and you'll put him in the middle, because I have spent six weeks putting it into your hands, and it lives in your hands now and not in your heads, and that is exactly what I wanted and it is exactly wrong for tonight." He said it perfectly evenly. "I'm telling you first. I want that on the record, because I didn't tell you first at the pumphouse and a boy has walked with a stick ever since, and I have thought about that every day."

"Then why tell us," said Wyck, "if it won't help?"

"Because knowing it is worth nothing," Rook said, "and you can't start paying for it until you know it."

---

Milo went out at full dark.

It was his own idea in the sense that he was the only one who could have done it, and Rook's in the sense that Rook had worked out four hours earlier that it was the thing that would decide where the fight happened. A quarter mile up the north bank, on the top of the stone chute above the winding-house, where it could be seen from the whole length of the valley and from nowhere near the sluice-house on the south side: one small pale light, sitting on a stone, unattended.

"They'll come to it," Rook said. "They'll come to it because it's the only thing in eleven miles of dark that a man made, and by the time they've been up that chute and found nothing they'll be at the weir, and they'll be at the weir on the north end with sixty feet of concrete between them and us, which is what I want, and none of that happens without you."

"It's just a light," said Milo.

"It's the only piece of this I couldn't buy anywhere else. Go and put it down and come straight back and don't stop to look at it."

He went. He came back in nineteen minutes at a jog with his hands in his sleeves and his ears red with cold and said, "It's up," and sat down against the sluice-house wall in the middle of the south apron with his knees drawn up, exactly where six weeks of drill had taught him that the middle was the safe place, and no one moved him, because no one thought of it.

Toren had two feet of white light standing steady out of his father's Ember and had not noticed for some while that it was there.

Dessa was on the south end of the walkway with her back against the buttress and her boots braced and her eyes open on nothing. She had been holding for an hour. The weir made a noise that took her hearing away entirely, which was why Rook had put her there, and she had understood that before he said it.

At twenty minutes past midnight she said, in a flat and almost bored voice, into a dark that had nothing in it:

"Nine. Coming down the north bank in two groups. Four hundred feet."
