# Chapter 22 — Milo

Milo Faust died a little before four in the morning on the south apron of the old weir, with his head on a man's hand and his coat open and four people around him, about eight hours after he had taught a child in Greenmill to make a flat stone go across water instead of into it. He was thirteen years and six months old. He did not say anything at the end and he was not frightened, and Toren was holding his hand and would not have been able to say afterward which of them had taken hold first.

Then it was morning, and there were things to do.

---

Rook did them.

He got up off the wet ground at about half past four and put his coat on and went round the four of them one at a time, and he did not say a word to any of them that was not an instruction.

He sent Wyck into the sluice-house to sit down and get his boot off, and then he went in after him ten minutes later and looked at the leg and came out again. He got the stove lit himself, which he had not done since Milo took the cooking over on the grade, and he put water on it and he made all three of them drink some of it, standing over them, until it was gone.

He took Dessa by both wrists and turned her hands over and looked at them and said, "You did everything. There wasn't anything else to do and you did all of it," and she said, "I know," and then about four seconds later she said, "I don't," and he kept hold of her wrists and did not let go for a while.

He went and got Milo's blanket off the barrow and did not let anybody else do it.

And at some point in the middle of all of that he came and crouched down in front of Toren, who was sitting against the mill stone with his hands on his knees, and said, "Voss."

"Yes."

"You had it on the whole way through. From when they came up the tailwater to when the last of the rift-things went into the concrete. You had two feet of it and you held it while everything in the world was happening and you did not put it out one time." Rook's face had nothing on it. "That's Flame. I'm telling you now because I'd tell you today whatever had happened, and because if I leave it a week you'll always wonder whether I only said it to give you something."

Toren looked at him.

"All right," he said.

"That's all," said Rook, and got up.

Toren had wanted that word for six weeks. He had lain awake on the ballast and in the arch and in wet grass on the grade wanting it, and had asked *how long* four separate times and been told not to ask, and now it had arrived and it sat in him the way a stone sits in a pocket, with no weight on either side of it at all.

---

His grandmother came out of the sluice-house at six in the morning on her own feet.

Nobody helped her, because nobody dared. She came out through the door with one hand on the frame and the other on the wall and her splinted leg going out stiff in front of her at each step, and she got four paces onto the apron and stopped and stood there, eighty-one years old, and looked at the blanket.

Then she looked at the three of them.

"You're all standing wrong," she said.

Toren heard himself say, "What?"

"You're standing about like people waiting for somebody to tell them where to go. I've seen it forty times and I've done it twice myself and it doesn't get better while you're doing it." She took another step and put her hand out and Toren got under it without thinking. "Right. Cole. Is there ground up on that shoulder or is it all stone?"

"It's—" Dessa's voice went. She started again. "There's ground. Above the mill ruin, where the track goes up. It's field."

"Then that's where he goes, and somebody wants to go and look at it before we all walk up there, because if it's wet at the bottom I want to know now and not at the top of the hill." Senna Voss stood on the concrete with her hand on her grandson's shoulder. "That's the first one. There's about nine and they all want doing and none of them want thinking about, and I'll say them out one at a time, and every one of you is going to be glad of it by tonight and none of you is going to be glad of it now."

---

They buried him at noon in the field above the mill ruin with the Reave going below and the weir making the only noise there was.

Rook dug. He did not ask any of them to and he did not stop any of them when they took turns, and Wyck took a turn that went on far too long — standing on one leg with the other braced out behind him and the stick lying in the grass, working, for thirty-five minutes, with his face doing nothing at all — and nobody said anything about that either.

Dessa said the words. She had said them for her mother on a bank above a road on the fifth of the month and had got most of the way through, and she got most of the way through again, and Senna finished them from the ground where she was sitting with her leg out, and nobody had to ask her to.

Toren could not say anything at all. He had been building toward it since first light and when it came to it there was nothing in him, and he stood at the end of the turned earth with his hands at his sides and his mouth open and no sound, and Wyck Tanner put a hand between his shoulder blades and left it there until it was over.

---

Afterward he went back down to the sluice-house and got his father's Ember out.

It was two o'clock in the afternoon. The sluice-house had a stone lintel over the door, eight inches deep and dressed flat on the face, seventy years of weather and a green stain down one side of it.

He lit up and got two feet of white and put it into the stone.

It was harder than he had expected and it took a great deal longer, and the light went out four times and came back four times, and he had to stop twice because his hand would not close. Nobody came and looked. Dessa came as far as the corner of the ruin once, and stood there, and went away again.

At half past four he had it. The letters were an inch high and cut poorly, and they went a finger-joint deep into dressed stone, and they said:

MILO FAUST

He stood back and looked at them for a long time.

He had seen an inch-high knife-cut line on a slate in a burned wayhouse and he had watched a man take the board off the wall with no ceremony at all and carry it out face-in against his chest and put it in a fire, and he had sat across that fire and watched slate not burn.

There were three graves in a top field at Greenmill with nothing written on them. There were three more above a road eleven days east of Coldwater with names in a warden's ledger and nothing on the ground, because there had been a shovel and no chisel and nobody had thought of it.

This would be there in a hundred years.

"That's good," said Rook, from behind him.

Toren did not turn around.

"It's crooked."

"It's in stone," said Rook.

---

Nobody ate until dark and then somebody had to, and it was Toren who got up and did it, because he worked out at about seven in the evening that the reason nobody had eaten was that all four of them were waiting for a thirteen-year-old boy to hand them something in a particular order, and that he was not going to, and that somebody had to break that or it would still be true in a week.

He got the stone hot and mixed the meal and put the first flat cake down and turned it when it needed turning.

When it came off he broke it in two. Then he broke a piece off the corner of one half and put the piece on a flat stone at the edge of the coals, and set it aside.

Then he handed the rest round. Senna. Wyck, because of the leg. Whoever was on watch. Everybody else.

He did it the way you put the lid back on a crock.

Across the fire, Rook looked at the piece of bread on the rock.

He looked at it for about three seconds, and his face did not change, and then he looked up and found Toren looking straight back at him, and for the first time in six weeks the man was the one who could not hold it.

---

Toren went up onto the shoulder at ten at night where Wyck was sitting with his leg out and his stick across his knees, and Dessa came up about four minutes later without being sent for, and sat down on the other side.

"I'm going to tell you the whole of it," Toren said.

Neither of them said anything.

"I'm not asking either of you whether you want it. I've thought about that all day and I've decided I don't get to make it a present, because if I ask you then you have to say yes and then it's yours because you chose it, and I don't want to do that to anybody again." His voice was steady, which surprised him. "It's mine and I'm putting it down. You can do what you like with it."

"Toren," said Dessa.

"There was a research station four hundred miles north of here in a bowl of hills and it was called Homura."

---

It took until about three in the morning.

He told it in the order he had been given it, which he understood by about the middle to be the wrong order, and he could not fix it because it was the only order he had. He told them about the machines and the tube and the nine thousand pictures, and about a charter that bought a physician for a village for six years. He told them about a man in a kitchen saying that it was a shame a machine could not show the difference between a man's own power and a stick's, and about a woman at the next table putting her cup down.

He told them her name.

He told them about two years in a field, and four more, and a word that came out of a coastal clan with two hundred words nobody else had, which he did not repeat, and told them why he was not repeating it.

He told them about the sixteenth of the eighth month nine years ago at about half past three in the afternoon, and a valve that had been dripping for a fortnight, and an instrument room, and a corridor.

He told them about the grip on Rook's Ember, worn through in one place at the bottom where no part of a hand naturally goes.

He told them about a village two years later and a contract on a tear and a bad night in bad ground, and about four people, and about how it took another two years to find out it was the same company. He told them about a tally-board in a trading post with a date on it.

And then he told them about the bread, and that was the one that finished all three of them, and it took him three goes to get it out.

---

The sky had started to go grey at the bottom edge.

"He does it because it's hers," Toren said. "He's done it every night for nine years and he doesn't know what it means and neither did she and neither did her grandmother. And Milo picked it up off him in four days on the grade without ever once being told, and he did it every night for six weeks, and he never knew, and I could have told him in one sentence, and I didn't."

Nobody spoke for a while.

"He'd have been unbearable," said Wyck.

Toren made a sound that was not a laugh and was in the same family.

"He'd have been completely unbearable about it," Wyck said. "He'd have told every single one of us he was doing an ancient thing. He'd have brought it up at least twice a day for the rest of his life. He'd have done it at Meridian in front of strangers." He shifted the leg with both hands. "And it would have been true, and he'd have been right, and he'd have got to be right about something for once."

Dessa had her forehead on her knees.

"I'm glad you told us," she said, into them. "I want that said now, tonight, in case it stops being true in a fortnight. I'm glad you told us and I'd have wanted it and I'm not going to take one single part of it back off you later."

"Nor me," said Wyck Tanner.

He got his stick under him and stood up, the careful way, in two parts.

"You've been carrying that on your own for four days," he said, "and I told you at the junction it was worse than the not knowing, and I want you to know I wasn't guessing."
