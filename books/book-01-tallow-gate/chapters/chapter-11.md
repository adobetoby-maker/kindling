# Chapter 11 — What Was Taken

Toren's eye opened on the second day after the ballast, about a third of the way, which Milo found very funny and said so at intervals for six hours.

They came down off the high ground through a long shallow cutting where the rock walls sweated and the swallows went in and out of holes in them, and at the bottom of it the grade crossed an old road at a level place, and there was a building.

Rook put his hand up two hundred yards out.

"Cole."

Dessa stopped where she was, in the middle of the grade, and did not sit down and did not close her eyes. She stood with the wind pulling her coat and her face doing nothing at all for about fifteen seconds.

"Nobody," she said. "Not in it, not behind it, not in the trees. Nothing out to — " she turned her head slowly, left to right — "call it two hundred feet, and it's clean all the way out."

"Anything under two hundred that isn't people?"

"There's something small in the roof space. It's a bird."

And they went on, and nobody said anything about it, and Toren realized four steps later that a week ago that exchange would have been impossible and that not one of them had remarked on it, including Dessa.

---

It had been a wayhouse.

That was Senna who said the word, from the cart, sitting up on one elbow with her hand shading her eyes. It was a long stone building set into the bank with its back to the grade and its face to the road, one storey, a slate roof, a well-housing at the near end and a yard of packed earth in front, and it had burned.

Not recently. The fire had gone through the roof at the east end and taken the rafters there and stopped, or been stopped, halfway along, and the rain had been at it since. There was grass growing in the char. There was a dock leaf a foot high standing up out of the black where the doorframe had fallen.

"Four months," Rook said. "Maybe five."

The door was open.

It was the only thing that Toren registered as strange, and he registered it without knowing why, standing in the yard with the wind going through the empty window holes. The door was off one hinge and hanging, and it was open, and it had been opened. There was no break in it. Nobody had gone through that door who was not let through it or who did not have all the time in the world.

Rook stood in the yard for a while and then went in.

---

There were three graves at the west end of the yard, past the well.

They were proper graves. That was the thing Toren could not get past, and he stood looking at them for a long time with the wind in his face. Somebody had dug them to a decent depth in hard ground. Somebody had squared the corners. Somebody had gone to the trouble of setting a flat stone at the head of each one and standing it upright and packing it with smaller stones so it would not fall over in a winter, and had then not written anything on any of them.

Milo came and stood beside him and did not say anything for once.

"Who does that," Toren said.

"Somebody with a shovel," said Milo.

"Somebody with *time*."

---

Inside, the wayhouse had been a trading post.

You could see the whole of what it had been from the doorway even with the east end open to the sky. There was a long counter of two-inch plank with a hatch in it. There was a stove on a stone pad with its pipe gone. There was a sleeping loft over the west end, part of it fallen. There was a sack of meal on the floor behind the counter, split, four months gone green and sprouted, and a rack of dried fish on a wire, and a stone crock of salt with the lid still on it, and a tin lamp, and a coat on a peg.

And along the back wall there was a rack, floor to shoulder height, built out of heavy timber in a double row of cradles.

The cradles were shaped. They were cut and lined and padded, every one of them the same, for something round and heavy about the size of a small barrel.

They were empty.

Rook went down the length of the rack with two fingers trailing along the timber and did not touch anything else in the building.

"They didn't take the food," Dessa said.

"No."

"There's salt. There's a *coat*." She was standing in the middle of the floor with her arms wrapped round her ribs. "Anybody who — if it was people on the road, if it was anybody desperate, they'd have taken the salt before they took anything. My mother used to say salt's the first thing that walks."

"It is," said Rook.

"Then—"

"Then it wasn't anybody desperate."

---

The tally-board was by the door, on the wall that had not burned.

It was a slab of slate the size of a cart's tailgate in a wooden frame, ruled off in columns with a scribe, and it was the sort of thing Toren had seen at the mill in Coldwater: a board where whoever came in wrote what they brought and what they took, so that at the end of a season a man with a ledger could settle it all up.

Most of it was chalk, and most of the chalk had gone with four months of weather coming in the open door. There were ghost-lines of it, half-words, a column of dates in a small clear hand.

The last line was not chalk.

Somebody had cut it into the slate with a knife point, slowly, pressing hard, in letters an inch high:

*CINDER CO — 4 csk — PAID OUT*

Dessa read it aloud because she was standing closest, and she read it the way you read something before you have understood it, flatly, and then she stopped with her mouth still open.

Nobody moved in the burnt room.

"Read me the dates," Rook said.

"What?"

"The column. On the left. Read me the last date you can still make out."

Dessa leaned in and put her face close to the slate. "It's — fourth month. Fourth month, something. Eleven, maybe. Or seventeen, the top's gone."

"The one above it."

"Fourth month, ninth."

"And above that."

"Third month, twenty-eighth."

Rook stood looking at the board.

Toren was three feet away and watching him, and this was not the sluice. At the sluice the man's hands had gone and his breathing had stopped and everything about him had changed while his face did nothing. Here his hands were perfectly still. His breathing was ordinary. He read the board the way a man reads a notice about a bridge closure, top to bottom, twice.

And then he said, "Four months," in a voice with nothing in it whatsoever, and Toren understood, with a small cold shock, that the flatness was the tell. That the man was doing arithmetic. That the arithmetic had come out to a number he did not like, and that the number was *how far away*.

"Who's Cinder Company?" said Milo.

"A hunting company."

"What's that mean?"

"It means men who kill rift-things for money," Rook said. "There are forty of them between here and the coast. Most of them are four brothers and a wagon. Some of them are bigger than that."

"Is that a big one?"

"Yes."

Milo, who asked everything, and who had asked a man on a road how his own mule had died and taken the answer without flinching, said, "You know them."

Rook took the tally-board off the wall.

He did it with no ceremony at all. He got a thumb behind the frame at the top corner and worked it, and the whole thing came off the stone with a crack and a fall of old plaster, and he turned it face-in against his chest and carried it out into the yard, and over his shoulder he said:

"Get the water on."

---

They camped in the yard because the well was good and the wind was less.

Wyck sat with his back against the well-housing and his leg out and watched the door of the wayhouse for a long time, and when Toren came to sit near him he said, without any preamble at all:

"He wasn't surprised."

"He was—"

"He read a thing off a wall that said a company came through here and took four casks and nobody's alive to say what happened, and there are three graves out there with nothing written on them, and he read the *dates*." Wyck turned his head. His eyes in the firelight were completely steady. "You know what you ask for when you ask for a date? You ask for a date when you already know the who and the what and the only thing left you want is the when."

Toren did not have anything to say to that.

"He picked this grade," Wyck said. "There's no road on it. There's no town on it. He walked off the loop road at a place with no turning, which means he knew it was there, which means he's been on it." He shifted the leg with both hands. "He told you lot we're not going east. Did he ever once say what we *are* going toward?"

---

Milo cooked. He had taken the cooking over somewhere in the last four days without anybody deciding it, and he was better at it than he had any business being, and he had gotten into the habit of handing things round in a particular order — Senna, then Wyck because of the leg, then whoever was on watch, then everybody else.

Toren sat by the fire with his cut eye and his bad hand and ate, and did not notice for some time that the tally-board was not leaning against the cart where Rook had set it down.

He looked for it. It took him a while to find, because he was not looking in the right place.

The fire had a piece of wood in it that was not theirs. It was a length of frame-timber, painted, and the paint was going up in a green flame along one edge, and under the timber there was a flat dark shape in the coals that was not burning because slate does not burn, and the letters cut into it an inch high were still there and would still be there in a hundred years.

Toren looked across the fire.

Rook was sitting on the far side of it with his forearms on his knees, doing nothing at all, watching the slate not burn.

---

Dessa came and sat down beside Toren much later, when the others were asleep and Wyck was on the watch at the top of the yard.

"I've been thinking about the graves," she said.

"Don't."

"No, listen. I've been thinking about them the wrong way round all evening and I've only just got it." She had her chin on her knees. "It's not that somebody was kind. That's what I kept — I kept thinking, whoever did that was at least a bit *decent*, and it made me feel better, and then about an hour ago I worked out why it doesn't."

"Why doesn't it."

"Because whoever dug those had all afternoon," Dessa said. "Nobody was coming. They weren't hiding and they weren't hurrying and they weren't frightened of anything on this road at all. They took four casks and they wrote it on the board and then they buried three people properly and went to bed."

The fire popped. Out at the top of the yard Wyck shifted his weight off the bad leg, and went still again.

"Toren," Dessa said. "When you found that thing in the sluice — when he found it — what did he say to you?"

"He said sew your pocket."

"That's all?"

"That's all he ever says."

Dessa Cole looked across the fire at the dark shape of a man asleep with his back against a cart wheel, or not asleep, and put her chin back on her knees.

"He's not running away from anything," she said. "I want you to notice that with me, because I don't think I can hold it on my own. He's not running away from anything. He walked us off a road and onto a line that goes somewhere and he's the only one who knows where, and this afternoon he stood in a burned house and found out that something is four months ahead of us."

"He might have wanted it to be behind us."

"He might," said Dessa. "That's exactly the part I don't like."
