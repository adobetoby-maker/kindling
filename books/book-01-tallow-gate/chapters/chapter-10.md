# Chapter 10 — Ballast

The grade climbed for two days after the rain, so gradually that you could only tell by looking back, and on the third morning it came out on a shoulder of open hill where the wind never stopped and somebody had once laid four miles of dead-straight track across the top of the world.

Up here, the grass had never taken. The ballast lay bare from edge to edge — a wide stone bed of fist-sized grey rock, every piece of it broken and angular, laid down by hand seventy years before anyone in the party had been born.

Rook walked out onto it, turned around, and said, "This'll do."

"For what?" said Milo.

"For the part you're all going to remember."

---

He took the cart off the grade and put it on the grass with Senna in it and the wheel chocked, and he put Dessa on the tailboard with her back against the boards.

"You're not drilling," he said. "You're holding it, and you're holding it on me and on whichever of them is in front of me, and if I hurt one of them worse than I meant to, you say so before he does. He won't say so. That's the whole reason you're up there."

"What am I looking for?"

"You'll know." He was shrugging his coat off. "You've had your hands inside people. If something's wrong inside a person you'll feel it and you won't know how you know. Don't try to know how. Just say it."

Dessa sat up straighter on the tailboard and did not say anything, and Toren, watching her, saw that she had just been handed something and that it was the first thing anybody had handed her since her mother died.

"Tanner. You're first."

---

The drill was this, and Rook explained it once.

"You stand there. You light up and you hold a full Edge. I come at you." He had a length of ash out of the cart, six feet of it, the shaft he had cut for a cart-pole and not used. "I'm not using mine. I'm using this, and I'm using the middle of it, and I'm not going to open you up. You're going to get hit and it's going to hurt and you're going to have marks tomorrow. What I want to know is whether the light stays on."

"That's it?"

"That's it. And it isn't, and you'll find out which in about four seconds."

Wyck lit up. Three clean feet of white, steady as a lamp.

Rook came at him.

He did not use the light around his legs, not for Wyck, and it still took less than a second to cross fourteen feet and Toren's whole body jumped. The ash shaft came around low and Wyck got his arm down and took it across the forearm and did not move his feet, and the Edge did not so much as flicker.

Rook stepped back.

"Again."

He came again, high, and Wyck took it on the shoulder, and the light held. He came at the bad leg and Wyck pivoted off it onto the good one in a way that cost him something visible, and the light held. He came three more times fast, one after another with no gap, and the light held through every one of them, and on the last one Wyck got the Edge up and turned the shaft aside with it, which nobody had asked him to do.

"Stop," Rook said.

He stood in the middle of the ballast with the shaft down and looked at Wyck Tanner for a while.

"That's as good as I've seen from somebody your age," he said. "I'm saying that in front of all of them on purpose. Two years on a fence post with nobody watching and no reason, and here's what it bought you: I can hit you and nothing happens. Nothing at all. You don't even go pale."

Wyck's chin came up about half an inch.

"Now make it four feet."

"What?"

"Four feet of Edge. Not three. Make it longer."

Wyck's jaw set. The white light out of his fist wavered and went out and came back, three feet again, and stood there.

"Longer."

He tried. Toren could see the whole of it — the shoulders coming up, the breath going in and locking, the entire two years of a boy making himself do a boring thing every day arriving at once and being applied, hard, to a thing that did not care.

Three feet.

"Longer."

"I'm *trying*—"

"I know. Keep going."

It took about four minutes. At the end of it Wyck Tanner threw his Ember down on the ballast — actually threw it, hard, so that it skipped twice and rang — and stood with his hands in his hair and his back to everybody.

Rook let him have it. He did not say a word. He picked the Ember up off the stones, checked it the way you check a tool somebody's dropped, and set it on the cart's tailboard where Wyck would have to walk past Dessa to get it, and said, "Voss."

---

Toren got hit eleven times before anything happened in his head at all.

The first one took him across the ribs and he did not even light up before it landed. The second one he was lit for and the light went out in the instant between seeing the shaft move and feeling it, so completely and so far in advance that he understood with a sick lurch that the light had not been knocked out of him. He had put it out. He had put it out himself, a full quarter-second before the shaft arrived, the way you close your eyes before a thing hits you.

"Again."

He went loose. He rolled the shaft in his fingers and thought *please* and got five inches, and Rook came at him and the five inches went out and the ash caught him low and put him down on the ballast on his hip and his palm.

Bare broken stone. It took the skin off his hand in one pass.

"Get up."

"I'm—"

"You're up or you're done, and you're not done." Rook backed off four paces. "Again."

He went again and got nothing at all. He went again and got four inches and lost them. He tried the other thing, the thing he had been carrying since the arch, the thing he had believed completely for three days and had not once been able to use: *it's mine, it's mine, it was always mine, come here* —

— and the Ember gave him nothing whatsoever, and Rook's shaft came round and Toren flinched so hard he fell over without being touched.

Rook stopped.

"Say what you just did," he said.

"I told it to come."

"You demanded."

"You said it was *mine*—"

"It is yours. Demanding is still asking somebody." Rook crouched down on his heels in front of him, on the ballast, at eye level. "Listen to me. You've got two settings and both of them are the same setting. Yesterday you begged a stick. Just now you ordered a stick around. It's still you standing in a road talking to something outside yourself, and it doesn't matter how polite you are about it, because there's nobody out there to talk to. There has never been anybody out there. You're alone in it and that's the good news and you keep hearing it as the bad news."

Toren's eyes were streaming and it was mostly the wind.

"Then what do I do."

"You were doing it in the arch," Rook said, "when you told me about the room."

He stood up and backed off four paces.

"Again."

---

Here is what Toren worked out, lying on his back on broken stone on the top of a hill with blood coming into his eye from somewhere above it.

He had understood the room for three days. He had thought about the room constantly — in the traces of the cart, at the water, lying awake — and every single one of those times he had thought about the room from somewhere else. He had stood outside it and considered it. He had admired it. He had told himself, over and over, with enormous sincerity, that there was a room at the back of him and it was his.

He had not gone in.

And the reason he had not gone in, the real reason, the one that arrived all at once and unwelcome, was that the room had opened on the night his father knelt down in the dirt in front of him. The last time he had been all the way inside it, there had been two people alive who were dead now, and there was some part of him that had been very carefully not going back in there since the fifth of the month.

"Again," Rook said.

Toren got up.

He did not go loose. He did not reach. He did not ask anything of anybody. He stood on the ballast with his ruined hand at his side and the wind going over the top of the hill and he went back, all the way to the back of the house, and stood in the middle of the enormous quiet room and made himself stay there.

The Ember lit.

It came up out of the end of the shaft in one clean stroke and it did not stop at four inches or five inches or a foot. Fourteen inches of hot white, steady, no wavering at all, and it was still climbing when Rook came at him.

And Toren saw the shaft move, and the room went away, and the light went out, and the ash took him across the face above the left eye and he went down on the stones and did not get up for a while.

---

"Cole?"

"He's all right." Dessa's voice, from a long way off, and then much closer, and then her hands on his jaw turning his head. "It's the eyebrow. It's just the eyebrow, they bleed like that. Nothing's broken. His hand's bad."

"How bad."

"Skinned. Not deep. He's jammed two fingers."

"Fix the eyebrow. Leave the hand."

"Why leave the—"

"Because he needs to look at the hand for four days," Rook said, "and he doesn't need to look at his own face."

---

Dessa closed the eyebrow in about three minutes, sitting on the ballast with her thumbs either side of the cut and her tongue between her teeth.

She had done it before. Toren had watched her do it once in Coldwater, on a boy who had gone through a window, and it had taken her most of an afternoon and she had gone and lain down in the dark for four hours afterward and Ilsa had made everybody be quiet.

She finished, and sat back, and looked at her own hands.

"Huh," she said.

"You all right?"

"I'm fine." She flexed her fingers. "That's — I'm *fine*. That's the thing. I'd normally be — "

She did not finish it. She got up and went and sat back on the tailboard and picked up the holding again, and about ten minutes later Toren saw her look at her own hands a second time.

---

"Sit down," Rook told him, "and tell me what happened on the last one."

"I got fourteen inches."

"You got about sixteen and it was still going up when I moved. What happened when I moved?"

"I left."

Rook's head came round.

"Say that again."

"I left the room." Toren had his ruined hand cradled in the other one and his voice was thick and his left eye was closing. "The whole time I was standing in it, it was fine. It was — it wasn't even hard. And then you moved and I went out to look at you and there wasn't anybody in the room anymore and the light went out because there wasn't anybody in the room."

Rook sat down on the ballast beside him, which he had not done before.

"That's it," he said. "That's the whole of it and it took you eleven hits, and I was told it in words by somebody patient and it took me most of a year. You're not slow. Nobody's ever said you were."

"But I still can't—"

"You can't do it while anything is happening. That's right. That's the actual problem and now you know its actual shape, which you did not know this morning, and which is worth every mark you're going to have tomorrow." He was looking out along the ballast. "Here's what you did four times today and didn't notice. First time I came at you, you put the light out a quarter second before the stick landed. Last time, the light went out when I *moved*. That's about a second later in the same event. You didn't get stronger. You stopped leaving quite so early."

"That's not—"

"It's the whole thing," Rook said. "That's the only thing that's ever going to change. Everything else is arithmetic on top of it."

Toren sat on the stones with the wind going over and thought about that.

"How long," he said.

"Don't ask that."

"How long?"

"Months," said Rook. "And it won't be a day you can point at. It'll be some morning when you notice you haven't been leaving for a while."

---

Milo got his light an inch off his palm at four in the afternoon.

It lasted about a second and a half. It came up out of his hand the way it always did and then sat there in the air an inch above his skin, small and pale and completely unsupported, and Milo Faust made a noise like a man stepping into cold water and it went out.

"Did you see that," he said. "Did you — did anybody—"

"I saw it," said Wyck, from the grass.

He had been sitting there for an hour with his Ember in his hand, not lit. He said it flatly, without turning around, and it was the only thing he had said to any of them since he threw the thing on the stones, and Milo stopped mid-sentence and said, "Oh. Thanks," in an entirely different voice.

---

They came off the ballast at dusk and made camp in the lee of the bank.

Toren could not use his right hand for anything. He sat by the fire and let Milo cut his food for him and did not have the energy to be embarrassed about it, and his eye closed completely somewhere around nine at night and stayed closed for two days.

Before he lay down, he went out past the cart to where Rook was doing something to the axle by feel in the dark.

"Can I ask one thing?"

"You can ask."

"When it worked. At the end. It was going up and it hadn't stopped." Toren's voice came out very carefully controlled. "How far was it going to go?"

Rook did not look up from the axle.

"I don't know," he said.

"You must—"

"I don't know, Voss. That's the honest answer and you can have it." He wiped his hands on the grass. "Go to bed."
