# Chapter 3 — What It Cost

There were two places to be and Toren could only be in one of them.

That was the whole shape of the next four minutes and he understood it immediately, standing with his back against the tailboard and his grandmother's fingers dug into his shoulder through the slats. Up the hill there was Wyck, alone in open grass with a light in his fist. Down here there was the cart, and the cart had one wheel a hand's width from the shoulder of the road, and below the shoulder there was forty feet of drop into thornbrush, and inside the cart there was Senna Voss, who weighed less than a sack of grain and could not stand up.

"Garrick!" Ilsa Cole was already climbing. "*Garrick, the boy—*"

"I know."

"Then go—"

"I can't leave this cart." Toren's father had the splitting axe in both hands and blood coming out of his hairline into his eye and he did not look up the hill even once. "Ilsa. I can't leave this cart."

She went without another word.

Toren would remember that his whole life: that she did not argue, and did not make him say it twice, and did not look back at her own daughter. She went up a forty-degree grass bank in her mill apron with a bill-hook in one hand, at fifty-three years old, because somebody had to and the somebody was her.

She got most of the way.

Dessa was screaming from the upper slope. Milo had both arms around her waist and his heels dug in and the little useless light still burning in one hand because he had forgotten to put it out. And Ilsa reached Wyck and did the only correct thing, the thing Toren had been screaming at him to do for thirty seconds: she hit the boy's wrist and knocked the Ember out of his hand and it went dark in the grass, and the whole hillside's worth of turned heads lost the thing they had been turning toward.

They kept coming anyway. They were already coming. Momentum is not attention.

Toren looked away.

He did not decide to. His body did it, turned his head hard down and to the left, and by the time he could make himself look back there was warm grey ash coming down through the grass in a slow drift and Wyck Tanner was on his knees in it with both hands pressed flat to the ground.

---

"Toren." His father's voice, absolutely level. "The wheel."

"Dad—"

"*The wheel.* Get the stone under it. Now, son."

There was a wedge stone on a rope hanging off the cart's side rail for exactly this, for hills, and Toren's hands were shaking so badly it took him three tries to get it free and two more to work it into the gap in front of the tire. The near wheel was over the edge as far as its rim. Every time Patience shifted, the whole cart leaned another finger's width and the gravel under the wheel poured away down the slope in a hissing runnel, and there was a point coming — Toren could see it as clearly as he could see his own hands — where the cart would stop being a cart on a road and become a weight on a hill.

"Cut the mule loose," Senna said from inside.

"What?"

"*Cut the mule loose, Garrick, she'll take the whole thing over—*"

"Not yet."

Three of them came up the roadbed at once.

Toren's father stepped off the cart step into the road and met them with a splitting axe, and Toren, crouched at the wheel with a stone in both hands, finally had the thought he had been carrying since the top of the hour.

*He gave me the Ember last night.*

Garrick Voss had been an Ember-user for twenty-six years. He had held three feet of light on the wall at Coldwater and Toren had watched him do it. And last night at a fire under a broken overpass he had put the thing into his son's hands, because that was what you did on the day, because he had thought there were eleven quiet days of road left, and this morning he had walked out onto it with a tool for cutting firewood.

The axe was not nothing. That was the thing. Toren's father was a big man who had swung that axe every winter of his adult life and he knew exactly what the head weighed and exactly where it wanted to go, and he took the first one across the throat-joint and it came apart in ash, and he stepped through the drift of it and took the second one low, in the fold of a limb, and it went down and did not get up.

The third one put him against the cart.

Toren heard his father's back hit the boards. He came up off the wheel with the Ember lit and loose in his fingers, thinking *please, please* as hard as he could think anything, and got four inches of white light, and drove it into the thing's flank from behind, and it went to ash, and Garrick got an arm around him and put him bodily behind the wheel.

"That's good," his father said. He was breathing wrong. "That's good, that's — where did you learn that?"

"I didn't."

"That's good," Garrick said again.

And then the big one came back for the tailboard.

---

Toren never got a clean look at what happened in the next ten seconds, because for six of them he was under the cart.

He knew this much. The big one — the first one, the one with a forelimb missing at the second joint, which had been sitting eight feet down the road for two full minutes doing nothing at all — came in low and fast at the cart's rear and took the tailboard off its pins. He knew that his father got between it and the gap and shoved, with the whole of himself, with his boots skidding, and that for a moment it was just a large man pushing against a large animal on loose gravel, ordinary and physical and almost stupid, the way the biggest moments turn out to be.

He knew that the cart's near wheel came off the road.

He knew the sound the cart made going over the shoulder, and the sound his grandmother did not make, and the long tearing crash of it going down into thorn, and Patience's scream as the traces took her sideways off the edge after it.

And he knew that he came out from under the axle into open road with no cart to stand behind and nothing in his hand but a rod that gave him four inches of light when it felt like it, and that there were eleven of them on the road with him, and that his father was down on one knee in the gravel with one hand flat on the ground and the other arm hanging.

The eleven of them turned toward the lit thing in Toren's fist.

*It's the light. It's always been the light. It's not us, it's the—*

Something came down the hillside.

---

Afterward, when Toren tried to put it in order, he could never get it to sit still. He would have it clear for a second and then it would slide, the way the pattern on the disk slid when he looked away and back.

There was a man on the bank above the road. He was there and then he was not on the bank, he was forty feet down the slope, and forty feet is not a distance a person crosses without steps in between. There was light around his legs — not on them, *around* them, a bright bracing frame that ran from hip to heel and put a hard blue-white edge on every stone he touched. He hit the roadbed on the outside of one boot, and the gravel went out from under him the way it had gone out from under Toren, and he did not fall. He turned the slide into the turn.

He had an Ember in his right hand. Toren saw him roll it once against his palm, thumb over the grip, a small unhurried motion like a man checking the time — and then it lit, and it kept going, and it did not stop at three feet.

It came out as a spear. Not a blade: a spear, seven feet of it, tapering to a point so thin the far end was hard to look at.

He put it through the big one from the side without slowing down.

The thing came apart in a wash of ash that went up over the man and past him, and he was already moving, and the frame of light was still burning around his legs.

Toren's mouth was open. He could not close it.

*You can't do that.*

He knew four Ember-users. He had known them all his life. He had listened to every one of them say the same sentence in the same tone — *one at a time, you pick and you live with it* — and he had said it back to his father twelve hours ago, kneeling in the dirt, and he had been told he had it right.

The man on the road had both.

He went down the length of the roadbed and he did not fight in exchanges. That was the other thing Toren could not make fit. Every fight he had ever seen — Wyck on his fence post, his father on the wall — had been a thing you did in beats: set, swing, recover, set. This man never set. He came out of a long low glide with the spear already dropping to half its length for the close work, and the shortening was the strike, and then it was two short blades, one in each hand, and the second hand had been empty a moment before and Toren had no idea where the second blade had come from or what it was made of.

Four of them went to ash in the time it took Toren to draw two breaths.

The rest broke off the road and went for the bank, for the high grass, for the three children standing in it — and the man stopped dead in the middle of the road, turned his shoulders uphill, and put his left hand out.

Nothing came out of his hand. That was what Toren's eye insisted on, and his eye was wrong. The *air* changed. Fifty feet of hillside went hard and thick and wrong, the grass all leaning at once as if a wind had come from directly above it, and the things on the slope stopped as though they had run into a wall that had always been there.

It lasted about three seconds.

In the third second the man's arm began to shake.

Then the pressure let go, and the grass came back up, and the man was already crossing the distance with that bracing light around his legs, and it was finished on the hillside in a way Toren mostly did not watch, because he had finally turned around and seen his father in the road.

---

Garrick Voss was sitting with his back against the cart's broken step, in the place where the cart had been.

"There you are," he said.

Toren got down in the gravel.

"Don't." His father's good hand came up and caught him by the front of the coat before he could get at the wound, and it was strong, and that was the worst thing, that the hand was still strong. "Don't. Listen. Your grandmother's in the scrub."

"Dad—"

"She's in the scrub and she's alive, I heard her, and you're going to go and get her out. That's the job." Garrick's eyes came around and found him, and focused, with visible effort. "Where's your Ember."

"I've got it."

"Show me."

Toren showed him. His father looked at it — the family Ember, glass-smooth in a band around the middle where four generations of thumbs had sat, twelve hours in the fifth generation's hand — and something went out of his face that Toren did not understand until much later, and then he nodded once.

"Toren. The other thing. The disk."

"I've got that too."

"River," his father said.

"Dad—"

"*River.*" The hand tightened in his coat and then, over about two seconds, did not. "If it comes to it. That's all. That's all I wanted."

There was more that Toren said. He could never afterward remember any of it, and he was fairly certain none of it was answered.

---

The man came down the road.

He stopped eight feet away, which was, Toren would understand eventually, a deliberate distance. He put the Ember away inside his coat without looking at it. Both hands were shaking and he shoved them in his pockets to stop it.

He was not old. He was somewhere in the country between thirty and fifty where road-worn men stop giving a number. He had a flat-planed, unremarkable face gone rough with a week of not shaving, dark hair cut short by someone in a hurry, and a long grey coat with the elbows gone through and re-sewn in a different grey. There was a leather wrap on the grip of the Ember he'd put away, worn through in one place near the base, showing metal.

He looked at Garrick Voss for a while. He did not say anything about him.

"How many of you," he said.

Toren couldn't make words.

"Boy. How many of you were there."

"Nine," Toren said. "Eight. Nine with — the guide. Odo."

The man's eyes went up the hill, where Dessa was coming down through the grass with Milo behind her and Wyck behind Milo, all three of them moving like people underwater. He counted them, watching them come down through the grass, and then stopped.

"That's four of you," the man said.

"My grandmother's in the scrub. She's alive."

Something went across the man's face, fast, and was gone. He did not look pleased about it.

"Any grown," he said. "Any grown at all. An uncle. Anyone with a claim on you."

Dessa reached the road. She stood at the edge of it with her arms wrapped around her own ribs and her mother's ash in the weave of her coat and she said, in a voice that was almost normal, "No."

The man stood in the middle of the road with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders down and looked at the four of them, one at a time, unhurried, the way a man looks at a load he has just been told is his.

"No," he said, to nobody, and it did not sound like an answer to her.

He turned and went down the shoulder into the thornbrush after the cart without another word, and eleven minutes later he came back up carrying Senna Voss in both arms, and her leg was broken in two places and there was ash all through her white hair, and she was awake, and she was looking at him with a very strange expression.

"What's your name," she said.

"Rook."

"That's not a name."

"It's what I'm called," the man said, and set her down in the road as gently as if she were something he had been asked to look after, and did not say anything else for a long time.
