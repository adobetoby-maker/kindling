# Chapter 7 — The Grade

Warden Braid came out to the tank tunnel at first light to weigh Rook's ash, and she did it herself, on her own scale, in front of everybody, which Toren understood later was the point.

She had the balance set up on a barrel head just inside the doors, out of the wind. Rook set the jars on the table one after another, and she unstoppered each one and looked into it before she weighed it, tipping the mouth toward the lamp so the light could get down into the grey, and the grey moved.

"Flask," she said. "Flask. Flask. That one's short."

"That one's a Handful and a bit of a second one I didn't get all of."

"Then it's a Handful and I'm writing a Handful, and you can be annoyed about it." She made a mark. "How many?"

"Eight in the shed. I drew five."

"Where'd the other three go?"

"Into the floor of a flooded pump house," said Rook, "at a rate I couldn't keep up with, while I had both hands inside a boy's leg."

Braid grunted and went on weighing.

Toren stood against the curve of the tunnel wall with his hands in his pockets and listened to two adults trade in a currency he had known about his whole life and never once seen anybody actually use. In Coldwater the ash came in on somebody else's cart twice a year, in sealed grey jars that went straight to the smith and the wound-woman, and nobody ever said where it had come from beyond *the hunters*. He had assumed, in the vague way you assume things you are not required to think about, that the hunters had some method.

The method, it turned out, was a man putting his palm flat on the ground in the dark and drawing it up before it got away.

"Four and a Handful," Braid said. "I'll give you the barrow, the canvas, meal for eleven days, and the rest in salt. Don't tell me that's low. It is low, and it's low because I've got exactly one buyer for ash within sixty miles and she's standing here, and I'd rather say that out loud than have you find it out in Meridian and think I cheated you."

"Take the salt back and give me a second axe."

"Done."

---

The barrow was a two-wheeled hauling cart with iron-shod rims and a pair of long shafts, the sort of thing a man stands between and pulls, and it had come off the quarry line and was twenty years old and had been kept.

They put Senna in it on a folded canvas with her splinted leg straight out along the boards. They put the bundles in around her. They put Wyck in it too, and that took some doing.

"I can walk."

"You can walk in three weeks if you're an idiot," Rook said, not looking up from the strap he was working, "and five if you're not. It's been four days. Get in the cart."

"I'll slow us down. I'll walk at the back and I'll—"

"Tanner." Rook pulled the strap tight. "There is one road out of here that your leg is going to survive, and it's a graded one, and I'm not putting you on it with a stick out of pride for something you did fine. Get in the cart. You'll walk this afternoon when the ground's good and you'll be in it again by dark and that's how it's going to go for a while."

Wyck got in the cart.

He sat with his back against the rear board and his bad leg out and his face doing nothing at all, and Senna Voss, from the other end, with her own leg out in exactly the same attitude, looked him over for about ten seconds.

"You're going to hate this," she said.

"I don't hate it."

"You're going to hate it by noon and you're going to be very polite about hating it, which is worse." She resettled her shoulders against the canvas. "I've been in a box since the fifth of the month. You'll want to pace yourself."

Wyck did not answer that, but Toren, taking up the near shaft to help push the thing out through eighty feet of fallen fuel tank, saw the boy's mouth move very slightly at one corner, once, and then stop.

---

Warden Ottoline Braid walked them to the outside of the doors and no further.

"The page is closed," she said. "I want you to hear the exact shape of that. It's closed; it isn't torn out. If any one of you comes back through this gate in a year, or five, and wants it opened, I'll open it and I'll honor what's on it. That's not me being kind. That's me telling you the terms don't expire, so that none of you spends a winter somewhere worse thinking you burned your bridge here at thirteen."

"Not all of us," said Milo. "Wyck's fifteen."

"Congratulations to him too, then." She put her glasses up into her hair. "Go on, then. You're losing the light you haven't got yet."

---

They went west along the loop road for four hours and then Rook turned off it.

There was no turning. That was the thing Toren kept looking back at: no fork, no marker, not so much as a rut. Rook simply walked off the crown of the road at a place that looked like every other place, down through the wet grass of the shoulder, and out across a flat of thistle and old broken concrete toward a long low bank running away north-west, and after a moment the rest of them followed him because there was nothing else to do.

The bank was a railway.

It took Toren most of a mile to be sure, because there were no rails and no ties and hardly any stone left proud of the grass, and because he had never seen one. But the shape of it was unarguable once you were up on top: a raised bed thirty feet wide running dead straight across country, holding its level where the land fell away and cutting down into the land where the land rose, so that it went through a low ridge in a trench with rock walls instead of going over the top. Somebody had spent an unimaginable amount of somebody else's labor on making one line of ground refuse to go up or down.

"They took the iron off it," Rook said when Milo asked. "Everybody did, everywhere, in the first ten years. There's not a mile of rail left standing between here and the coast."

"Then what's it for?"

"It's flat," said Rook, in the traces of the cart, "and it's straight, and it doesn't have a town on it."

That last was the answer and Toren knew it was the answer. He walked along the top of a seventy-year-old railway with the whole country opening out grey-green on both sides of him, no fence, no roof, nothing at all between here and the hills, and he understood that they had just stopped being on a road.

---

Rook drilled them at midday, and it was not like the yard at Tallow Gate.

In the yard it had been the diamond, over and over, turning and stopping, a thing you could do poorly and be shouted at about. Out here he split them up.

"Cole. Fifty paces ahead, on the bank, and I want you holding it — not checking it, holding it — until I call you. Faust, with her, no light. Voss, back down the grade two hundred. Sit on the top of the cut where you can see both ways, and if you see anything you don't understand, you don't shout; you come and tell me." He set the shafts down. "Tanner. Out of the cart."

"You said—"

"I said you'd walk this afternoon. It's afternoon." Rook pulled a bundle off the cart and dropped it on the grass. "You're going to stand on that leg for as long as you can stand on it and hold a full Edge while you do it, and when the leg goes you're going to sit down and keep holding it, and when the Edge goes we'll see which one of them gave out first. I want to know."

Wyck looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because I don't know," Rook said, "and I'd rather find out on an empty grade at noon than somewhere it matters."

---

Toren sat on the lip of the cutting two hundred paces back with his knees up and the country going away in both directions and nothing at all happening in it, and discovered that this was the hardest thing anyone had asked him to do since the road.

There was nothing to look at. That was the trouble. There was grass and there was wind in the grass and there was a hawk working the far side of the ridge in long slow passes. After a while his eyes started sliding off all of it and going back down the grade to where he could see the others as four dark marks against the sky, and he had to haul them back and start again. Twice he caught himself thinking about his father's hands and had to stop.

He was still up there when the sun came off the vertical and Rook whistled him in.

He came down the grade at a jog and found Wyck sitting on the bundle in the grass with sweat running off his jaw and three feet of white light standing steady out of his fist, and Rook crouched on his heels eight feet away watching it the way a man watches a pot.

"How long?" Toren said.

"Don't talk to him."

The light held. It held while Toren stood there, and it held while Dessa and Milo came back down from the fifty-pace mark, and it held while Rook shifted his weight from one heel to the other, and then it went out all at once the way a candle goes out, and Wyck sagged forward over his own knees and swore once, quietly, at the ground.

"Five minutes and change," Rook said. "Leg went at two."

"It didn't go, it just—"

"It went at two and you held the light for three more minutes on a leg that had gone, which is the part I wanted." Rook stood up. "That's good."

"You said that on the first night."

"It's still true." Rook picked up the near shaft of the cart. "You're Flame, Tanner. You've been Flame about a year. Nobody told you because the man who'd have told you was your aunt's brother-in-law and he made barrels."

Wyck's head came up.

Toren, watching, saw the whole thing arrive in the boy's face — saw it land, and saw what it landed on, and saw Wyck decide in about a second and a half to be furious about it instead of pleased.

"What's *Flame*?"

"A rung."

"That's not—"

"Cole's Ember," Rook said, in exactly the same voice, going down the line as if he were naming the weather. "Voss is Ember and barely. Faust I haven't got a word for yet and I've been looking for four days."

"Hey," said Milo, obscurely delighted.

"What are *you*?" said Wyck.

Rook put the shafts on his shoulders and leaned into them and the cart came up out of the grass, and he was twenty feet down the grade before he answered, and he did not turn around to do it.

"Further up," he said.

---

It was Senna who gave them the rest of it, that night, at a fire built too small in the bottom of the cut where the rock walls took the light.

Milo had been at it for an hour. He had been at it in the way Milo Faust got at things, from six directions, cheerfully, without any apparent awareness that he was being refused, and Rook had answered exactly none of it and had gone off up the bank to see to the watch line.

"Oh, *stop*," Senna said at last. "Boy. Come here."

Milo came and sat by the cart wheel.

"Kindled," she said. "Ember. Flame. Fire. Torch. Blaze. And Glory, which nobody has ever met and which they only put on the end so the thing would finish properly."

Milo said, "That's it?"

"That's it. They teach it to children as a song in the west — my mother had it, and her mother, and it's a stupid song." She looked into the fire. "Kindled at the door and Ember in the hand. Flame to hold and Fire to hold longer. Torch to stand the night, and Blaze to—" She stopped. "I don't remember. It doesn't matter. It's a song for making children sit still."

"What's Torch?"

"Torch is as far as anyone gets," Senna said.

Dessa, who had been lying on her back on the far side of the fire with her forearm across her eyes, said, "That's not true, though. He's further up. He said so."

"I said as far as anyone gets." Senna resettled her hands in her lap. "My husband was Ember his whole life. Forty years with a light in his hand you could read by and never once anything you could cut with, and he went out at fifty-four doing something that wanted a man who could cut." She was quiet a moment. "He knew every rung of that ladder by name and it never bought him one inch of the next one. So I'd be careful what you do with the words, all of you. They're a way of talking about people. They're not a way of being one."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Up on the bank, out beyond the firelight, Rook was walking the line of the cut with his hands in his pockets, stopping now and then, going on.

---

Dessa found Toren at the water, later, filling the cans.

"I did the wall thing," she said.

"What wall thing?"

"At Tallow Gate. He told me on the first night — try it through a wall and tell me tomorrow." She crouched on the stones with her arms around her shins. "I didn't tell him tomorrow. I told him this morning, in the tunnel, while she was weighing the jars."

"Does it work through a wall?"

"It works through a wall. It works through two walls and a tank. It doesn't care about walls at all, and I've had this thing for a year and a half and I never tried it once because when they gave it to me in Coldwater they said *she can feel where people are*, and I decided that meant line of sight, and then I stopped thinking." She dug a stone out of the gravel and turned it over. "He didn't even look pleased. He said, all right, that's the wall. Now hold it."

"Hold it how long?"

"That's what I said." Dessa threw the stone into the black water. "He said, until it's boring."

They carried the cans back up the grade together. Behind them, the cut went away south-east in a straight black line toward the country they had come out of, and ahead of them it went away north-west toward country nobody had told them the name of, and Toren realized, somewhere in the middle of that walk, that in four days of drilling at Tallow Gate he had believed, without ever once checking, that they were being taught how to stay alive on a road.

Nobody was teaching them a road. There was no road.

They were being taught something, and it had a shape, and the shape had rungs on it.
