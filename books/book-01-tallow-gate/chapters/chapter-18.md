# Chapter 18 — East

The barrow was loaded when Toren woke.

That was the first thing he understood, lying on his side in the grass with his coat over him and the light coming up grey behind the hedge: the canvas was on, the bundles were in, the chock was out from under the wheel and stowed on its nail, and Senna was sitting up against the front board with her blanket round her shoulders and her hair done, which she did herself, every morning, with three pins, and which took her the better part of half an hour.

Rook was standing at the shafts with his back to the camp.

"Up," he said, without turning round. "All of you. We're walking out to that junction and then I'm going to tell you something, and I'd rather do it standing on it than a mile off."

---

They went out along the grade in the early light with the wind coming off the flat and nobody talking.

Toren walked at the back.

He had not been at the back since the loop road. He had been at point every day for four weeks and everybody in the party had an opinion about that, and this morning he had simply fallen in behind the barrow and nobody had said anything about it, which was worse than if somebody had.

Dessa walked at the off shaft with her hands in her pockets. She had said "clear" twice since they started, both times in the same flat voice, both times without turning her head, and she had not looked at him once.

Milo dropped back to him about halfway.

"Was it bad?" he said.

"Milo—"

"No, that's not — I'm not asking what it was. I know I'm not allowed and I've had it explained to me twice by Wyck and once by Dessa and I've worked it out on my own as well, which nobody credits me for." Milo had his hands shoved in his coat and his shoulders up round his ears. "I'm asking whether it was bad. That's a different question and it's about you and I'm allowed it."

Toren walked about ten paces.

"Yes," he said.

"Right," said Milo. "Right. Okay."

And he stayed at the back with him the rest of the way, talking about absolutely nothing, about a heron he had seen and about whether you could eat a heron and about whether you would want to even if you could, and Toren understood exactly what was being done for him and had nowhere to put it.

---

Rook took the barrow up onto the middle of the triangle and set the shafts down beside the round stone pad with the four bolt-stumps standing out of it, and turned round.

In the daylight you could see the whole of it at once. The single bank came in from the south-east behind them. The left-hand line went out north-west across a flat of rough pasture, dead straight, holding its level, until the eye lost it. The right-hand line curved east through its long shallow bend and dropped, very gradually, toward the shoulder of hill and whatever was behind it.

"Left-hand line," Rook said. "It runs four days north-west to a place called Undern. It's a market town on the edge of the hill country and its whole business is ash. Every hunting company in three hundred miles brings its season's take there to be weighed, and the companies that are big enough to have a roof of their own winter there, because the weighing goes on till the ground freezes and nobody wants to be paid twice.

"Cinder Company winters at Undern. They've wintered there for six years. They came through that wayhouse four months ago going north with four casks, and everything they do between now and the frost ends up on a scale in that town."

Nobody said anything at all.

"Right-hand line," Rook said, "runs east. It comes down off this flat in about eleven miles and meets a river called the Reave, and it follows the Reave for two days, and where the Reave turns south there's an old road that's still a road, and that road goes to Meridian. Nineteen days at the pace we make. Twenty-two if the fords are up."

He let that sit.

Toren watched the four of them take it. He watched it go into Milo, who opened his mouth and closed it. He watched Dessa's head come round. He watched Senna Voss, in the barrow, lift her chin off her blanket and look at the right-hand bank with an expression he had never seen on her face in thirteen years and never afterward forgot, because it was not hope. It was a woman measuring a distance against what she had left, and getting an answer.

And he watched Wyck Tanner, who had not moved at all, and who was looking at the left-hand line.

"You've told us where we're going," Wyck said.

"I have."

"You've never done that. Not once. Not at the Gate, not when you walked us off the loop road, not for a month." He turned his head. "Why today?"

"Because today it's a choice," said Rook, "and I'm not going to make a choice for six people and let five of them find out about it from the shape of the country."

---

"What's down the left one?" Wyck said. "For you. Not the town. You."

Toren's whole chest went tight.

Rook looked at him for a moment, and then answered him, straight, in about the time it takes to breathe in.

"Men who killed people I was responsible for," he said. "Nine years ago and then again seven, and the second time I didn't know it was the same men until four years after that, and I've been walking toward them ever since. That's what's down the left one. You've had a name off a wall and a burned wayhouse and me not answering you, and you can have the rest of that much."

The wind came across the triangle.

"Is that all of it?" said Wyck.

"No."

"All right," said Wyck Tanner, and did not ask for the rest.

---

Senna said, "Put me down."

Everybody turned.

"I'm not being dramatic; I'm being practical, and I'd like it heard by everyone standing here so that nobody has to have it out with me later." She had both hands on the front board and she had pushed herself up straighter than Toren had seen her sit in three weeks. "That right-hand line is nineteen days of walking that you don't want to do and it is entirely about me. There's no other reason for it. The boy's not sick. Tanner's leg is closing. Nobody in this barrow needs a machine except the woman in it.

"So put me down. Not here — take me down the east line as far as the river, and there'll be a place on a river, there always is, and leave me at it the way you were going to leave all of us at Tallow Gate. I'd have a roof. I'd have a winter. It's a better end than most people get and considerably better than I had coming to me on the fifth of the month."

"No," said Rook.

"You haven't thought about it."

"I've thought about it for two days and a night," he said, "which is exactly as long as I've been standing on this bank, and I want you to hear that plainly, because you're owed it and because you'd know if I lied. I thought about it. I took it the whole way down and looked at it. And the answer's no."

"Why?"

"Because you'd do it," Rook said. "You'd get out of that barrow at a landing on the Reave and you'd send the boy on and you would not make one single sound about it, and every one of them would spend the rest of their lives knowing they went on. That is not a cost you get to pay by yourself. You've been trying to hand it round since the road and I'm not taking any."

Senna Voss sat in the barrow with her hands on the board.

"That's the same answer you gave the warden," she said.

"It's the same answer I gave the warden."

---

He picked up the shafts.

He did it without any ceremony at all, the way he did it every morning, and the barrow came up out of the grass, and he turned it.

He turned it east.

That was the whole of it. There was no line said and no look given and nothing to see except a man swinging two wooden shafts through a quarter-turn and starting to walk, and Toren stood on the triangle and watched him do it and felt something go over in him that he could not have described to anybody.

They had gone perhaps twenty paces when Wyck said, "Rook."

He stopped.

"The four months," Wyck said. "From the board. That was four months when we found it, and that was ten days ago."

"Yes."

"And nineteen days east, and whatever's at the other end, and then back."

"It's a year," Rook said. "Give or take. Undern's four days from here and it'll be four days from here when I come back to it, and by then they'll have wintered and split and hired on and half of them will be in a different company with a different name on the ledger, and after a year it isn't a trail. It's a rumor about a rumor, and I've walked those, and they don't come to anything."

"So you might not get them."

"I might not get them."

Wyck stood there in the middle of the junction with his stick planted and his bad leg braced and looked at the man for a long moment.

"Right," he said.

And he started walking east, at the back, at his own speed, and that was the end of it.

---

It was a good day and Toren could not feel any part of it.

The right-hand line came off the flat in a long easy fall, and the country softened as it went, and by the middle of the afternoon there were hedges with things growing in them and a smell in the air that Toren did not recognize for nearly an hour and then placed, with a jolt, as cut hay.

Somebody was farming out there. Somebody was farming within a mile of this bank.

Dessa said "clear" every ten minutes in her flat voice and did not speak to him.

Then, at about two, she came up the off side and walked at the shaft.

"I've got a question and it's mine," she said. "It isn't about last night."

"Ask it."

"The night I got to Flame. On watch." Her eyes were front. "You said it would settle and it has. I held two hundred and forty feet yesterday, and a fortnight ago I couldn't hold two hundred. So whatever you're doing to me is working."

"Yes."

"Then what does it cost?" She kept walking. "You've taught me every day since the loop road and you've never once said what any of it took off you to learn. I've watched you for two days and I think the answer's a great deal, and I think you've decided we don't need it. And it's going in *me*. I'd like to know what I'm carrying."

Rook walked a while with the shafts in his hands.

"It costs," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's the true half of one, and it's the half you can use." He shifted his grip. "Every single thing I've put into you is a door into the inside of your own head. The cost of a door is that it doesn't shut afterward. You will see more than you want to see for the rest of your life, and there is no version of this where you get the holding and not that, and you have already been charged for it."

Dessa walked about twenty paces with that.

"You could have said."

"I've just said," said Rook.

She went back up the off side, and that was the first thing anybody had got out of the man all day.

---

At about four she fell back and walked beside Toren for a hundred paces without saying anything, and then said, "I'm not angry at you."

"You are."

"I'm angry, and it isn't at you, and it took me all day to get those two things apart, so don't make me do it again." She had her hands in her pockets and her eyes front. "I'm angry because he took you out there and not me. And I've had all day to look at that and it's the ugliest thing I've ever found in myself, because my mother went up a bank for a boy who isn't you and I've never once been angry about that, and I've been angry about this since dusk yesterday."

"Dessa—"

"I'm his second," she said. "Nobody's ever said it out loud. He puts me on the tailboard when somebody's going to get hurt and he tells me to say a thing before the person says it, and he asks me for distances before he asks anybody for anything, and that's — that's mine, that's the one thing out here that's *mine*." She stopped and started again. "And he walked past me in the dark to get to you."

Toren walked.

"It isn't about who's worth more," he said.

"I know."

"It's about a thing I've got in my pocket that I never asked for."

Dessa's head came round.

"I know that too," she said. "That's the other reason I'm not angry at you."

---

They made camp at dusk in the lee of the bank with hay-smell coming up out of the dark, and Milo cooked.

He got the stone hot and mixed the meal and put the first flat cake down and turned it when it needed turning, and when it came off he broke it in two and then broke a piece off the corner of one half and put the piece on a flat stone at the edge of the coals, and set it aside.

Then he handed the rest round in his order. Senna. Wyck, because of the leg. Whoever was on watch. Everybody else.

He did it the way he put the lid back on a crock.

Rook set the watches after, the way he did every night, and when he said Cole for the first, Wyck said, "I've got it."

Rook looked at him.

"You're second," said Dessa.

"I've got the first as well."

"That's the whole of the dark on that leg, and you were on your feet all—"

"I've got it," said Wyck, and got his stick under him in the two careful parts it took, and went up the bank.

Nobody said anything else about it. Rook watched him go the whole way up. Then he put another stick on the fire and let it stand.

---

Milo brought the water can round the fire afterward and stood in front of Rook with it and did not put it down.

"You're tired," he said.

Rook looked up.

"I'm not saying it to be rude, I'm saying it because everybody else has decided not to and I think that's daft." Milo set the can down. "You've not slept for two nights. You've had the shafts the whole way down off that flat and you wouldn't give them up when Dessa asked. If you go over, we're four people and a cart in a field, and I'd rather say it now while it's a small thing than in a fortnight when it isn't."

"Noted," said Rook.

"That's not really a—"

"It's noted, Faust. I'll sleep tonight." He got his boot off and turned it over and knocked it out against the stone. "Was there another one?"

Milo shifted his feet.

"The bread," he said.

Rook did not move.

"I've been doing it. The bit off the corner, on the stone. I didn't decide to — I put my hand out one night about a week back and it had already gone and done it, and I stood there like an idiot working out why." He was not looking at Rook and he was not looking away either; he was doing the thing he did, which was running straight at it. "I know it's yours. I'm not asking what it's for. I asked that the first night and you said it wasn't for anything, and I've thought about it since and I've decided that was a lie, but a polite one, and you're allowed one. I'm asking whether it's all right that I do it. That's the whole of the question. Because if it isn't, I'll stop."

The fire moved.

"It's all right that you do it," said Rook.

"Right," said Milo. "Good."

And he picked the can up and went and put it where the can went, and sat down and ate his supper and started telling Senna about the heron.

---

Toren had been sitting six feet away with his bread in his hands the entire time.

He knew what it was now. He knew whose it was and what clan it had come out of and that a woman had asked her own grandmother about it once and been given nothing, and had stopped asking at about eleven, and had gone on doing it anyway for the whole of a life that had ended in a room under a hill on the sixteenth of the eighth month, nine years ago, at about half past three in the afternoon.

And Milo Faust, who was thirteen and three months and had just stood in front of the only man alive who could have told him, and had asked the one question that was not it, did it every night because he had watched somebody do it and his hands had picked it up on their own.

Across the fire, Rook looked at the piece of bread on the rock.

He looked at it for about three seconds. Then he looked away and ate his own and said nothing, exactly as he had every night for five weeks, and Toren sat with his own bread going cold in his hands and found that he could not eat it and could not put it down and could not say one word to anybody about why.

---

He went out past the fire at nine to drill and got nothing at all.

Not four inches. Not a flicker. He stood in the dark grass with his father's Ember in his fist and went to the back of the house and the house was shut, and he stood there for a quarter of an hour and then came back in.

Rook was at the tailboard with the other boot off.

"Nothing," Toren said.

"No."

"Not one—"

"No. Not today, and probably not tomorrow." Rook did not look up. "You had two feet of it three days ago and you held it while you were walking and you're not going to get near that again for a few days, and when it comes back it'll be where it was, and you'll be frightened between now and then that it won't. That's the whole of it. Go and sit down."

"Is that because of last night?"

"It's because you're thirteen and you were up till four in the morning and somebody put something heavy in your arms." Rook worked the boot back on. "Sleep."

"I can't tell them," Toren said.

Rook stopped.

"No."

"Not any of it. Not even the part about the bread." Toren's voice had gone somewhere strange. "He stood right there and asked you and you could have — I could tell him what it is in one sentence and it'd cost nobody anything, and I can't, because the second I give him one true thing he'll want the next one and I'll have to start lying to him, and then it's—"

"Then it's what he's got," said Rook. "Instead of what he had."

"Yes."

"That's right. That's exactly right, and you got there in a day, and it took me a lot longer." He stood up. "I'm not going to tell you what to do with it. I want you to notice that I'm not. You can hand the whole of it to Tanner tonight if you decide to and I won't say a word about it tomorrow, and it'll be yours, and that's the actual difference between this morning and yesterday."

"That's not fair."

"No," said Rook. "You'll find it isn't."

---

Wyck was on the top of the bank on a watch that was not his, sitting on his coat with his leg out and his stick across his knees, and Toren went and sat down next to him because there was nowhere else in the world to go.

They sat for a while with the hay-smell coming up and the fire behind them going down.

"You've got it, then," Wyck said.

Toren did not answer.

"That's not a question and I'm not asking. I want that clear." Wyck had his eyes out on the dark. "You went out to that junction with him in the middle of the night and you came back at four in the morning and you haven't eaten anything all day and you can't look at Faust. So you've got it. Whatever it is. He's given you the whole thing and you've got it now and nobody else on this bank ever will."

"Wyck."

"I'm going to tell you something and then I'm going to shut up about it for good," said Wyck Tanner. "Five weeks ago I thought it was about which of us was better. Then I thought it was about which of us he liked. Then I worked out it was about a thing in your pocket and I stopped asking, and I've been fairly pleased with myself about that ever since, because it was the only grown-up thing I've done since my aunt died and I knew it while I was doing it, which is the best part."

He shifted the leg with both hands.

"And then this morning at that junction he turned the cart east," he said. "And he'd have gone west. Every single thing he's done since the loop road says he'd have gone west, and I've been watching him do it for a month and I'd have put my hand in a fire on it. And he turned it east because of an old woman he met five weeks ago in a road."

"Yes."

"So I've been walking all day thinking about how much that cost him," said Wyck, "and about how I'll never know the number. And about how you do."

The fire went down another notch behind them.

"That's what it's like from here," Wyck said. "That's all. I said it in the birch and I'm saying it again with the other half on: you've got something, and now you've got the rest of it as well, and every one of us is still walking along behind you. And it's fine. I want you to hear the fine part. I'm not building anything out of it this time."

He got his stick under him and stood up, the careful way, in two parts, and looked out at the dark where the country went east.

"But you're going to be carrying it on your own from here," he said, "and I've done five weeks of that, and it's worse than the not knowing. I'd tell you to put it down if there were anywhere to put it."

He went off along the top of the bank to walk the line.

Toren stayed where he was.

A long time later the fire was nothing but a red seam in the ash, and the middle of the night came and went, and Dessa's watch came and went with it, and nobody woke her; and up along the top of the bank a man with a stick went on walking it out to the end and back at the pace of a man who could not walk it any faster.

Toren Voss sat in the grass with his hand flat over two rows of stitches and the hay-smell coming up out of the fields and a piece of bread going cold on a stone by a dying fire, and did not put it down, and did not take his hand away.
