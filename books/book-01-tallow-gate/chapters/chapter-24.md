# Chapter 24 — The Fourth Beat

It was Toren's turn with the water can on the sixth morning off the weir, and he got it wrong.

He did the order the way it was done. Senna first, in the barrow, who took two swallows and said the water was better than it had been yesterday, which it was not. Then Wyck, because of the leg. Then he turned to hand it to whoever was on watch, and stood there for about two seconds with his arm out and nobody in front of it.

Nobody said anything. Dessa took the can out of his hand without any particular expression and drank and passed it to Rook, and they went on walking.

That was the whole of it. It happened perhaps once a day and it happened to all of them and they had all worked out, separately and without discussing it, that the correct thing to do was let it happen and keep going.

---

They had come off the Reave on the second morning and onto the old road, and the old road was a different country.

It had been built. That was the thing Toren kept noticing about it. It went where it wanted to go instead of where the water went, and where a hill had been in the way somebody had taken a piece out of the hill, and there were still low concrete posts standing at intervals along the verge with nothing left on top of them. It was wide enough for four carts abreast. Grass had it in the middle and small trees had the edges, but the crown of it was hard under the wheels and the barrow ran on it better than it had run on anything since the grade.

"Eleven days," Rook said, on the first afternoon. "Maybe twelve. It's a road, so it's faster, and it's a road, so everything else on it is faster too. Nobody walks ahead any more. Cole stays inside a hundred feet."

Dessa said "clear" every ten minutes, in her flat voice, and did not go ahead.

---

Wyck cooked, on the fourth night, and it was poor.

He got the stone too hot and the first two cakes came off burned on one face and wet in the middle, and he did the second batch with the stone pulled back off the coals and those were better and still not right. Nobody remarked on it. Senna ate hers and said nothing, which from Senna Voss was an act of enormous violence against her own nature.

He broke the corner off the first one and put it on the flat stone at the edge of the fire before he handed anything round.

Toren watched him do it. Wyck did it carefully, the way a man does a thing he has been taught and does not want to get wrong, and it was completely unlike the way Milo had done it, which had been the way you put the lid back on a crock.

Rook looked at the piece of bread on the rock for about three seconds, the way he had every night for nine years, and then ate his own and said nothing.

"Should we stop?" Dessa said, afterward, when Rook had gone out to walk the road.

"Stop what?"

"That." She did not point at it. "I want somebody else to have the thought so it isn't only mine. He did it because he'd seen it done. He didn't know what it was. We know what it is and we're doing it anyway and I can't work out whether that's the right way round or the worst possible—"

"We're not stopping," said Wyck.

"I'm not saying stop. I'm saying I've thought about stopping and I'd like that not to be a secret."

"Right," said Wyck. "It's not a secret. We're still not stopping."

---

On the sixth day, in the middle of the afternoon, Senna Voss put her hand on the front board of the barrow and said, "Set it down."

Rook set it down.

He did it without asking why, which Toren noticed, and which told him what was about to happen about four seconds before it happened.

"Sit," she said. "All of you. On the road, I don't care, it's dry. This will take six minutes and I've had eleven days to get it in order so don't help me with it."

They sat down on the crown of the old road in a line. Rook stayed standing with his hands in his pockets.

"There's a fourth beat in my cough," said Senna Voss.

Dessa's head came round.

"It started before the junction. It's three and then a pause and then a fourth one, lower down, and it's the sound of a thing having got into a part of me it wasn't in before, and I have had it explained to me by two people in twenty years and they both used the same words." She had her hands folded on the blanket. "My grandson has known since two nights before the junction. He has lain on his side in the grass every single night since, with his eyes open, waiting for it, and he has told nobody, and he was going to go on telling nobody all the way to the wall."

Toren looked at the road.

"Now I'm going to say the part I actually got you down here for," she said, "because that first bit's only the reason. Seven weeks ago there was a man on this road who knew a thing about a boy and wouldn't say it, and it cost a different boy the whole of a summer, and everybody standing here watched it happen and everybody standing here agreed afterward that it was a bad way to run a family. And then the minute one of you got hold of something heavy, you did exactly the same thing with it."

"That's not the same," Toren said.

"It's the identical thing, and you're thirteen, so you don't get to say it isn't." She said it without heat. "You thought you were sparing us. He thought he was sparing Tanner. Every man who has ever done it has thought he was sparing somebody, and not one of them has ever once asked the somebody."

Nobody said anything for a while. A bird went over, going south.

"I'd have wanted to know," Dessa said.

"I know you would."

"I'd have—" She stopped, and started again, and her voice was perfectly level. "I put my hands on you on the second day out of the weir and I had a rate off your breathing and I wrote it in my head and I've had it since, and I knew something was wrong with the shape of it, and I asked *him*"— she did not look at Toren —"and he said you were tired."

"He did," said Senna. "Well. He's learning it off a man who's had thirty years' practice and he's picked up the worst of it first, which is normal. Voss."

Toren looked up.

"I'm not angry with you," his grandmother said. "I want that heard, because you'll make it into something by tonight otherwise, and I'd rather do the work now than dig you out of it at midnight. You were carrying it because there wasn't anybody to hand it to. There is now. That's all that's changed and it's the only thing I wanted changed."

---

"How long," said Wyck.

He said it to Rook, not to her, and everybody understood why, and Senna let him.

"I'm not going to give you a date."

"I'm not asking for a date. I'm asking what you've got."

Rook took his hands out of his pockets.

"I've had my hands on her three times since the weir," he said. "Here's what I can actually tell you and I'll tell you the whole of it. Four weeks ago a night's sleep gave her back most of a day. Two weeks ago it gave her back about half. Nine days ago it gave her back an hour and a half and she spent the hour and a half organising a burial, and she has not got any of it back since.

"That's not a number and it's not a date. That's a slope, and I've watched enough of them to tell you that a slope like that one does not flatten out on its own, and that the distance between where she is now and the place where she stops being able to take a road is shorter than eleven days is long."

"Then we go faster," said Toren.

"We go exactly the speed we're going," Rook said. "If I push that barrow another mile a day I'll get her there four days earlier in a condition where the machine can see the whole of her and nobody can do one useful thing about what it shows them. She's not a cask of ash. You don't get her there sooner by carrying her harder."

Senna Voss said, "That's the first sensible thing anybody's said on this road in a week."

"You're welcome."

"I didn't say thank you."

---

They went on, and made four more miles before dark, and camped in the lee of a bank where the road had been cut through a rise.

It was a better evening than it had any right to be. That was the thing Toren could not account for afterward. Dessa got the stove going and did not do it in Milo's order and did not do it in her own, she did it in the order that was fastest, and complained about the wind; and Wyck said something about her cooking that was genuinely unkind and genuinely funny; and Senna made all four of them tell her what the road had looked like that day, one at a time, because she had been facing backward in a barrow since the fifth of the month and had seen the whole of this journey going away from her.

Toren sat with his back against the bank and found, at about nine in the evening, that his chest had come loose for the first time in five days, and that he did not have to decide whether that was allowed.

Then Rook stood up and said, "Voss. Out here."

---

They went forty paces down the road to where it was flat and open and the grass was short.

"Right," Rook said. "Put it out."

Toren lit up and got two feet of white standing steady out of his father's Ember, which he could now do on a bad day in a wind while thinking about something else.

"Good. Now put it out."

He put it out.

"You've had that thing since the fifth of the month and you've used one half of it," said Rook. "Tonight you start on the other half, and you're going to be worse at it than you have been at anything since the road, and I want you to hear that from me now instead of finding it out on your own in about ninety seconds."

Toren's mouth went dry.

"Stride."

"Stride."

"Why now?"

"Because it doesn't sit in your hand," Rook said. "Edge sits in your hand, and when you drop it, you've dropped a light. Stride sits in your legs. When you drop that you go down at whatever speed you were doing, on whatever's under you, and if you happen to be eleven feet in the air at the time you go down from eleven feet.

"There is no version of putting that in a boy who loses the first one when a bird gets up. So I've had it in my pocket for seven weeks waiting on you, and at that weir you held two feet of white through the worst ninety minutes of your life and did not put it out one single time, and that's the whole of the reason it's tonight and not tomorrow and not in the spring."

---

It was not like anything.

That was the first thing and it was the only useful thing Toren learned in the first hour. Edge had always started as a reach — a going-to, a thing he did with the front of himself. Stride did not want the front of him at all. It wanted a place low down and behind, under the hinge of the hip, and it wanted him to not be holding on with his feet, and his feet had been holding on since he learned to walk.

He got it on the ninth try and it lasted about a second and a half and put him flat on his back in the road.

"Good," said Rook.

"That's not good."

"That's the right kind of bad. Again."

He got it eleven more times and went down eleven more times, and by the eighth of those he had learned to take the fall on his shoulder instead of his elbow, which was a thing his body had worked out entirely without him. His shins were skinned. There was grit in the heel of his right hand, which was the hand that did not fully close and which now would not fully open either.

At about eleven at night he sat down on the verge with his forearms across his knees and could not get up for a while.

"Will it come faster than the other one did?"

"No idea," said Rook, sitting down on the concrete post opposite. "Tanner's had one for two years and can't do it at all. Cole hasn't got anything to do it with. I did it in four months and I was twenty-six and I had somebody standing in a field telling me what I looked like from the outside, which is worth more than anything I've said to you tonight."

"Vera."

"Vera."

Toren turned his father's Ember over in his hands. The band around the middle was worn glass-smooth where four generations of Voss thumbs had sat, and his own thumb had started, very slightly, to find the same place without being asked.

"Can you do both at once?"

"Yes."

"Both. Edge and Stride. Together."

"Yes," said Rook.

"How?"

Rook did not answer for a moment.

"That's the one I can't say," he said.

"You say everything. You've said—"

"I've said everything I can put in a sentence. I've told you what the room is and what the door is and why you were thanking a stick, and none of it ever once made the light come, and you know that better than anybody alive because you stood in wet grass for a fortnight agreeing with me and getting nothing." He had his hands on his knees. "This one's worse than that. I've had it since before your father could walk and I have never got it into words, not for her, not for you, not for myself at two in the morning. Everybody I've ever watched do it did it in the middle of something going wrong, and every single one of them was asked afterward how, and every single one of them said the same thing, which is nothing useful."

"Then what do I do?"

"You go on doing the thing you did tonight, poorly, every night, until your legs stop arguing," said Rook. "And then one day something will happen too fast for you to pick, and you'll find out whether you stopped believing that you had to."

---

He could not sleep.

He lay on his side in the grass with his coat over him, on the bank of a made road with the bad hand curled against his chest, and listened, and got three small dry sounds and a pause long enough to believe in, and then the fourth, lower, coming up from somewhere further down.

After a while he took the disk out of the pocket with two rows of stitches in it and held it flat on his palm in the dark.

Nine narrow channels ran across the face of it. He had been doing this since the fifth of the month — looking at them and trying to make the pattern into a shape, and failing, and looking away, and looking back and finding it had rearranged itself, which he knew perfectly well was not true. He had asked Dessa once to look at it and she had said *it's nine lines, Toren*, and had not seen anything else in it at all.

It was warm the way a room you had lived in was warm.

Seven weeks ago his father had knelt in wet grass and closed his fingers over it one at a time and told him to put it in a river if it ever came to it, and Toren had said yes, and had been lying, and both of them had known.

He put it back in the pocket and lay with his hand flat over the stitches.

Eleven days.

---

In the morning Rook stood at the shafts and looked south down the road before he picked them up.

"One thing before we go," he said. "So nobody has to hear it for the first time when it matters.

"The last three days of this road are the worst ground any of you have walked and the worst I've walked in six years. Meridian sits where it sits because there's a building in it that nobody could move, and everything that's happened to this continent since has happened around it, and the tears in that country have been opening since before I was born. That's not a story. That's why there's a wall there and not somewhere nicer.

"You'll know when we're in it. Everybody knows when they're in it. And when we are, the drilling stops, and the lights stop, and everything I've spent seven weeks putting in your hands goes in your pockets and stays there until I say otherwise."

"Why?" said Wyck.

"Work it out."

Wyck worked it out. Toren watched him do it, and watched his face go.

"Because they come at the nearest one," Wyck said.

"Because they come at the nearest one," said Rook, and picked up the shafts.
