# Chapter 1 — The Kindling

Toren Voss had been thirteen for six hours, and so far it had not made him any better at splitting wood.

The axe came down and skidded off the round of ash instead of biting it, which was the fourth time it had done that, and the fourth time he had felt the shock of it run up through both wrists and settle somewhere behind his teeth. He set the round upright again. He looked at it. He had been told by three different adults that there was a grain to wood and that a man who could read it never had to swing twice, and he believed them, in the way he believed the road went all the way to Meridian — as a thing that was true for other people.

"You're aiming at the wood," Dessa Cole said.

"That's generally the idea."

"You're aiming at the *top* of the wood." She was sitting on the concrete lip of the overpass footing with her boots off and her heels drumming, sorting through a tin of dried apple like she expected one of the pieces to be better than the others. "Aim at the ground under it. The wood's just in the way."

Toren swung. The round came apart in two clean halves and one of them tipped off the block and knocked over the stack he'd already made.

"There," said Dessa, not looking up.

It was the fifth of the month and the light was going long and orange across the valley, and their camp sat in the shelter of a highway that had stopped being a highway seventy years ago. Two great spans of it still stood, root-thick pillars going down into the grass, the roadbed above them broken off at both ends like a bridge in a story about a bridge that had failed to be one. The overpass made a roof. Under a roof you could have a fire without the whole valley seeing it, and Odo Prentice, who had walked this route eleven times and had eleven times been paid for it, said that mattered more the further east you went.

Eleven times. Toren had counted the tally marks scratched into the shaft of the man's walking staff on the second day out and had not been able to stop thinking about them since. Eleven times out and eleven times back. Twenty-two crossings of a country that Toren had been told, all his life, in a hundred different tones of voice, was not crossable.

They were five days out of Coldwater. There were eleven days left, if the weather held and the fords were where Odo said they'd be. Eleven days to the wall at Meridian, where there were machines that had survived the Fall and people who knew how to make them work, and where — Toren's father said this the same way every time, flat, like reading a number off a page so it couldn't turn into a hope — where they would look at his grandmother and tell them what could be done.

Behind him, in the cart, Senna coughed.

It was not a dramatic sound. That was the part Toren had never gotten used to. It was a small dry thing, three or four beats, over almost before you registered it, and then she would be quiet for an hour and you could pretend you had imagined it. He had been listening to that cough since he was old enough to listen to anything. It had gotten a little worse every year in a way you could only see if you looked across a long stretch of years, the way you couldn't see the road wearing down but could see that it had.

"Toren." His father's voice, from the far side of the fire. "Enough wood."

"There's more of the ash—"

"There's enough wood." Garrick Voss straightened up from the mule's near foreleg with a hoof pick in one hand and a look on his face that Toren had learned to read at about the same time he'd learned to read. "Come here and hold this animal's head so she stops trying to sit on me."

---

The mule's name was Patience, which Odo said was a joke that had been funny for about a week eleven years ago.

Toren held her halter and put his forehead against the long warm plane of her cheek while his father worked a stone out of the frog of her hoof, and he watched the rest of the camp assemble itself into evening the way it had every night for five nights.

Ilsa Cole had the cook pot and was doing something to a handful of grain that she would insist was soup. She was a wide, sunburnt, immensely capable woman who had run the mill wheel in Coldwater and who had, Toren was fairly sure, decided somewhere around the second day that she was in charge of feeding all eight of them whether or not anyone had asked. Dessa had her mother's hands and none of her patience.

Milo Faust was on the far side of the fire with a stick, drawing something in the dirt and then rubbing it out and drawing it again. Milo was thirteen too — three months ahead of Toren, a fact he had brought up an unreasonable number of times over the last five days — and he had been Kindled at the end of summer, and what he had gotten out of it was the ability to make a small pale light sit in the cup of his palm. It didn't burn. It didn't do anything. It went out if he sneezed. He had spent two months being the most disappointed person in Coldwater and had recently arrived, with visible effort, at making jokes about it instead.

And Wyck Tanner was up on the concrete, twenty feet above the fire, sitting on the broken lip of the roadbed with his legs hanging into the air and his aunt's voice floating up at him telling him to come down from there.

Wyck did not come down from there.

He was fifteen. He had been Kindled two years, and he had an Ember, and he was, on the strength of those two facts, the most important person in the traveling party as far as Wyck Tanner was concerned. Toren had watched him work it that morning while the others were breaking camp: standing off in the wet grass with the thing lit in his fist, running it out into a blade as long as his arm and holding it, holding it, holding it, jaw set, until the light guttered and he had to shake his hand out. Forty seconds. He'd counted under his breath and Toren had heard him counting.

"He's going to fall off there," Dessa said, arriving at Toren's elbow with the tin of apple.

"He's not."

"He's going to fall off there and then we're all going to have to be very kind to him about it for the whole rest of the trip." She held out the tin. "Nervous?"

"No."

"Liar."

Toren took a piece of apple. "Little bit."

"It doesn't hurt," she said. "Mine didn't hurt."

"Milo says his hurt."

"Milo says a lot of things." She tipped her head back and looked up at the underside of the overpass, at the rust-stains bleeding down out of the concrete in long streaks like something had wept there for seventy years. "It's more like — you know when you've been asleep on your arm, and you wake up, and there's nothing there, and then it all comes back at once and it's awful and also sort of interesting?"

"That sounds like it hurts."

"That's not hurting, that's *sensation*." She said the word carefully, the way she said all the words she'd gotten out of books. Dessa had been Kindled at fourteen, a year and change ago, and what she had gotten was small too: she could feel where people were with her eyes shut, out to maybe thirty feet, and she could close a shallow cut if she worked at it for a while and then had to go lie down. In Coldwater they had said that was a waste of a Kindling on a girl who could already find people by shouting. Dessa had said, to their faces, that it was better than what any of *them* had gotten, since none of them had gotten anything, and then she had been in a certain amount of trouble.

"Dessa." Ilsa, from the fire, without turning around. "Leave him be. It's nearly time."

---

They did it at last light, because that was when it was done.

Toren did not know why. Nobody did, exactly. His grandmother said the ceremony had been made up out of nothing by frightened people in the first years after the Fall, back when children started coming into things nobody could explain and the adults needed a shape to put around it so they wouldn't scream. She said this without any particular reverence and also, Toren had noticed, without ever once suggesting they skip a single part of it.

The fire was built up. The eight of them made a ring around it, which meant it was a small ring and everybody's shins got too hot. Senna was carried out of the cart by Garrick and Odo together and set on a folded blanket on the concrete footing, and she waved them both off with a hand like a bundle of sticks in a glove and said she was fine, she was fine, get away.

"Eldest speaks," she said.

"You're the eldest," said Odo.

"I know I'm the eldest, Prentice, I was telling you what happens next."

Toren knelt in the dirt in front of the fire because that was the part he remembered from watching it done for other children in Coldwater — you knelt, and you put your hands out palms up on your knees, and you did not close your eyes, and you did not look away from the fire even when it made your eyes water, which it did almost immediately.

His grandmother's voice went out over the top of him. It was not a strong voice anymore. It was very clear.

"There was a world," Senna said, "and it burned. There was a road out of it and most people did not find the road. We found the road. Toren Voss, thirteen years old today, son of Garrick, son of my son — you are on the road because we carried you here. From tonight you carry yourself."

He was supposed to say *I'll carry myself*. He said it. It came out cracked in the middle and Milo, on the other side of the fire, made a face at him that was not unkind.

"There was a world and it burned," Senna said, "and something came in after through the hole it left, and it woke up something in us that was already there and sleeping. We don't know what it is. We don't know why it's ours. Anyone who tells you they know is selling you something. Toren Voss. Do you take what's yours, knowing that nobody living can tell you what it is?"

"I take what's mine."

"Then take it," his grandmother said, "and be careful with it, because your grandfather wasn't."

That was not part of it. Toren looked up, startled, and saw the old woman's face above the fire, absolutely composed, and saw his father's face beside her, absolutely not.

"Ma," Garrick said.

"He's thirteen, not stupid." Senna settled her hands in her lap. "Give him the thing."

---

His father came around the fire and knelt down in the dirt facing him, which nobody had done at any Kindling Toren had ever seen, and which made his chest go tight in a way he did not have a word for.

Garrick Voss was a big, slow-moving, quiet man who fixed things. That was the whole of him as far as Coldwater was concerned and as far as Toren had ever needed him to be. He had large square hands with a permanent grey line under every nail, and when he was working out how to do something he made a small sound in his throat, and he was making it now.

He took the Ember out of his coat.

Toren had seen it perhaps twenty times in his life. It lived in a wooden case under the floorboards and it came out when the settlement had a problem, and the problem was usually something with too many legs that had come down out of the hills, and Toren had always been sent inside before the actual solving of it. It was about the length of his father's forearm and about as thick as two fingers, dark grey, tapering slightly at both ends, and it was worn glass-smooth in one band around the middle where four generations of Voss thumbs had sat in exactly the same place.

His father did not hand it to him right away.

"You know what it does," Garrick said.

"Edge and Stride."

"Say the rest."

Toren swallowed. "Edge is the weapon. It'll take whatever shape you can hold in your head, and the bigger the shape the harder it is, and if you get greedy you'll drop it. Stride is — the other one. Speed. Jumping. It runs down your legs and holds you up." He hesitated. "One at a time. Never both. You pick, and you live with what you picked until you can put it down and pick again."

"Why never both?"

"Because that's not how it works."

His father looked at him for a moment across the top of the fire's heat.

"That's what I was told, too," Garrick said, and put the Ember into Toren's hands.

It was warm. That was all, for one long stupid second — it was warm, like a stone out of the sun, and Toren had time to think *is that it* with a lurch of complete despair —

— and then something behind his eyes opened.

There was no other way to have it. Something opened, some door he had never known was in him because it had never once moved, and past it there was a *space*, and the space had been there his entire life. It was not full of anything. That was the part he could never explain afterward to anyone, including himself. It was not a light, or a voice, or a power. It was room. It was an enormous quiet room in a house he'd been living in for thirteen years and had never gone all the way to the back of, and the door to it had just come off its latch, and cold air was coming through.

He heard himself make a sound.

His hands closed on the Ember without being told to. The warmth ran up his forearms and into his shoulders and down into his chest and stopped there, low, behind the breastbone, and settled in like something taking a seat. And every hair on his arms stood up, and his eyes flooded, and the fire smeared into a long gold streak, and through the streak he saw the metal in his fists put out light.

It ran the length of the Ember in one clean stroke, root to tip, pale and steady.

"There he is," said Odo Prentice, very quietly.

Toren could not speak. He was thinking, with the small remaining functional part of himself, that everybody had lied to him about this, every single one of them, all of them who had said *it's not much* and *don't expect anything*, because this was not *not much*, this was the biggest thing that had ever happened, this was —

The light went out.

"Oh," he said.

"Yeah," said Wyck from the dark behind the fire, and there was something almost sympathetic in it. "It does that."

---

Later, when the fire had burned down and Ilsa had made everyone eat the grain, his father walked him out past the pillars to where the grass started.

The valley went dark blue and then black. There were no lights anywhere in it. Toren had never in his life seen a light he did not know the source of.

"I've got one more thing," Garrick said.

He took it out of an inner pocket, out of a scrap of oiled cloth, and held it flat on his palm, and Toren leaned in.

It was a disk. It was about the size of the round bottom of a cup, and it was heavier than it had any business being — Toren knew that before he touched it, from the way it sat in his father's hand. It was some dark metal that wasn't quite any color. Nine narrow channels ran across its face, cut in a pattern that his eye kept trying to make into a shape and failing, so that every time he looked away and back it seemed to have rearranged itself, which he knew was not true and could not stop thinking.

"That's not an Ember," Toren said.

"No."

"What is it?"

"I don't know," his father said. "My father didn't know. His mother didn't know. It's been in this family five generations and not one of us has ever gotten a single useful thing out of it, and every one of us carried it anyway." He turned his hand and offered it. "Take it."

Toren took it.

It was warm too, and that was not the same warmth. The Ember had been warm like a held stone. This was warm like a room you had lived in. He stood in the wet grass in the dark with the thing in his two hands and felt, so strongly that his throat closed, that he had *found* it — that it had been missing, that he had been looking, that some part of him he did not have access to had been quietly missing this object for thirteen years and had just stopped.

He had never seen it before in his life.

"You feel that," Garrick said. It was not a question.

"What is it?"

"Everybody feels it. Every one of us. My father called it the homesickness." His father was quiet for a moment. Out in the valley something small moved through the grass and went still. "There's a story that comes with it. You want the story?"

"Yes."

"It's a stupid story."

"I want the story."

"It says that one day that disk is going to save the world," Garrick said, "or end it. And that nobody has ever been able to tell which, and that anybody who tells you they can is lying, and that it's the same object either way." He reached out and closed Toren's fingers over it, one at a time, with his big careful mechanic's hands. "Five generations we've handed that down. And every single one of us has handed it down the same way, with the same words, and Toren — I've thought about those words my whole life, and I want you to hear what I actually think."

"All right."

"I think somebody a long time ago got given that thing," his father said, "and loved whoever gave it to them too much to say the true version out loud. So they made it sound like a gift. It isn't a gift. It's a debt. Somebody handed it to us and we've been carrying it ever since and not one of us has ever once been able to put it down." His hands tightened. "If it comes to it — if it ever comes to a choice between that and you — you put it in a river. Do you understand me? You put it in a river and you don't tell anyone where."

Toren stood there with the disk in his fist and the Ember heavy in his coat and the enormous new quiet room open at the back of his head, and he said, "Yes."

He was lying. His father knew he was lying. Neither of them said so.

---

They left at first light and made good time until midmorning, and then Odo Prentice stopped in the middle of the road with his hand up.

Toren came up beside him. The road here ran along a shoulder of hill with the land dropping away to the left into scrub, and there was a marker at the bend — one of Odo's own, a slab of concrete stood on end with a notch cut in the top edge for each safe crossing.

There was a fresh gouge in it. Not a notch. A gouge, four parallel lines the depth of a finger joint, raked from the top of the stone down across the face of it, through the old marks, cutting them out.

"That's new," said Ilsa.

"That's new," Odo agreed.

Toren became aware, slowly, of a thing he had been hearing for some time without hearing it. He turned his head. He looked up the hill, and down the road, and out across the whole broad bright valley falling away to the south, and there was wind in the grass and there was Patience shifting her feet in the traces and there was nothing else at all.

"Where are the birds," he said.

Nobody answered him. Down the road, in the dry scrub, something clicked twice and stopped.
