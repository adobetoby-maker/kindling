# Chapter 23 — Nine Days

On the first morning, before any of the rest of it, Rook had gone across the weir four times.

He had brought the three of them up out of the tailwater pool and off the walkway and laid them out on the north apron above the flood line, side by side, with a canvas over them and a stone on each corner of it. He had done it before he lit the stove and before he went in to look at Wyck's leg, and he had not asked anybody to help and had not explained it, and Toren had watched him do it from the sluice-house door with his hands hanging and had understood, in a dull far-off way, that he was watching something he would be glad of later.

There was a fourth under the canvas by noon. Rook had gone up the north bank alone at about eleven and come down again, and he had not said one word about the winding-house then or at any point afterward, and none of them had ever asked, and Toren went the whole rest of his life without knowing the name of the woman who shot Milo Faust.

---

Whitlow came down the north bank at ten in the morning on the second day, alone, with his hands out from his sides and no coat on so that they could see he had nothing.

He stopped at the north end of the walkway.

"Anse Whitlow," he said, before anything else. "I'd rather you had it than not."

Rook went out fifteen feet and stopped in the same place he had stopped two nights before, and they stood on sixty feet of concrete with the weir going white between them, and for a while neither of them said anything at all.

"I'll say it before anything else," said Whitlow. "I've come for the bodies."

"They're up above the flood line under canvas. You'll want four men and a hurdle for Orsk; he's heavy."

Whitlow's head went back very slightly.

"You put them up."

"I put them up."

"Why?"

"Because they'd have gone down the water by this morning," said Rook, "and there's a hamlet four miles below us that's drinking it."

---

"Right," said Whitlow, after a moment. "Then here's mine, and I'd like it heard out, and then you can do as you like.

"Orsk is dead. Petrek is dead. Cadec is dead and so is the lad who came down that walkway first, and there are five of us left up that valley with a tear that isn't shut and a paper with Pell Hoy's name on it. Four days is what Orsk said and four days is what it is. Five people can do it. It'll be harder and it'll be uglier and one of us will probably get hurt doing it, and we're going to do it, because eleven houses put in everything they had.

"After that we're going up the line to Undern with what we've got and we'll weigh it and take what we're owed and the company will do what a company does at the frost, which is split and hire on and be somebody else's list by spring.

"That's nine days. Four and five. After the ninth day there's no such thing as Cinder Company anywhere on this continent until somebody writes it on a board again next summer, and I know that, and you know that, and I've known you knew it since I looked at you in a mill yard."

He put his hands down.

"So I'm going to stand here," said Anse Whitlow, "and you're going to decide, and I'd rather it happened on a walkway than in my sleep."

---

Toren was on the south apron eleven feet behind Rook with two feet of white light in his fist and no memory at all of having lit it.

Dessa was at his shoulder. Wyck was at the mouth of the walkway with his stick in the grass and his weight off the bad leg, and he had been standing like that for four minutes without moving, and Toren could hear him breathing.

"Five of you," said Rook.

"Five."

"Up a valley with a tear in it and no captain."

"No captain," said Whitlow, "and I'll tell you plain, I'd have had four days of arguing with Orsk about coming down here at all, and now I've got nobody to argue with and I've found I'd rather have the argument." He said it without any self-pity at all. "I'm sixty-one. I've been at this since I was nineteen and I've been with this company eleven years and there is not one single thing in that time I've done that I couldn't say at a table. Except one."

"Don't," said Rook.

"I've carried it nine years—"

"I said don't."

"Marsh," said Anse Whitlow.

---

Nothing happened.

That was the part Toren could not get over, standing on the concrete with a light in his fist. The weir went on making its noise. A bird went down the length of the river and turned at the bend. The man at the north end of the walkway stood with his hands down and his bare arms and the wind going through his shirt, and Rook stood fifteen feet out on the concrete with his hands in his pockets, and neither of them moved at all.

"Elias Marsh," said Whitlow. "That's what was on the list. Subject, west building, magic side. I read it off a page in a yard with the buildings going up behind me, and there were four names left on it that weren't crossed through, and yours was one of them, and I have known your face from that page and from a set of steps for nine years." His voice was perfectly level. "I've never said it out loud. Not to Orsk and not to any of them, because the day I say it, it's a thing the company owns and not a thing I do. And I've said it now because I'm standing on a river in my shirt and it's the only thing I've got that's worth anything, and because I'd like somebody who was there to have heard me say it before I go up that valley with five people and an open tear."

---

Rook did not say anything for a long time.

When he did, he did not say it to Whitlow. He turned his head about a quarter and spoke over his own shoulder to the three of them on the south apron, in the ordinary flat voice he used for fords and firewood and how long a leg was going to take.

"Here's the arithmetic," he said. "All of you get it and you get it now, because I'm not having anybody work it out for themselves in a month and get it wrong.

"There are five of them and one of me and they are four miles up that valley and they will be there for four days. I know exactly where. I know the ground, because I went and looked at it yesterday morning while you were all asleep, and it's a quarry cut with one way in, and I could be up there by two o'clock this afternoon.

"They would not all die. Some of them would run and I'd get most of those inside a week, because a man running from a thing like me runs where I'd run, and I've been doing this five years and I'm good at it. And the ones I didn't get here would be at Undern on the eighth or ninth day with everything they own on a scale in a market square, and I'd have them there, and after the ninth day I would never have any of them again as long as I live.

"That's what's in front of me. That's the whole company, every man who was on that ridge on the sixteenth of the eighth month, inside nine days, and I have wanted it for five years and I have walked two hundred and forty miles this season alone toward it and I have turned a cart east at a junction knowing I was giving it up, and it has come and found me anyway."

He stopped.

"And I'm not going," he said.

---

"Why?" said Wyck Tanner.

It came out of him flat and fast and everybody heard exactly how long it had been sitting there, and Toren realized, with a jolt, that he had heard it before — in a yard in front of a burned wayhouse, in another life, about who stood at point.

Rook turned round.

"Because I'd have to leave you here," he said. "That's it. That's the only reason and there isn't a better one underneath it. There are three of you and one of you can't put weight on a leg for more than six hours and there's a woman in that sluice-house who is a fortnight from a machine that can see the whole of her at once and has been getting worse since the junction. And four miles of that valley is full of rift-things that came down on a light two nights ago and have not gone home.

"If I go up there this afternoon then you sit on this apron for a week and hope. And there is a version of this where I come back down that valley on the ninth day having done every single thing I've wanted to do for five years, and I come round that bend and there's a weir and nobody on it."

He put his hands in his pockets.

"I've stood in that exact place once already," he said. "I'm not doing it twice."

---

Wyck stood at the mouth of the walkway with his weight off the bad leg.

At the junction he had asked a question with the whole of his chest and had got an answer he did not want and had said *right* and started walking, and Toren had watched him do it and had not known, at the time, what it had cost him, because Toren had known everything else and had not known that.

He knew now.

"Right," said Wyck, and this time it was an entirely different word.

---

Whitlow took his dead away at noon.

He came back with four of them and a hurdle and they did it in two trips and they did not look at the south bank at all, and at the end of the second trip Whitlow stood on the north apron for a moment and put a hand up, once, not high, and then went up the bank after the others and out of sight.

"You didn't ask him," Toren said.

"No."

"He was there. He'd have known who paid—"

"He'd have known nothing." Rook was coiling the canvas cord round his hand. "A man that age in a company that size doesn't get told who bought the contract; he gets told where to stand. But he'd have given me a name, because I was standing over him on a walkway and he had five people up a valley, and a man will hand you a name to keep his teeth.

"And then I'd have had it. I'd have had a name I could not check, off a frightened man, for the rest of my life, and I would have gone and found whoever it belonged to." He put the cord in the barrow. "I told you on a step at a junction. I don't know their name. I'm not going to. That's not me being noble, Voss. That's me knowing exactly what I'm like."

---

They ate at dusk on the south apron with the barrow loaded and the sluice-house swept out and the door pulled to, because they were leaving at first light and Rook did not want the morning to have any work in it.

Toren made the bread and broke the corner off the first one and put it on a flat stone at the edge of the coals.

Dessa watched him do it. She had been watching for two days, since three in the morning on a shoulder above a weir, and she was not good at not watching things yet.

Rook saw her watching.

Toren saw him see it, and saw the whole thing go across the man's face in about a second and a half — saw him understand that his own hands had been read, and by whom, and when, and saw him go back through four days and work out exactly which night it had happened on.

He did not say anything to Toren.

He looked at Dessa Cole for a moment, and then at Wyck Tanner, and then he said, to the fire:

"My name's Elias Marsh."

---

Nobody said anything.

That was the thing Toren felt, sitting with a piece of bread in his hands — not the name, though the name went through him like cold water and stayed. It was the shape of the quiet on the other side of it.

Because there was a place in that quiet where a voice should have gone. There was a gap about four seconds wide, immediately after, that had been filled every single time for six weeks — at a fire under an arch in the rain, in a yard between two tanks, on a ballast bed at the top of the world — by a boy with nothing in his hands who asked everything, out loud, without any idea that he was doing them all a kindness.

"Elias," said Senna Voss, from the barrow.

"Yes."

"That's a name." She resettled the blanket. "The other one isn't."

"No," said Rook. "It isn't."

"Well," said his grandmother. "You can have both. Most people only get the one."

---

They went east at first light.

There were two on the shafts now instead of one. That was the practical shape of it and it had been worked out the previous evening without discussion: Rook in the traces, Toren on the near shaft, Wyck on the off one for as long as the leg would take it and then not, and Dessa out front where she could stand in it.

At about the second hour Dessa came back down the line with the water can and gave it out.

Senna first. Then Wyck, because of the leg. Then whoever was on watch, which was nobody, so she skipped it and went on. Then everybody else.

She did it without any ceremony whatsoever. Nobody had told her to and nobody remarked on it, and the next morning it was Toren who did it, and the morning after that it was Wyck, and it went on being the order in which water happened for the whole of the rest of that road.

---

The light on the stone went out on the third night.

It had been up on the top of the chute above the winding-house since a quarter past eleven on the night of the weir. It had burned through the fight and through the morning after and through a burying and through two more days of grey weather, small and pale and entirely unsupported, needing nobody.

They were eleven miles east by then and could not have seen it.

Toren woke at some point in that night and lay on his side in the grass with his coat over him and his hand flat on a pocket with two rows of stitches in it, and listened for the fourth beat of his grandmother's cough. He did not think about the light at all.

He was never afterward able to forgive himself for that, which Rook told him, much later, was the most ordinary thing about him.
