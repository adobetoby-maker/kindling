# Chapter 8 — What Dessa Held

On the second morning on the grade, Rook stopped walking an hour after they started, put the cart shafts down in the grass, and said, "Cole. You're not marching today."

"I'm fine to march."

"I didn't say you weren't." He got a canvas off the cart and threw it down. "Sit there. Faust, you're on the shafts with Voss. Tanner, back of the cart, eyes behind. We're going to go about a mile an hour today and you're going to hate every part of that except the part where nobody's dead at the end of it."

Dessa sat on the canvas in the grass with her boots off and her hands loose on her knees, and they went on around her, and she held it.

Toren did not understand at first what he was watching, because there was nothing to watch. A girl sat down in the grass. The girl's face did what a face does when someone is doing arithmetic they are not good at. That was the whole of it, and it went on for a very long time.

He took a turn on the shafts and gave it back to Milo and took it again, and the cut walls fell away on both sides and the grade came out into an open country of low hills with standing water in the bottoms of them. Behind him, a girl sat in the grass with her eyes open and her hands on her knees, moving perhaps once in a hundred paces.

At something past eleven in the morning, she stood up, walked six steps to the edge of the bank, and was sick into the grass.

---

"Four minutes," Rook said.

"That's nothing."

"It's four minutes." He gave her the water can. "What did it feel like at the end?"

"Like—" Dessa wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist. Her nose had begun to bleed, unhurriedly, and she blotted it on her cuff without seeming to notice that she was doing it. "Like holding a full bucket at arm's length. Not heavy. Just — it stops being about the bucket. Your arm starts making the decision without you."

"Good. That's exactly the right complaint."

"It isn't a *complaint*—"

"It's a complaint and it's the correct one and I've been waiting three days to hear it." Rook crouched down in front of her. "Listen. You've had this a year and a half and every single thing you know about how to use it, you worked out by yourself at fourteen with nobody to ask. That's not an insult, that's a fact about your circumstances, and it means some of what you worked out is wrong and you've never had any way to find out which parts."

"Which parts."

"You throw it," Rook said. "You've been throwing it and catching it. Every fifty steps on that road with Prentice — throw, catch, throw, catch, and a headache by noon, and you told the Voss boy that and you said it like it was the weather." He took the can back off her. "You never once put it down and stood in it."

Dessa looked at him.

"That's the same thing."

"It isn't. It's the opposite thing, and you're going to be sick four more times before your body agrees with me." He straightened up. "Go again."

"Now?"

"When the bleeding stops."

---

She quit at about three in the afternoon.

That surprised Toren more than anything else in the day. He had been on the shafts for the last mile with his shoulders burning and Milo chattering beside him about nothing whatsoever, and he looked back and Dessa was standing in the middle of the grade with her arms down at her sides, not walking, and when Rook came back along the line she said, in a voice that was almost conversational:

"No. I'm done. I'm not doing another one."

Toren waited for it. He had been at Tallow Gate for the pen set down squarely alongside a ledger; he had been in the pumphouse doorway for *that's the arithmetic and it's ugly*; he knew by now more or less what the man sounded like when somebody told him no.

Rook said, "All right."

"I'm not being — I'm not sulking, I just—"

"I heard you." He put the shafts down. "We'll stop here. Faust, get the stove out. Voss, water."

And that was all, and he walked away up the bank, and Dessa Cole stood in the middle of the grade with her fists closed and her mouth open and absolutely nothing to do with any of it.

Toren got the cans and went down to the standing water at the bottom of the hollow and Dessa came with him, which she had not been asked to do.

"He's doing something," she said.

"He's letting you stop."

"He's *doing* something." She kicked a stone off the bank. "He had four days at Tallow Gate to tell me what I'd been doing wrong and he didn't say a word, and then the second we're out here where there's nobody, he — and now I say no and he says *all right*, like it doesn't cost him anything, like he'd worked out I was going to say it—"

"Maybe he had."

"Then that's worse!"

Toren crouched at the water's edge and let the can fill and did not say what he was actually thinking, which was small and mean and which he had been carrying since eleven in the morning.

What he was actually thinking was: *he spent the whole day on you*.

---

He drilled that evening and Rook watched him for about six minutes.

Four inches. Nothing. Five inches. Nothing four times running. A foot, once, for the better part of two seconds, and Toren's whole chest came up off the ground with hope — and then nothing again, eleven times, until his hand cramped shut and he had to work the fingers open with the other hand.

"Right," Rook said, and went to see to the cart.

That was the whole of it. No *again*. Not even *fine*.

Toren stood in the grass in the dark with a dead rod in his fist and understood, with a clarity that made his eyes sting, that he had just been given six minutes out of a day, and that the six minutes had been generous.

---

Rook put Dessa on the first watch at nine at night and told her to hold it the whole way through.

"That's three hours."

"It's three hours."

"I did four *minutes* this morning and I—"

"You did four minutes of the hard thing this morning," Rook said. He was sitting on the cart's tailboard with a boot off, working at something in the sole of it with a knife. "Sitting in a field with nothing in it, at noon, with all of us in front of you where you knew exactly where we were. That's the hardest version there is. Doing it on a watch is the easy version and you're going to find out why."

"Why?"

"Because there's a reason to."

---

Toren woke at some point in the night because Milo, beside him, had rolled over and taken the blanket.

The fire was down to a red eye in the bottom of the pit. The cart was a black shape against the sky with Senna's breathing going in and out of it. And out on the edge of the bank, thirty feet off, Dessa Cole was sitting on the canvas with her boots on and her arms around her knees, looking at nothing, absolutely motionless.

He watched her for a while.

There was nothing to see. That was the fourth time that day he had thought it, and this time something in it turned over. She was not doing anything. She had been sitting there for — he did not know how long, but the fire had been built up when he lay down and it was down to an eye now, so a long time — and she was not doing anything, and her face was not doing arithmetic anymore.

Her face was doing nothing at all. Her face looked like the face of somebody on a porch.

*Oh*, he thought.

And she said, out loud, perfectly conversationally, into the dark: "Rook."

He was up before the word was finished.

"Where."

"Back down the grade. On the bank, on the left side, coming up toward us on the flat." Her head had not moved. "One. It's one."

"How far."

"I don't—" and then, for the first time, something went through her voice. "It's a long way. It's a *long* way. It's past where I—"

"Distance, Cole."

"Seventy feet. Eighty. It's outside, it's outside my—" She stopped. She started again, and her voice came out flat and level and almost bored, which Toren would remember for the rest of his life as the sound of somebody doing the hardest thing they have ever done and refusing to let it show. "Eighty feet and closing at a walk. Left of the crown. It hasn't clicked."

"Good," said Rook. "Nobody light."

---

It took about six seconds and Toren saw almost none of it.

There was the shape of a man going away down the grade at a speed that men do not go, with a hard blue-white frame burning around his legs and throwing the ballast stones into sharp relief for twenty feet on either side of him. There was a stroke of white, short — much shorter than the spear on the road, a thing not much longer than a cleaver — that came out and went in and was gone. There was no sound at all except the wind, and the small collapse of a great deal of something soft.

Then there was a man walking back up the grade with his palm held flat and low at his side, unhurried, gathering as he came.

He stopped at the fire and tipped the jar and set it on the tailboard.

"Flask," he said. "Maybe a little under."

Dessa had not moved off the canvas.

"Is that it?" she said.

"That's it."

"That's *it*? I called it at eighty feet and you went and — and that's—"

"That's what it looks like when it works." Rook stoppered the jar. "You gave me ninety seconds. Ninety seconds means I choose the ground, I choose the distance, I choose whether anybody wakes up. It was never going to get closer than sixty feet to this fire and it was never going to be a fight." He looked at her across the red eye of the coals. "On the road with Prentice you had thirty feet and you were throwing and catching, so what you actually had was thirty feet about every fifty steps, which is nothing, which is why four people are buried above a road."

Nobody said anything.

"That's not on you," Rook said. "You were fourteen and nobody taught you. I'm telling you what changed tonight because you're going to lie down in a minute and feel like nothing happened." He put the jar in the cart. "That's Flame, Cole."

Dessa Cole sat on a piece of canvas on the edge of a seventy-year-old railway embankment with a nosebleed starting again and her hands shaking in her lap.

"Oh," she said.

---

She could not sleep afterward and neither could Toren, so they sat by the built-up fire with the blanket over both their shoulders like two people on a bench.

"It didn't feel like a door opening," she said.

"What did it feel like?"

"Nothing. That's what I'm telling you. It felt like *nothing*." She had her hands out to the coals, turning them. "All day it felt like holding a bucket. And then somewhere on that watch it stopped feeling like holding a bucket and it just — it was on. And I didn't do anything to make it be on. I stopped doing something."

Toren thought about that for a while.

"That's what it's like for me," he said. "When it works. When I stop telling it."

"Yeah, but you think that's the Ember being nice to you."

"It is the Ember being—"

"Toren." She turned her head and looked at him, and her eyes had gone red all around from the nosebleeds, and she was almost laughing, and there was something underneath the almost-laughing that he did not have a word for. "I haven't got an Ember. I haven't got anything. There's no device. There's nothing in my hands and nothing in my pockets and nothing anybody gave me at a fire." She looked back at the coals. "It didn't feel like something coming in. It felt like something I was doing."

Across the fire, in the dark on the far side of it, where Toren had assumed for the last hour that there was nobody, a man who had not moved and had not spoken shifted his weight very slightly on the tailboard of a cart.

Neither of them heard it.
