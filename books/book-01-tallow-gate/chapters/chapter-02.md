# Chapter 2 — What the Road Brought

Odo Prentice had a rule about the scrub, and the rule was that you never went into it.

He said it twice on the way down the hill shoulder, once to the group generally and once to Wyck specifically, and Toren understood by the second time that Odo was frightened and was managing it by giving orders. The scrub started forty feet below the roadbed and went on for a mile and a half of grey-green thornbrush the height of a man, and there was no path through it, and there was no seeing into it, and the road was the only thing in the world that was not it.

"We walk," Odo said. "We walk at the pace of the cart and nobody gets ahead of the cart and nobody gets behind it. Tanner, that means you."

"I'm scouting."

"You're a boy with a stick and a light on it. Get behind the cart."

Wyck's face did something ugly and then closed up, and he fell back, and Toren made a point of not looking at him.

They walked. Patience did not want to. She had gone stiff-legged and high-headed at the marker stone and she stayed that way for the next mile, walking like an animal that expects the ground to be taken away from her, and Garrick walked at her head with a hand flat on her neck the whole time, saying nothing at all in a low continuous murmur.

Toren had his hand in his coat pocket. He had the Ember in it. He had had his hand in that pocket since the marker stone and his fingers had gone numb around the middle of the shaft and he could not make himself let go.

"You're going to wear a hole in that coat," Dessa said.

"Do you feel anything?"

"I feel eight people and a mule and nothing else, and I've been checking every fifty steps, and I'm going to have a headache by noon." She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "It's thirty feet, Toren. Something could be at forty feet and I'd never know."

"But you'd know at thirty."

"I'd know at thirty."

That should have been comforting. Toren worked out, over the following quarter mile, exactly why it was not: because the road was twelve feet wide, and the scrub started at forty.

---

It came out of the brush at the bend where the road cut back on itself, and it did not make any sound at all until it was already up on the roadbed.

Toren's first thought, and he was ashamed of it later for years, was that it was beautiful.

It came up over the shoulder in one long pour of motion, four limbs and then a fifth and then he understood he had counted wrong, and it was the height of a horse at the back and lower at the front, and its hide was the flat wet grey of slate under rain. The joints were in the wrong places. That was the thing his eye kept catching and dropping and catching again — every limb had one more bend in it than a limb should have, so that when it moved it did not step, it *folded* and *unfolded*, and the folding was smooth and unhurried and terrible.

Down the center of its chest there was a seam. As it came up onto the road the seam brightened, a thin hot line the color of a coal split open, and Toren felt the light of it on his face from thirty feet away.

Odo Prentice shouted one word.

It was not a word. It was the front half of one. He got his staff up crosswise in both hands, which was the correct thing, which was the thing a man who had crossed this country twenty-two times would do, and the creature went through the space where he was standing without appearing to change speed at all, and Odo went off the shoulder of the road into the scrub.

He did not come back up.

Ilsa Cole screamed. Patience went straight up in the traces and came down and tried to run and the cart's near wheel came off the roadbed and hung, and Garrick had both hands in the halter and his boots skidding through gravel, hauling her head around, and the cart came back down on the road with a crash that Toren felt through his feet.

Senna was in that cart.

Toren got the Ember out of his pocket.

It lit. That was the one thing that worked — it lit the instant his skin was properly on it, the pale line running root to tip, easy as breathing, and for one heartbeat he thought *yes* —

— and then it was a warm metal rod in his hand and nothing else.

*Edge.* He knew what Edge was. He had watched his father hold a blade of light three feet long out of this exact object, standing on the wall at Coldwater, six years ago, when they had forgotten to send Toren inside. He knew what it looked like. He had the shape in his head as clear as anything he had ever thought.

Nothing happened.

He thought it harder. He thought it so hard his eyes watered.

Nothing happened.

The creature turned on the road. It did not turn the way an animal turns. The back end came around first and the front end followed as an afterthought, and the split-coal seam in its chest slid its light across the gravel, and then it was facing the cart, and it clicked twice in its throat.

Something in the scrub clicked back.

Then something else did, further along. Then two more, and then a fourth from up the hill on the *other* side, above the road, where nobody had been watching at all.

"*Off the road!*" Garrick roared. "Everybody up the bank! Ilsa, get them up the *bank* —"

---

Toren did not go up the bank.

He would think about that later too. He would think about it constantly, in the specific way you think about a thing you cannot change: that his father had given an order, a clear one, the first clear one of the worst hour of his life, and Toren had not gone up the bank because his grandmother was in the cart and the cart was on the road.

He ran toward it instead, and the second one came out of the scrub eight feet in front of him.

It was smaller than the first. It was still bigger than he was. It unfolded up out of the thorn with its head low and its seam banked down to an ember-glow. It looked at him — it had no eyes he could find and it absolutely, unmistakably looked at him — and its head tracked, very slightly, down, to the lit thing in his fist.

*It's looking at the Ember.*

That was the first real thought he had in the whole fight and it was correct, and he did not know it was correct, and he threw it away immediately because there was no time to have thoughts.

He swung.

He swung a two-foot metal rod at a thing with too many joints, with no blade on it, with no training, with his whole shoulder behind it and his feet in completely the wrong place, and he missed, and the miss spun him most of the way around, and his boot went off the edge of the roadbed.

The world tipped. Gravel went out from under him. He went down hard on his hip and slid, and thornbrush came up and caught him across the ribs and the face, and his hand — the hand with the Ember in it, the hand he had clenched shut the way you clench a hand when you are falling —

Light. A stroke of hot white a hand and a half long, straight out of the end of the shaft, there and gone in less than a second.

It went through the thing's forelimb just below the second joint.

The creature came apart from the wound outward. Not messily. It went to *ash* — warm grey ash with light still crawling through it, folding in on itself the way a paper does in a fire, and the whole shape of it collapsed into a drift that hung in the air and then came down over Toren in a soft warm rush that smelled like a struck match and tasted, faintly, like the back of his own throat.

He lay in the thorn with his heart going like a bird in a box.

*The Ember did that.*

He looked at it. It was dark again. It was a warm dull rod in his fist, and thirty seconds ago he had wanted a blade out of it more than he had ever wanted anything, and it had given him nothing at all; and then he had fallen down a bank and not wanted anything except to not be falling, and it had opened up and cut a thing in half.

*It did that on its own. It decided.*

The idea arrived whole and he believed it entirely, the way you believe the first explanation that fits, and it settled into him and became the floor he stood on for a long time afterward.

---

Getting back up the bank took nine seconds and cost him the skin off both palms.

The road, when he came over the lip of it, had turned into something he did not recognize.

There were four of them on it now. The big one had the cart — it was up against the tailboard with its forelimbs hooked over, folding and unfolding, working itself in, and the cart was rocking and Patience was screaming, and Garrick Voss was standing on the cart's step with a splitting axe in both hands.

*He doesn't have the Ember.*

Toren had no time for that thought either. It went in and lodged somewhere and waited.

Nell Tanner was on the upslope with the children behind her — Milo, Dessa, Wyck, all three of them scrambling for the high ground with Ilsa hauling at their coats — and Nell had a bill-hook off the cart and she was standing across the line of the bank. She was fifty-one years old and had run a tannery, and she planted her feet and swung at the thing coming up at her and connected, and it did not matter.

Toren saw the whole of it. That was the worst part. He was forty feet away with a dead rod in his hand and he saw the entire thing happen from beginning to end with perfect clarity and it took less than two seconds and he could not have crossed forty feet in two seconds if he had been the fastest thing alive.

Wyck made a sound Toren had never heard a person make.

"*Up! Up up up—*" Ilsa Cole had Wyck by the back of the collar and was dragging him bodily backward up the slope with Dessa shoving from below, and Milo had both hands out in front of him and there was a small pale light sitting in his palms, and he was holding it out toward the thing on the bank like a man holding out a lamp, because it was every single thing he had.

The creature ignored him completely. It went past Milo's light. It went up the slope past all three of them without slowing, and Toren, staring, understood in a cold flat way that made no sense to him at all: *it isn't going for them.*

It was going for Wyck.

Wyck had his Ember out. Wyck had it lit and had it half-shaped, a stub of blade wavering at the end of it, because Wyck had been Kindled two years and had trained on a fence post every day of it and this was the one thing Wyck Tanner could actually do.

"*Put it out!*" Toren screamed. "Wyck — put it out, put it—"

Wyck did not hear him, or heard him and did the opposite, and the blade steadied and lengthened and got bright, and every clicking thing in the scrub for a hundred yards turned toward the light on that hillside.

---

Toren ran for the cart.

He had worked one thing out and he had worked it out wrong, and the wrongness of it would take a long time to surface and would cost more than he could imagine standing there in the road. But he had worked *something* out, and it was this:

*It didn't cut when I told it to. It cut when I stopped telling it.*

So he ran with the Ember held loose. Not gripped — loose, rolling in his fingers, the way you carry a tool you're not using. He did not picture a blade. He did not picture anything. He got to the tailboard of the cart with the big one's foreclaw eight inches from his grandmother's blanket and he brought the rod up flat under it and thought, as clearly as he could think anything, *please*.

The Ember put out four inches of light.

Four. Not three feet. Not even the hand and a half from the bank. Four inches of hot white at the tip of the shaft, and it lasted about as long as it took him to shove it upward, and it opened the thing's forelimb across the second joint, and the limb came off in a slur of warm ash and the rest of the creature dropped off the tailboard and staggered sideways down the road.

It did not die. It backed off eight feet on its remaining limbs and stood there, and the seam in its chest brightened, and it clicked, and Toren stood with his back against the tailboard and his grandmother's hand fastening on his shoulder through the slats, and he thought, with a savage lurching joy that made him briefly sick:

*It listened to me.*

"Toren." Senna's voice at his ear, thin and completely steady. "Boy. Look at me."

"I did it — Gran, I did it, I asked it and it—"

"*Look at me.*"

He turned his head.

His grandmother's face was between two slats of the cart, grey as ash, and her eyes went past him down the road, and Toren turned to follow them.

The scrub was coming apart along a hundred yards of its length. Not at one place. All along it, from the bend behind them to the long straight ahead, the thornbrush was bending and folding and opening, and they were coming up out of it in ones and twos and fours, unhurried, every seam in every chest lit, and there were more of them than Toren could hold in his head at once.

And on the hillside above the road, a hundred feet up and utterly alone, Wyck Tanner stood in the open grass with three feet of white light burning in his fist and every single one of them turning toward it.
