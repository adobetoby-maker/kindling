# Chapter 28 — The Register

Physician Kell came out at eleven in the morning and told them the whole of it standing up in the yard, because Senna Voss had instructed her to.

"She's had a machine over her and I've seen the whole of her at once, which is the thing you walked nine weeks for, and I'm going to tell you what it showed and then I'm going to tell you what we can do, and neither of those is going to be what you've been carrying in your heads since the fifth of the month."

"Say it," said Toren.

"Twenty years ago somebody did something to her that stopped the thing in her lungs from going anywhere." Kell had her hands in her coat pockets. "I don't know who and I don't know how, because it isn't our work and we don't do it here and I'd be lying if I said I could describe it. But it was done well and it has held for two decades, and what has happened this year is that it has started to fail, and the fourth beat your grandson has been lying awake listening to is the sound of it failing.

"It cannot be renewed. Not by me, not by anybody in this enclave, and I've read enough to tell you that the people who could do it wouldn't take it on at her age with the thing already moved. So that road is shut. I want that said plainly, because everybody's first question is whether we can put her back the way she was, and the answer is no, and it is the only part of this that isn't a choice."

Nobody said anything.

"Here's ours," said Kell. "We can take out what the disease has made and replace what it's spoiled. It's a long course. It's eighteen months and the first nine days of it are very bad and I am not going to dress that up.

"And the two things will not run together. To start ours she has to come off theirs, entirely, and once she's off it there is no going back onto it, because the thing that's holding her is holding her in one place and when you take it away the thing moves, and where it goes is not up to any of us."

"How many?" said Rook, from the bench.

"Eleven, in nineteen years. Seven are alive."

"Of the four?"

"Two were older than her," said Physician Kell, "and one was not."

---

They were allowed in at two in the afternoon, one at a time, four minutes each.

Toren went last, because he asked to.

His grandmother was in a bed with rails on it in a room with white light coming out of the ceiling, and she looked exactly the same, which he had not been prepared for at all. Somebody had taken her hair down. It was the first time in his life he had seen it down in the daytime.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've come in here with a face on you and I can read it from the corridor." She got a hand out from under the blanket. "Sit down and listen, because they've given me four minutes and I've spent two of them on your face.

"I'm taking it. That's decided and it was decided before that woman finished her sentence and there isn't any part of it for you to carry. I've had twenty years I wasn't owed and they were all borrowed off something nobody can explain to me, and now somebody has offered to lend me some more out of a machine, and I'd be a fool and an ingrate to say no because the terms are hard."

"You could—"

"I could not," said Senna Voss. "I could lie in this bed for a year and go down the slope I'm on and die in a clean room with the boy in the corridor, and that is the alternative and it is the whole of the alternative, and I've looked at it and it's worse." She squeezed his hand, which did not take much. "There's one thing I want and it's the only thing I'm going to ask any of you for.

"Whatever happens in the nine days — I don't want anybody sitting up with it. Not one of you. You've buried four people since the fifth of the month and I have watched every single one of those go into three of you, and I am not going to be the fifth thing that happens to you in a corridor."

"That's not—"

"It's not fair," she agreed. "No. You'll find it isn't."

---

The nine days were nine days.

Toren could not afterward assemble them into an order. There was a room in the outer sheds with four beds in it and a stove, and there was a yard, and there was a wall you could not go up on, and there were meals at times that somebody else decided, and there was nothing whatsoever to do.

Rook lay down on the fourth day and did not get up again for five, which frightened all three of them more than anything that had happened at the Drownings. Kell had put eleven stitches into his side and had said the word *clean* about the wound in a tone that suggested she had expected not to, and had then asked him nine questions about how fast he usually healed and had written all nine answers down.

"You're in a book now," said Wyck.

"I've been in a book before," said Rook. "It didn't end well for the book."

Wyck walked the yard. He did it four times a day, deliberately, on the stick, working the leg; and on the sixth day he came in and sat down and said, to nobody, "Three feet. Moving. Backward, even," and put his head against the wall and closed his eyes, and Dessa put a hand on the top of his head as she went past and neither of them said anything about it.

Toren was given his Ember back for one hour on the fifth day, in a stone yard behind the gate store with a proctor standing there, because Kell wanted to see him work.

He got nothing.

Not two feet. Not four inches. He stood in a walled yard with his father's Ember in his fist and reached for a room he had walked into the middle of eleven days ago and the house was shut, and he had been told this would happen, in a hedge on the grade, by a man taking a boot off, and knowing it did not help even slightly.

"It'll come back where it was," said Rook, from the wall, when they told him.

"You don't know that."

"No," said Rook. "I don't. I've been wrong about you twice."

---

She lived.

That was the whole of it and there was not any more to it than that, and Toren had spent nine days building an enormous architecture for the moment it would be said and in the event Physician Kell put her head round the door of the shed at seven in the morning on the tenth day and said, "She's through it. She's asleep and she'll sleep till noon. Go and eat something," and went away again.

Dessa sat down on the floor.

---

The cost came out over the following fortnight, in pieces, the way costs do.

She was deaf. The left ear was gone entirely and the right had about a third of what it had, and Kell said the word *nerves* and said that this was where the thing had gone when it moved, in the nine days when there was nothing holding it, and that it was where it had stopped, and that on the whole they had been lucky, and she said that last part without any apology at all, which Toren respected.

Her hands shook. Not badly. It was a fine steady tremor in both of them that was worst in the morning and never entirely absent, and it meant she could not hold a cup full and could not thread anything, and it meant, on the third morning after she was moved into the long ward, that Toren came in and found his grandmother sitting up against the pillows with three pins in her lap and her hair down her back and both hands flat on the blanket, not trying any more.

She had done it herself every morning of his life. Three pins. The better part of half an hour.

He did not know how. He stood in the ward and worked out, in about two seconds, that in thirteen years he had never once watched her do it, because it happened before anybody was up.

Dessa Cole came in behind him with a tin of water and looked at the three pins for a moment and put the tin down.

"Right," she said, loudly, because she had to be loud now. "I'm going to be terrible at this for about a week."

She was terrible at it for about a week.

---

And she could not travel.

Kell put that on the table in the second week without being asked, because she had worked out by then which of them would ask and had decided not to make him.

"Eighteen months. That's the course and it does not shorten and it does not move. She's in a bed in this enclave for nine of those months and inside that wall for the rest of them, and at the end of it, if it goes the way I'd like, she is an eighty-two-year-old woman with a third of one ear and no lungs to speak of, and there is no road on this continent she will ever walk again."

"Then we're here," said Toren.

"That's not my department," said Kell.

---

Registrar Quist came down to the sheds on the sixteenth day with a folder under her arm and asked for Elias Marsh first.

Toren was not sent out. He was not invited to stay either, and he stayed, and nobody remarked on it, which he understood by then to be how most of the permissions he had in the world had been granted.

"Ferrow," said Quist. "Lapsed charter, nine years. I've got the page."

"I know you've got the page."

"You knew I'd look."

"I knew you'd look," said Rook, "because I gave you a village and a date and you're a registrar. I'd have done it."

"There's a second page attached to it." She did not open the folder. "A research charter, held eleven years, in a bowl of hills four hundred and six miles north of here. Instrument licences for three imaging machines, which is why it's ours — nobody runs those without a licence and the licences come from this building. It ran out of a lapse notice nine years ago on the twenty-first of the eighth month."

Toren watched the man's hands. They did not do anything.

"Is the holder's name on it?" said Rook.

"Yes."

"Is the buyer's?"

"Charters name the holder," said Quist. "They don't name who paid. They never have."

"No," said Rook. "I knew that."

"Then why did you ask?"

"Because I told a boy on a step at a junction that I was never going to ask anybody," he said, "and I've been sitting on a bed in a shed for a fortnight, and it turns out that when somebody puts the page in the room with you, all the things you decided about yourself on a step at night are worth about as much as you'd expect."

Quist did not say anything for a moment.

"There's one more," said Rook. "And then I'll have done."

"Ask it."

"Is there a company on your roll called Cinder?"

"No," said Registrar Quist. "Nothing of that name has registered here in three years. I'd have to write to Undern and it would take a season and I'd get an answer that was a season old."

"Don't write," said Rook.

"You're sure."

"I'm not remotely sure," he said. "Don't write."

---

She gave Toren the page on the seventeenth day, in the Register itself, which was a long low room under the west end with racks in it going back further than the light went.

"Sit down," she said. "Read it twice. I'll be at the end of the rack and you can call me when you want me and not before."

It was one page. It was ruled in columns in faded red with a hand-written entry across them and two lines of margin note, and at the head of it there was a date sixty-one years old.

Toren read it four times.

*Presenting: VOSS, Hester. F. 34. Origin: Wealdon, 310m W.*

*Reason for presentation: Requests destruction of object.*

*Object: disc, non-ferrous, 84mm, 610g. Nine channels across face. Function not determined.*

*Examination: submitted to full instrument. Result returned as for an empty tray. Instrument fault suspected. Instruments checked against control and found sound. Repeated 3rd day with second set, second operator. Same result.*

And then, in the margin, in a different and much smaller hand:

*Second operator asked that it be recorded that he does not accept this result and has no alternative to offer.*

*Disposition: DISPOSAL DECLINED. The enclave does not destroy what it cannot enter. Object returned to bearer. Bearer requested that the refusal be written down and it has been written down at her request.*

And at the bottom, in the field the gate ledgers keep for it, because people say things at gates:

*Statement of bearer: that the object will one day save the world or end it; that nobody has ever been able to tell which; that it is the same object either way.*

*Bearer asked where she had this. Bearer states the man who gave her the object gave her the words with it. Bearer states he was not family and gave no name and she did not see him again.*

---

He sat in the Register for a long time with the page flat on the table in front of him and both hands on the edge of the table.

He had been told that story in wet grass under a broken overpass by a big slow-moving man who fixed things, on the last good night of his life. *It's a stupid story.* He had heard it in his father's voice and he had assumed, the way you assume the road goes to Meridian, that it had started somewhere in the family and got worn smooth by five generations of being repeated over a fire.

It had not started in the family. It had been handed over with the object, by somebody, to a woman who was already thirty-four, and she had carried it three hundred and ten miles and asked a wall of machines to destroy it, and the machines had looked at it and reported that there was nothing on the tray.

*I think somebody a long time ago got given that thing*, his father had said, *and loved whoever gave it to them too much to say the true version out loud.*

He had been wrong about the first half and exactly right about the second, and he had been dead for nine weeks, and there was no way on this earth to tell him either part.

---

"Have you heard the name?" Toren said.

He said it loudly, sitting on the edge of his grandmother's bed that evening, and when she did not get it the first time he wrote it on the back of a requisition slip with a pencil and held it up.

Senna Voss looked at it for a while.

"No," she said. She had got very careful about her volume since the ward, and pitched it a little low, the way she had worked out was best. "And I'll tell you what that's worth, because it's worth more than nothing. I came into that family at nineteen. Your grandfather was Ember forty years and he talked about his people the whole time, and I sat in a kitchen in Coldwater for six decades and I buried three of them, and I have never once in my life heard that name spoken."

"She's a Voss."

"She's on a page," said his grandmother. "That's not the same as being spoken of, and the difference is the whole of what's just happened to you." She got a shaking hand onto his wrist. "Somebody in that family took a decision to stop saying her name, and they were probably frightened, and they were probably right to be, and it worked, and it worked for sixty-one years until a woman with a folder went down a rack.

"Write it down, Toren. Whatever else you do with the rest of it. Don't be the one who decides which bits the next one gets."

---

The roll was done on the twenty-first day, at a table in the gate yard, and it took about ten minutes.

Quist read it out because they had a right to hear it said.

Conditional residence, five persons, renewable at thirty days and at ninety thereafter. One person admitted to long care. Three Embers held in the gate store under receipt, issued on call-out and returned within the hour. One magic-side practitioner registered, offered assessment for the setter line, answer wanted inside thirty days. One object entered in intake, returned to bearer, subject to production on request.

And the fifth thing.

"Entered on the enclave roll as a working company," said Registrar Quist, "under a named officer, liable to call-out on the outer line, paid at the standing rate against the register, and not free to leave the district while the call-out stands."

Nobody said anything at all.

"I know what this looks like," said Rook, after a moment. "I put a pen down on a desk at a fuel depot ten weeks ago and told four children we weren't going back to that ledger, and three of them are standing here."

"It isn't that ledger," said Quist.

"It's a bit that ledger."

"Yes," said Registrar Helen Quist. "It is a bit."

She turned the page round and put the pen on it, and Elias Marsh picked the pen up and looked at it for about three seconds, which was as long as he had looked at anything since the tip road, and signed his own name at the bottom of a piece of Meridian paper.

---

"Last item," said Quist. "The roll wants a name for the company."

"We haven't got one."

"Everybody says that."

"We're not a company," said Rook. "I've told you that twice and I'll keep telling you. There's a woman in your ward and there are three children and there's me, and the only thing any of us has ever been is people who didn't get left behind."

Quist looked at the four of them for a moment.

"Then I'll tell you what I'm going to do," she said, "and you can be as annoyed about it as you like. I'm going to leave the field blank for thirty days. At thirty days, if there's nothing in it, the roll writes one for you off the officer's name, and you'll have it for the rest of your lives and you will not like it."

"That's a threat," said Wyck.

"It's a filing system," said Quist. "They're very often the same thing."

---

She was packing the ledgers when Toren said, "There's a book here from Tallow Gate."

Quist stopped.

"There is."

"A warden called Braid. She wrote our names in it the day we walked in off the loop road, and she told us it comes here twice a year and it doesn't get lost."

"It came up with the spring wagons," said Quist. "It's in the west rack. Do you want to see it?"

---

It was a green ledger with a cracked spine and Ottoline Braid's small square hand all through it, and the page was near the back, and it was exactly as Toren remembered it because he had watched it being written.

Coldwater. The date. The milepost.

*Prentice, Odo. Guide. Twenty-two crossings.*

*Tanner, Nell.*

*Cole, Ilsa.*

*Voss, Garrick.*

And in the column beside, in the same hand, four surviving: *Voss, T., 13. Cole, D., 14. Tanner, W., 15. Faust, M., 13.*

Toren stood with both hands on the edge of the rack.

"He's in the wrong column," he said.

Quist came and looked over his shoulder.

"When?"

"The thirteenth of the ninth month. A little before four in the morning, on the south apron of the old weir on the Reave, about eleven miles east of a place called Greenmill." His voice was doing something and he let it. "He was thirteen years and six months. He's buried in the top field above the mill ruin and his name's cut into the sluice-house lintel and I did it myself and it's crooked."

"Full name."

"Milo Faust."

Registrar Helen Quist went and got a pen.

She did not use the green ledger. She took down the current book, the one with this year on the spine, and turned to the running page, and wrote the date and the place and the name in a hand so plain that a stranger would be able to read it in a hundred years, and then she turned the book round so that he could see it.

"That's permanent now," she said. "It doesn't get lost."

---

The setters went out at dusk.

There were about forty of them and they came out through the gate yard in twos and threes with canvas bags over their shoulders and nothing in their hands, and they were between thirteen and nineteen years old, and they were talking to each other the way people talk when they do a thing every day.

Toren Voss stood against the wall of the yard with his hands in his pockets and watched them go.

He had, in one pocket, a paper receipt for a grey rod the length of his forearm with four generations of Voss thumbs worn into a band round the middle of it. He had, in the other, under two rows of stitches, a flat dark thing with nine cuts across the face of it, which had been put through the best instruments on this continent twice, sixty-one years ago, and had come back both times as an empty tray, and which a man with no name had put into the hands of a woman nobody in his family had ever spoken of, along with a sentence about the end of the world.

He did not know what it was.

His father had not known. His father's father had not known, and Hester Voss had not known, and she had asked a wall of machines to take it off her and they had given it back, and every single one of them had carried it anyway.

Out past the ditch and the cleared ground, half a mile out where nobody lived, the first of the lights came up.

Then another, further along. Then, all the way round, the rest.
