# Chapter 23 — Wider at the Bottom

There was no stone on the Level.

Toren worked that out standing on the platform at half past seven in the morning with the sun coming up out of four miles of water, and it was the first thing that whole day that got through to him properly, because it was a practical problem and practical problems were a place he could stand.

They had built three cairns in a rock cut, out of scree, a hundred and forty yards each way. Thirty-one trips.

Here there was a bank made of earth, a road made of rammed hardcore, and eleven miles of water with a drowned country in it.

"Rook."

"I know." Rook was sitting on the coping of the low wall with his left forearm across his knee and his face the colour of the brick behind him. "Look at what you're sitting in the middle of."

Toren looked.

Three storeys of dark brick with the roof gone, and the bottom of it full of rubble.

---

They were four people who could work and one of them had one arm.

That was the arithmetic. Nobody said it out loud and everybody knew it. Rook could not lift a brick. He tried twice and then stopped trying in front of everybody, which cost him something and which he did anyway, because he had been told at a stone heap what happens when a man arranges things for other people without telling them.

Wyck carried them two at a time against his own ribs, the wrong way, which was the only way he could do it, and made ninety-odd trips and never once let anybody take one off him.

Dessa carried them in a folded blanket, dragging it, step and drag and stop, because she could not carry and walk at the same time and had found the one way round that in about four minutes.

Toren used his good arm and did thirty-one trips, because thirty-one was the number, and because at some point in the middle of it he understood that he was going to do thirty-one and no other number for the rest of his life, and that this was not a thing he was going to be able to explain to anybody.

Senna sat propped in the cart at the edge of the platform and gave directions about the bricks.

"Wider at the bottom," she said.

"Yes, Grandmother."

"That's a cairn, not a chimney. Wider at the bottom or it's down by the first frost."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Senna Voss, in a completely different voice, and neither of them said anything else for an hour and a half.

---

They put it at the landward end of the sluice wall.

Rook chose the spot and did not explain it, and Toren did not need it explained. It was the last flat place on the platform before the stone went out. If a person stood at the foot of that cairn and looked west, there were eighty yards of pale stone going away into the water, with three black gate frames in it, and then nothing.

It took them until two in the afternoon. It came up about four feet and went seven feet across the bottom. It was the ugliest thing any of them ever made, and it was still standing there when Toren Voss was an old man.

---

Amery came up the bank at about noon with a barrow.

He had walked four miles of his own road pushing it, at seventy, having seen nine people come off the south ramp at first light and go away south down the road without stopping at his gate.

There were two full cans of water in the barrow. There was also a spade, a coil of cord, half a cheese, and a bag of pears off a tree that had been trained flat against a gable by somebody patient.

He put the barrow down at the edge of the platform and looked at what they were building and did not ask anybody one single question about it.

"How many?" he said.

"One," said Rook.

Amery took his cap off.

Then he put it back on and got the spade out of the barrow, and went and cut turf out of the verge for two hours, in squares. He brought them over one at a time and laid them round the bottom of the cairn so the bricks would not shift.

He would not let any of them help him do it.

---

"I told them where you were," he said, at about four, to Rook, on the low wall.

"I know you did."

"I've been three days deciding how to say that to you."

"You said it at the gate in your own garden before I'd asked you a single question," said Rook. "There isn't a better version and there is nothing I want from you. Sit down and eat your own cheese, you've walked four miles with a barrow."

Amery sat down on the wall.

"Was he yours?" he said.

Rook did not answer that for some time.

"No," he said at last. "He was his mother's, and she's under a cairn at the south mouth of a rock cut two hundred miles that way, and I put bread on it for her without ever having met her."

---

He did the bread at about six o'clock in the evening, in front of all of them, without any ceremony at all.

It was the last of it. It had gone hard four days ago and Milo had cut most of it up at two in the morning in the dark and it had been in Rook's coat since, uneaten, because Milo had given it to him second and he had not got round to it.

Rook broke it in half.

He put one half back inside his coat.

Then he crouched at the head of the cairn and set the other half on the flat brick they had put on the top for it, square in the middle, and turned it a quarter turn so the flat side faced the sky.

Then he stayed crouched, with his forearms on his knees, and looked at it.

Toren stood four feet away and waited for the thing the man said, and found that he was frightened of it, and made himself stay.

"I knew him thirty-four days," said Rook.

Nobody moved.

"He asked me four hundred questions and I answered about eleven of them, and every single one of the ones I didn't answer, I didn't answer because I had decided it would be better for him to find it out himself. And he did find them out himself. All of them. Every last one, and faster than I would have given them to him." His voice did not change. "That is a thing I am going to be carrying, and I want it said out loud here rather than found out about me later.

"He was the only one of the five who never once asked me for anything for himself.

"And he did the arithmetic on that bank on his own account, at fourteen, in the dark, and he got it right. It was right. I have been over it eleven times since four this morning looking for the place where it was wrong and there isn't one. The road opened because he opened it. The cart went through because he made it go through. Every single person standing at this brick heap is standing here because of what a boy worked out on a low wall at half past three in the morning and did not ask anybody's permission for."

He stopped.

"I'm no good at this," he said.

And he got up and walked away down the platform to the far end of it and stood with his back to them. Nobody followed him. Nobody said one word about it.

---

Dessa did the rest.

She did it about an hour later, when the light had gone orange on the water, and she did it without warning anybody. Toren understood afterward that she had been putting it together in her head all day, the way she put a rota together.

She had the last of the bread — Milo's own piece, the end crust and the ruined bit, still in the fold of cloth he had put it in and not eaten. She cut that into six with the small knife, poorly, which was not an accident.

Then she stood up with her bad leg under her and went round.

"Senna," she said, and gave her one.

"Wyck."

"Me," she said, and took her own, and did not make anything of it.

"Toren."

She stopped in front of the cairn with the last two in her hand.

"Milo," said Dessa Cole, and put one on the brick beside Rook's half loaf, and turned it a quarter, because she had watched a man do it that afternoon and she was not going to get any part of this wrong.

Then she turned round.

Rook had come back up the platform at some point and was standing eleven feet off with his hands at his sides.

"And him last," said Dessa.

---

He did not move.

"He said it twice," said Dessa. "He said it and then he made me say it back so he knew it was had. *Senna, Wyck, Dessa, Toren, Milo, and him last, and he's to actually eat it, and somebody's got to make him, because he won't.*"

Rook stood on a brick platform in the middle of eleven miles of drowned country.

"That's the order now," said Dessa. "It's not mine. I'm only the one carrying it. Come and get it, please."

He came and got it.

And Toren watched a man of thirty-one take a ruined end of four-day-old bread out of a fourteen-year-old girl's hand and eat the whole of it standing up, while she watched him do it, because a boy had told her to make him.

---

*Here is what nobody tells you about the day after.*

It does not stop. That is the whole of it. There is no part of it where anything is put down.

The water can had to be filled from Amery's barrow and the barrow had to go back down the bank, and Amery would not take the cheese back and had to be argued with about it for eleven minutes. Wyck's strapping had come loose across the front and had to be redone. Rook could not do it left-handed. Dessa did it poorly twice and then did it well. Toren's shoulder had to be put back and it was Senna who did it, from a cart, with Wyck holding him, because Rook said he could not be certain of the strength in his hands and would not risk it, which frightened Toren more than anything that had happened in the dark.

And at some point in the evening Toren found himself standing at the tailgate with the horn scoop in his hand, about to go and do a thing, and could not for a moment think what it was.

It was the water.

It had always been the water. Somebody had always gone and done the water, every single evening for thirty-four days, without being asked, usually before anybody thought of it.

Toren stood at the tailgate of a hand cart on an eleven-mile bank with a scoop in his fist and found out what that is like.

---

Wyck took the watch that night because there was nobody else who could.

Rook did not argue about it. That was the measure of the day. He sat down against the near wheel at about ten o'clock with his bound forearm across his knee and his back on the iron and did not get up again, and at some point after midnight Toren looked over and found that the shape of him had changed.

His eyes were shut.

Toren lay still for a long time and looked at that, and did not go and tell anybody, and understood that he was looking at the only good thing that had happened on the whole of the Level.

---

He was awake, then, for the other thing. He never told anybody that either.

It was about three in the morning. Wyck was on the low wall at the east side of the platform with the iron bar across his knees, facing south down the bank, the way he had been facing since ten.

And Rook got up off the wheel and went over and sat down on the wall beside him.

Toren lay eleven feet away with his cheek on his arm and his eyes open in the dark, and did not move a muscle. He heard the whole of it. He never repeated one word of it to a living soul until he was a very old man.

---

"Wyck."

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"I've had four hours. It's more than I've had in five weeks and I'll take it and be grateful." A pause. "I'm going to do a thing now and I'd like you to let me get to the end of it before you say anything, because I have been thirty-four days working out how to do it and about eleven years before that."

Wyck did not say anything.

"At a stone heap on an open shoulder you asked me for something. You said that when it was finished there'd be a thing left over that I still wouldn't have said, because there always is, and you asked me to say it to you first. And I said yes, and I said *you first, when it's finished.*

"And at a fire in a ruined yard you held me to it in front of everybody, and I said good, and I meant it."

"I remember."

"Then here it is," said Rook. "It's finished."

---

The water made the small noise water makes against stone facing at three in the morning. Away south, four miles off, nothing at all was happening on eleven miles of bank.

"You asked me at that fire why not tonight," said Rook, "and I told you it was because of what I'd have to do the morning after, and four faces looked at me and not one of you understood it, and I did not help you.

"So I'll say it properly, because you're owed it properly.

"Rook isn't a name. It's a thing I got called when I was nineteen and had nothing else. Everything that happened before I was nineteen happened to somebody with a different name, and that somebody made a promise in a room to a girl with four minutes left, and did not keep it, and I have spent eleven years not being him on purpose.

"And I could go on not being him for exactly as long as I was a man walking toward a thing. That's what it's for. A man walking toward a thing does not have to be anybody. He has a direction and that's enough, and it will hold a person up for eleven years and I am living proof of it.

"I gave the direction away yesterday morning on a road at about five o'clock, in front of your friend Toren, in exchange for a hundred and fifty yards. I am not going to get it back. She'll be four hundred miles from here inside a fortnight and there will not be one line in any book anywhere that says where.

"So the morning after is this morning, and what I have to do is be somebody, because there's six people and seventy miles and nothing else left to be."

---

Wyck Tanner sat on a low wall in the dark on the Level with an iron bar across his knees.

"Say it, then," he said.

"Elias Marsh."

---

Toren lay eleven feet away and heard a man say two words out loud on a bank in the dark, and found that his own eyes had gone, and did not move his hands to do anything about it in case he was heard.

For a long moment nothing happened at all.

Then Wyck said: "Say it again."

"Elias Marsh."

"Which one did she use?"

Toren heard the breath go into the man and come out again.

"Elias," said Rook. "Nobody else ever did. Not my mother, who used the whole of it when she was displeased and *Eli* the rest of the time, and not one person at that station in five years, because at that station you were whatever your tradition would have called you and mine would have called me Marsh."

"And she wouldn't."

"She would not." A pause. "She thought the tradition thing was a stupid game. She said so at fifteen, at the top of her voice, to a man who ran the place."

---

Wyck did not say anything for a while.

"I'm not going to thank you," he said at last. "You'd hate it and it isn't the shape of the thing."

"No."

"And I'm going to ask you one, and you can say no."

"Ask."

"The strap's cut," said Wyck. "I've been looking at it since yesterday. It's gone through at the tongue end and you've had it knotted round your wrist all day and you think nobody's noticed, and it wants a new hole put in it with an awl, and you have got one hand." He did not turn his head. "Let me do it in the morning. I'm good at it. My father did harness."

There was a silence of about four seconds.

"Yes," said Rook.

---

He gave it to the rest of them at first light, at the cairn, and it took eleven seconds and there was no ceremony in it whatever.

They were all there. Senna was propped up in the cart with the tailgate down and Amery's blanket over her knees. Dessa sat on the bricks at the foot of it with her leg out straight. Wyck stood. Toren stood.

Rook came and stood at the head of the cairn with his left forearm bound in a strip of shirt and Vera Kess's Ember strapped over the top of the binding on a strap with a fresh hole punched in it by a boy with an awl at six o'clock in the morning.

"Three things," he said, "and then we're going, because we've eleven miles of this and a woman in a cart.

"One. Wyck has had this already, an hour ago, on a wall, because he asked me for it at a stone heap sixteen days ago and I said yes and I'm not going to make it smaller by pretending anybody got it at the same time. He got it first. That's what was agreed and that's what happened.

"Two. My name is Elias Marsh."

Toren watched Dessa Cole put her hand flat on the bricks beside her and leave it there.

"Rook will do. It's done thirty-four days and eleven years and I'd rather not have to answer to something new on a bank. But you'll have it, and you'll have it from me, and none of you is ever going to have to wonder about it again."

---

"And three," he said.

He looked at the brick heap.

"Milo Faust was Kindled. That's the word and it's the bottom rung, and it is the only word anybody could honestly put on him, and every single one of you has been carefully not saying it for four weeks and he knew you were doing it and he told Toren so, three hours before the end, in the dark, in a building with no roof.

"I'm not going to improve it. If I stood here and gave him a better word than he had, that'd be a thing I was doing for me.

"So instead I'll tell you what that ladder is.

"It's a measure of how much of a person is lit. That's all it has ever been and it is a good measure and it is the best anybody has ever made. At Homura we had four years and two instrument benches and nine traditions of children and we measured that thing to within a hair, every ninth day, for four years, and we were going to end the oldest argument in the world with a column of numbers.

"And there is no rung on it anywhere for what a person will do.

"It never occurred to a single one of the sixty of us that there could be. It has not occurred to me in eleven years. It occurred to me at about a quarter to four yesterday morning, standing on a stone wall, and it is the only thing I have learned in eleven years that Orrin Vell did not teach me."

He put his hand flat on the top brick for a second.

"Right," he said. "That's the whole of it. Get the cart round."

---

They went north off the platform at about seven in the morning on the thirty-fifth day.

Amery walked with them as far as the arch and no further, because his road went the other way and because he had four books in a pear tree and a copy to make. He shook hands with every one of them, including Senna, leaning into the cart to do it, and he got to Toren last.

"You'll be counting," he said.

Toren stared at him.

"I've watched you at it all day and half of yesterday. You do it with your mouth shut and your eyes going." Amery let go of his hand. "I've walked eleven miles of a bank for twenty-nine years and I count the yards between every soft place on it, and I'll tell you the one thing about it nobody ever told me.

"It doesn't stop. And it isn't a fault."

Then he went south with his barrow and they did not see him again.

---

They went under the arch at half past seven.

Toren looked up at the brick as they came through it, eleven feet of road with a parapet either side and the drain going away black underneath. He made himself look. Then he looked forward.

Six and a half miles of dead straight bank went away in front of them with water on both sides and nothing standing on it anywhere.

Wyck was at the tailgate on his elbow, facing backward, saying what he could see out loud every two minutes even when it was nothing. Especially when it was nothing.

Dessa was at the head of the shafts calling the ground. She did not say a number of anything all morning, and she would never say a number of people again as long as she lived.

Senna slept, poorly, and coughed, and did not complain about the road surface once, which Toren minded more than the coughing.

And Rook walked on the near side of the cart with one arm bound and a name, at exactly the pace the slowest of them could manage, forty yards closer in than he had ever walked before.

---

Toren pulled the near shaft with his good arm and did the sums, because the sums were the handrail.

Six and a half miles of bank. Then seventy miles of country nobody in that cart had seen. Then a gate, and a wall, and a doctor, and whatever Meridian turned out to be.

Five of them.

He had it in his head as a number before he could stop it arriving. It came up wrong. He had to put it down and pick it up again.

*Five.*

He carried it about a mile and a half, and then he did the thing he had learned to do at the top of a lock flight and had done exactly twice since.

He put the number down.

It went, the way a number goes when you let it.

And then there were only five people and a hand cart going north up a raised road across eleven miles of drowned country in the early morning, with the water flat and bright on both hands, and a brick heap somewhere behind them on a platform with the flat side of a piece of bread facing the sky.
