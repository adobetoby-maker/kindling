# Chapter 20 — Eight Feet

The man at eighty yards said the three things, and he said them in the right order.

"Nine of us. None hurt. Going north."

Toren heard Wyck's breath go out behind him in the cart. He did not turn round. He stood at the near shaft with his hands on the wood and his feet where they had been put, and he found that every single part of him wanted to look at the water instead of the man.

"Six of us," Rook said. "One old, one mending. Going north."

"Then we've a difficulty," said the man, pleasantly, "because there's one road."

"There is."

"Hoyle would like to talk to you about it. Not shout at you about it across eighty yards of bank, which I'd take as a favour, because I've been shouting since breakfast and my throat's gone."

"Who's Hoyle?"

"Hoyle's the one who'll be doing the talking," said the man. "My name's Lask, if it's any use to you. It generally isn't."

---

Toren looked at him for a long time afterward, in his head, and could never make him into anything.

He was about thirty-five. He had a spear, upright, butt on the road, with his hand well down the shaft where a hand goes when a man is not thinking about it. He had a coat with a repair on the shoulder that somebody had made a nice job of. He stood easy with his weight on both feet and his palms turned out, and he was the first person Toren had ever seen do the eighty yards properly who was not Rook.

"That's ours," Wyck said quietly, from the cart.

"It isn't," said Rook. "It's the road's. Everybody who's been alive on it ten years works it out. I didn't invent it and neither did she."

"It's still ours."

"Yes," said Rook. "It's still ours."

---

He put them two hundred yards short and he did it in front of Lask, at a walking pace, without any attempt to hide what he was doing, which Toren understood about a minute later had been the point.

Two hundred yards up the bank the road was sixteen feet wide and had nothing on it. No verge worth the name. No cover. Nothing to stand behind and nothing to stand on.

"Here," Rook said. "Cart across the road, not along it. Both wheels chocked. Wyck on the tailgate facing north, Dessa on the bed facing south, and neither of you comes down off it for any reason at all."

"Why across?" said Milo.

"Because across, it's a thing they have to get round, and along, it's a thing they can walk beside." Rook chocked the near wheel himself with a piece of brick out of his pack, which he had been carrying for four days and which nobody had known was in there. "Milo. Stones out, in your hand, not your pocket. Stand at the off wheel. If anybody comes past me on this bank you will have about four seconds and you are not going to get a better four seconds than that for the rest of your life."

"All right."

"Dessa."

"Hands," said Dessa.

"Hands. All the way through. Say them out loud even if I can't hear you, because Wyck will hear you and Wyck is going to remember them." Rook turned round. "Toren. With me and one step behind."

---

They walked two hundred yards up a bank in the early afternoon with the water flat and brown on both hands. Toren felt every one of them. Afterward he could not remember a single thing about that walk except that Rook's shadow went along the road on his left and never once got longer or shorter.

At forty yards he could see the platform properly.

It opened out west off the bank the way Amery had said, a made yard of brick and stone about sixty yards long, with a low wall round two sides of it and the engine house standing in the middle of the yard, three storeys of dark brick with the sky going through the window holes. A broken chimney stub. A heap of rotted coal that had gone to a black hummock with willowherb on it.

There was a blue two-wheel cart on the platform with a tarpaulin over it, and a fire with a pot, and washing on a line between two iron rings set in the engine house wall.

Washing on a line. Toren had not seen washing on a line since Stell Cross.

And out at the west end of the platform, the sluice wall ran off into the water, pale and straight and eighty yards long, with three black gate frames in it and nothing at the end.

---

Nine people, and Toren made himself do the count that Dessa would have done, and got it wrong twice.

Three on the low wall. Two at the fire. One up in a window hole of the engine house, first floor, sitting in it with his legs down the outside and a crossbow across his thighs, entirely relaxed, looking at them the way a man looks at weather.

One at the cart with the tarpaulin half back, doing something with a strap.

Lask, coming back at an angle to be neither in front of anybody nor behind anybody.

And a woman standing in the middle of the road at the edge of the platform with her hands loose at her sides, waiting.

Hands. Eighteen of them. Fifteen showing.

Toren did the arithmetic and his stomach went down a hole.

---

She was about fifty-five, small, grey, wearing a good coat that had been mended in three places by three different people. She had a short-handled billhook through her belt and she did not put her hand anywhere near it at any point in the next quarter of an hour.

"Stop there, please," she said, at about fifteen feet. "That's near enough for both of us and I'd rather not do the dance."

Rook stopped.

"And the boy stays where he is."

"The boy goes where I go."

"The boy," said Hoyle, "is thirteen or fourteen and has a warmth on him you could find in a fog, and if he comes past that brick in the road there's a man in a window who has instructions, and I would rather not spend the afternoon apologising to you about it." She said it kindly and without any pressure on it. "Not a threat. A fact about my arrangements. Stop at the brick."

There was a brick lying in the road. Toren had walked past forty of them and had not seen this one, and he understood, standing on it, that it had been put there some time before they arrived.

He stopped at the brick. Rook went on four paces and stopped at eight feet.

---

"Right," said Hoyle. "I'm going to do this poorly, because I've had eleven days to get it right and it's come out worse each time.

"For eleven days I've been walking into yards where somebody already knew my name. Do you know how strange that is? It's never happened to me. Not once in nineteen years." She put her head slightly on one side. "At Wenn's Ford the Ridleys had already split their book. Three ways. Copies made in two nights and the originals gone and a whole family standing on a shingle bank being polite at me with their hands showing. At Marram Head a woman of ninety put a box in my arms and told me to help myself, and the box had nothing in it, and she laughed at me. *She laughed at me.*"

"Good," said Rook.

"It was good. I thought so at the time and I've not changed my mind." Hoyle's mouth did something dry. "And at that ford there's a mark cut on a stone that my people didn't cut, and at Marram Head there's a woman walking the water saying my company's name out loud in every yard she comes to, and she's got three of my chits in her coat, and she didn't buy them.

"So. Here's what I want, and there's only the one thing.

"I want the names of everybody you've told."

---

The water went out flat behind her for four miles.

"No," said Rook.

"Don't do that yet."

"I'm not doing anything yet. I'm telling you the answer now so you don't spend twenty minutes buying it."

"You'll have the twenty minutes anyway. I've come a long way for them." She did not move her feet. "Here's what's on the other side of it, and I'd ask you to hear the whole of it before you decide you've heard it.

"Your people go north. All of them. Today, past me, up that road, with the cart, and nobody follows them and nobody is on the bank behind them. I'll put a man at the arch to make sure of it and you can pick which man.

"You go north too. That's the part people don't believe. I don't want you off the road. I don't want a fight on a bank with a woman in a cart in it. I've got a broken axle on a two-wheel cart and four books I'd like to get to a dry place and eight people I have been feeding out of a pot for five days."

"And the names."

"And the names. Said out loud, here, in front of your own, so that I can watch you say them and know you're not inventing." Hoyle spread her hands. "That's the whole trade. It's a worse trade for you than it looks and a better one than you're about to call it."

---

Toren stood on a brick in a road and discovered that he was doing exactly what he had been told for twenty days not to do.

He was listening.

Not to the sense of it. He had the sense of it inside four words. He was listening to the *shape* of it, the way you listen to somebody good at singing. Every sentence out of that woman had a small door in it that he wanted to walk through. He had turned his head twice already without meaning to.

*He will talk. Every single word out of his mouth in those eight feet is a hand pushing you somewhere.*

Toren put his feet flat and looked at the middle of Hoyle's chest and did not look at her face again.

---

"There's a third thing," Hoyle said, "and I'll put it last because you'll like it least.

"The old woman in that cart is going to Meridian. You're nine days out with a hand cart on a bank, and you'll do seven of them at four miles an hour if the weather holds. I've a mule and a two-wheel and a man who has driven that road eleven times, and I can have her at the north gate in five."

Rook did not say anything at all.

"That's not a trick and it's not a hostage," said Hoyle. "You can send one of your own with her. Send two. I'll take the girl with the leg as well, and she can hold a knife on my driver the whole way for all I care."

"Why would you do that?"

"Because it costs me a mule for nine days and it buys me the thing I want, and because I have carried an old woman before now and it did not kill me." Hoyle's chin came up half an inch. "And because you've just done twenty minutes of arithmetic in your face and you've got to the part where she doesn't make it, and I would rather be looking at a man who's had that offer than a man who hasn't."

---

Rook stood at eight feet on a raised bank over eleven miles of drowned country.

"I'll take that one back to her," he said. "She's the only person who can answer it and I'm not going to answer it for her."

"That's fair."

"It isn't fair. It's correct." He did not move. "Now I'll ask you two things and then I'll go.

"The eleventh of the ninth month. Eleven years back."

Hoyle's face did not do one single thing.

"What about it?"

"Where were you?"

"I've no idea," she said. "Eleven years ago I was on the Marches with a different crew and a different man over me, and I could tell you what I was doing in the ninth month of that year about as well as you could tell me what you had for your dinner. Is that a real question?"

"It was."

"Then it's a poor one and you've wasted it." She shifted her weight for the first time. "Ask the second."

"The paybook," said Rook.

And Toren, standing on a brick twelve feet away with his hands open at his sides, watched a woman of fifty-five come off the road she had been standing on for a quarter of an hour.

It took about a half second. Her feet did not move and her hands did not move. Something went out of her shoulders and something else came into them, and when she spoke again the pleasantness had gone off it like paint off a wall.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You've asked. You asked when you said the word." Hoyle looked at him for a long moment. "Who are you?"

"Nobody you've met."

"That's not an answer, and you knew it wasn't when you gave it to me." She stepped back one pace, which put her at nine feet, which Toren understood afterward had been the whole conversation ending. "Go and ask your old woman. Come back and tell me. And when you come back, be somebody who has decided, because I have eight people on this platform who have been very patient for five days and I am running out of ways to describe you to them."

---

Toren looked round at the platform once, on the way out, because he could not help it, and because he wanted to be able to say numbers to Dessa that were true.

He got seven of them.

Lask with the spear butt on the road. The big one at the fire, forty-odd, wide as a door, with a boarding axe leaning on the wall beside him. The woman at the blue cart with the strap, thirty or so, small hands, who looked up and looked at Rook and did not look away when he looked back. The one in the window with the crossbow. A young one at the fire with nothing in his hands and everything in his face, nineteen at the outside, who had been staring at Toren since the brick. Two on the low wall whose names Toren never learned and never found out.

And an eighth, sitting on the coal hummock with his back half to them and a bit of soft wood in his left hand and a small knife in his right, taking a curl off it, and not looking up.

Toren looked straight at him for about two seconds.

He was never afterward able to say what colour that man's coat was.

---

Senna took eleven seconds over it.

He gave her the whole offer at the tailgate with everybody standing round, in order, without improving any part of it, including the mule and the five days and the part about sending two of them with her.

"No," said Senna Voss.

"Senna —"

"Be quiet, Toren." She had her hands folded on the blanket and her chin was level. "Young man. Say the thing you left out, because you left something out and you did it on purpose and I'd like it in the open."

Rook put his hand flat on the cart rail.

"She meant it," he said.

"Yes. I thought she did." Senna nodded slowly. "That's the thing about it, isn't it. If she was lying it'd be easy."

"It would."

"So say the rest."

"There's no rest that's a fact. There's what I think, and what I think is worth exactly what any of you decide it's worth." Rook's voice did not change by one degree. "She'd take you. She'd get you there. She would very likely get you there alive, and I'd put it at better than even. And somewhere between here and Meridian somebody would say a thing in front of you, or you'd see a thing in a cart, and then there would be an old woman and two children who had been inside a company's arrangements for five days.

"And that's a record," said Rook. "That's the only kind they've never been able to buy."

---

Nobody said anything for a while. The bank went north and south, empty both ways. Out east a wind came across four miles of water and did nothing to it at all.

"Well," said Senna. "Then I'd have said no anyway, and I'd like that noted, because I want it perfectly clear that I'm not being saved from anything."

"It's noted," said Rook.

"I have had one job on this road for thirty-three days and it is to lie in this cart and not be an argument. I'm not going to become one now because a woman was decent to me at eighty yards." She lay back down and shut her eyes. "Go and tell her no. And Toren — come here."

He came.

"Don't do that with your face," said Senna. "Whatever it is. I've watched it since you were six and it has never once helped."

---

Rook went back up alone and was gone four minutes and came back walking exactly the same way he had gone.

"Right," he said. "She's taken it well and that's the worst news I've had all month.

"There's a thing you should all have. It's this. She has not once, in twenty-five minutes, been unpleasant to me, and she is not going to be. When it starts it will start in the middle of a sentence and it will be a perfectly civil sentence, and there will not be a warning, because a warning costs her a second and a half she does not intend to spend.

"Now here's what they've done, and I want you to understand it before you see it, because you'll not have time when you do."

He turned and looked north.

"They've gone off the platform."

---

Toren went up the cart rail onto the bed beside Dessa. Dessa did not tell him to get down. He looked north over the roof of the engine house.

The platform was empty. The fire was smoking on its own. The washing was still on the line.

Four hundred yards further up, where the bank humped into a low green swell against the sky, there were people standing.

"Seven," said Dessa. "Fourteen hands. Twelve showing."

"Where's the other two?"

"Not there," said Dessa.

---

"The arch," said Rook. "That's their ground and it's been their ground since the day they walked up this bank and looked at it, which is five days ago, and the platform has been a kitchen.

"Say why."

Wyck said it, from the tailgate, without any hesitation at all.

"Because it's eleven foot wide."

"Because it's eleven foot wide." Rook nodded once. "This bank is sixteen. Sixteen feet is a road. Eleven feet with a parapet either side is a door, and it is the only door between here and the north end of the Level, and everything north of it — Meridian, a gate, a doctor, the rest of all our lives — is on the far side of it.

"They do not have to come and get us. They do not have to do one thing. They have taken the gate, which is the first thing they do and the only thing they have ever done, and now they will stand on it and wait, and in two days there will be no water in that can and this will be finished without anybody touching anybody."

"Then we take the platform," said Dessa.

"We take the platform. It's got a wall and a door and a roof I don't trust and eighty yards of stone going out into the water, and it is the only thing standing on eleven miles." Rook picked up the near shaft. "And then, at about eight o'clock this evening, I am going to go and try that arch, and I am going to tell you now that I do not think it will work."

---

*Here is what it is like to walk into a place that other people have just left.*

The fire was still going. There was a pot on it with barley and onion in it, hot, more than half full. Somebody had put a flat stone on the lid.

There were nine bedrolls in the lee of the engine house wall, laid out in a row with a gap of exactly the same width between each one, and every single one of them rolled and strapped.

The washing was three shirts and a pair of stockings, and it had been pegged out with the pegs going the same way.

And on the inside of the engine house doorway, out of the weather, there was a tally chalked up on the brick: five gates, five dates, and a running figure at the bottom that Toren could not make anything of.

"Don't eat that," said Rook, without looking at the pot.

"It's not —"

"I know it isn't. Don't eat it anyway, and I'll tell you why once and not again." He put the cart in against the engine house wall where the brick came round in an L and made two sides of shelter out of nothing. "Because in about two hours you are going to want to think of them as people, and you will be right, and you will be no use to me at all. Eat your own bread."

---

Wyck stood in the doorway for a long time.

It faced east onto the road and it went five feet wide. The lintel was a brick arch with a length of iron in it. The threshold was a stone slab worn into a dish in the middle by about two hundred years of boots.

A seventy-year-old man had described it to him in the dark in a shed, and he had asked eleven questions about it. Toren had listened to every one of them lying on a hurdle with his coat over him. *Which way does it face. How wide. Is there rubble in it. Is the lintel sound. Is there anything above it that comes down.*

"It's four inches narrower than he said," Wyck said.

"Is that bad?"

"It's better." He put his hand flat on the jamb. "Four inches better."

Then he sat down on the threshold with his back against one side and his boots against the other and the iron bar across his knees, and he did not get up again for six hours.

---

They went for the arch at eight, in the last of the light.

Toren had it in his head as a running thing, a charge, the sort of thing a person imagines when they have never done it. It was not that at all. It was four people walking up a road at an ordinary pace with a hand cart, in the open, for three hundred and eighty yards, with Rook forty feet out in front. It took nine and a half minutes. They were the single worst nine and a half minutes of Toren's life to that point.

Nobody on the arch moved.

At two hundred yards Dessa said, "The bow's changed hands."

"Say that again."

"The one who had it in the window's on the south end with nothing in his hands at all. The bow's up on the north parapet now and it's somebody else holding it." She had her chin down. "It's the one off the coal heap. The one with the knife and the bit of wood."

"Why would they do that?" said Milo.

"Because the north end is where the road goes," said Rook.

At a hundred and fifty she said, "Two of them have got down behind the parapet on the far side and I can see four hands and there should be six."

At a hundred Rook said, "Stop."

They stopped.

---

The arch stood across the road against a sky that had gone the colour of a bruise.

The bank humped up about four feet to get over the drain beneath it, so the road rose to it and went over it and came down the other side, and on the top of the hump the parapets came in and made the way through eleven feet wide and about thirty feet long.

Six people stood on it. Lask at the south end with his spear down at last, the big one with the axe behind him, the young one behind that. Hoyle back at the north end with her hands at her sides. The crossbow up on the parapet. And a woman standing exactly in the middle of the eleven feet with her boots apart and her palms turned down at waist height, the way a woman settles a sheet onto a bed.

Toren's chest went to water.

He had seen that four times in his life. Three of them were Marta Faust and one of them was a man on a road above a rock cut on the worst morning there had ever been.

"Rook," he said.

"I've seen her."

"That's —"

"I know what it is." Rook did not turn round. His voice had gone down to almost nothing. "That's why they picked eleven feet. On sixteen she'd be holding a curtain with a hole in it. On eleven she shuts the road."

---

Here is how it went, and Toren had it in order for the rest of his life, because he had been given four weeks of being taught to keep things in order and it turned out that was the part that did not switch off.

Rook went at it and he went at it fast.

He put the blade out at nine paces — it came off the back of his right hand the way it always came, without any ceremony at all, three feet of dark metal that did not shine — and he did not go for the woman and he did not go for Lask.

He went for the parapet.

He got up onto the south end of it in one movement that had no separate parts in it, ran four steps along the top of eighteen inches of brick with a fourteen-foot drop on his right, and came down inside the arch behind Lask's spear, which was a thing no person on that road had expected, including Toren, including Lask.

Lask went round. He went round fast and well and got the butt of the spear across and Rook took it off him.

He did not take it off him the way a person disarms somebody. He hit the shaft two inches below the head, downward, with the flat of the blade, and the whole spear went out of Lask's hands and into the drain below the arch and was gone.

And then Rook hit the wall.

---

It was not a sound. That was the thing about it that Toren could not ever describe properly.

There was no crash and nothing flashed. A man going at speed simply stopped, three feet short of a woman standing with her palms turned down, the way a boot stops in thick mud, and every bit of what he had put into it went somewhere and did not come back.

He got a hand on it.

Toren saw that and did not understand it for a year. Rook put his left hand flat on nothing, in the air, at chest height, and leaned. The leaning had the effort in it of a man pushing a cart out of a rut.

The woman's boots slid about two inches on the brick.

She put them back.

"That's *held,*" somebody on the arch said, in a voice with real surprise in it, and Toren thought afterward that it had been Hoyle.

---

"*Rook, north side, two!*" Dessa screamed from the cart.

He was already going. The big one came over the parapet on the north side of the hump with the axe. Rook was not there any more. The axe went into brick and took a lump of it out. After that a great deal happened in eleven feet of space, and Toren saw about a third of it.

He saw the young one come in with an edge out. It was a straight blade off the back of the left hand, plain, well made, no wider than a hand's breadth, and the boy handled it the way Toren handled the hook, which was to say that he put it where the trouble was and did not think about it.

He saw Rook take that on the flat and turn it down into the road.

And he heard the crossbow.

It made almost nothing — a flat wooden knock, like somebody dropping a plank on a floor two rooms away. Then the cart rail jumped eighteen inches from Dessa's hand and a four-inch splinter of white wood stood straight up out of it.

Dessa did not look at it. She did not stop calling for a half second. Toren looked at it for the rest of his life.

And he saw Rook go backward out of the arch. On his feet, facing them, giving ground one pace at a time, down the slope of the hump, with the blade up and his left arm low and bent — and Toren, who had watched that man for thirty-three days and had never once seen him give a single inch of anything to anybody, understood with total clarity that this was not a trick.

"*Back!*" Rook said. "Cart. Now. Turn it and go."

---

They turned it and went and it took eleven seconds and it was too long.

Toren had the near shaft. Milo had the off. Dessa called from the bed on her knees with both hands on the rail. She called it perfectly. It made no difference at all.

"Two off the parapet, south side, *in the water* —"

"What?"

"They're in the *water*, they've come down the stones, they're —"

They came up out of the drowned ground on the west side of the bank about forty feet behind the cart.

Toren had spent a day and a half looking at that water and thinking about it as an edge. It had gone down out of sight four feet below the line and it had looked like the end of the world.

It was four feet deep.

They came up the rip-rap on their hands and knees, soaked to the chest, with the black water running off them. One of them was Lask, with no spear and a knife instead. The other Toren never got a name for.

And they came at the tailgate, where an old woman lay, because the tailgate was the part of the cart with nobody at it.

---

Wyck was in the doorway of an engine house two hundred and eighty yards away.

Toren thought about that for a long time afterward, and about how the whole of that evening turned on a boy who had chosen a door fourteen hours early and then had to watch it happen somewhere else.

Milo threw.

He did it without a word and from about thirty feet, backhand, off the wrong foot, with a stone he had been holding in his left hand for nine and a half minutes. It went into the second man's face above the eye and the man's head snapped back and his hands came up.

That was the whole of it. Half a second, and a man with his hands on his own face.

"*Toren!*" Dessa, from the bed. "Left hand!"

Lask's left hand was behind his hip.

Toren dropped the shaft and put his arm out where the trouble was and did not reach for the warmth and did not look at it.

The hook came.

---

He did not aim it at the body and he did not aim it at the middle.

He put it low, behind the knee, at a man standing on wet stone at the top of a slope of rip-rap with his weight on his back foot, and he got his hips under him and he hauled, exactly the way you haul a full bucket up a well.

Lask came off his feet.

He went down onto the stone facing and he went down hard. He slid about six feet on his shoulder, hit the coping at the bottom of the slope, and stopped there, half in the water. He did not get up.

He never made any sound at all.

Toren stood on the top of a bank with a hook on the end of his arm and both his boots on the road and he could not move one single part of himself.

---

Then the second man came and there was nothing behind Toren but a wheel and an old woman.

Toren swung and missed and got hit.

He went down on the road on his back with all the air out of him and the sky going about. A boot came down on his wrist and stayed there. A man's weight came onto his chest.

*And,* Toren thought, quite clearly, *this is it, then.*

Something came over the top of him.

It was Milo. Milo came over the cart shaft in a way that was not any kind of technique and got both arms round the man's head from behind and simply hung there, fourteen years old and eight stone of him, with his feet off the ground.

The weight came off Toren's chest.

He rolled and got his knees and got up.

And Rook arrived.

---

Toren never saw what he did.

He knelt on the road with his ears roaring and one arm not working properly. Behind him something went onto the ground with a sound like a sack. By the time he got round, a man lay on the road on his side, quite still, and Rook stood over him with the blade already gone.

"Milo."

"I'm here — I'm —"

"Get on the shaft. Go."

---

They went the two hundred and eighty yards back to the platform at a run. The cart bounced. Senna held the rails and said nothing, which was her own kind of help. Wyck shouted from the doorway the whole way in and Toren could not hear one word of it.

Nobody followed them.

That was the part that made no sense until later. Seven people stood on that arch. Two of their own lay down on the road behind a running cart with the way wide open. Nobody came.

At the platform Toren got the wheel chocked with his good hand and turned round and looked back up the bank.

Four people were coming down it at a walk. Not at them. Off to the west side, down the rip-rap, to the two men lying at the bottom of it, and they went into the water up to their thighs and they got the first one up under the arms and carried him.

Toren watched Cinder Company carry their own dead man four hundred yards up a bank in the last of the light, and found that his hands had started, and could not make them stop.

---

The other one lived.

Rook told them so at about eleven that night, sitting on the threshold beside Wyck with his forearms on his knees, and he said it because Milo asked and because Rook did not lie to Milo about things like that.

"The one you took the legs out from under is dead. He went down eight feet of stone facing onto a coping stone at the bottom and it will have been his neck or his head and it will have been immediate, and he did not know anything about it. That is not a comfort. I'm not offering it as one."

Toren sat with his back against the cart wheel and his right arm strapped across him and did not say anything.

"The other one is sitting up. He'll have a bad head for a fortnight and he'll be no use to anybody with a bow for longer than that." Rook turned his hand over. "Milo. Where did you put it?"

"Over the eye. I aimed at the eye."

"You aimed at the eye and you got the bone above it, at thirty feet, backhand, moving, in bad light." Rook looked at him. "That's the best thing anybody did on this bank today and I want it said out loud in front of everybody before anyone goes to sleep."

Milo Faust put both his hands over his face and did not take them down for some time.

---

Here is what the day cost, and Toren had it all written on a slate by midnight because Dessa would not let anybody sleep until it was.

One of theirs dead. One of theirs hurt.

Wyck: nothing at all, because Wyck had sat in a doorway for six hours and had not been needed, and it had taken four people to talk him out of the position he took about that.

Toren: a shoulder that had come half out and gone back in, strapped, not to be used for three days, and a wrist with a boot print on it.

Milo: nothing.

Dessa: nothing.

And Rook, who had gone up a bank at a third of the room and had come back down it, sat on the threshold of an engine house with his hands open on his knees and would not let Dessa put two fingers on his wrist, which he had never refused before.

"Tomorrow," he said. "You'll get a truer one tomorrow and it will be a worse one, and there's no sense having two."

---

"Say the part we don't know," said Wyck.

He was on the threshold with the bar across his knees and he had not moved off it since eight o'clock.

Rook looked out at the water.

"The part you don't know is that I was wrong about the shape of this," he said. "I have been thinking about that arch since two o'clock this afternoon and I have had it as a door. It isn't a door. It's a door with a wall in it, and I cannot go through that wall, and I could not go through it at full and I am not at full.

"And the second part is worse and you all saw it and nobody has said it yet."

Nobody said it.

"That water isn't a wall either," said Rook. "It's four feet deep and it has hedges in it you can hold on to, and it goes all the way round this platform, and they have been in it once already today and found out how easy it was.

"There is no edge on this place. There is no back of it. We have got a wall and a door and eighty yards of stone going out into nothing, and everything I have taught the four of you about where to stand assumes there is a direction the trouble cannot come from."

He got up.

"There isn't one," he said. "Not here. Get some sleep. I'll be in the doorway."
