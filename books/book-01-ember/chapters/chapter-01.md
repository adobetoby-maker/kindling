# Chapter 1 — The Kindling

Toren woke to the sound of his grandmother trying not to cough.

She was good at it. She had been practicing for twenty years. The trick was to let it happen low in the chest and turn her face into the straw so the canvas took the noise, and it worked well enough that nobody sleeping more than six feet away would have heard her. Toren slept four feet away, under the cart, with his head against the near wheel, and he heard everything.

He counted the coughs, because counting was what he did with things he could not fix. Nine. Then a long gap. Then five more, wetter than the first nine.

He got up before the sky did.

The fire had gone to a red seam in a bed of ash. He fed it a handful of dry thorn and blew until the seam opened, then set the kettle on the flat stone and went to check the mule. Tuck stood hipshot at the end of his line with his ears out sideways, which was Tuck's opinion of mornings. The off-hind shoe had been loose since the ford. Toren ran his thumb around the clinches and found it looser.

"Day six," he said to the mule, because the mule did not care and would not tell anyone. "Thirty-one miles. About."

It was not about. It was thirty-one and a half by his own count of paces against the road stones. He had checked it twice against the mile markers that still stood, and the markers agreed with him wherever they hadn't been knocked flat. He liked knowing. Knowing was a thing you could hold when nothing else would sit still.

Behind him the canvas moved and his father came out of the cart's lee, already dressed, already looking north.

Garrick Voss was a wide, unhurried man who had spent nineteen years on Sallow Creek's gate and had the habit of counting bodies before he said good morning. He counted them now: Senna in the cart, Marta and Milo Faust in the blanket-roll on the fire's warm side, Dessa Cole where she always slept, which was sitting up against the wheel with her boots on, and Wyck Tanner a little apart from all of them because that was where Wyck put himself.

"She's worse," Toren said.

"I heard."

"Nine, then five. Yesterday it was four and four."

His father took the kettle off the stone before it caught, and set it on the gravel, and did not answer for long enough that Toren understood the answer.

"We'll do fourteen today," Garrick said.

"We did eleven yesterday and it took until dark."

"Then it'll take until after dark." He crouched and put his hands over the little fire, not for the warmth, just to have somewhere to put them. "There's a ridge two days up. Road goes through it instead of over. After the ridge, the country flattens and we make better time and we start meeting people who are going where we're going." He looked at Toren sideways. "Fourteen today. Fourteen tomorrow. Then the ridge."

"That's four miles more than the mule likes."

"The mule doesn't get a say. Neither do I." Garrick stood. "Go tell Marta we're moving early. Don't tell your grandmother why."

Senna Voss had been told exactly nothing her entire life without noticing it. She was awake when Toren came around the tailgate, propped on the rolled coat she used for a pillow, her hands folded on the blanket like a woman waiting politely for a meeting to start.

"Fourteen miles," she said.

"Grandmother."

"Don't make the face. Your father's face already does it and there isn't room in this cart for two of them." She shifted, and the shifting cost her something, and she spent it without comment. "Is it today?"

"Is what today?"

"Don't." She looked at him until he stopped. "Thirteen years ago this morning, at a bad hour, in bad weather. I was there. Your mother had opinions about the weather. So. Is it today, or have I lost a day?"

"It's today," Toren said.

"Good." Senna closed her eyes. "Then get me up, because I am not attending my only grandson's Kindling lying down like a sack of turnips."

---

They made eight miles before noon, which was good, and then the road gave them back four hours of it.

The old highway ran north through scrub and burnt-over hill country, two lanes wide where the surface survived, one lane wide where the frost had taken it, and nothing at all where a culvert had let go sometime in the last seventy years and dropped a forty-foot section into the wash below. They had to take the cart down the bank and across and up. Up meant all of them on the ropes with Tuck, and Marta at the wheel spokes. Senna sat on a rock in the shade of a dead elm with her chin up, pretending she had chosen to sit there.

Wyck Tanner set his shoulder into the tailgate and pushed the way he did everything, which was without any part of him held back for later.

"Who built a road down a hole," he said.

"They built it over a bridge," Marta said. "The bridge is the part that left."

"Then who built a bridge out of something that leaves."

"People with more to do than argue with a boy about it." Marta Faust was a small, square woman with gray in her braid and a mender's hands, and she had the trick of sounding mild while she got exactly what she wanted. "Lower. You're lifting when you should be pushing. Push."

The wheel came up over the lip. Tuck took three steps and stopped and had his opinion, and Milo got in front of him and told him he was the best mule on the road, which was a thing Milo believed and said often and had never been able to prove, since Tuck was the only mule on the road.

They ate standing. Dessa Cole did not eat standing. She walked out to the top of the bank with a strip of dried apple in her hand and stood there looking north for as long as it took the rest of them to finish, and when she came back she said, "There's smoke on the ridge line. Left of the notch. Thin."

"Cook fire," Garrick said.

"It's the wrong time of day for a cook fire."

"Somebody's late with their breakfast."

"It's the wrong color for a cook fire."

Garrick looked at her. Dessa looked back. She was fourteen, a half inch taller than Toren and unbothered about it, and she had a way of holding a thing she had seen out flat in her hand until somebody took it from her.

"All right," Garrick said. "Thin, left of the notch. I'll want to know if it's still there at dusk."

"It won't be. It's already going." She put the apple in her mouth. "I just thought somebody besides me should have seen it."

That was the thing about Dessa. She never said *I told you*. She just made sure, ahead of time, that there would be somebody to tell.

---

They stopped short of the fourteen. Twelve and a bit, by Toren's count, at a place where the road ran along the bottom of a shallow bowl and a ring of old foundation stones sat off to one side — a house once, or a shed, or a signal hut. Whatever it had been, it had left behind a low horseshoe of cut stone that broke the wind, and Garrick walked the ground twice and said it would do.

Toren spent the last hour of light not thinking about it.

He fed the fire. He watered the mule. He carried the water in two trips instead of one because one trip would have been faster and then there would have been nothing left to carry. He straightened the cart traces, which were straight. He found the loose shoe again and looked at it for a while with no hammer in his hand.

"You're going to wear a path," Dessa said. She had the other end of the horseshoe wall and had not moved from it in an hour.

"I'm busy."

"You're circling." She drew her knees up. "It takes about a minute. Then it's over and you'll have had it."

"Or I won't."

There. It was out in the open, in the last of the light, and it sat there between them and neither of them could put it back.

Dessa did not flinch. "Or you won't," she agreed.

Toren made himself look at her. "I'm sorry."

"For what? For saying the true thing?" She pulled a thread off the hem of her sleeve and wound it around her finger. "Thirteen months ago. My uncle's yard at Sallow Creek. Everybody came who could walk. My mother's sister brought a cake, which was a whole day's flour, and I want you to understand that I have thought about that cake more than I have thought about almost anything else in my life." She flicked the thread away. "They asked me the three questions. I answered them. My uncle put his hands on my head. And I stood there and I waited and I kept waiting and after a while people started talking about other things so I would have somewhere to look."

"Dessa —"

"It's not a sad story, Toren, it's just a story. It happens to about one in nine." She said the number the way he said his mile counts, like a thing she had gone and found so she would not have to wonder about it. "So. Or you won't. And if you don't, I'll be the one who already knows how the evening goes, and I'll get you through it, and nobody else will have to be clever about it." She shrugged. "That's the whole offer. Take it or don't."

Toren discovered that his throat had closed. "Take it," he said.

"Good." She stood up and brushed off the back of her legs. "They're ready. Your grandmother's been upright for ten minutes and she's not going to say anything about it, so somebody should hurry."

---

They did it at the fire, with the stone wall at their backs and the sky going the color of a bruise over the ridge.

There should have been eighty people. That was the thing Toren could not stop knowing. There should have been the whole of Sallow Creek in his uncle's yard with the long tables out, and the Tanner boys on the fiddle, and old Hesper who cried at every single one of them, every year, without fail, and made a joke about it afterward. There should have been a cake that cost a day's flour.

There were seven people and a mule.

Senna stood up out of the cart with her hand on Marta's arm and would not use the arm for more than balance. She took her place on the fire's north side. Garrick took the south. Marta and Milo and Wyck and Dessa made up the rest of the ring, which was not a ring so much as a gap with people standing in the parts of it they could reach.

"It's a made-up thing, you know," Senna said.

"Mother," said Garrick.

"He should hear it." She had a voice like dry paper and she used every inch of it. "This ceremony is sixty-one years old. I was a girl when they put it together. A committee did it — actual grown adults, sitting in an actual room, arguing about words, because the children had started waking up and nobody knew what to do with their hands." She looked around the fire. "They made it up out of nothing, and it was beautiful, and it still is, and the two facts have never once got in each other's way. That's the only real lesson anybody ever gets, so I've given it to you first and now you can have the rest."

Milo, quietly and with total sincerity: "I think it's a very good ceremony."

"Thank you, Milo. It is." Senna put her hands out. "Give me the fire."

Toren had carried the whistle in his coat since Sallow Creek. He had cut it from elder wood when he was nine, badly, with a knife he had not been allowed to use. It had never made more than one note. The note had been wrong. He had kept it for four years anyway. He had no idea why, and he understood, holding it over the flame, that he had been keeping it exactly for this, without knowing, the way you keep a coin in a drawer for a road you haven't been told about yet.

He put it in the fire. The elder wood caught fast and went up green at the edges.

"What did you carry?" Senna asked. The first question.

"A whistle I made."

"Was it good?"

"No."

"Why did you keep it?"

He had an answer ready. He had had an answer ready for a year. Standing there, with the thing burning, he found the ready answer had gone somewhere and something truer had come and taken its place.

"Because I made it and it was mine," Toren said, "and I thought if I kept it long enough I'd get better and then I'd have the two things to put next to each other."

Behind him, Wyck made a small sound. Not a laugh. Something that had been on its way to being one and had changed its mind.

Senna's face did not move. "That's the right answer," she said. "Not the one they wrote down. The right one. Garrick."

His father came around the fire.

Garrick had carried the box since Sallow Creek without ever once letting Toren see where in the load it rode. It was the length of his hand, black wood, hinged with a strip of tin somebody had cut from something else a long time ago. He opened it and took out what was inside and held it up so the firelight had a chance at it.

It was smaller than Toren expected. That was the first thing. A flattened oval of dark metal, about the size of a plum stone, the color of wet slate, with one face worn to a shine in a shallow dip where five generations of thumbs had rubbed the same spot. Not a weapon. Not anything. A pebble with a job.

"This was my grandmother's," Garrick said. "Before that, her father's. Nobody knows past him and I've stopped pretending we do." He turned it over once. "It has been in a drawer for eleven years waiting for you. Give me your arm."

Toren gave him his arm.

His father pushed the sleeve back and set the Ember against the inside of the left forearm, two fingers below the wrist, and held it there with the flat of his thumb.

"Say the last one," Garrick said.

Senna said the last question. "What will you do with it?"

"Whatever it costs," Toren said, which was the written answer, and everyone at every Kindling said it, and it had always sounded to him like a boast people made because there was nothing else to say. He heard himself say it and he did not like the sound of it in his own mouth.

Then his father took his thumb away.

For a moment nothing at all happened, and Toren thought, *one in nine,* and his whole chest went to cold water.

Then the metal got warm.

It was not a large feeling. That was what nobody had ever told him. He had been braced for something enormous, a hand on the back of his neck, a voice, a blow. What he got was a warmth the size of a coin, low and central, under the breastbone and slightly behind it, in a place he had never had cause to think about. Then a second warmth arrived in his forearm, where the Ember lay. Then came the understanding, all at once and from no direction: those two warmths were the same warmth. They had always been the same warmth. They were simply, for the first time, in the same room.

The fire got brighter. Not really. The edges of everything got easier to see.

"There he is," Senna said.

Toren's arm came up. He did not decide to raise it. Something down at the bottom of him let go of a breath it had been holding for thirteen years, and the dark metal on his forearm opened along a seam he had not known was there, and ran.

It went up the back of his hand like water running the wrong way. He felt the weight of it arrive on the outside of his wrist, settle, and take a shape. He looked down expecting his father's blade. He had watched that blade his whole life — a long, clean, single-edged thing Garrick could put out in half a second and hold all day.

What he had instead was a hook.

A fat, ugly, lopsided hook. Blunt along the back, thick where it should have been thin, curved wrong, like something a person would use to drag a wet sack out of a ditch. It came off his knuckles at an angle that was not any angle. It was about as beautiful as a doorstop.

Wyck said, "Huh."

"Don't," said Marta.

"I didn't say anything. I said *huh.*"

Milo said, "I think it looks strong," which was the single kindest and least honest thing anyone said all evening.

Toren's face was burning. He tried to make it into the blade. He reached for the shape with his mind the way you reach for a word you know. The warmth under his breastbone stuttered. The hook went thin and wrong. Then the whole thing came apart into nothing, and the metal was a pebble on his arm again.

Silence.

"Well," Senna said. "That's that, then. Somebody find me something to sit on before I fall on the fire."

Toren stood there with his sleeve pushed back and his eyes stinging and could not make himself look at anybody. He heard Marta moving, and the cart's boards creaking as they got Senna down, and Milo asking whether they were allowed to eat now.

Then his father's hand came down on the back of his neck, heavy and warm, and turned him half around.

"Look at me," Garrick said.

"It's a hook."

"I saw."

"It's supposed to be —"

"It's supposed to be nothing in particular." His father's voice had gone flat and careful, which was what it did when he wanted something to land. "Thirty-one years I've had mine. Do you know what came out of it the first night? A spike. Just a spike, off the end of my fist, about as long as my thumb. It looked like a nail. My father laughed so hard he had to sit down." Garrick's grip tightened once and let go. "Yours came out with a *shape*, Toren. It came out with a job it already knew how to do. Some people wait a year for that."

"It's an ugly shape."

"It's a shape that catches things and pulls them," Garrick said. "I've spent nineteen years on a gate. Do you have any idea how much of that work is catching things and pulling them?" He let that sit. Then, quieter: "It's yours. It came from inside you and it looks like you. That's the only compliment there is. Don't go trying to trade it for somebody else's."

Toren wiped his face with the back of his wrist, above the Ember, where the skin was still warm.

"Dessa's uncle runs," he said. "At Sallow Creek. In the spring trials. He runs the whole length of the berm in about nine seconds and he goes over the thorn without touching it."

"That's a stride. Same stone, different job — it puts the metal down your legs and along your back instead of out your hand." Garrick was gathering the box up off the ground. "Edge or stride. Those are the two, and they're the two for everybody, and it has been the two for as long as anybody's kept a record."

"Can he do an edge as well?"

"He can. Not at the same time." His father snapped the tin hasp shut. "Nobody does them at the same time. You hold one. You put it down and you pick up the other, and the putting-down takes about as long as the picking-up, and in a bad half second that's the whole of your life right there." He looked up. "So you choose early and you live with it. That's not a rule somebody made. It's just what the thing is."

---

Later, when the others had eaten and the fire had gone low and Milo was already asleep sitting up with his bowl still in his hands, Garrick walked Toren out past the stone wall to where the road ran pale in the dark.

He didn't say anything for a hundred paces. Toren counted them.

"Hold your hand out," his father said.

Toren held his hand out and Garrick put something in it, and the weight of the thing was so much more than the size of it that Toren's hand dropped an inch before he caught up.

It was a disk, flat, about the width of his palm, in a metal he did not have a name for — darker than iron and warmer than iron had any business being, with a dull surface that did not throw the starlight back so much as swallow it and consider it. Nine fine channels ran from the rim toward the center. They did not reach the center. Each one stopped a hair short, and the nine stopping-places made a small ring of nothing in the middle. Toren found he did not enjoy looking at that ring for very long.

A leather cord was knotted through a hole at the edge. The cord was new. Somebody had replaced it recently and carefully, in a house, before this trip, at a table, in the light.

"What is it?" Toren said.

"That's the first thing everybody asks and there has never once been an answer." Garrick had his arms folded. He was watching the road, not the disk. "It came to us five generations back. That's all I know that's true. Everything else I'm about to tell you is what my grandmother told me, and what her father told her, and I've never been able to check a word of it."

"All right."

"They say it will save the world or it will end it." Garrick said it plainly, without ceremony, the way he'd say the ford was out. "Not might. Will. One or the other, and nobody has ever known which, and every single person who's carried it has died not knowing." He shifted his weight. "My grandmother put it shorter than that. She said it's a curse in a gift's coat."

Toren looked down at the thing in his hand. It was not doing anything. It was, as far as he could tell, a heavy metal plate with nine grooves in it, and a new cord, and a myth.

"Then why are you giving it to me?"

"Because I'm forty-four years old and I've got a hundred and nine miles of bad road in front of me, and your grandmother's sick, and I would rather you had it than spend one hour of my life wondering where it went." His father turned his head. "And because the other thing they all say is that it goes to the one who's just been Kindled. Not the oldest. Not the steadiest. The newest. I've never understood that either. I've decided to stop needing to."

"That's not a reason."

"No," Garrick agreed. "It's a habit five generations deep, and I'm keeping it, and you'll keep it too, and one day a boy of yours is going to stand on a road and tell you it's not a reason." He almost smiled. "Put it under your shirt. Not around your neck where it shows. Under. And don't take it out to look at in front of strangers."

Toren pulled the cord over his head. The disk dropped against his sternum and lay there, cold at first, then not cold — not warm either. It simply stopped being a temperature and started being a weight.

"Does it do anything?"

"Not in five generations," Garrick said.

"Then it can't be a curse."

His father was quiet for a second too long.

"That's exactly what I said," Garrick said. "When I was thirteen. Standing about where you're standing." He put his hand briefly on Toren's shoulder and left it there and then took it away. "Come on. Big day tomorrow and a bigger one after."

---

In the morning the sky came up the color of skimmed milk and there were no birds.

Toren noticed it the way you notice a clock you have stopped hearing. He had been walking for an hour before he understood what was missing. When he understood it he stopped in the middle of the road. Dessa, ten paces ahead, stopped too without turning around.

"Yes," she said.

"Since when?"

"Since we broke camp." She turned then. Her face was doing the flat thing. "There were crows in the elms last night. I counted nine. There's nothing in those elms now and nothing's gone over us all morning."

Garrick came back down the line at a walk that was not quite a walk.

"Show me," he said.

Dessa showed him. She did not point at the sky, which was the part that was missing, because you cannot point at a missing thing. She pointed at the road instead, at the soft gray dust that had drifted into the seam where the old surface had cracked, about thirty feet back and running off to the east through the scrub.

Tracks.

They crossed the road at a shallow angle, all going the same way. They were not shod. They were not deer. They were not any shape Toren could name. Something had put them down while walking on the sides of its feet. There were a great many of them, printed over each other in a mass, and not one of them had turned aside for anything at all — not the rocks, not the thorn, not the drainage ditch. Whatever had come through had gone straight, in a mass, without picking up its feet.

Nobody said anything.

Garrick crouched and put two fingers into the nearest print and did not pick up the dust, only touched it, the way you touch a stove to see whether it's still hot.

"Riftspawn," he said.

It was not a question and nobody treated it as one. Milo took one step closer to his mother without appearing to notice he had done it.

"How old?" Marta asked.

"Last night. Late." He stood and wiped his fingers on his thigh. "Going east. Away from the ridge, not toward it."

"That's good," Milo said hopefully.

"That's good," Garrick agreed, and did not sound like a man who thought it was good.

Ahead of them, five miles off and blue with distance, the ridge lay across the whole north of the world like a shoulder. The road went to it and did not go over it. There was a notch — a squared-off bite out of the skyline where men with machines had once cut a straight slot through eighty feet of rock rather than climb it — and the road ran into that notch and vanished.

Toren counted the miles to it twice and got the same number both times, and was not comforted.

"Four miles around, if we go over the shoulder," Dessa said. "I looked at it this morning."

"Four miles of open hill in loose ground with a cart and a sick woman," Garrick said. "Nine hours. We'd be on that slope in the dark."

"And in the cut we'd be through in forty minutes."

"In the cut we'd be through in forty minutes," his father said.

He stood looking at the notch for a long time. Then he took Tuck's head-rope in his hand, which he had not done since the first day, and clicked his tongue. The mule leaned into the collar. The cart came up out of its ruts with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Forty minutes," Garrick said. "We go through at first light tomorrow, not at dusk tonight. Everybody walks. Nobody rides but my mother. Wyck, you're on the tailgate."

"What am I doing on the tailgate?"

"Looking backward," Garrick said, and started north.
