# Chapter 14 — In the Open

Wyck asked Senna's permission first.

Toren was on the near shaft at the time and heard the whole of it over his own shoulder, at about eight in the morning, on a made road going north between two long fields of nothing.

"I want you awake this afternoon."

"I am generally awake, Wyck Tanner. I simply have my eyes shut, which is a different economy."

"I know. I want you awake and sitting up." Wyck was lying propped on his elbow with his back against the rail, in the place he had lived for eight days. "I put a thing off once because you were asleep. I'm not putting it off twice. So I'd rather you were there and heard it than be told about it afterward by one of them."

There was a pause in the cart.

"Well," said Senna. "That's the nicest thing anybody's said to me in about nine years."

"It isn't nice."

"No," she agreed. "It's better than nice. Prop that blanket behind me at the halt and I'll do the rest."

---

He picked the ground, too. Toren worked that out afterward and could not decide whether to be proud of him or frightened.

They halted at noon in a place that had nothing to recommend it. It was a bare shoulder of road with a stone heap on the west side and open field on the east, and there was no shade and no water and no wall. Rook had not chosen it. Rook had said *half an hour* about a quarter of a mile back. Then Wyck had said, from the cart, "Not here. Up there," and had pointed at the shoulder. Rook looked at it for two seconds and said "Fine."

There was no cover on that shoulder. Nobody could be at anybody's back. Everybody could see everybody's hands.

Wyck got himself down off the tailgate on his own. It took him a while and it hurt him. Milo started forward once, and Dessa got a hand on his arm without looking at him. When Wyck was standing he stayed standing, which was the part that cost.

"Right," he said.

Rook was eleven feet away with the flask cord over his shoulder. He put it down.

"Go on," he said.

---

"Three things first, in that order." Wyck's voice was the one he used for reading out a number. "There's six of us. Two are mending. We're going north."

"Wyck," said Dessa.

"I'm not being clever. I'm doing it the way he taught us, because I want him to know I'm doing it on purpose and not because I lost my temper." Wyck did not look away from Rook once. "I've had this ready for eight days and I've rebuilt it about forty times. What's left of it is short."

Toren's hands had gone cold on the water can.

"What am I standing in a line for," Wyck said.

The wind came across the open field and went through the stone heap and made the small noise stone heaps make.

"Say the rest of it," Rook said.

"There is no rest of it. That's the question."

"There's the rest of it and you know there is, because you've had eight days." Rook's voice had not changed at all. "Ask me the whole thing or don't ask me. I'm not going to answer the short version and let you feel you've had it."

Wyck's jaw moved once.

"All right," he said.

---

"Twenty-five days ago you told us we weren't going to learn to fight." He said it the way he said everything, flat, in order, without any heat on it. "You said it at a north gate at first light. You said fighting takes years and we don't have years and three of us haven't got anything to fight with. You said we were going to learn not to be standing where the thing is. I can say the whole of it back if you want it."

"I don't want it."

"For a fortnight after that it was ground and spacing and short steps and where the mass is. Not one word about people." Wyck set his feet slightly wider, which was what Wyck did instead of raising his voice. "Then we came off the canal. Then it was how to meet somebody at eighty yards. Then it was what three men standing across a lane are for, and you made us practice going through them four times and told Dessa to go again when she asked you why anybody would do that.

"And last night you stood in a field and taught Milo to throw at a man's face."

Milo looked at the ground.

"So." Wyck took a breath. It caught on the ribs, and he went on through it. "A fortnight of a thing that can't change its mind, and then, suddenly, everything about a thing that can. That's not a course. That's not somebody teaching four children how to stay alive on a road. That's a man getting something ready."

Nobody moved.

"And I'd like to know what for," Wyck said. "Not because I'm owed it. I said that at Coldharbour. I meant it, and I still mean it. I'd like to know because I'm in it, and because I said yes to nothing, and because there was a day at a sheep-fold when I stood in front of a boy and told him I'd rather say no myself than have somebody say it on my behalf." He stopped. "That's the whole of it. What am I standing in a line for."

---

Rook did not answer for a long time.

Toren watched his face and got nothing out of it, which was ordinary, and then watched his hands and got something, which was not. The man had his thumb moving very slightly against the side of his first finger, once, and again, and again.

Toren had seen that before. He had seen it on a wharf in the rain with a piece of brass in it.

"You've made an error in it," Rook said at last.

"Then say which."

"You've built it out of the last five days and it starts further back than that." He looked at Wyck. "But the rest of it's correct. It's better put than I'd have put it, and I'm not going to insult you by arguing with the shape of it."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a not-yet. I'll tell you what it's a not-yet to in a minute, and I'd like to answer the boy first." Rook turned his head. "Go on, Milo."

Milo went the color of a beetroot.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've been going to for about four minutes. I'd rather have it now than at the fire."

Milo looked at his own boots for a second. When he got it out, he got it out all at once, in the voice of somebody putting a hand into cold water.

"Are we bait?"

---

The whole of the shoulder went quiet.

"Because I was," Milo said. "That's the thing. Eight days ago I stood in a hole in a bridge and made a light on purpose, so that a thing would come at me instead of at somebody else. And it *worked.* And you told me never to do it again and then you told me it worked." He wiped his face with his wrist, angrily, the way he did. "And I've been fine about that. I've been completely fine about it. And then last night you taught us all how to be close to a man and I lay down and thought — right, so what's the light for, then. On a road. With people on it."

Toren had stopped breathing about a sentence and a half earlier.

Rook crossed the eleven feet.

He did it fast, which he never did, and he stopped four feet short of the boy and went down on one knee on the road so that Milo did not have to look up.

"No," he said.

The flat had gone out of his voice.

Toren heard it go. It was the exact same sound as a question on a towpath in the rain — *and did anybody count who came out* — and it went through him from the back of the neck down.

"No. Not now, not at Coldharbour, not at the arch, not ever." Rook did not raise it. "You were not bait at that bridge. You were a boy who did a clever, dangerous thing on his own account because a friend told him to. And I was twenty seconds away on a parapet. Both of us know exactly what twenty seconds is worth. I have carried that for eight days and I will carry it for a good while yet."

"But you said —"

"I said it worked. It did work. It also should not have been available to you, and the reason it was available to you is that I was in the wrong place." Rook stood up. "Ask me the question again in a year and I will give you the same answer in the same words, because it will still be true. Nobody in this group is bait. If I ever decide otherwise I will say it out loud to your face beforehand, and you can tell me what you think of it."

Milo's mouth was doing something unreliable.

"All right," he said.

---

"Right." Rook walked back to where he had been standing, which was a thing Toren noticed — he put himself back at eleven feet, in the open, where everybody could see his hands. "Then here's the not-yet. I'll do it in order. I'd ask all of you not to talk in the middle of it, because I'm only going to get through it once.

"There is an account," he said.

Nobody said anything at all.

"It's mine. It's old. It's got my whole life in it and there isn't anybody else left to tell it, which is a thing I have said to myself for eleven years in one order or another and have never once said out loud on a road." He said it flatly, as information, in the voice he read out a bill in. "Cinder Company is in it. Wyck's right about the brass and he was right about it at Coldharbour, in front of a fire, seven days before I was ready to have him be right about it.

"And I'm not going to give it to you today."

"Then when?" said Dessa.

"I don't know, and that is the honest answer and not a way of getting off this road." Rook looked at her. "I have got it packed a particular way. I have had it packed that way for a long time, and it is the reason I can keep walking. If I take it out here on an open shoulder at noon with half an hour before we move, I will do it poorly. Then you will have a worse thing than the true one, for the rest of your lives, and I will not be able to take it back off you. So no. Not standing up. Not in half an hour."

"That's —" Toren heard his own voice and did not much like it. "That's the same as no."

"It is not the same as no and you know the difference, because you have been marking the difference for a fortnight." Rook's head came round. "Ask me how many times I have said *no* to a question of that shape since the rock cut."

Toren's mouth opened.

"Eleven," he said.

"Eleven. And this is the twelfth and it isn't one." Rook let that sit. "That's what I've got. Take it or don't."

---

"There's one more thing," he said, "and it's the part I actually owe you. I'd rather not say it, which is generally how I know."

He looked at the four of them and then, deliberately, past them at the cart, where an old woman was sitting up against a folded blanket with her hands in her lap and had not said one word all morning.

"I am building something in you on purpose," Rook said.

Toren felt Dessa go still beside him.

"Not since last night. Not since Coldharbour. Since a morning at a beck, eleven days ago, before any of you were up." He did not look at Dessa when he said it and Toren understood that this was the courtesy again. "Something happened in a field that morning that I had not expected to see again in my life. And I went and stood in six inches of cold water for three minutes, and then I came out and started explaining things to Milo about how to pick a man up off a floor, which is a thing I had refused to do for fifteen days.

"That was a decision. I made it in the water. I made it about all five of you and I did not tell any of you I had made it. You have been walking north for eleven days inside a decision somebody else took."

"Why didn't you say?" Dessa said.

"Because I did not want to be argued with, and because I have done it that way for eleven years, and because it is exactly the same thing I did to Wyck at a lock flight and got him three broken ribs for." Rook's voice did not change by one degree. "I have had that pointed out to me once this month. I would rather not need it twice."

---

Wyck had not moved. He was still standing on the shoulder with his feet apart and his arm across his ribs, and Toren realized that he had been standing for nine or ten minutes now and was paying for every one of them.

"That's not the account," Wyck said.

"No."

"But it's the true part of it."

"It's the part that's about you," Rook said. "The account is about me. You asked what you were standing in a line for and I've given you the only half of the answer that's yours. The other half is mine and you'll get it."

"When you're ready."

"When I'm ready."

Wyck thought about that for a while.

"All right," he said.

And then he did the thing Toren had not seen coming.

"Then I'll wait," Wyck said, "and I'll not ask you again. I'd like something instead."

"Ask."

"When it's finished." Wyck's chin came up half an inch. "Whatever it is. Whatever finishing it looks like. There'll be a thing left over at the end of it that you'll still not have said, because there always is. I want you to say it to me first."

The wind went through the stone heap.

Toren watched a man stand on an open road with a flask cord at his feet and do nothing whatsoever for about four seconds.

"Why you?" Rook said.

"I don't know. Because I asked."

"That's not why." Rook picked up the cord and put it back over his shoulder. "It's because you're the only one of the five who has ever asked me for anything without wanting me to feel good about giving it. You asked me for an accounting at a handcart and you meant the numbers. You asked me for a go with the stone and you gave me three reasons and told me two of them were bad."

"Is that a yes?"

"Yes," Rook said. "You first. When it's finished."

---

They went at half past one and made eight miles before the light went, and the strange thing about the afternoon was how ordinary it was.

Nobody said anything about the shoulder. Milo talked about a dog somebody had owned at Sallow Creek. Dessa called the ground from the head of the shafts. Senna slept properly for two hours, which she had not done in daylight for a week, and woke up and complained about the road surface for nine minutes without drawing breath.

Toren pulled and turned it over and could not get to the bottom of it, because the thing that had happened was not the thing he had spent six days expecting.

He had expected an argument. He had expected somebody to win one.

What had happened was that a man had said *there is an account and I'm not giving it to you*, which was a refusal, and which was the clearest refusal any of them had ever had from him. And every single person on that road had accepted it.

Not grudgingly. Not for now. Toren checked, all afternoon, the way you check a tooth.

Milo hummed. Dessa did not once look at the back of Rook's head. And Wyck lay in the cart with his eyes shut and his ribs on fire and slept for the first time since the arch.

*We closed up round him,* Toren thought, on the shafts, with the sun going down on the left. *He told us he's hiding something and we closed up round him. That's what we did. That's the whole of what we did.*

He could not decide whether it was the best thing they had ever done or a thing that people who had been drilled for twenty-six days would do without noticing.

---

The rota changed again that night and this time somebody discussed it.

Rook came to the fire when the food was done, with the slate in his hand, which meant he had gone and taken it off the stone where Dessa left it.

"Whose is the last hour?" he said.

"Yours," said Dessa. "It's been yours every night since the drove road and you know perfectly well it has."

"Give it to Wyck."

Dessa did not move.

"He's got three ribs."

"He's got three ribs and he can sit. Sitting is the whole of that job." Rook put the slate down on the stone in front of her. "Last hour before light. That's the one that matters and it's the one I've never given anybody. I'd like him to have it tomorrow, and every third night after."

"And you?"

"I'll be awake anyway," Rook said, which was not an answer, and which everybody at that fire understood was the closest thing to one available.

---

Toren woke at the hour with no name and did not know why.

The fire was a seam. Senna's breathing came slow and even out of the cart. Somewhere off east a bird that was not a bird made one noise and stopped.

Six feet away, where Rook had sat against the wheel every night for twenty-six nights, there was nobody.

Toren got up on his elbow.

Wyck Tanner was sitting against the near wheel with a blanket over his shoulders and the iron bar across his knees, upright, with his hands open on top of the blanket, facing back down the road they had come by.

He had got himself there somehow. Toren had not heard him do it and did not like to think about how long it had taken.

And out at the edge of the light, thirty feet off in the dark, the man stood with his back to all of them, doing what he did, looking at a country nobody else could see.

Toren lay down again and watched the two of them for a long time.

It was the first night in twenty-six that there had been two people in the camp facing the wrong way, and he could not work out, lying there, whether that made it twice as safe or twice as bad.
