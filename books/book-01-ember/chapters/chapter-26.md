# Chapter 26 — Nothing Was Put Down

"I'm telling you first," said Toren.

He heard the words arrive in his own mouth. He had not chosen them and he did not take them back, and he watched what they did to the four faces in that glasshouse bed and made himself keep standing.

"Say it, then," said Wyck, who had gone white.

---

"There's half a mile of open field between this range and that bank," said Toren. "There is no cover on it. There is nothing on it but a road. If that thing gets onto it while we're crossing, it does the whole half mile in eleven seconds and there is not one single thing any of us can do about it.

"So nobody crosses that field while it can see them.

"Wyck takes Senna on his back and Rook walks. The two of them go out of the east end of this range and up the inside of the hedge and across. They do not stop. They do not look back. They do not come back for anybody.

"And it doesn't watch them go, because it's busy."

"Busy with what," said Rook.

"Me."

---

Nobody said anything for about three seconds.

"It comes at worked power," said Toren. "You said that last night, twice, and you said it like a thing you were sorry about. It isn't a thing to be sorry about. It's the only lever on that thing anybody has got and we've been treating it as weather."

"It comes at *me*," said Rook. "That's the whole difficulty. You're an Ember and I'm a Blaze, and from where that thing stands I am a bonfire and you are a candle in a jar."

"I know."

"Then you know what you're proposing is arithmetic that doesn't work."

"It works if the candle is the only one out of the jar," said Toren.

---

Here is the thing Toren Voss had been turning over since ten o'clock, sitting in broken glass with an old woman's hands in his sleeve.

He had been taught how to be quiet by a man who could not be quiet.

Thirty-eight days of it. Go round a gap, not through it. Don't camp in a hollow. Don't put a light in your hand. Everything Rook had ever taught any of them about Riftspawn came down to *be a smaller thing than you are*, and Rook had taught it while carrying a fire he could not put out, every hour of every day, for eleven years.

And there was exactly one instruction the man had ever given that went the other way, and he had given it on a towpath above a flight of eight locks with the light going.

*You don't hold it. You stop letting go of it.*

An Ember is small. That is the whole of what the word means. But an Ember that is out and stays out, held and not checked and not put down, minute after minute after minute, is a thing that is *on*, and a thing that is on is a thing that can be gone toward.

"I can't be louder than you," Toren said. "I can be louder than you for twelve minutes, if I never once put it down, and twelve minutes is the field."

---

Rook did not answer for a long time.

"Say what you want from me," he said at last.

"You already gave it to me. You gave it to me on the Level in front of everybody and you have not taken it back." Toren's hands had gone cold. "*Nobody in this group is bait.* And you said you'd never decide otherwise without saying it to our faces first."

"And?"

"And you never once said that nobody in this group gets to decide it for themselves."

---

The whole of the Long Lights went quiet.

Toren watched it go into him. He watched a man of thirty-one, sitting in a bed of broken glass with eleven inches of open shoulder and an eighth of the room, hear the exact door that a fourteen-year-old boy had gone through at nineteen minutes to four in the morning, and understand that it had been standing open the entire time, and that he had built it himself, and that he was going to have to stand in front of it again.

Rook shut his eyes for about a second and a half.

Then he opened them, and he did not argue. Toren understood that the not-arguing was the most expensive thing that man ever did for him.

"Two things," said Rook.

"Go on."

"Keep your feet moving. Every second. The instant you stand still you have given it a place to arrive at, and it is better at arriving than anything you will ever meet."

"And the second?"

"Don't count," said Rook.

---

"That's it? That's the whole —"

"That is the whole of it and it is the most useful thing anybody has ever told you." Rook got himself up the brick to standing, which took him three tries and which nobody helped him do, because he had not asked. "You are going to be out there on your own with a shape on the end of your arm and one job, which is to keep it lit. And the entire time, there is going to be a boy in the back of your head wanting to know how long it's been.

"Every time you ask him, you check. Every time you check, you pull. And the thing you are pulling on is the only thing keeping that animal's attention off an old woman on a boy's back in an open field.

"So don't count. Not once. You will not know when twelve minutes have gone. That is not a flaw in the plan. That is the plan. And you are the only person I have ever met who is going to find it harder than the fighting."

---

Dessa said, "I'm staying."

"Dessa —"

"Do the arithmetic out loud and then tell me I'm wrong." She had not got up and she had not raised her voice. "He needs the call. He cannot hear the click and I can. If nobody calls it, he is a boy standing in front of a thing that cuts, guessing, and he will guess right four times and wrong once. I am a hundred and forty feet away behind a brick wall with nothing lit on me at all, because I am Ember and I am quiet and it has been past me twice this morning without knowing I was there.

"I can't run, Toren. That's a fact about today and it's been a fact about every day since the beck. I would be the slowest thing on that field by a distance and Wyck would have to choose between me and your grandmother, and I am not going to make him do that on open ground."

Nobody argued with her, because nobody could.

"Right," said Dessa Cole. "Seventh footing, east end. I'll be flat on the ground behind the brick and I will call the click and the grinding and the yardage and nothing else, and I will not stop calling for any reason at all."

---

Wyck got Senna up onto his back.

It took the three of them four minutes to do it and it was the ugliest thing Toren had ever helped with. Senna weighed almost nothing, which was the worst part. She had her arms crossed over Wyck's collarbones and her chin on his shoulder. Wyck had three ribs and something new on the same side, and both hands cut to pieces in a bed of glass.

He did it without one word.

"Wyck."

"Don't."

"I only —"

"Don't, Toren, because I've got about a minute of being able to do this and I'd like to spend it walking." Wyck stood up under her and got his feet apart and settled the weight. "I'll tell you what I'm going to do instead. Then you'll know, and neither of us has to say the other thing.

"I'm going to walk out of the east end of this range with your grandmother on my back and I am going to hear every single part of what happens behind me. All of it. And I am not going to turn round, because there is a woman on my back with nobody in front of her."

He looked at Toren for the first time.

"He said that to me at a door once," he said. "I hated him for it for about a fortnight. Then I found out he was right, and I have been carrying it ever since. So now you know what I'm holding. Don't you dare make it heavier."

---

Senna Voss put one hand out from Wyck's shoulder and Toren went and took it.

Her hand was cold and her grip was nothing at all.

"Thirteen years ago this morning," she said, "at a bad hour, in bad weather."

"That was forty-five days ago, Grandmother."

"I know precisely when it was and I'm making a point, so be quiet." Her chin came up half an inch. "Your mother had opinions about the weather. I have opinions about this. I am not going to say any of them, because you have made your arrangements and they are good ones and it is not my place to make them harder."

She let go of his hand.

"But I am going to be extremely cross with you later," said Senna Voss, "and you are going to have to sit through the whole of it."

---

And then Rook, last, on the footing at the east end, with a boy's fresh awl-hole in the strap on his forearm.

He did not say anything about the plan and he did not say anything about Milo.

"Elias," said Toren, because he had never once used it and because there was not going to be a better hour.

Rook's face did something.

"That's the first time anybody's said that out loud to me in eleven years," he said.

"I know."

"Then say the other thing. The one you've been carrying since the glass."

Toren stood in a ruined glasshouse with his heart going.

"I don't want to do this," he said.

"Good," said Rook. "The ones who want to are no use to anybody. Go and be loud."

---

They went east along the outside of the seventh footing at twenty past ten in the morning and Toren did not watch them go.

He turned round and walked west up the middle of the fourth bed, alone, through a hundred and twenty feet of broken glass, with the iron hoops standing up out of the brick on both sides of him like the ribs of something.

At the fourth hoop he stopped and put his left arm out and let the hook come.

It came the way it had come for forty-six days, ugly and lopsided off the back of his knuckles, blunt along the whole of its back, thick where a blade would be thin. It dragged his wrist over slightly, the way it always did.

He looked at it once.

Then he stopped looking at it, and started walking, and did not let go of it again.

---

*Here is what it is like to be the loudest thing in a basin.*

Nothing happens. That is the first and worst discovery. There is no sensation of shining. Nobody hands you a signal. A boy walks up and down a hundred and twenty feet of glasshouse bed with a hook on his arm and his own boots crunching and a wind coming off a dead orchard, and there is no evidence whatsoever that he is doing anything at all.

And underneath that, all the time, in the back of him where the counting lives, a voice going: *how long? Is it working? Has it turned? Has it seen? How long?*

Every question was a small hard tug on the warmth under his breastbone.

He could feel each one. That was the thing Rook had never told him, because Rook could not have known it — at eleven paces on a towpath it had been a flinch you had to hunt for, and at four minutes of holding it steady in an open glasshouse with your life on it, every check landed like a thumb pressed into a bruise.

He walked the fourth bed. He walked it end to end. He did not count the lengths and he did not count his paces. He did not once look in to see whether it was still lit. About eleven times in the first four minutes he caught himself going in to look and stopped, with an effort that felt physically like taking his hand off a rail at a height.

Then, from a hundred and forty feet east, Dessa's voice came across the brick at ordinary volume.

"It's turned."

---

"Three hundred yards. West-south-west, out past the orchard corner. Coming."

Toren's whole body tried to do four things at once and he did not let it do any of them.

"Two-forty. It's not running. It's walking it in."

*Why,* he thought, and did not say.

"Two hundred."

He got to the west end of the fourth bed and turned and came back, and made himself go at a walk.

"Hundred and sixty. Toren, it's come round the corner of the orchard. It's on the grass strip on the south side of the range and it has gone *along* instead of at you." Dessa's voice did not change by one degree. "It's looking for a line."

---

Here is the thing about a place made of seven parallel walls.

There is no line in it anywhere.

The glasshouse range at the top of the Thirl orchard ran east and west. There were seven brick footings a foot and a half high, standing a hundred and twenty feet long, with ten feet between each pair. Out of the top of every footing came a rusted iron hoop at four-foot intervals — most of them still standing, some of them leaning, a few of them down.

Nothing on four legs the size of a cart horse could take a straight run of more than ten feet in any direction inside that range without arriving at a wall with iron growing out of it.

It came in at the south side, between the first and second footings. Toren was three walls north of it, and it could not run at him.

So it had to come over the walls, one at a time, and every wall was a changeover.

---

That was the whole of the plan, and it worked. Toren never knew how long it worked, because he never counted one minute of it.

He worked the beds.

He learned it in the first two crossings, and after that it was a trade. The Breach came over a footing with its weight forward, in a run, because a wall is a thing you go at. It landed in a bed with ten feet in front of it and a wall behind that. It could not run again. So its weight went back and down, and there was the flat click.

*"CLICK,"* said Dessa.

And Toren was already two walls away, going, because he had started before the word.

Then it had to come out of the set. It had to get the weight forward again to cross the next footing. That was the grinding. That was another half second, and in that half second he crossed a bed.

Half a second is not enough time to do anything. Half a second is enough time to already be doing something.

---

Here is one of them, whole, because they were all the same one and Toren did about twenty of them.

He is in the fourth bed, halfway along it, with his right hand on the brick of the fifth footing and his feet already turned north.

The Breach comes over the third wall into the same bed. It lands sixty feet west of him with its weight still forward.

Sixty feet of a ten-foot-wide glasshouse bed is a line, and he is standing on the end of it.

"*Grinding,*" says Dessa, from a hundred and forty feet east, at ordinary volume.

Toren goes north. Not along the bed — across it, over the fifth footing, a foot and a half of brick and an iron hoop, and he is over it and down on the far side on both knees before the run has covered half the sixty feet.

And four hundredweight arrives at a wall with a boy on the wrong side of it.

It cannot go through. The wall is a foot and a half of Victorian brick footing and the thing has come at it flat out with no weight left over to lift itself, and it stops the way it always stops, and its weight goes back and down.

"*Click.*"

The cut comes over the top of the fifth footing at the height of his ear. It takes the head off an iron hoop and rings it like a bell.

Toren does not look at it. He has been told for thirty-eight days that looking at a thing is how a thing moves you, and this is the first morning he has ever got the lesson entirely right.

Now the trade.

It cannot cut twice. It has spent the weight. To come over that wall after him it has to bring the weight forward. That is the grinding. That is half a second.

"*Grind—*"

He is over the sixth footing before the word is finished.

---

He thought about the disk exactly once, at about the sixth crossing, lying flat behind the fifth footing with his face in the glass and his heart going like a bird's.

It was under his shirt where it had been for forty-six days. He put his right hand on it through the cloth.

It was a weight.

That was all it was and all it did. He had known that before he touched it. He had touched it anyway, because a person in that much trouble will try the door of a locked house.

He took his hand off it and got up and went west.

---

It cost him.

It cost him his knees and the heels of both hands, because a glasshouse bed a hundred and twenty feet long is a hundred and twenty feet of glass, and a boy going over a foot-and-a-half footing at speed lands on his hands about half the time. By the fourth crossing his palms were leaving prints on the brick.

It cost him the skin off his right forearm on an iron hoop.

It cost him his right boot, which came off in the sixth bed under a fallen hoop and stayed there, and he did the rest of it with one boot on.

And it cost the range.

The Breach took the top eighteen inches off four hoops and the whole standing gable end of the second house, which came down in about two seconds and filled the second bed with brick. It took a four-foot section of the fifth footing out at the ground and left a gap in the wall, and Toren saw the gap and understood immediately what it was and why it was the worst thing that had happened to him all morning.

A gap in a wall is a line.

---

He used the bar for that.

Wyck had put it in his hand at the east end before he went, without one word about it, the way Wyck did everything. Four feet of flat iron off a cart rail with a hook filed into one end by a boy's father who had done harness.

Toren got the bar into the footing of the sixth wall under a standing hoop, and put his shoulder on it, and levered the hoop out of a hundred and twenty years of brick and mortar in three goes. It came away with about eight inches of footing still on the foot of it. It weighed as much as he did and he could not lift it, so he did not lift it. He dragged it, one-handed, with the hook, across ten feet of glass and dropped it across the gap in the fifth wall.

An iron hoop lying on its side across a four-foot gap is not a wall. It is not strong and it will not stop anything.

It is a thing that makes a straight line stop being straight.

The Breach came at the gap on the next crossing and could not take it. It had to set. Dessa called the click, and Toren was two beds away.

---

He got greedy at about the eighth minute.

He would have said, afterward, that he got frightened, and both were true and neither was the real reason. The real reason was that he had it working, and a boy who has a thing working starts to believe the thing is his.

Here is what he knew by then. It was good, hard-won, expensive knowledge, and he had earned every piece of it.

He knew the click and the grinding. He knew the half second. He knew that the ground chose the expression: open ground meant a run and a boxed bed meant a cut, every time, without one exception all morning.

And he knew there were three sag holes in the strip of pasture along the north side of the range, because he had counted them at four o'clock the previous afternoon from the road, from three hundred yards, with the wrong light on them, because they were there to be counted.

The middle one was about eleven feet across. It had gone down a foot and a half without breaking up, and there was nothing whatever underneath it.

*It sits down on its own back end.*

Toren went out of the north side of the range on purpose, into the open, and put a sag hole behind him, and stood in front of it with a hook on his arm and let it come.

---

It worked exactly the way he had built it.

The Breach came over the first footing and out onto the grass with its weight forward. It had open ground and a lit thing standing still in the middle of it. So it ran. And Toren stepped left off the line at the last possible instant, the way he had been drilled on a shale fan and in a walled field and on eleven roads.

It went past him at a distance he felt in his teeth. It went out onto the sag. It went to stop.

Its weight came back and down.

And the ground was not there.

---

The whole back end of it went through the sagged crust to the hocks.

There was no click.

Toren stood eleven feet away with the hook out and his chest going and watched the thing he had spent a morning learning fail to do the one thing he had learned about it, and he had about a quarter of a second of being right.

And then it did not stall. It did not sit there. It did not come apart.

With no ground under its back end to set against, it did the other one.

The weight was already going forward. It did not have to change over at all. The grinding started while its hocks were still in the hole. It came up out of a foot and a half of collapsed subsoil in a spray of dry grey earth, and it took him from six feet.

---

Toren Voss went about twenty feet.

He went over the north footing of the seventh house and landed in the bed on his back with a hundred and twenty feet of broken glass under him and no air anywhere in the world.

Something down the left side of him had gone very badly wrong.

He lay in a glasshouse bed with the flat white sky above him and the iron hoops standing round him and could not get one single breath in, and understood the exact size of his own mistake.

It had never been choosing.

Not once, all morning. Not on the road and not in the rows and not over the walls. It did not read the ground and pick. It did whichever one the ground would let it do, in that instant, with whatever its weight happened to be doing — because there was nobody home in it to want either.

He had built a beautiful trap for a thing that decides.

He had put a thing that does not decide into a hole and it had simply done the other one.

---

"*TOREN.*"

He got a breath. It came in wrong and half of it did not arrive.

"*Toren, it's gone off you.*"

He rolled over onto his front in the glass.

"*It's gone north out of the range and it is on the open field and it is going toward the road.*"

---

Here is what the seventh bed of a ruined glasshouse range looked like at about half past ten on the forty-sixth day.

A boy on his hands and knees in broken glass with one boot on. The left side of his ribs not doing what he told them. Both palms open. An ugly grey hook still on the back of his left knuckles, because he had not let go of it, because he had been told once not to let go of it and had built his whole self around the instruction.

And half a mile of open field to the north, with a road up the middle of it.

And two people on that field, four hundred yards out, one of them carrying the other, and neither of them going to turn round.

---

And this is the part Toren Voss never told anybody until he was a very old man, and he told it then only once.

He knew the answer already.

He had known it since a lay-by on the Ninety with the light going, when a man who could not close his left hand had said *there's a thing everybody does in the middle,* and had said *it isn't the putting-down, it's the looking,* and had said *I have tried to teach it four times to four people and it has not worked once.*

He knew it was there. He had always known it was there. He had spent a fortnight knowing exactly where the four-second hole in the middle of him was, the way you know where a bad tooth is.

What he had never once had was a reason to put his whole life on the other side of it.

---

He got up onto one knee.

He did not have a plan. He wanted that understood, afterward, by anybody who ever asked him. He said it in the same flat words every time, and nobody ever entirely believed him.

He had a hook on his left hand and a thing going away north across open ground toward two people he loved, and he needed, in that order and in that instant, to be somewhere else and to still be holding what he was holding.

So he asked for both.

---

The question came up in him. He felt it come. It arrived exactly where it had always arrived, in the place where the counting lived. It was small and reasonable and it sounded like sense. It had four seconds folded up inside it.

*Put the hook down?*

He did not answer it.

He did not refuse it. Refusing is an answer. He simply did not go in and look, the way he had not gone in and looked on the fourth lock of a flight of eight with the hawthorn on his right and the water dropping away on his left.

He kept his attention on the field.

And he asked the warmth under his breastbone, which was small and low and central and about the size of a coin, for a hook and for legs, at the same time, without once checking whether he was allowed to have them.

---

Nothing was put down.

Nothing was picked up.

The dark metal came out of the seam on his forearm a second time, while the first was still out, and went down.

It went down the outside of his left leg from hip to ankle in a single strut, and down the right in another, and up his back in one thin rib on the left side of his spine and nothing at all on the right. It was crooked. It was half a stride, and it was not even a symmetrical half. One leg had about four inches more metal on it than the other.

It was as ugly as the hook.

It arrived in the same instant as the rest of it and it did not flinch.

And in a bed of broken glass in a ruined glasshouse range in the Thirl, for about four seconds, a thirteen-year-old boy on the first rung of the ladder was the loudest thing in eleven miles of country.

---

Four hundred yards out on the open field, the Breach stopped in the middle of a run, in a way that nothing with its weight forward had any business stopping, and turned round.

---

It came back across four hundred yards of open pasture in under six seconds and Toren was moving before it had finished turning.

That was the whole difference. Not speed. He was not fast — a crooked half-stride on a boy who had never held one carried him at about the pace of a grown man running downhill, and the thing coming at him crossed the same ground eleven times quicker.

But he did not have to stop to have his hands.

He went out of the seventh bed and over the footing and back west into the range, with the hook out and his feet doing something they had never done. The wall went under him instead of over him. He did not put anything down to do it.

He got to the fourth bed.

"*It's coming over the second wall,*" said Dessa, from a hundred and forty feet east, flat, at ordinary volume, in the voice she used for reading out the number of crows in an elm. "*Weight's forward. It'll run the bed.*"

---

He went to the gable end.

It was the only high thing left in the range — the standing brick triangle at the west end of the third house, eight feet at the peak, four feet thick at the base, with the rafter sockets still in it. Four feet of brick was why it was still there and the second house's was not.

He put his back to it.

And then he did the thing he had been drilled to do for forty-six days by a man who taught nothing else half so hard, and he did it in a place where it finally meant something.

He chose the ground.

Ten feet of boxed bed in front of him. A wall behind him it could not come through. Two walls either side. No line anywhere, at any angle, from any direction.

There was exactly one thing available to it in that bed.

And that meant he did not need the tell. He did not need to read it. He knew what was coming before its weight had started to move, because he had taken every other option off the table with brick.

Toren Voss stood with his back against a gable end and both feet apart and a hook on his arm, and waited, on purpose, for an eight-foot rising cut with nothing whatever between him and it.

---

"*CLICK,*" said Dessa.

He was already going.

Not backward. There was no backward. Eighteen inches left and low, on a crooked half-stride, underneath a line that came up off the ground at a slant and arrived where his chest had been at a speed that took the top off four courses of brick behind him.

And the hook came round.

---

He did not put it in the edge.

That was the thing Rook said afterward, once, in a corridor, and then never again, and Toren heard it about nine times a year for the rest of his life in the man's exact voice.

He put it round the *back*.

The blade shape a Breach holds is flat along the outside of the long front limb. It is hard and it has an edge on it. And the back of it is a flat grey ridge of nothing at all — the way the back of a blunt, ugly, lopsided hook is a flat grey ridge of nothing at all.

*It's a shape that catches things and pulls them.*

His father had told him that on a road at night, thirteen years to the day after he was born, with a tin box under his arm. He had said it as a compliment. Toren had spent forty-six days not believing him.

The hook went over the back of the limb at the top of the cut and caught.

And Toren pulled.

---

He did not have the weight for it. He never pretended he did. A hundred and ten pounds on a crooked half-stride does not move four hundredweight.

He did not need to move it. He needed to move it a foot.

The cut was on a rising line, and the whole mass of the thing was committed forward into it. Toren Voss got a foot of it, sideways, with his own body going the other way and two struts in the ground.

And the eight-foot rising cut that had been going into his chest went into the brick footing of the fourth wall instead.

It went in about nine inches and stopped.

And the hook was still on the back of it.

And Toren did not let go.

---

Here is what happened next, and it took about a second and a half, and Toren Voss had every part of it for the rest of his life and never once got any of it out of order.

The Breach went to run.

It was what it always did. It had come to the end of a cut, and the cut had gone into brick, and there was nothing else in that bed for it. So its weight came up and went forward, and the long dry grinding started, the way sand goes down a chute.

And the blade shape could not go soft.

It could not go soft because there was a hook round the back of it. The hook was blunt and thick and built for exactly one job. And on the other end of the hook was a boy with two struts driven into the ground and his heels against a gable end and both eyes shut.

So the front of it was holding a cut and the back of it was already running.

It was doing both at once.

---

There was nobody home to do both at once.

The grey went out of it from the limb outward.

Toren felt the pull on his arm change in a way he had no words for — not slackening, but the whole enormous fact of it ceasing to be attached to anything. For about a second there was a shape standing in the fourth bed of a ruined glasshouse made out of nothing whatsoever, four hundredweight of nothing, holding a cut in a brick footing with a hook round the back of it.

And then it came down.

It came down all at once, the way a sack comes off a shoulder, and the sound of it was the sound Toren had heard at the south mouth of a rock cut on the worst morning of his life and had never wanted to hear again. Dry. Flat. Enormous.

A Barrel's worth of reserve-ash came down into a glasshouse bed and went up in a grey cloud eleven feet high and hung there in the still air and began, slowly, to settle.

---

The arm went as it came apart.

Toren had a hook through a thing that weighed four hundredweight, and his own weight on the other end of it, and two struts in the ground holding him where he was. When the mass stopped being a mass it went two directions at once, and it took his left forearm with it. Both bones went, about four inches below the elbow.

He heard it happen.

He went down into the ash and the glass on his right side and lay there.

---

The hook went out.

He did not put it out. It went, the way a thing goes when the hand holding it opens, and it did not come back.

And then the warmth went.

It went all the way down. Not the flicker of the first fortnight and not the guttering of a bad night — it emptied, the way a can empties, steadily and completely, over about four seconds, from a small low central place under his breastbone that he had never had cause to think about before he was thirteen.

And then there was nothing there at all.

Toren lay on his side in four inches of Riftspawn ash with a broken arm under him and went looking for it, and could not find it, and went on looking.

---

Dessa Cole came a hundred and forty feet in a time nobody counted, on a leg that could not run, over four brick footings.

She went down on her knees in the ash beside him.

She did not touch the arm. She got her hand flat on his chest, which was where Rook had taught all four of them to put their hands, in a dry canal, a long time ago, and she held it there.

"I've got you."

"Dessa —"

"Don't move that. Don't move any part of that. I'm going to sit here and be extremely dull at you until somebody comes and I want you to let me."

Toren lay in the ash with his eyes running and his whole left side gone to fire.

"Dessa. It's out."

"I know. It's dead. It's all over the —"

"Not that." He got his right hand up onto his own chest, over hers, over the place. "*It's out.* I can't find it. Dessa, I can't *find* it —"

Dessa Cole put two fingers above his right wrist without being asked, which is a thing you do from the other end.

She was quiet for about four seconds.

"All right," she said. "Then we'll wait for it."

---

The ash settled over the fourth bed for a long time.

Toren lay in it and looked at the flat white sky between the iron hoops and did not ask the question for as long as he could hold out, which was not very long.

"Dessa."

"Mm."

"How long was it?"

"Fourteen minutes," said Dessa Cole. "From when they went out of the east end."

He shut his eyes.

Somewhere a very long way north, out past the seventh footing and half a mile of open field, there was an earth bank with poles on it, and a gate under the poles, and nothing at all coming back the other way across the grass.

"Dessa," said Toren Voss. "Did they get through?"

"They got through," said Dessa.
