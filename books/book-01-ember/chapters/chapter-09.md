# Chapter 9 — Keeping It Out

Toren tested it in the morning, because he could not leave it alone.

They were three miles north with the cart on a farm track and Rook forty yards ahead, and Toren went up and walked beside him and asked the worst question he had.

"Why do we go round hedges instead of through gaps?"

He had asked it on the fourth day out of Hobb's Wall. He had asked it well, with a reason attached, and Rook had said "Because I said so" and walked off, and Toren had carried the humiliation of it around for a week and a half like a stone in a boot.

Rook did not break stride.

"Because a gap is a thing somebody made," he said, "which means it's a place people go, which means it's a place things learn to be. A hedge you have to fight through is a hedge nothing's waiting in. It costs you four minutes." He glanced over. "And because a gap is a hole with a shape. Anything standing in it is standing in the only place you'd look, and anything standing beside it is standing in the one place you won't."

Toren walked on for about eight paces.

"You told me 'because I said so.'"

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because you'd have argued with the answer," Rook said. "You argued with everything for four days. It was the correct thing to do and it was costing me an hour a day."

"And now?"

Rook looked at the track ahead of him.

"Now it's costing me less," he said, which was not an answer, and which was, Toren understood by that afternoon, the closest thing to one he was going to get.

---

He tried it twice more before noon.

He asked why they never camped in a hollow, and got four minutes on cold air, on the way sound moves downhill, and on the fact that a hollow has a rim and a rim is a thing that can be stood on. He asked why Rook carried the flasks on a cord instead of in the pack, and got a look, and then: "Because a pack comes off. Ash doesn't come off."

He went back down the line and fell in beside the cart.

"He's answering things," he said.

Dessa was walking. She had been walking for an hour at a time since the morning at the beck, and it cost her every step, and nobody said anything about either fact.

"I know," she said.

"Since when?"

"Since the barn." She took three of her bad steps and then three more. "You noticed it last night in the dark and thought you'd imagined it. I noticed it this morning when he told Milo why you tie a cart's shafts high instead of level, which is a thing Milo has asked him three separate times."

"So what changed?"

Dessa did not answer straight away.

"Something that happened in a field before anybody was up," she said. "Which I was in the middle of, and which I can't see from the middle of."

"Do you want me to tell you what it looked like from outside?"

"No," Dessa said. "Not yet. Ask me again in a week."

---

They stopped at midday on the rise above the valley and that was where they first saw the canal.

It came up out of the south-west and went away to the north, and it was the straightest thing Toren had ever seen in the world. Whoever cut it had not gone round one single thing. It crossed the valley floor on an embankment and went into the rise on the far side through a cutting and did not deviate by a yard in four miles, and from up on the shoulder of the hill you could see the whole of it lying across the country like a rule laid on a table.

"The Marl," Rook said. "Old navigation. Hundred and sixty years, maybe more."

Water lay in it in some places and not in others. Where it held water it was flat and black and had reeds down both sides. Where it did not, the bed showed as a long trench of cracked silt with the stonework standing up out of it. About two miles north there was a step in the whole line, a place where the thing came down the country in stages, with a row of black timber gates standing in it one after another.

"Locks," said Senna, from the cart, with real pleasure. "Well. There's a thing I didn't expect to see again."

"You've been on one?"

"I've been *up* one. Both directions. Twice." She got her chin up. "My mother's uncle had a share in a boat on the Wend and I spent two summers on it being a nuisance, and I will thank all of you to remember that I was once an extremely capable person."

"Nobody's forgotten," said Milo.

"Milo, you are the only one of these people with any manners and I want that noted too."

Rook was not listening to any of it. He stood on the shoulder of the hill with his hands at his sides and looked at the line of the thing for a long time.

"Right," he said. "That's our road for four days."

Wyck said, "Why?"

"Because it's flat, it's made, it's got a hard path on the south bank the whole way, and that cart will do fourteen miles a day on it instead of nine." Rook picked up his pack. "That's the good half."

"What's the other half?"

"The other half is that it's four miles of straight line," Rook said, "and that's a thing I want all five of you to have looked at properly before you stand on it."

---

The question came out at the halt above the locks, and it was Wyck who asked it, which surprised nobody who had been paying attention.

He had been quiet for two days. He had pulled the shafts more than his share and had not made anything of it, and he had watched the whole of the morning at the beck being retold without contributing one word.

Now he sat on the bank with his forearms on his knees, in exactly the way Rook sat, which Toren was fairly sure Wyck had never once noticed about himself, and said:

"What are you?"

"Say the question properly."

"You know the question." Wyck did not look up. "Everybody in every yard in the world has a word for it. My father used them. *He's an Ember, he's barely a Flame, that one's a Fire and he knows it.*" He turned a bit of grass over in his fingers. "So what are you. On the ladder."

Rook considered him for a moment.

"Blaze," he said.

Toren did not know what that meant. He knew, from the way Wyck's hands stopped moving, that Wyck did.

"All right," Rook said. "Since it's out. All of you sit down, because you're going to hear these words used at you at every gate between here and Meridian, and I'd rather you didn't learn them from somebody selling you something."

He drew nothing in the dust. He told them off on his fingers instead, flatly, the way he read out a bill.

"Kindled. The door's open and nothing's come through it yet. That's everybody the morning after, and it's where four of you are standing.

"Ember. It glows and it warms and it stays where you put it. It's small and it's contained and it doesn't go out every time you look away from it. That's the first rung and it's the one that takes longest, and the reason everybody underrates it is that the word also belongs to a lump of metal.

"Flame. It catches. That's the whole difference — it holds on its own without you standing over it with your teeth clenched.

"Fire. It does actual work. It's the one a camp gathers round, not the one you carry in your hand.

"Torch." He stopped for a second. "Fire that's yours. Chosen, portable, reliable. Everything you can do, you can do because you decided to. And that's as far as one door goes. Not as far as one *person* goes — you can be a Torch for thirty years and get better every year of it. But the ladder stops there if you've only got the one door, and almost everybody has only got the one door."

Toren heard himself say, "What was my father?"

He had not planned to.

Rook did not soften it and did not hurry it.

"Torch," he said. "Comfortably. For a long time."

"Then that's —"

"That's the top of his ladder, yes. And I watched him hold a gap eight feet wide with two of them coming at him at a time for about four minutes, and then run out, which is what happens to every Torch who ever lived if you keep the road coming long enough." Rook's voice did not change. "It is not a small thing to be a Torch. Most people who train their whole lives get to Fire and stop. I'd have been glad of him on any road I've ever walked."

Toren looked at the grass and did not say anything for a bit.

"And Blaze," said Dessa.

"Blaze is where it stops staying inside one door." Rook held his hand out flat, and turned it over. "That's it. That's the whole definition. There's more than one way in and a Blaze isn't stuck with the one he was handed."

"That's why you did Marta's thing," Toren said. "In the cut. With the struts still on."

"Yes."

"Then what's above it?"

"One more," Rook said.

He said it the way a man closes a door with his foot, and moved on before anybody's mouth was open.

"There's a cost to it and you should have that as well, since you're getting the rest. At Blaze the heat stops being only heat. It shows. Not much and not always, but it shows, and anything within about a mile that hunts worked power knows exactly where I am and what I am." He looked at Milo. "Same as a light in a palm. Exactly the same, only bigger and harder to switch off."

Milo's ears had gone red.

"So you've got it all the time," he said.

"I've got it all the time."

"That's —" Milo stopped, and started again, in a small voice. "That's awful."

"It's a trade," Rook said. "Like every other one."

Wyck said, without any expression at all: "And what's she?"

Everybody looked at Dessa.

"Kindled," Rook said.

"After that? After the thing in the field?"

"Kindled. Same as she was the day before yesterday." Rook got up and shouldered the pack. "She did something for four seconds and she can't do it again, and there's no word for that on any ladder anybody's ever written down. If I told you different I'd be doing the thing I said I wouldn't, which is selling you something."

Dessa, who had not said one word during any of it, gave him a small nod, the way she had nodded in a weigh-yard at a form filled in wrongly.

---

The towpath was cinder, packed hard, four feet wide, with the water on one hand and a hawthorn hedge gone to a wall of thorn on the other. The cart ran on it like a cart running on a floor.

They made eleven miles that afternoon without anybody having to put a shoulder into anything. By the end of it Milo had started singing, which he had not done since Sallow Creek, and had stopped as soon as he noticed. Nobody had told him to go on, because nobody wanted to be the reason he had noticed.

They stopped at the foot of a flight of eight locks, where the canal came down the country in steps. The chambers were stone-lined and deep and the black timber gates still hung in most of them. Two of the pounds between were dry, with cracked silt in the bottom and stone coping standing four feet proud on both sides. At the tail of the flight the path went under a road bridge, a single stone arch with the towpath running through it, and the hole under the arch was about seven feet wide and had a dip of water at the bottom of it and old rope-grooves worn a hand deep into the stone at the corner.

Rook walked the whole flight twice before he let anybody put the cart down.

Then he came back and said to Toren, "Bucket."

---

"Fill it."

Toren filled the water can from the pound and came back with it and put it down.

"Hold it out. Arm straight."

He held it out. It took about twenty seconds for his arm to start shaking and about forty for it to start being a problem.

"Now put it down. Shake that out." Rook took the can and held it out himself, and the difference was visible immediately and Toren could not have said what it was. "Look at my hand. Not the can, the hand."

Rook's fingers were hooked through the handle. That was all. The hand was not closed. The whole weight of a full can hung off four fingers and the arm below the shoulder was doing nothing whatsoever.

"You gripped it," Rook said. "You put your whole hand round the handle and squeezed, and you were holding the can *and* holding your hand shut, and you paid twice for the same can." He set it down. "You've been doing that with the hook for a fortnight. Put it out."

Toren put the hook out.

"Now," Rook said. "Walk to that gate and back and tell me what you do while you're walking."

He walked to the gate and back and by the time he got there the hook was gone, and he had done twelve paces, which was about his average.

"Tell me what you did."

"I held it."

"Say the actual thing."

Toren thought about it properly, the way Dessa had been made to think about it in a field.

"I kept checking," he said slowly. "I keep — every couple of paces I sort of go back in and look at it. To see if it's still there."

"And what does looking cost?"

"Nothing. It's looking."

"Put it out and look at it once, deliberately, and tell me what you feel."

Toren put it out and looked in at the warmth under his breastbone on purpose.

The hook flinched.

It was tiny. It was about as much as a candle flame does when a door opens somewhere else in the house. But he felt it happen in his arm and he felt where it came from, and he stood on a towpath in the last of the light with his mouth open.

"It's not looking," he said. "It's *pulling.* It's a little pull, every time —"

"Every time. And you do it about forty times in twelve paces, because you're frightened it'll go out, and every check is a small piece of the thing you're checking." Rook crouched down. "That's the whole of what's wrong with you and there is nothing else wrong with you. You don't need more of anything. You've got a shape that comes when it's needed and most people your age would trade you a year for it. You just can't stop paying a toll at your own gate."

"Then how do I —"

"You don't hold it," Rook said. "You stop letting go of it. Those are two different movements and everybody learns the wrong one first."

"That's the same —"

"It isn't. Holding is something you do. Not-letting-go is something you *don't* do. You can keep not doing a thing all day. You cannot keep doing a thing all day. That's the difference between eleven paces and a morning." He stood up. "Put it out and walk up the flight. Don't count."

Toren opened his mouth.

"I know," Rook said. "Don't count."

---

Here is what it is like to stop counting when counting is the only thing you have ever had.

It is like being asked to walk across a plank without looking at your feet. Everything in him wanted the number. The number was the thing he put between himself and how bad a situation was. He had counted coughs in the dark and paces on the road and miles to a notch in a ridge and thirty-one trips to a scree slope and eleven seconds of a man coming down a shale fan, and every single one of those numbers had been a handrail.

He put the hook out at the bottom lock and started up the flight and made himself not count.

The first thirty yards were awful. He kept arriving at a number anyway and having to put it down, and each time he put it down he checked the warmth to see whether the putting-down had cost him anything, which was the exact thing he had just been told not to do, which he then also had to put down.

Somewhere around the second lock he stopped arguing with himself and just walked.

The towpath went up beside the black gates with the water dropping away on his left in steps. The hawthorn was on his right, tangled and old, full of the small noises of birds settling. His boots made a dry scuff on the cinder. The weight of the hook hung off the end of his arm and dragged his wrist over slightly, the way it always did, and he did not think about it, and it did not go anywhere.

At the fourth lock he realized he did not know how long it had been.

The thought went through him like cold water. He braced for the flinch and the thinning and the coming-apart, and he reached in to check, and caught himself doing it, and — with an effort that felt physically like taking his hand off a rail at a height — did not.

The warmth under his breastbone did something he had never felt it do.

It stopped moving. That was all. For a fortnight it had been a thing that flickered and surged and guttered and had to be coaxed, a candle in a room with a bad door. Now it simply sat there, low and small and entirely steady, about the size of a coin, doing nothing at all except being on.

It was not more. He was clear about that afterward, every time he tried to describe it. It was not one degree warmer or one hair larger. It had stopped being a thing that was *happening* and started being a thing that *was.*

He got to the top gate of the eighth lock with the hook still on the end of his arm and turned round.

Rook was standing at the bottom of the flight where he had left him, two hundred yards down, a small dark upright shape with the last of the light on the water behind him.

Toren could not see his face at that distance. He did not need to. He saw the chin come up half an inch, and go back down, and nothing else about the man move at all.

---

"That's Ember," Rook said, when Toren came back down.

The hook had gone out somewhere on the return trip and Toren had not noticed it go, which was its own strange piece of news.

"It doesn't feel like anything."

"It never does. That's why people go looking for a better feeling and spend four years getting worse." Rook was already turning up the path. "It'll be gone in the morning."

"What?"

"Not gone. It'll feel gone. You'll wake up and reach for it and it'll be the old flickering business again and you'll think you dreamed today." He said it as a plain matter of scheduling. "You didn't. It comes back inside a week and then it stays, and the week is the part everybody remembers hating. Eat something."

Toren stood on the towpath with his heart going and his arm aching and a great deal he wanted to say, and found that there was nobody available to say it to, because Rook had walked on and the others were forty yards up making a fire in the lee of the lock wall.

He went up.

Milo shouted something at him about the fire. Senna wanted to know whether the top gates still had their paddles on, as though anybody there could have told her. Dessa was sitting on a coping stone with her leg out straight, and she looked up at him as he came into the light and looked back down again, and said, to the fire, in a perfectly ordinary voice:

"Well done."

Wyck was on the other side of the fire with his back against the stone, mending a strap.

He did not look up and he did not say anything at all. His hands went on doing the thing his hands were doing. After a moment he shifted a little, so that the strap caught the light better, and went on with it.

---

The thing on the far bank came at last light and none of them would have seen it if Dessa had not been sitting facing that way.

"Stop," she said. "Everybody stop. North side, across the dry pound, on the top of the far coping."

Everybody stopped.

It was three hundred yards off, standing on the stone lip of the empty canal bed with the gray sky behind it, and Toren's first thought was that somebody had left a set of surveyor's poles standing up in the dark.

It was mostly legs.

That was the shape of it and there was not much else to say. Four of them, or possibly six, thin and straight and very long, coming up to a small hard body carried high — higher than a man's head, higher than a horse's back. There was no neck and there was no head that Toren could distinguish. It stood absolutely still with all that height on all that thinness and did not sway one inch in the wind coming down the cut.

"Is it a —"

"Quiet," said Rook.

He had not moved. He had not stood up. Toren looked at him and saw that his eyes had gone to the thing and the rest of him had gone somewhere else entirely, which was the face he had worn on a road eight days ago looking west at three loud hounds.

The thing moved.

There was no gather. Toren had seen a verge-hound come out of stillness with nothing in between and had thought at the time that he would never see anything worse. This was not the same. A hound had gathered, once, fast, in a way you could at least argue you might have caught. This did not gather at all. It was standing on the coping and then it was a hundred yards further along the coping, dead straight, level, with all those legs going in a blur underneath a body that did not rise or fall by a finger's width.

It stopped.

It stopped the way a thrown thing stops when it hits a wall, except that there was no wall and nothing had hit anything. Full speed to standing still, in the open, on a stone lip four inches wide.

And then it stood there. It did not look around, because there was nothing on it to look with. It simply stood.

"Off the path," Rook said quietly. "All of you. Behind the lock wall, now, without standing up first."

They went. Dessa went poorly and Wyck took her weight and neither of them said anything about it.

From behind four feet of stone coping Toren watched through the gap under the beam of the gate. The thing stood on the far side for a while. Then it went again — same speed, same absolute straightness, a different line — and it went up the far bank and away across the fields to the north-east, and it was out of sight in about four seconds.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"What was it?" said Milo.

"Tomorrow," Rook said. "In daylight, on ground I've chosen, with me standing there. Not tonight."

He was still looking at the place where it had gone. He had not moved off his knee.

"Rook."

"It's a Flask and a half and there's one of them, and one of them is a thing five people can do something about," he said. "Which is why we're going to talk about it in the morning with the light on it, instead of tonight, in the dark, where the only thing any of you would take away is how it looked."

Toren lay down that night against the lock wall with the fire out and his shoulder aching and the warmth under his breastbone sitting quiet and steady where he had left it.

He was nearly asleep before it arrived.

They had walked eleven miles that afternoon on a made path, four feet wide, cinder-hard, with water on one side and a thorn hedge on the other, and it had run without one bend in it from the embankment to the foot of the flight.

A mile and a half of straight line. And they had been strung out along the whole of it, singing.
