# Chapter 19 — The Level

Milo did the bread the way he always did it, and on the second night after Ferrin Ridley went back down the water road Toren finally understood that it was a thing and not a habit.

There was a loaf and a half of it, dark and close-grained, out of a Ridley cousin's oven at Wenn's Ford. Milo cut it on the tailgate with the small knife, and he cut it poorly, because he always did. Then he took it round the fire in an order.

Senna first. He said her name when he put it in her hand.

Then Rook, who was standing up at the edge of the light with his back to them and had to be gone to.

Then Wyck. Then Dessa. Then Toren.

Then himself, last, sitting down, with the end crust and whatever the knife had ruined.

"Why do you say it?" Toren asked.

"Say what?"

"Our names. You say them every time. You've said mine about ninety times and I've only just noticed."

Milo looked faintly embarrassed, which on Milo took about a second and a half and then gave up.

"My mother did it," he said. "She said a thing given without a name isn't given. It's just put down." He turned the crust over. "That's the whole of it. She wasn't religious about much. She was religious about that."

"And the order?"

"Oldest first." He said it as if it were obvious, which to him it plainly was. "Senna, then him, then Wyck, then Dessa, then you, then me. That's the order. It's not complicated, Toren, it's just how old everybody is."

"I'm three months older than you."

"I know," said Milo. "I've decided not to count them."

---

Toren lay down that night with a small thing in his head that he did not say, and did not say the next night either.

Rook had put a word on three of them now. Toren had got his at the top of a lock flight, standing in the dark with a hook still on the end of his arm. Wyck had got his lying in a cart with his ribs strapped, and had been told he had been carrying it since the rock cut and nobody had thought to mention it. Dessa had got hers in wet grass at first light with two fingers on her wrist.

Milo had not got one.

Nobody had said so. Nobody was going to say so, because the only way of saying it was unkind, and because Milo would have agreed with it instantly and cheerfully and then gone and done the water.

*Kindled. The door's open and nothing's come through it yet.*

Toren lay on his side in the dark with that sentence in his head. Eight feet away a fourteen-year-old boy slept with both hands under his chin. There was no way to turn the sentence that made it come out kind, and after a while Toren stopped trying and went to sleep.

---

Rook did not sit down that evening and he had not sat down the evening before.

Toren noticed it the way he noticed everything now, which was in numbers, and the numbers were not good. Four days since the steading. Four nights of a man standing at the edge of the light with his hands open at his sides. Two of those nights he had not walked out past it at all, which was new, and was worse than the walking.

And every evening at the halt he went and knelt at the tailgate for eleven or twelve minutes with his hands on Senna Voss, and did not explain it, and came away and drank a cup of water and stood up for the rest of the night.

Dessa let it run. Then she stopped the cart at noon on the fourth day out of the steading.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'd rather —"

"I know what you'd rather. Sit down on the step, please, and give me your wrist."

Rook looked at her for a moment. Then he sat down on the step of the cart, in the middle of a road in open country, and pushed his left sleeve back and put his forearm out.

Nobody had ever seen him do that.

"I don't know how," Dessa said.

"You do. It's the same thing from the other end. Two fingers above the wrist, and don't press. You're not taking a pulse." He turned his hand palm up. "Then you ask me how much of the room's lit, and I say a fraction, and then you say yours, and then we find out whether we agree."

Dessa put her fingers where he had put his eleven days ago.

"How much of the room's lit?"

"About a third."

The wind went over the field. Toren stood on the shafts with his hands stopped on the wood.

"Say it again," said Dessa.

"A third. Maybe a shade under." Rook's voice did not change. "Say yours."

"A third." Dessa did not take her fingers off. "What's it usually?"

"Most of it."

"Say a number."

"I don't have one. That's an honest answer and I know it's a poor one." He took his arm back and pulled the sleeve down. "I've never had cause to put a number on the top of it, because nobody's ever had cause to ask. What I can tell you is that a third is the lowest it has been since I was nineteen years old."

---

Wyck worked it out first and said it at the tailgate an hour later, flatly, with his arm across his ribs.

"It's her."

"It's a number of things."

"It's her." Wyck did not move out of the way. "You go down on one knee at that tailgate every night at the halt and you're there eleven minutes and you come away grey. I've watched you do it fourteen times. I thought it was checking. It isn't checking. Checking takes a minute."

Rook put the water can down.

"No," he said. "It isn't checking."

"Then say what it is."

"It's exactly what you think it is, and I'm not going to make it sound better." He did not look at the cart. "There is a thing you can do for another person from outside, and it is one of the two things my mother's village traded in, and it is slow and it does not cure anything and it costs the person doing it more than it gives. It buys days. That's all it does. It buys them one at a time and it charges for them."

"How many days has it bought?"

"I don't know. Four. Six." Rook picked the can back up. "Ask her what she thinks. She'll tell you, and she'll be ruder about it than I've been."

Wyck stood in front of him for a while longer.

"Does she know you're paying for it?"

"She worked it out on the second night," Rook said, "and she has not said one word about it since, which is the only argument that woman has ever lost in her life and she's losing it on purpose."

---

They came up onto the last of the rising ground at about four that same afternoon, on the thirty-second day, and the country stopped.

That was the only way Toren could put it afterward. The road went up a long shoulder of rough grass, and at the top of the shoulder there was nothing.

There was water.

It went out in front of them further than he could see. It was not a lake, because a lake has a shape and this had none. It was flat and grey-brown and absolutely still, and it was full of things that had no business being in water — the tops of hedges, in lines, going away for miles. Fence posts in rows. A dead orchard standing up to its waist with every branch bare. A church tower a long way off with a jackdaw on it, and the roofs of four or five buildings below the tower, and the water coming up their walls to the tops of the doorways.

And running north through all of it, dead straight, from where they were standing to the edge of what a person could see, there was a bank.

"Oh," said Milo.

"That's the Level," said Senna, from the cart, without opening her eyes. "I wondered when we'd get to it."

---

They stood on the shoulder for a long time.

The bank was an earthwork. Somebody had built it, and had built it well, and had built it a very long time ago — a raised road running out across the drowned country on a made embankment, with stone facing down both sides into the water and a grass verge and a made surface on the top of it about sixteen feet wide.

Fourteen feet up, Toren guessed. Maybe fifteen. The stone went down the sides at a steep slope into black water and you could see about four feet of it below the line and then nothing.

There was no cover on it anywhere. Not a tree, not a wall, not a fold in the ground. It was a line drawn across water.

"How long?" Rook said.

"Eleven miles," said Senna. "It was eleven miles when I was a girl and I don't suppose anybody's moved it."

"Is it the only one?"

"It's the only one. That's the whole point of it." She got herself up onto her elbow. "That was all farmed once. All of it, out to the tower and past it. They pumped it. There were engines that ran day and night and took the water off it and put it in the cuts, and they did that for about two hundred years. Then in 'eighty the engines stopped. It came back up in eleven months." She lay back down. "My mother's people were off there somewhere. I've never once been able to make myself look at it properly."

"And round it?"

"You don't go round it, love," said Senna. "You go round it in four days."

---

Rook walked out onto the top of the ramp on his own and stood there.

Toren watched the back of him. He had spent thirty-two days learning to read the length of a man's standing, and had got nowhere with it at all, and knew only that some of them were arithmetic and some of them were not.

This one was arithmetic. He could see it in the shoulders.

Rook came back and did something he had done exactly twice on that road. He got everybody into a half-circle at the head of the cart, including Senna, propped up, and he laid it out.

"Right. I'd like all of you to have this at the same time, because in about an hour I'm going to make a decision with it and I'd rather not be the only one holding the numbers.

"Eleven miles of that, on a bank sixteen feet wide, with water on both sides. There is no cover. There is nowhere to put the cart that isn't in the middle of the road. There is no way off it except the two ends, and there is no water on it you could drink, because that stuff out there has had a drowned country standing in it for seventy years." He counted it on his fingers as he went, the way he read out a bill. "With the cart and with the halts, that is two days on the bank and one night on it. There is no version where it's one day.

"Going round is four days east or five west, and both of those roads are worse than this one in every way except the one we're discussing.

"And Senna has not got four days of slack in her."

"Don't," said Toren.

"He's right, love." Senna's hands were folded. "Let him say it in numbers. It's the kindest way anybody's ever said it to me."

---

There was a cottage at the foot of the ramp.

It sat off to the west side on a little raised platform of its own, with a lime-washed shed and a barrow and about forty yards of dug garden holding on above the waterline. There was a pear tree against the gable that had been trained flat by somebody patient.

Rook stopped the cart at eighty yards and put his hands out from his sides and said the three things in the right order, and the old man who came out of the shed did not put his billhook down for a full minute.

His name was Amery. He had been the bankman on the Level for twenty-nine years, which meant he walked eleven miles of it and eleven miles back, in sections, forever, looking for slips and rabbit runs and soft places, and filling them, one barrow at a time, on his own.

"There's no wages in it," he said. "There hasn't been wages in it since before the Fall. There's a cottage and a garden and there's the fact that if I stop, it goes, and then there's no road."

"Who else walks it?"

"Nobody else walks it," said Amery. "That's rather the position."

---

Rook gave him the whole of it standing in the garden path.

Coldharbour. The tally-boards off the nails and the clean dry rectangle on a shelf. Bram Stell's loss book, bought for eleven times its worth by a woman who held it in both hands and said thank you. Wenn's Ford, and the Ridley outfit, and a fresh chevron cut into the east face of a marker stone.

*Who else keeps a book.*

"And you keep one," Rook said.

The old man's face had gone a bad colour somewhere around Coldharbour and had stayed there.

"Twenty-nine years," he said. "Every slip, every soft place, every yard of facing that's come away and what month it came away in and what the water was doing that week. It's in the house. It's four books." His mouth moved. "It's not worth anything. It's not worth anything to anybody. It's a list of holes."

"It's a list of where a bank fails," said Rook, "on the only road across eleven miles of water. Get it out of that house."

"I can't get it out of the house. Where would it go? There's nowhere for it to go. There's water."

"Then copy the last four years of it and give the copy to somebody at the north end, and put the books in the top of that pear tree in a bag, and tell nobody. I don't care. It doesn't have to be clever. It has to be in two places."

Amery put the billhook against the shed wall so that he could stop holding it.

"You've come too late," he said.

---

Toren was standing at the head of the shafts with the water can in his hand and he felt the whole garden go small and cold.

"Say it," said Rook.

"Nine of them." Amery would not look at any of them. "They came up the south road five days ago. They watered at my trough. They paid me for it, coin, and one bit of brass that isn't money, and they told me the brass was better than money on the right road and not to be too quick about showing it."

"Go on."

"They bought the book. The woman did." The old man's voice had gone flat and fast, the voice of somebody who has been saying a thing to himself in a shed for five days. "Two of the four books. The old ones. She said the new ones would be no use to her and I could keep them, and she gave me eleven bits for the two. Eleven bits. There's been nine years since anybody gave me a bit of anything." He wiped his mouth. "And she asked me who else keeps a book."

"And you told her."

"I told her about the woman at Marram Head." Amery's hands had started. "I'd never met the woman at Marram Head. She's a name I've heard at a ford twice in my life. That's what I gave her, because it was the cheapest thing in the house and I wanted them gone off my path, and I have thought about it every hour since."

"That's not the worst of it," said Rook.

"No."

"Tell me the worst of it."

---

Amery looked up then.

"They came back," he said.

The pear tree moved against the gable. Out past the garden the water lay grey and flat with a line of drowned hedge going away into it, and nothing on the whole of the Level moved at all.

"They went north onto the bank five days ago and I watched them go, and I was glad. And two mornings back they came south down it again, all nine, and they stopped at that gate." He pointed. "And she didn't ask me about books.

"She asked me whether a man had come up the south road with a hand cart and four young ones and an old woman lying in it."

Nobody said anything.

"I said not yet," said Amery. "God help me, that's what I said. Not *no.* *Not yet.* I've had two days to think about that as well.

"And she said thank you. She paid me again, which I would not take and which she put on the wall. Then they went back north up the bank.

"And they haven't come off it. I'd know. I'd have seen them at this end, and there's nobody at the north end to see them at that one."

---

Rook stood in an old man's garden with his hands at his sides for a while.

"How wide is the bank at the engine house?" he said.

Amery blinked at him.

"You know it."

"I've never been on this road in my life. I know what an engine house is and I know there'll be one, because you don't pump two hundred square miles out of a hole in the ground with good intentions. How far along and how wide?"

"Four mile and a bit. It's the old Skeat's Level engine, and it's a ruin, and it's the only thing standing on eleven miles." The old man got hold of himself and became, for about ninety seconds, entirely competent, which Toren thought afterward was the kindest thing that happened in that garden. "There's a platform there. The bank goes wide — call it sixty yards by thirty, west side, where the coal yard was. The engine house is three storeys of brick with no roof and one door on the road side. There's a sluice wall runs out west into the water off the end of the platform, eighty yards of good stone with three gate frames in it, and you can walk the top of it if you've a head for it, and it goes nowhere. It ends."

"And north of it?"

"Four hundred yards north there's the arch. The bank humps up over the main drain and there's a brick arch under it, and the road narrows over the top to about eleven foot with parapets." Amery looked at him. "That's the whole of the furniture. Eleven miles, and a ruin, and a wall that ends, and an arch."

"Thank you," said Rook.

---

They had it out on the ramp, in the open, at about six in the evening, with the light going flat and orange across the water.

Dessa said the arithmetic because Dessa always said the arithmetic.

"Four days round, and she hasn't got four days. Two days on the bank, and they're on the bank." She had the slate on her knees and was not writing on it. "Those are the only two numbers and they're both bad. So say the third thing, because there's always a third thing with you."

"The third thing is that they will not go away," Rook said. "We can sit at the foot of this ramp for a week. They've got two of Amery's books, they've got coin, and they've got a boat's worth of patience. They are not in a hurry, and I have never once in six years known that company to be in a hurry. We would be sitting here on day seven with a woman four days worse and nine people still on that bank."

"So we walk into it."

"So we walk into it."

Wyck said, from the tailgate, in the voice he used for reading out a number: "Say the part you don't want to say."

Rook turned round.

"The part I don't want to say is that I've got one trade and it's the only one I've ever had," he said. "You pick the ground. Everything I have taught the four of you is a way of picking ground early — eighty yards, eight feet, a door instead of a wall, four minutes before you need it. It is the entire content of my head and it took eleven years to get in there.

"And there is one road across that water. It is sixteen feet wide and I did not build it and I cannot leave it, and somebody who is good at this has had five days to look at it and has already chosen where she wants me.

"I have never walked onto ground another person chose. Not once. Not in six years. That is what I am about to do, and I want it said out loud at the bottom of the ramp and not remembered afterward as something that just happened."

---

Toren put his hand out for the near shaft and found it was already shaking, and put it in his armpit, and then took it out again and put it on the shaft anyway.

"Then tell us what we do," he said.

"I will. Not tonight." Rook looked at the four of them. "Tonight you eat and you sleep in Amery's shed with a door on it, because it is the last door there is. Tomorrow we go up onto that bank at first light and we walk, and I will tell you the whole of it while we walk, in order, and you will be sick of the sound of my voice by the fourth mile.

"Two things now.

"Wyck. There's a building four miles up with one door in it. I want you to have chosen that door tonight, lying down, in the dark, before you have ever seen it. Amery will tell you which way it faces and how wide it is, and you will ask him about the threshold and the lintel and whether there's rubble in it, and you will not take my word for any of it."

Wyck did not say anything for a second.

"That's four minutes," he said.

"That's about fourteen hours. Use them."

"And me?" said Dessa.

"You stop counting people," said Rook. "You've been doing it well for three weeks. From tomorrow morning I don't want a number of people out of you at all. I want hands."

---

They went up onto the Level at first light on the thirty-third day and Toren found out inside a quarter of a mile that he had been frightened of the wrong thing.

He had expected the water. The water turned out to be nothing. It lay flat and brown on both sides and had a smell like a cellar, and after a mile his eye stopped going to it.

What he had not expected was the width.

Sixteen feet is a wide road. It is wider than the ridge lane and much wider than a towpath. On top of a bank with the ground gone from under both sides, sixteen feet is the size of a table. He walked the near shaft with the wheel four feet from the edge and the stone facing going down into the black at an angle you could not have stood up on, and he understood by the second mile why a man would call it a line and not a road.

"Off the verge," Rook said, about every ten minutes. "Middle. Both wheels on the hard."

Dessa called the ground from the head of the shafts, which she had done for twenty days, and did not once say a number of anything.

---

He paid the debt in the second mile.

Dessa asked for it. She did it without any preamble at all, from the head of the shafts, with her eyes on the road and her hand out flat over them against the glare off the water.

"Sixteen days ago I turned something down," she said. "I said I'd like it the next day instead, all of it, and that I'd write it on the slate. Nobody's mentioned it since and I've not asked, because there hasn't been a day where asking wouldn't have been a knife."

"No," said Rook. "There hasn't."

"There is now."

"There is now." He came back from where he had been walking, which was forty yards out in front, and took the near side of the cart, and walked there. "Get the slate out. You'll want it."

Dessa gave the head of the shafts to Milo and got the slate out. She walked the far side of the cart with the nail in her fist. Toren pulled and listened and did not say one word for an hour and ten minutes.

---

"Nine," Rook said. "That's not the size of the company. That's the size of a crew. There's about forty of them altogether that I've ever been able to account for, in four or five crews, and they do not work in the same country as each other and they do not, as far as I can tell, ever meet.

"A crew is nine, and it is nine because nine is what you need. Two who talk. Two who go round the back while the talking's happening. Three who can fight properly if it comes to it, and two who carry and count and write. It does not vary. I have looked at four crews in six years and every single one of them is nine, and every single one of them has the same jobs in it, and that is the most frightening thing about them and nobody I have ever said it to has understood why."

"Why?" said Milo.

"Because it means somebody wrote it down," said Rook. "Somebody sat in a room a long time ago and worked out what a crew needs, and then made every crew that. That is not a band of men on a road. That is a trade with a method in it."

Dessa's nail went across the slate.

"And what do they take?"

"Records. Only records. That is the whole of it and it has never once varied." He held his hand up and put a finger down for each one. "Loss books. Tally boards. Weigh ledgers. Hunting grounds. Bad-ground tallies. Anything with a place and a date in it.

"They pay well for it, and they pay in front of witnesses. Where they can, they buy rather than take. About two times out of three they leave a yard better than they found it — a mended gate, a bought book, two nights' drinking with a woman of ninety.

"And the third time somebody says no."

---

"What happens the third time?" said Wyck, from the cart.

"They take the gate first," said Rook.

Nobody said anything. The bank went on. Somewhere off to the east a heron got up off a drowned hedge and went away low over the water with its legs trailing.

"Say the rest of it," said Dessa.

"They put three people across the way out, and then they go through the place, and when they are finished they go on to the next one. They do not burn anything, because a burn brings people. They do not take ash, because ash has a price and a price has a record. At Coldharbour they walked past four hundredweight of it with the lids off the bins." He watched the road go under the wheel. "There were five under stones behind that shed and two a woman dug half a day for and never found. That is what the third time costs, and it is not a rage and it is not an appetite. It is a cost they have decided is acceptable and they have decided it the same way you decide what a wheel repair is worth."

"How many third times?" said Toren.

"I know of nine yards in six years. There will be more that I don't know of, because the way you find out about the third time is that somebody is left alive to say so."

---

"Now the brass," Rook said.

He undid the button of his coat and took the two chits out and held them flat on his palm, and for the second time in his life Toren looked at a chevron with a line struck through it.

"It isn't money. I told you that at Coldharbour and I told you the part of it that's useful. Here's the whole of it.

"A chit is a day. One man, one day, on a job. It is struck when the day is owed and the line goes through the chevron when the day is paid." He turned one over with his thumb. "That's what the line is. Everybody who has ever looked at one of these and thought it was a badge or a threat or a piece of a pattern has been looking at a receipt with the account squared on it. The number underneath is the line in the book."

"What book?" said Milo.

"The paybook."

The word came out of him exactly like a nail going into a plank, and Toren felt the whole cart change.

"Every crew has one and it is carried by whoever counts. It has a line for every day that has ever been worked by anybody in that company, with the number and the crew and the date, and it goes back to whenever they started, because a trade with a method in it does not throw its own book away." Rook buttoned the chits back into his coat. "They have spent eleven years buying every record on four roads, and the reason nobody has ever caught them at it is that the only complete record of what they have done is in their own coat pocket."

Dessa had stopped writing.

"The eleventh of the ninth month," she said.

"Yes."

"It'd be a line in it."

"It would be nine lines in it," said Rook, "with nine numbers and nine names against them, and one of those names is a man who was put off the books at Lowry Bend two years later, and I do not know which."

He walked on for about forty yards.

"I have been six years on this road," he said, "and I have never once been closer to that book than a bootmaker's tray. I would like all four of you to know, before anything happens on this bank, exactly what it is I want, so that afterward you are not left guessing about it."

---

At about eleven Dessa said: "Rook."

"Go on."

"Smoke. Small one. Long way up." She had her hand flat over her eyes. "And there's a straight line in the road about four miles on that isn't the road."

"That's the engine house."

"It's a building?"

"It's a building," said Rook.

---

They made four miles by two in the afternoon, which was better than anybody had expected and which nobody was pleased about.

The engine house came up out of the flat at about a mile and a half and got slowly, steadily larger, and for an hour and a half it was the only thing in the world. Three storeys of dark brick with the roof gone and the sky through the window holes. A broken chimney stub beside it. A low wall round a yard.

At a mile Toren could see the sluice wall running west off the platform into the water, a straight pale stone line going out eighty yards and stopping in the middle of nothing.

At three-quarters of a mile Dessa said, "Cart on the platform. Two-wheel. Blue."

At half a mile she said, "Somebody's on the roof of the low wall and they've been there since I started looking."

And at four hundred yards a man came out from the engine house doorway, walked down the middle of the road toward them at an ordinary pace, stopped at a distance that Toren did not have to measure because he had been made to pace it out eleven times in a green lane, and put his hands out from his sides with the palms showing.

"Eighty yards," said Wyck, from the cart.

"Yes," said Rook.

"He's doing it right."

"Yes," said Rook. "He will."
