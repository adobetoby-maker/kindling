# Chapter 7 — Everything Gets Harder

He got up at the exact moment the road became a thing you could see the edges of, and he did not stretch, and he did not look at anybody.

"Shafts," Rook said. "Toren and Milo. We're moving."

Nobody argued. Toren had been awake for an hour and had spent most of it deciding what he was going to say, and now that there was a man standing in front of him with a coat on, he discovered that all of it had been about last night and none of it was any use this morning.

"She can't be moved," he said.

"She can be moved poorly or she can be left on a road where five of those things died last night." Rook was already lifting the near shaft and walking it round. "Ash draws things. Blood draws things. Both of those are on this bank and neither of them is going anywhere, so we are. Four miles, then we look at her again."

"Four miles is —"

"Four miles is the number." He set the shaft down in Toren's hands. "Take the weight low and don't fight the ruts. Milo, other side."

They went north before anybody had eaten, on a drove road with a drainage ditch on one hand and a dead poplar every thirty yards on the other, and it took Toren about half a mile to understand that his shoulders had been given a job this morning so that his mouth would have less to do.

---

Dessa lay in the bed of the cart with her leg splinted between two bits of the cart's own side-rail and her head at the tailgate end, next to Senna's feet.

She did not make a sound for the first mile. Toren knew because he was listening for it, between the shafts, with his back to her, over the noise of the wheel.

At the mile and a bit the near wheel dropped into a rut, and the whole bed jarred, and Dessa said one word into her own sleeve.

"You'll want to save those," Senna said, without opening her eyes. "You've got a fortnight of this and there's only so many of them in a person."

"How many."

"In my experience, about nine hundred."

"That's not very many."

"It's more than you'd think. I've been rationing since 2130." Senna shifted an inch, which was as far as she went these days. "Give me your hand, girl."

"I don't want my hand held."

"Nobody's holding it. I want something to squeeze that isn't the blanket, and your hand is closer than the blanket." A pause. "There. Now we're both being helped and neither of us has to say so."

Toren looked back once. Dessa's eyes were open and fixed on the canvas hoop's shadow going up and down the boards. Her jaw was set in a way he had seen on exactly one other face in his life. That face was under a pile of stones at the south mouth of a rock cut.

---

Rook stopped them at four miles, exactly, at a place where the ditch ran out into a wet meadow and there was a spur of gravel above the road.

He checked Dessa's leg. It took a long time. He did not do the thing with his hands that made Toren's ears pop, and Toren waited for it, and it did not come.

"Why aren't you —"

"Because I did it last night and there is a cost and it isn't mine." Rook folded the blanket back down. "She's got a bone that has to knit and a body that has to do the knitting. I can hold a door shut for an hour. I can't build the wall."

"You built one for my grandmother."

"I stopped one. There's a difference and you've heard me say it." He straightened. "Two hours here. Eat. Then we work."

Milo looked up from the water can. "Work?"

"Work."

"She's —"

"She's got a broken leg and five people who don't," Rook said. "Which is a thing she can be angry about later, and I recommend it."

---

Here is how the drilling changed.

It was not that there was more of it, although there was. It was that Rook stopped teaching them how to stand and started teaching them how to keep doing something while something else went wrong.

The first exercise of that afternoon was an old one. He set them a stretch of bad ground below the gravel spur — a slope of tussock and a wet runnel and about forty yards of rabbit-holed turf — and told them to cross it in the spread he had taught them at the sheep-fold, at speed, without bunching.

They did it. It was not bad.

"Again," Rook said. "Milo, this time you've got a turned ankle."

"I haven't."

"You have now. Sit down." Milo sat down. "Right. Three of you, same ground, same spread, and you're bringing him. Go."

It fell apart in nine seconds. Wyck went for Milo's shoulders and Toren went for the same shoulders from the other side and they got in each other's way, and by the time they had sorted it out they were standing in a knot of three people inside a yard of ground, which was the exact single thing they had spent two days at the sheep-fold learning not to be.

"That," Rook said. "Every time. The second something goes wrong you all run to the same square foot of the world and stand in it." He walked in among them the way he had before. "A person who is down is a job. A job has a shape. Somebody takes the weight, somebody takes the legs, and somebody stands off and looks at the rest of the field, and *the one who looks does not put their hands on anybody.* Again."

They did it eleven times. On the eighth one Toren was the one standing off, with his hands empty and his whole body screaming at him to go and help. He made himself hold still and watch the top of the slope instead, and hated every second of it.

"Better," Rook said.

Toren noticed it and put it away somewhere without knowing why.

---

They made nine miles the next day and eleven the day after. The country came up out of the low ground into long open sheep pasture with stone walls falling down across it. The drilling went on every evening, at the end of the walking, which was the part that made it drilling and not a game.

The exercises got more specific. That was the change Toren could actually describe afterward.

At the sheep-fold Rook had taught them a thing and then made them do the thing. Now he took a thing they already did and broke it into parts and made them do the parts wrongly on purpose, so they would find out which part was carrying the weight.

He made Dessa call from the cart with her eyes shut, from sound alone, while Milo moved rocks around at forty yards. She was better at it than anybody expected, including her, and said so, flatly, as a fact about the world.

He made Wyck hold a line against all three of the others at once, on a slope, with his boots on wet grass, and did not tell him how. Wyck worked it out in about four attempts and never explained what he had worked out.

He made Milo throw things.

That one turned into its own hour. Milo Faust could put a stone into a fence post at twenty yards and he could do it four times out of five with either hand, and none of them had ever once thought about it as anything but a thing Milo did.

"Where'd you learn that?" Rook said.

"Nowhere. Crows in the barley."

"How long?"

"Since I was about six?" Milo turned a stone over, embarrassed. "It's not — it doesn't *do* anything."

"It knocked one of those things' head round so it looked at you instead of at Wyck's back, six days ago, on a road, and it cost you nothing and it took a half second." Rook picked up a stone himself and handed it over. "Twenty-five yards. Post."

Milo hit the post.

"Thirty."

He hit the post.

"There's people who've trained nine years to be able to reliably put something where you just put it," Rook said. "So we'll stop calling it nothing."

Milo went the color of a beetroot and could not think of anywhere to look, and Toren, watching, thought: *that is two.* Two things in three days. He had counted them without deciding to.

---

The light happened on the fifth evening and it was Rook who went and got it, deliberately, the way a man goes and gets a splinter out.

They had finished. Milo was doing the water. Rook sat on a wall with his forearms on his knees and said, in the flattest possible voice, as though asking about the weather:

"What did you get at your Kindling?"

Milo stopped with the can half tipped.

"Nothing," he said.

"That's Dessa's answer and it's true for her." Rook did not look round. "It isn't yours. You've had a fortnight to say it and you've stood behind somebody every single time the subject came near you."

Nobody breathed. Wyck, sitting against the wall, turned his head very slightly.

Milo put the can down.

"It's not —" He stopped. Started again. "It's not a thing. It's not like his." He did not point at Toren. "It doesn't cut anything and you can't carry anything with it and my mother —"

He stopped again, at the word, and everybody let him have the second he needed.

"My mother said not to bother with it," Milo said. "She said it was more trouble than it was worth and I should keep it to myself, and she was the one who could do things, so I did what she said."

"Show me," said Rook.

Milo looked at his own hand for a while. Then he put it out, palm up, in the last of a gray evening on a stone wall in a country nobody had ever heard of, and made a light.

It came up off his palm about the size of an apple, and it was white with a blue edge to it, and it was cold. That was the wrong part. That was the part Toren's eye kept refusing. A light that size should have thrown heat and a color. This one lay in Milo's hand like something under ice. It made the bones in his fingers go dark inside the flesh. It did not flicker at all.

It lit the wall. It lit Milo's face, from below, poorly. Out past the wall it lit exactly nothing, because there was nothing out there for it to reach.

"Oh," said Dessa, from the cart.

"That's *beautiful,*" said Senna.

"It isn't," Milo said, and his voice cracked. "It *isn't.* It doesn't do anything. It doesn't warm anything and you can't cook on it and it goes out if I'm tired, and —" He shut his hand. The light went. "— and every single thing on that road can see it from a mile off. You *said.* You said if you put an edge out in a closed place everything inside a mile knows where you're standing. Well, it's the same. It's exactly the same and it's worse because it's *light.*"

He wiped his face with his wrist.

"I hate this," Milo said. "That's the whole of it. I've had it since I was thirteen and I hate it and I've never once been glad of it, and now everybody knows."

Nobody said anything.

Toren waited for Rook to tell him it was useful. He had a whole speech assembled and ready on Milo's behalf, about how it would be useful one day, and he was going to be furious when Rook said it, because it would be the sort of thing you say.

Rook did not say it.

"It's a liability," he said. "You're right. Out here, at night, that thing is a beacon and it will get somebody killed, and the fact that you worked that out for yourself at thirteen with nobody to tell you is the only reason I'm not annoyed about the fortnight." He stood up off the wall. "So we're going to treat it as what it is. It goes in the same box as the drag-chain and the water can. It's a tool you own, and it's got a cost, and the cost is *everything in the dark knows.* You don't use it because you're frightened and you don't use it because it's dark. You use it when the thing you're buying is worth what it costs, and you tell somebody before you do it."

Milo stared at him.

"That's it?"

"What were you expecting?"

"I don't know." Milo laughed, wetly, the way he did. "Something nicer."

"There isn't anything nicer," Rook said. "There's just what it does and what it costs. Water, please."

---

The Wyck thing happened on the sixth day and took about four seconds, and nobody but Toren would have called it anything at all.

They were crossing a beck with the cart. Rook was forty yards up the far bank looking at something. Toren had the near shaft and could see the stones under the water and Wyck could not, because Wyck was behind the tailgate with his back to the whole business, pushing.

"Wyck — there's a flat one about three feet on, if you can get the off wheel onto it —"

"He said the off side."

That was all of it. Wyck said it without heat, without turning round, in the voice he used for reading out a number, and he put the off wheel exactly where Toren had said to put it.

*He said the off side.* Meaning: the instruction already existed and it came from somewhere else. Meaning: you are not the one giving it. Meaning nothing at all, if you wanted it to mean nothing, and Toren spent the rest of the beck deciding to want that.

His knuckles had healed. Toren noticed it in the same four seconds, which was a strange thing to notice. The splits had closed over and gone shiny and slightly wrong. Wyck had stopped holding that hand any differently from the other one. At some point in the last week he had simply started using it again and had not mentioned it, because of course he hadn't.

---

Toren's own drill was the worst of all of them and it was three minutes long.

"Put it out."

He put the hook out. It came the way it came now, easily, without being asked twice, as long as he did not look at it.

"Walk to that wall and back and keep it."

He got eleven paces.

The warmth under his breastbone did not gutter this time. It did something subtler and more humiliating. It *thinned*, gradually, like a man's attention thinning in a warm room. Somewhere around the tenth pace the hook stopped being a weight on the end of his arm and started being a shape he was arguing with. On the eleventh it came apart into nothing.

"Again."

Nine paces.

"Again."

Fourteen, which felt like a victory until he tried for fifteen and got seven.

"That's enough," Rook said.

"I can —"

"You can't, and you'll be sick in an hour if you keep going, and then you'll be no use to anybody in the morning." Rook crouched and looked at the Ember on Toren's arm without touching it. "You can put it out. Most people take a season to put it out the way you put it out. You can't *keep* it out, which is a different problem with a different answer, and we'll get to it."

"When?"

"When you've stopped doing the wrong thing hard enough that doing the right thing has somewhere to go," Rook said, and that was all Toren got.

---

On the ninth night he started putting them on watch.

He did it without ceremony, at the fire, in the middle of talking about something else. Two-hour shifts, two people to a shift, one awake at the fire and one out at the edge of the light where their eyes worked.

"You're not fighting anything," he said. "If you see something, you don't shout. You come and put your hand on my shoulder and you say what you saw and where, and then you do exactly what I say for the next minute. That's the whole job. The hard part of it isn't the seeing. The hard part is that you'll be doing it every night for the rest of your lives and most nights nothing will happen, and the night something does happen will be a night you've stopped believing in."

"You've been doing it," Milo said. "Every night. On your own."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

Rook looked at the fire.

"Long enough that I'd rather not," he said.

---

She got out of the cart on the tenth day, which was four days early, and she did it while everybody was busy so that nobody could stop her.

Toren turned round and Dessa Cole was standing on the towpath side of the road with one hand on the cart rail and both feet on the ground, white to the lips, shaking from the hip down.

"Don't," she said, before anyone moved. "Don't come over here. If somebody comes over here I'll have to say something to them and I haven't got the air."

She let go of the rail.

She got four steps. They were terrible steps. The bad leg came through stiff and swung wide and landed flat, and she put her weight on it and something in her face went white around the edges, and she took the fifth one anyway.

Then she stopped, because she had to, and stood in the middle of an old sheep road breathing like a woman who has run a mile.

"Right," she said.

"Dessa —"

"I said don't." She got her chin up. "Ten days. I've been in a cart for ten days, next to a sick woman, being *carried,* and every one of you has come past that tailgate about forty times a day to look at me and check I'm still there. Milo brings me water I haven't asked for. Toren tells me things I already worked out from the noise." Her voice did not rise. That was the worst part. "And every night I lie in that bed and listen to the four of you learning things."

"That's not —"

"It's exactly what it is. It's not cruelty. Nobody's done anything wrong." She looked down at the leg as though it belonged to somebody who had let her down. "I said *there's two on the right.* I said it out loud, four seconds early, and I was right, and the correct thing happened to me because I did what I was told. And now I'm cargo."

Nobody had anything for that.

"I've got one thing," Dessa said. "One. I see things and I say them. That's my whole contribution to this group and I can do it lying on my back with my eyes shut, which I have, for ten days, and I am so tired of it that I could put my fist through that wall." She looked up. "And what I want to know — and nobody has to answer this, I'd just like it said out loud where everyone can hear it — is what happens to somebody like me in a year. When all of you can do things. When he's got a shape he can hold and Wyck can hold a road and Milo can put a rock through a man's eye at thirty yards." She let it sit. "What am I then? I'm the one who tells you where it is. You'll all be able to see it yourselves by then."

Senna said, from the cart, quietly: "Dessa."

"I'm finished," Dessa said. "I'd like to sit down now and I'd like somebody else to be the one who says something next."

Wyck got a crate under her before Toren had finished turning round.

---

Rook came to her that evening after the food, when the fire was down and Milo was doing the water and Toren was pretending to check a strap on the cart's near side, four feet away, where he could hear everything.

He did not sit down. He stood in front of her with his hands at his sides.

"You asked a question this afternoon," he said.

"I did."

"I'm not going to answer it."

"Fine."

"I'm going to do something else instead, and you can decide afterward whether it was an answer." Rook pushed his left sleeve back. "Tomorrow. First light, before anybody else is up. Down at the bottom of the pasture by the water, where the ground's flat."

Dessa looked at the sleeve.

Toren looked at it too, from four feet away, with his hand on a strap he had stopped pretending to check. He had seen that strap a hundred times in a fortnight. Old leather gone soft as cloth, oiled and re-oiled, three sets of holes punched one above another, and a fourth hole at the very end put through with an awl by somebody in a hurry. The man wore it on the tightest set and had for years.

Rook put two fingers under the buckle and undid it.

He did not take it off. He let it hang loose on his forearm, which was somehow worse, and the small dark oval underneath sat there in the firelight looking like nothing at all — narrow, neat, sized for an arm that was not his.

"Bring nothing," he said. "Don't eat first. Don't tell the others, not because it's a secret, but because the only thing worse than this not working is four people watching it not work."

Dessa's voice came out very level.

"What is it you think I'm going to do?"

"Nothing, probably," Rook said. "First light."

Then he did the buckle up again, on the tightest set, and walked off to the edge of the light and sat down facing north, and did not speak to anybody else for the rest of the night.
