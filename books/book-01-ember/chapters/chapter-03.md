# Chapter 3 — What It Cost

His father did the arithmetic out loud, because there was no time to do it privately and no point pretending it was anything but arithmetic.

"Gully's thirty yards south. Twenty coming down it. Cart's finished — don't look at the cart, nobody look at the cart, it's finished. Mother can't walk. Marta can't move and hold at the same time." Garrick's blade was out and he did not appear to know it. "So. Wyck. Get on that tailgate and keep the drain side of this cart clear for two minutes. Not three. Two."

"Yes."

"Milo, Dessa — side-board off, blanket on it, and get my mother onto it. Toren, with me."

"With you where?"

"Up the gully," Garrick said. "We can't go under them. We're going to go *through* them, uphill, and here's why: a Husk on a slope can't brace. Tip one at the top and it takes two with it on the way down and does the work for you." He was already moving. "It's the only good news in this entire cut. Come on."

"Dad, I can't —" Toren held up his arm, and it was the most useless gesture of his life. The Ember sat dark and shut on his forearm. Under his breastbone there was nothing at all, no warmth, no coin, not even the memory of one. "It's gone. It's all gone, I can't —"

His father did not slow down.

"Then bring a rock," Garrick said.

Toren went after him. Half the arithmetic was still sitting in his head where it had landed, and the wrong half of it came out of his mouth.

"Put a stride on."

"No."

"You could have her at the top in four seconds. You could have *all* of us up in four seconds, one at a time, you'd just —"

"And then what?" Garrick did not turn around. "A stride is legs, Toren. It is not hands. I'd be standing at the top of that fan with my mother in my arms and *nothing in my hands,* and Wyck and Milo and Dessa would be at the bottom of it with nothing in theirs, and I'd have to come back down four times through a slope full of those things with no way to cut a single one of them off me." He stepped over a Husk that was still coming apart. "One shape. One job. You pick the job that keeps the most of you standing and then you don't think about the other one again. Not once. Not while it's happening."

"That's —"

"That's the whole trade," his father said. "Everybody makes it. Everybody's always made it. Rock."

---

Wyck Tanner got up on the tailgate of a broken cart with an iron bar in his hands and held the drain side for two minutes.

Toren saw perhaps fifteen seconds of it. Wyck had no Ember. Whatever his own Kindling had given him, it was not a thing he could hold in his hand, and he had never once discussed it with anybody. He had a bar, a pair of boots, and a stance that was not a stance so much as a decision. The first Husk out of the drain hit him full on, and Wyck did not move.

That was the thing. He did not brace and give ground the way a man braces. He did not go back a step and then come forward. The Husk came into him at a run with all its weight, and Wyck Tanner stood exactly where he had been standing. The thing bounced off him. He brought the bar down on it.

The second one did the same. So did the third. On the third one he came off the bar and hit it with his fist, which was a mistake, and he split three knuckles open on something inside it that had no business being that hard. He did not seem to notice. He shifted the bar to that hand anyway and kept the drain side clear for two minutes, exactly as asked. When Dessa shouted that Senna was off the cart, he came down off the tailgate backward without ever once turning around.

---

They got her onto the side-board with a blanket under her, and she helped, which was worse than if she hadn't.

"Don't fuss," Senna said. Her voice had gone to almost nothing. "Milo. Milo, look at me. Take the front. You're the steadiest."

"I'm not, though."

"You are, and I've watched all of you for sixty years, and I'm not going to be argued with on a road." She got her arm across her chest. "Go."

Marta stood twelve feet north of them with her hands out and her palms turned down, and she had not moved her feet in six minutes.

That was the part Toren had not understood until that morning, and he understood it now the way you understand a stair that isn't there. The wall was not a thing Marta carried. It was a place she made. She could make it in front of her. She could hold it, and she could make it smaller. She could not take one single step in any direction without it coming down.

"Marta," Garrick said. "We're going. Thirty yards."

"I know, love."

"Can you shorten it and walk it?"

"No."

Nobody said anything. The ash drifted down off the north end of the cut, soft, like a mill in winter.

"You'll go," Marta said, "and you'll get to the bottom of that gully, and when you're at the bottom you'll shout, and I'll come." She had not turned her head once. "That's about twenty seconds of road I'm going to have to walk with nothing in front of me. I'd rather walk twenty than forty."

"Marta —"

"Garrick Voss, I have been mending this family since before your son could stand up, and you are not going to stand there and argue geometry with me." Her arms were shaking. Her voice was not. "Milo."

"Mum —"

"Eyes forward, love."

Milo made a sound. Then he picked up the front of the board, and Dessa picked up the back, and they went.

---

Thirty yards.

Toren went in front with a fist-sized piece of limestone in his right hand because his father had told him to bring a rock and there had not been time to decide whether that was a joke. It was not a joke. It worked about as well as anything works. A Husk went down under Garrick's blade and half rose. Toren put the rock into it. The thing came apart around the rock and left him holding the rock.

The base of the gully was a fan of loose shale that had washed down off the old farm track over seventy years, twelve feet wide at the bottom and narrowing as it rose. It was a bad climb. It was the only climb.

Garrick went up the first six feet at an angle, cutting across the slope rather than straight up it, which put the Husks above him on his blade side and made them come down at him one at a time on ground where none of them could set their feet.

It worked exactly as he had said it would. The first one he took at the knee. It went down and slid past him on its back, and it took the second one off its feet on the way. Both of them ended up in a rolling gray mess at the bottom of the fan with Wyck standing over the pile.

"*Now,*" Garrick shouted down. "Board up! Go, go, go —"

Milo and Dessa started up the shale with Senna between them. Toren got underneath the middle of the board with both hands and his shoulder. Wyck came behind and put his back into the whole business from below.

They made nine feet.

At nine feet the shale gave under Dessa's boot and the back of the board dropped. Senna slid four inches and caught herself on the rail. A Husk came down off the step above them and got an arm into the litter.

Toren was under the board. His hands were full of the board. There was nothing under his breastbone and nothing on his arm and no rock in his hand anymore.

He hit it with his head.

It was the stupidest thing he ever did and it worked, in the sense that the thing came off the litter. It also raked its other hand down as it went, across the blanket, across his grandmother's side, and Senna Voss made a short flat sound that was not a scream and was much worse than one.

"*Grandmother —*"

"Keep — going —"

They kept going.

---

They got to the top of the fan, where the shale met the old farm track and the ground went solid. Fourteen feet up. From there the bank stepped back in two more shelves to the rim. The rim had sky on it, and the sky had nothing in it at all.

Garrick stood at the bottom of the gully with his back to them and his blade out.

"*We're up!*" Toren shouted. "*Marta! We're up!*"

Down on the road, sixty feet away and directly below, Marta Faust dropped her hands.

Toren saw the wall go. It was not a sound and it was not a light. The gray air north of her simply stopped being wrong and started being ordinary air again, and every Husk that had been standing against nothing for six minutes came forward all at once.

She ran. She was fifty-one years old and she had been holding a wall up with her hands for six minutes. She ran like a woman who had been asked to and had said yes.

She made eighteen of her twenty yards.

The one that got her did not come from the north. It came out of the drain mouth, the square concrete hole in the west wall, four feet from her shoulder as she passed it, and there was no warning and no way to have known and nothing anybody standing fourteen feet above her could have done about it.

Milo went off the top of the fan.

Wyck caught him around the chest before his second step and took him down onto the shale. Milo fought him — screamed, kicked, got an elbow into Wyck's mouth. Wyck Tanner held on, and did not let him go, and did not say a single word the entire time.

Toren went down.

He did not decide to. He went down the fourteen feet of shale in a controlled fall, hit the road, and got to her. His father's blade came across in front of him and took the thing off her. Toren got an arm under Marta's shoulders.

Her eyes found him. Not Milo. Him.

"Is he up?" she said.

"He's up. He's up, Marta, he's at the top, he's —"

"Good." Her hand came up about four inches and stopped. "Tell him eyes forward."

Then she was not there anymore, which is a stupid way to put it, and Toren never afterward found a better one. She was a woman who had set his wrist when he was six. She had a braid with gray in it. Her hands lay open on the road. And she was simply, completely, not there.

"*Toren.*"

He did not move.

"*Toren.*" His father's hand closed on the back of his coat and lifted him bodily off the ground. "Up the bank. Now."

"We can't leave —"

"*Up the bank.*"

---

Here is how a man runs out.

Toren had watched his father use the Ember his whole life and had never once thought about where it came from, because it came from his father, and his father did not run out of things.

At the bottom of the gully, Garrick Voss had twin knives, short and wide, one off each fist. He was working close, which was what the knives were for, and the fan of shale funneled them down to him two and three at a time.

Then he had one knife and one empty hand.

Then he had the blade — the long plain single-edged one, the one Toren had grown up watching — because a blade is one shape and one shape is cheaper than two. He backed up four feet onto the shale and kept it in front of him and it was still enough. It was still enough. Toren, climbing, looking back over his shoulder every third step, told himself it was still enough.

Then the blade got shorter.

That was the thing nobody warned him about. It did not go out. It *shortened,* an inch at a time, over about forty seconds, like a candle doing the only thing a candle does, and his father kept fighting with it and kept not mentioning it.

"*Dad!*"

"Go *up!*"

"*Come up with me!*"

Toren was eight feet from the solid ground at the top of the fan. His father was twelve feet below him with a blade the length of a bread knife and eleven Husks on the shale.

Garrick got a hand on Toren's belt and threw him.

There is no other word. His father put one hand in his belt and one under his thigh and put him up the slope the way you put a sack of grain onto a cart, and Toren landed on his chest on the old farm track with the breath gone out of him and shale in his mouth.

He rolled over.

"I'm right behind you," Garrick said.

Then the blade went out.

---

It did not come back.

His father stood at the bottom of a gully in a cut in a ridge on a road in the middle of nowhere with his hands empty, and looked up, and did the last thing he was going to do, which was to spread his arms and step sideways across the mouth of the fan so that nothing could get past him and up.

Toren screamed. He had no memory of it afterward. Dessa told him, much later, that it went on for some time.

He got to the edge and Wyck's arms went around him from behind. Wyck weighed a stone and a half more than he did and had a grip like a gate hinge. Toren fought him. It made no difference at all.

That was when the man came off the wall.

---

He came along the top of the west bank, opposite, eighty feet above the road — a dark shape moving flat out along the rim, and moving *wrongly,* covering ground the way a thrown thing covers ground rather than the way a running man does.

He did not stop at the edge.

He went off it.

Toren watched a person fall eighty feet into a rock cut and understood that he was about to see somebody die for the third time in ten minutes, and his mind, which had stopped being any use, supplied the thought that at least this one would be quick.

The man hit the road.

He did not break. He came down through it. It was a hard, deep, absorbed landing that put him onto one knee with one hand flat on the pavement. The impact banged off both walls. Gray ash jumped a foot into the air in a ring all around him.

Toren saw why.

There was dark metal down the outsides of both his legs. It ran in struts from hip to ankle, and up the back of him in a line either side of the spine, thin as ribbing, and it was the same dull slate color as the pebble on Toren's own forearm.

*That's a stride,* Toren thought. *That's a stride, that's all, that's just —*

The man came up out of the landing and stopped, once, for about half a second.

Toren, from fourteen feet above, saw exactly what he was looking at, and understood the shape of the problem in the same moment the man did.

There were two of them. Not two Husks — two problems. Twenty-odd stood on the road, coming south off the drain and down from the north end, and they were between the man and the way he had presumably meant to go. Eleven were on the fan of shale. The fan of shale went up to an old farm track, and on the old farm track there were four children and a bleeding woman on a board, with nobody left standing over them.

Any sensible man clears his own road first. That is what a road is for.

The man turned his back on twenty-odd of them and went at the fan.

He crossed twenty feet of open road in what Toren's eye insisted was one movement. The struts were still down his legs. A blade stood off the back of his right hand.

Toren's whole mind stopped.

Because that was not a thing. That was not a thing that was. *You hold one. You put it down and you pick up the other, and the putting-down takes about as long as the picking-up.* His father had told him that thirty-six hours ago, standing by a fire with a tin box in his hands, and had said it the way you say a thing that has been true since before anyone kept a record.

The man went up into the bottom of the gully with both of them at once.

---

It took eleven seconds.

Toren counted them, because some part of him was still reporting for duty, and he got eleven and he was not sure about the last two.

The man did not fight the way Garrick had fought. There was nothing in it to watch.

Garrick had held ground. He had planted himself at a narrow place and made the world come to him. It had worked for a long time. At the end of it he had run out of the thing that made it work.

The man held nothing. He went up the shale on the same angle Garrick had cut across it, and he did not stop moving once. The blade went out three times in the first second and a half. Then he stood four feet further up the slope, and it went out three more times.

The Husks came down at him off the shelf. He was never standing where they came down. Twice he simply let the slope have them. A shoulder into a knee, a half-turn, and the thing went past him and rolled, taking the two below it with it on the way.

He never made a single movement Toren would have called fast. He simply arrived early, everywhere, one place after another, as though the whole thing had been written down in advance and he was the only one who had read it.

The fan went gray. The gray settled.

Six feet below Dessa's boots he stopped and looked up at the four of them. He did not say anything. He was breathing through his mouth.

Then he turned around and went back down to deal with the road.

---

There were perhaps twenty-five left in the cut, spread across the whole width of it, and they came at him, because there was nothing else in them to do.

He walked out into the middle of the road and let the blade go out.

*No,* Toren thought, with a lurch that had no sense in it. *No. Don't put it down.*

The man put his hands out at waist height with the palms turned down.

Toren, lying on his stomach on an old farm track fourteen feet up, with his father below him and his own throat raw, said out loud, to nobody: "That's *Marta's.*"

The light across the cut went wrong.

It went wrong all the way across, wall to wall, twenty feet north of the drain mouth. The sound behind it dropped by half. Twenty-five Husks walked into the side of a house that was not there. The first three went into it at a run and stopped dead. The ones behind them piled into those.

And the struts stayed down his legs the whole time.

That was the part Toren could not stop looking at. Whatever the man was doing with his hands, he was doing something else entirely with his legs in the same instant, and the two things had nothing whatever to do with each other. He carried them the way a person carries a bucket in each hand.

He held the wall for about twenty seconds. Then he let it shrink.

Toren watched the wrongness pull in from both walls until it was eight feet across, with the whole rest of the road standing open on either side of it, and the Husks came flooding around both ends of it in two streams. It looked like a catastrophic mistake.

It took Toren three full seconds to understand that the man had built himself a gap — the same gap Garrick had paid eleven kills for at the slab, made out of nothing, in the middle of an open road, at a spot of his own choosing — and had walked into the mouth of it.

The blade came back off his hand.

Then it was two at a time, into a narrow place, against a man who did not run out.

Toren watched him and felt something turn over at the bottom of his chest that he did not have a name for yet, and which was not gratitude.

---

When the wall came down there was nothing left standing in the cut.

The man stood in the middle of the road with his hands on his knees. The struts went out of his legs. The blade went out of his hand. Toren saw him put one hand flat against the west wall for a second, the way a man does when he needs a wall.

Then he straightened up, and looked at the cart in the drainage channel, and at the mule, and at Marta Faust lying eighteen yards from where she had made her stand, and finally at the bottom of the gully.

He walked over and stood there for a moment.

Toren was already coming down. He went down the shale on his heels and his hands and he did not remember getting there.

There was nothing to be done. There had been nothing to be done from the moment the blade went out. There would have been nothing to be done if the man had arrived forty seconds earlier. Toren would spend two months refusing to believe either of those facts.

He knelt on the shale with his hand on his father's shoulder and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say and no air to say it with.

Above him, the man waited.

He did not put a hand on Toren's back. He did not crouch. He did not say that he was sorry, or that it had been fast, or any of the other things people say. He stood six feet off with ash on his coat and let it go on for as long as it went on.

At some point in the middle of it, Toren's eyes, which had stopped taking instructions, fixed on the man's left forearm, and on the strap around it, and stayed there.

It was old leather, soft as cloth, oiled and re-oiled. It had three separate sets of holes punched in it, one group above another, the way a strap gets when a thing outgrows it. The man was using the tightest set. He had taken it in as far as it went and then gone further, and somebody had put a fourth hole through at the very end with an awl and done it poorly.

The Ember under the strap was small. It was smaller than Toren's own, which had been sized for a man's wrist five generations back, and it sat narrow and neat against the inside of a grown man's arm like a coin in a purse made for a smaller coin.

Toren looked at it for a while without one single thought in his head about it. He was thirteen and his father was dead on the shale in front of him. He noticed it the way you notice the pattern on a plate at a funeral. It went down into him somewhere, and stayed, and did not come back up for a long time.

Then, when Toren finally looked up, the man's eyes went past him and up the gully, and Toren turned and looked too, and saw what he was seeing.

Four faces at the top of the fan. Milo with his mouth open and nothing coming out. Dessa with her hands on Milo's shoulders. Wyck standing a little apart, with his knuckles wrecked, looking down at all of it without any expression whatsoever.

And a board with a blanket on it, and an old woman on the board, bleeding through a blanket.

The man's face did something then. It was small, and it was fast. Toren, at thirteen, with his father's body under his hand, had no idea what it meant. It looked like a man reading the end of a letter he had already guessed the contents of.

"Who's hurt?" the man said.

It was the first thing he had said. His voice was flat and low and had no edges on it at all.

Nobody answered. Dessa got there first, the way she got to most things.

"Her side. It's bad. His hand." She pointed at Wyck. "Nothing on Milo. Nothing on me. I don't know about Toren."

"Nothing on me," Toren said.

"Is there another adult?"

Toren looked at him.

"Is there another adult," the man said again, without impatience, the way a man reads a list. "Anybody else on this road. Anybody at the next settlement who's expecting you. An uncle. A registered guardian. Anyone."

"No," Dessa said.

The man stood very still.

"No," he said.

He did not say it to her. He said it to the air in front of him, quietly, with his jaw set, the way a man says a word he has been holding in his mouth for a while and has finally run out of reasons not to spend.

Then he took his pack off, and dropped it in the ash, and went up the shale past Toren toward the old woman on the board.

"Somebody boil water," he said. "There won't be any. Find some anyway."

---

Dessa found some. There was a two-gallon can lashed under the cart's bed and it had survived, because things that do not matter always survive.

By the time Toren got back up the fan with it, the man had the blanket folded back off Senna's side and was looking at what was underneath. He looked for about four seconds. Then he put the blanket back, which told Toren more than four minutes of looking would have.

"What's your name?" the man said to her.

"Senna Voss."

"How long have you been ill, Senna Voss? Not this. The other thing."

She got her eyes open. "Twenty years."

"Reactor?"

"You've met one of me before."

"I've met a few." He opened the pack one-handed without looking at it, the way a man opens a drawer in his own kitchen, and took out a roll of cloth and a flat tin. "This is going to be unpleasant and then it's going to be worse and then it's going to stop. Somebody hold her hand. Not the boy."

Dessa knelt down and took Senna's hand.

The man set his palms against Senna's side, over the cloth, and did nothing whatsoever for eleven seconds.

Then Toren's ears popped.

That was all. No light. No sound. The gray afternoon over the cut did not change in any particular. Nobody cried out. The man did not move his hands or his shoulders or any part of himself at all.

Toren's ears popped the way they did on the hill road above Sallow Creek. The small dead nothing under his own breastbone twitched once, like a limb in sleep. And Senna Voss stopped making the sound she had been making since nine feet up the shale.

Her breathing changed. It went from something high and fast and ragged down to something slow. Her face came back from wherever it had been.

"Oh," she said.

"Don't get comfortable." The man took his hands away. He was gray around the mouth. "I've stopped it. I haven't fixed it and I'm not going to be able to. That's a four-week wound on a body that doesn't have four weeks' worth of anything to spend, and if it goes bad we are not going to catch it in time out here."

"Who are you?" Milo said.

The man did not answer him. He was already up, already looking north up the road, already working something out behind his eyes.

"There's ground at the south mouth that isn't rock," he said. "You'll want it before dark. Six hours of light. It's going to take you most of them."

Dessa understood first. She always did.

"You want us to bury them," she said.

"I want you to be *finished* burying them," the man said, "before the next lot comes down that ridge to see what all the noise was about."

Then he went down the fan, past Toren, past his father, out into the middle of the road, and stood there with his back to all five of them and did not move for a long time.

He was not resting. Toren, sitting in the ash at the top of the shale with his arms around his knees, understood that much even at thirteen.

He was trying to think of somewhere else to be.
