# Chapter 10 — The Wrong Second

Wyck asked for it at breakfast, in front of everybody, in the voice he used for reading out a number.

"Yesterday you said not this week."

Rook did not look up from the flask he was capping. "I did."

"I'd like it to be this week."

The fire made its small noises. Milo looked at the ground. Dessa, sitting on a coping stone with her leg straight out and a bowl on her knee, went on eating and did not miss a beat, which was her way of being loudly present at something.

"Why?" Rook said.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters more than anything else about it."

Wyck set his bowl down.

"Because she got it in a field with nobody watching and I've had to hear about it for two days," he said. "Because everybody in this group can do one thing except me, and now she can do a thing she can't even do twice, and I still can't do one." He was not shouting. He never shouted. "And because you strapped that off your own arm onto somebody who'd been told her whole life she was nothing, and it worked, which means it isn't about what you've got. So I'd like my go."

"That's three reasons and the first two are bad ones."

"You didn't ask for good ones."

Rook looked at him for a moment.

"No," he said. "I didn't."

He put the flask down and pushed his sleeve back.

---

It went wrong in a way Toren did not have any equipment for watching.

Rook set it up exactly as he had at the beck — the strap two holes in, the Ember against the inside of the left forearm, Wyck sitting on the cinder with his back against the lock wall. He gave the same four things in the same order and in the same flat voice, except for the second one, where he stopped.

"I told her this couldn't hurt her," he said. "I can't tell you that."

"Why not?"

"Because she's got nothing in her to spend and I don't think that's true of you." Rook sat back on his heels. "If something answers, it's going to answer from wherever you actually keep it, and I don't know where that is, and neither do you. So if I say stop, you stop inside the same second. Not at the end of the try. Inside the second."

"All right."

"Empty head. Nothing to ask for and nobody to ask. And then don't hold anything shut."

Wyck shut his eyes.

He was better at it than Toren expected, and worse in the way that mattered. Dessa had spent nine minutes trying and had been visibly, obviously wrestling with something. Wyck did not wrestle. He simply sat there against the stone, absolutely still, with his hands open on his knees and his face doing nothing at all, and Toren realized after about thirty seconds that he was watching a person who had been not-letting-anything-out for so long that he had no idea which part of himself to stop doing it with.

At a minute and a half, Rook said, quietly, "Stop trying to be good at it."

Wyck's shoulders dropped.

And something came.

It was not like Dessa's. There was no thinning of the dark and no three inches of metal running over the back of anybody's hand. What happened was that the seam on the Ember opened about a finger's width, exactly the way Toren's own had opened and closed in a rock cut on the worst morning of his life, and it made a sound — a small hard tick, like a stone cooling.

Wyck's whole body went rigid off the wall.

"*Stop,*" Rook said.

He had his hand on the strap before the word was out. The buckle came undone and the thing came off Wyck's arm and Rook had it in his fist and was already reaching for the back of Wyck's neck with the other hand.

"Head down. All the way down. Breathe out first, not in."

Wyck went over onto his hands and was sick on the cinder.

It went on for a while. Milo got the water. Rook sat with one hand flat between Wyck's shoulder blades and did not say anything for about four minutes, and Toren watched a boy who had held a tailgate for two minutes against things that did not stop, shaking like a dog in a river.

"That's the floor coming up at you," Rook said eventually. "It'll pass. Don't stand up yet."

"What was that?"

"Something answered."

Wyck lifted his head. His face had gone the color of the silt in the dry pound.

"Something?"

"Not the stone." Rook was buckling the strap back onto his own arm, on the tightest set, and he did it without looking. "The stone did nothing at all. It opened a finger's width because something else in the room shouted at it. That's what that tick was."

"Then what —"

"I don't know yet," Rook said.

Which was, Toren thought afterward, sitting in the ash and the wreckage of the whole day, the single most expensive sentence anybody said that morning.

"Right." Wyck spat, and wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist, and stayed on his hands and knees because his arms had not finished. "So it didn't work."

"It answered from the wrong place and it answered loudly enough to put you on the floor. Nine people in ten get nothing at all and stay upright."

"So it didn't work."

Rook stood up.

"It didn't work," he agreed.

---

The lesson happened at nine in the morning with the sun on the stone and lasted twenty minutes. Every one of them was standing in it.

Rook squatted on the towpath and drew in the cinder dust with a stick. A long line for the canal. A second line beside it for the path. A mark for the flight and a mark for the arch.

"Hunters call them stilts," he said. "That's as good a name as any and it tells you the important thing, which is that the creature is mostly legs and the legs are the whole of it.

"Three rules and then I'm going to tell you why the three rules are going to get somebody hurt anyway.

"One. It only goes in straight lines. Not mostly. Not usually. Straight, flat, and level, and it will not deviate by a hand's width once it has gone, and it cannot go up over a thing or down under a thing while it's moving. That's what it is. It's a line with legs.

"Two." He put the stick down flat. "There is nothing in it that has to gather. Stopped and going flat out are the same state for that creature and there is no instant in between them. You will not see it start. Nobody has ever seen one start. You will see it standing still and then you will see it a hundred yards away, and if you are between those two places you are dead and you were dead before it moved.

"Three. And this one is the whole of your business." He tapped the dust. "It has to stop to choose. It cannot pick a new line while it's on the old one. So it runs a line, and it stops, dead, in the open, wherever the line ran out — and it stands there and it chooses. That takes about four seconds. Sometimes three. I've counted six once.

"And while it is standing there choosing, it cannot touch you. Not you, not anything. It is the most harmless object in this valley for four seconds."

He looked up at the four of them.

"Say what that means."

Dessa said it first, the way she said most things first.

"The stop is the window."

"The stop is the window. The stop is the *only* window and it is the whole of what you get." Rook stood up. "So when it stops, you do not hold, you do not wait it out, and you do not get small. You close, or you get off its next line, or you put something in the way that it will have to stop for again. Four seconds is enough to cross forty feet. It is not enough to think about crossing forty feet."

Milo put his hand up, which nobody had told him to stop doing.

"How do you know where the next line's going to be?"

"You don't, and that's why the answer is nearly always to be somewhere a straight line can't reach." Rook pointed with the stick down the flight. "Look at the ground. There is a mile and a half of towpath behind us with nothing on it, and that is a road built for that animal and nothing else. There's the far coping, same thing. There's the dry pound, which is a trench twelve feet across and five deep with slabs in the bottom, and a thing that travels level cannot come down into it and back out again without stopping twice. And there is that arch."

They all looked at the bridge hole. Seven feet of stone, with the path going through it and a dip of standing water at the bottom and rope-grooves worn a hand deep at the corner.

"That creature stands about nine feet at the body," Rook said. "It cannot go through there moving. It has to stop and it has to get low, and getting low is not something it is built to do quickly. If I could pick one piece of ground in this county to meet one of those on, it would be that hole, and I want all four of you to have that in your heads before anything else happens today."

He let it sit.

"Now here's the part that matters."

He came back along the line of them and stopped.

"Eight days ago I stood on a road in the dark and taught you that a thing going still is the wind-up. I taught it to you with Dessa's leg, which is the most expensive way there is to teach anybody anything, and I did it on purpose, and it worked. It is *true.* It is true of verge-hounds and it will be true of most pack animals you meet between here and the sea, and it will keep you alive a hundred times.

"It is not true of this. The tell is the same and the clock runs backwards."

Nobody said anything.

"You all know that now," Rook said. "You've known it for about four minutes. And knowing it is free. It cost you nothing to be told and it will buy you nothing at all, because the lesson that comes out of a person under pressure is not the one they heard most recently. It's the one that cost the most." He picked up his pack. "So somebody is going to get this wrong today. I have told you everything I know, in daylight, standing still, with a stick, and somebody is still going to get it wrong, and I want you to have heard me say so beforehand."

"Then why are we doing it?" said Milo.

"Because the alternative is doing it in four months on a road with no one watching," Rook said. "Get your boots on."

---

He put them on the flight at the top of the dry pound, and he went south, over the bridge, and stood on the parapet of the road where he could see the whole of it.

"I'm not coming down," he said, before anybody asked. "If I judge that one of you is about to be killed I will come, and it will take me about twenty seconds to get off this bridge and round the wing wall, and you should assume the twenty seconds and not the coming."

Dessa went down the ladder into the tail of the empty lock.

It was her own idea and she said it flatly, the way she said everything, standing on the coping looking at her own leg as though it were a tool she had been issued. "I can't run. That's not an opinion, it's a fact about today. So put me in a hole with a sightline and I'll be worth something, and if you leave me on this path I'll be worth less than nothing because two of you will spend the whole time looking at me."

So she went down the ladder, poorly, with Wyck taking her weight on the top three rungs, and stood in the stone box of the lock tail with the black gates at her back and her eyes just above the coping, looking north up a mile and a half of straight line.

Toren and Wyck took the towpath at the middle of the pound. Milo went south toward the arch with a pocket full of stones.

It took an hour and ten minutes.

Toren spent most of it discovering that waiting was worse than anything that had ever happened to him. The sun went behind a high gray sheet and the light went flat. The reeds in the wet pound north of them moved and stopped and moved. A heron came down the canal at head height and Milo said a word that Marta Faust would not have liked, and everybody's heart went off like a dropped pan, and then it was quiet again for another twenty minutes.

"North bank," said Dessa.

She said it quietly and without any hurry and it went through Toren like cold water.

"Far coping. Four hundred out, coming south. It's on the line."

Toren turned.

It was exactly the same as the evening before and it was completely different, because this time there was light on it.

The legs were gray — the same wet-ash gray as every single thing that had ever come through — but they were not the same *material* as a Husk. Down the front of each one ran a line of something harder and slicker, and it caught the flat light the way the dull slate oval on Toren's own forearm caught it. The sight of that went into him somewhere underneath his thinking and stayed there. He did not have time to look at it.

It came down the far coping in a blur that did not rise or fall one inch. The stone lip up there was four inches wide. It did not matter to the thing at all.

It stopped.

Two hundred yards. Dead opposite them, across twelve feet of dry canal bed and five feet of drop, standing on four inches of stone with nine feet of nothing underneath it, absolutely still.

"Stopped," said Dessa.

And every single thing Toren had learned in eighteen days came up out of him at once and in the wrong order.

*When one of those things stops moving, that is the single most dangerous second of your evening.*

He heard it in Rook's voice. He heard it in the dark, on a road, four miles north of a wall, with a girl making a sound on the stones behind him.

"It's winding up," Toren said. "Off the line — back down the path, get behind me, *hold —*"

They moved. They moved fast and well, because they had drilled it, and every yard of it was wrong.

Wyck was the last off the mark, because Wyck was always the last off the mark, because Wyck's entire body had one instruction in it and that instruction was *stay*. He came south along the towpath three steps behind Toren with the iron bar in his hand.

They were nine feet apart on four feet of cinder, in the middle of a mile and a half of dead straight line, when Dessa started shouting.

"*It's not there anymore —*"

---

It came down the towpath.

Toren never worked out where it had crossed. Afterward he decided it must have gone north to the head of the pound, where the coping ran round the stone bar at the top gate and the two banks became one level thing, and taken that as its line, and then taken the towpath as the next.

He never saw it start. Nobody ever has.

What he saw was the middle distance changing. There was a hundred yards of empty path behind Wyck's shoulder and then there was not, and there was no moment in between, and the noise it made was not a roar or a scream or anything a mouth does. It was a fast dry clatter, like a hail of slate going across a floor, and it arrived at the same time as the thing did.

Wyck turned into it.

He did not dive and he did not go flat. Toren was eleven feet away with his own back half turned and he saw the whole of it, and what he saw was Wyck Tanner set his feet on a cinder path and face a thing coming at him at the speed of a thrown stone, because that was the only thing in him and he did not have another one.

It did not stop for him. Not that time.

The body went past above his head. The legs went through the space he was standing in like a gate closing, and there was a crack that Toren felt in the soles of his own boots, and Wyck came off the towpath sideways.

He went over the coping into the dry pound and dropped five feet and hit the slabs.

The thing ran on. It ran the whole remaining length of the path at that same flat impossible speed, straight at the arch, and at the arch it stopped — dead, from full speed, at the mouth of the hole, with the stone in front of it and the water at its feet.

Milo was standing forty feet from it with a rock in his hand.

---

"*Wyck!*"

"I'm —"

He was not. Toren went over the coping after him and landed poorly and came up on his knees on the cracked silt.

Wyck was on his side against the base of the stone wall with his hand at his ribs. There was blood coming through his fingers and more of it coming through his shirt lower down, in a long line that started at the hip and went round, and his face had gone the color of paper and he was still trying to get his feet under him.

"Don't —"

"Get off me. Where is it."

"*Don't get up —*"

"*Where is it, Toren.*"

"Arch!" Dessa's voice came from forty yards south and above them, flat and carrying, doing the thing she did. "It's at the arch and it's stopped. It's stopped, it's *choosing* — four. Three."

And Toren Voss, kneeling on a slab of dried mud in a canal that had been dug a hundred and sixty years before anybody in it was born, understood three things in the space of one second.

He understood that four seconds was not very long.

He understood that Milo was forty feet from that arch on an open path with a rock in his hand.

And he understood, with a clarity that made his ears ring, that the only thing in the entire valley that could make a creature which travels in straight lines stop somewhere *they* chose was a thing standing in the line that would not move out of it.

"Dessa!" he shouted. "*Count it out loud — every time — keep counting!*"

"Two. One. *Gone —*"

---

It took the towpath north again, back up the line it had come down, because that was the only line it had.

It went past forty feet over Toren's head and he felt the air of it. Then it was at the top of the pound and it stopped.

"Stopped!" Dessa shouted. "Top bar, four hundred — *four, three* —"

"*MILO!*" Toren was up on the coping with his hands scrabbling on the stone. "Milo, get in the arch! In the hole, in the middle, and stand there!"

"*What?*"

"*In the hole!*" He got a knee up onto the towpath. "And do the light!"

There was a pause of about a second, in a dry canal, under a flat gray sky, and Toren afterward thought it was probably the longest second of Milo Faust's life.

Then Milo shouted, at the top of his voice, to nobody in particular:

"*I'M DOING THE LIGHT!*"

And the inside of the bridge hole went white.

---

It was the first time Toren had seen it anywhere but a fire's edge and it was the first time he understood what it was.

Under the arch it was dark, properly dark, a stone tunnel seven feet wide with wet at the bottom. Milo stood in the middle of it with his hand up and his palm open and a cold white light the size of an apple sitting in it. The whole hole lit up from inside like a lamp in a window. The rope-grooves and the dripping stone and the small round shape of a fourteen-year-old boy stood in the middle of it all, as clear as a picture on a wall.

From the north, up a mile and a half of dead straight line, it would have been the only lit thing in the world.

"It's coming," said Dessa. "It's coming, it's on the path, it's —"

Toren got to Wyck.

He had got himself onto his feet down in the pound. He had his left arm across his ribs and his right hand on the iron bar. He had walked six paces to the place where the pound's stonework had fallen in. Now he was three feet up a slope of collapsed rubble with his boots sliding under him, bleeding into the silt with every step.

"Wyck —"

"Pull."

Toren pulled.

They got him over the coping onto the towpath about eleven feet north of the arch mouth, and Wyck Tanner turned round to face up the line, and set his feet apart, and stood.

"Go left," he said.

"Wyck —"

"*Left.* On the water side. Get flat." He shifted the bar. His breath came in a thin whistle that Toren did not like at all. "It has to come down this path. There's nowhere else it can be."

"It'll go through you."

"It went through me the first time because I was side-on," Wyck said.

Toren went left and got flat against the coping on the water side with his arm out.

---

*Find the mass.*

There was no mass. There was one thing, and it was coming down a straight line at a boy holding a light, and there was a second boy standing in the middle of that line with three broken ribs and an iron bar.

Toren lay on cinder with his cheek two inches off the ground and put his arm out where the trouble was going to be, and did not reach for the warmth, and did not look at it, and did not check.

It came.

The clatter arrived first and then the thing arrived on top of its own noise, and the light in the arch made the whole business worse, because now he could see it properly. Six legs. He got that much. Six legs and a body the size of a dog carried nine feet up, and the hard slick lines down the front of every leg throwing back Milo's cold white light in six thin stripes.

It hit Wyck.

And Wyck Tanner stood exactly where he had been standing.

That was all. He did not brace and give ground the way a man braces. He did not go back a step. There was a crash and a boy's whole body went up onto his toes and came back down again, three inches from where it had started, and the thing that had come down a mile of open path at the speed of a dropped stone found that its line was not clear.

It stopped.

"*FOUR!*" Dessa screamed from the lock tail. "*THREE —*"

Toren swung.

The hook came off his knuckles ugly and heavy and entirely his own and he did not aim it at the body and he did not aim it at the middle. He put it round the nearest leg about two feet off the ground, where the thing was standing on four points like a table, and he got his hips under him and he *hauled*, exactly the way you haul a full bucket up a well, exactly the way a voice had roared at him out of the dark of a rock cut eleven days ago.

He was thirteen and he weighed eight stone and the creature weighed a great deal more than that.

It did not matter, because it was standing on points, and it was not moving, and nothing in it had decided which way it was going next.

The leg came out sideways.

The whole thing went over. It went over the way a ladder goes over, with all the weight at the top and nothing to catch it, and it came down across the coping and into the dry pound and fell five feet onto the slabs with a noise like a barrow of scaffolding going downstairs.

"*Now!*" Toren was already going over the edge after it. "It can't get up — it's got no —"

It could not get up.

That was the other thing about stilts and nobody had told them that either, and Rook admitted afterward that he had never seen one down and had not known it for certain himself. A creature that is a line with legs is a magnificent thing on a flat surface and a hopeless one in a trench with its body on the ground. It threshed. The legs went everywhere and hit the stone walls and the slabs and each other, and two of them came out of the body entirely, and the fast dry clatter went on and on and did not become anything.

Wyck came down off the collapsed rubble on his backside with the bar in his fist and finished it in four strokes and then sat down in the silt.

The whole creature went to ash from the wound outward, all at once, a dry gray collapse with a sound like a sack of flour dropped on a floor.

The ash hung in the trench. Then it began, slowly, to settle.

Up in the arch, the light went out.

---

Rook came round the wing wall and down the towpath eleven seconds later and he was not running, exactly, but Toren had never seen him cover ground like that on the flat.

He went straight over the coping into the pound and put his hands on Wyck.

"Lie back. All the way. Don't help."

"It's not —"

"Lie *back,* please."

Toren did the one useful thing he did in the entire hour, which was to put both his hands where he was told to put them and keep them there without moving them, and not look at what was underneath, and hold on while a man worked.

Somewhere in the middle of it Toren's ears popped.

"Right," Rook said, at last, sitting back on his heels in the gray. "Three ribs. Maybe four. The tear's long and it's shallow at the bottom end and it bled a lot and it looks worse than it is, and there's no bone through anything and nothing's opened up inside you that I can find. You'll be on that cart for a week."

"No."

"You will be on that cart for a week," Rook said, "and you'll be pulled by people who are all going to be extremely pleasant about it, and it is going to be the worst week of your year."

Wyck put his forearm over his eyes and laughed, once, and then stopped because it hurt.

---

They got him up onto the towpath and got Dessa out of the lock tail and Milo came down from the arch with his hands shaking so badly he could not hold the water can.

Then Rook stood them all in a row on the cinder path and did the thing he did.

"Toren."

"I know."

"Say it anyway."

Toren looked at the ash settling in the trench.

"It stopped," he said. "And I said hold and back off. And back off *down the path,* which is the line, which is —" His voice went. He got it back. "It's the worst thing I could have said. It's the worst available thing. There isn't a worse one."

"Why did you say it?"

"Because a thing that goes still is winding up."

"Who taught you that?"

"You did."

"Yes," Rook said.

He crouched down on the path in front of them, which he had never done in the middle of a correction in eighteen days.

"I taught it to you on a road at night with a girl's leg broken under your hands, and I did that on purpose, because I wanted it to go in deeper than anything else you know. It went in deeper than anything else you know." He let that sit. "And this morning I stood in that dust for twenty minutes and told you it did not apply here, and you nodded at me, and eleven hours later your body went and got the expensive lesson out of the drawer because that is the only one it can reach in half a second.

"That is not stupidity and it is not your fault in the way you are currently deciding it is. It's what learning actually *is,* and it is the single most dangerous thing about it. You do not get to pick which lesson arrives under pressure. The one that cost the most arrives first. Every time. For the rest of your life."

Toren's face was wet and he did not do anything about it.

"So what do I —"

"You can't be told out of it. There's no sentence that fixes it and I've just proved that in front of everybody." Rook stood up. "It's repetition. We are going to do the stop-and-close on a dry path with a stick and a rope until your body is *bored* of it, and we'll do it every day for a month, and in a month it'll still not be as deep as the one that's in there now. That's the work. That's all the work ever is."

"That's not —"

"And now the part you've earned, and I want you to have it before tonight, because tonight you're going to lie down and turn this whole day into a thing you did to Wyck."

Toren looked up.

"Inside ninety seconds," Rook said, "with a friend bleeding in a ditch and no adult on that path, you worked out that the answer was to pick the place where it would have to stop, and to put a thing in the line that wouldn't move, and to give it a reason to take that line. Then you got the three people around you into those positions with four words each, and every one of them went." He said it flatly, as information, which was the only way he ever said anything. "That is a better piece of thinking than I had at thirteen, and it is better than most of what I saw at twenty. I'm not saying it to make you feel better. I'm saying it because it's true and because it will be gone by tomorrow if nobody says it out loud."

Nobody said anything.

"Milo."

Milo flinched.

"Don't ever do that again," Rook said.

"I know. I'm —"

"I'm not finished." Rook looked at him for a second longer than he needed to. "You told us first. You shouted it in a hole with a thing coming at you and you still told us first, because I said to, three days ago, on a wall. That's the whole of what I asked for and you did it under the worst conditions available." A pause. "And it worked. Don't do it again."

"When can I do it again?"

"When you tell somebody first," Rook said, and Milo Faust sat down in the middle of the towpath and put his hands over his face.

---

He went to Wyck last, and he went alone, and Toren was not meant to hear it and heard all of it anyway, because he was six feet away holding a cloth and nobody had told him to go.

"You stood in it."

"It was in the way," Wyck said.

"Yes." Rook was quiet for a second. "It was."

He sat down on the coping.

"I'm going to tell you a thing now that I was going to tell you in a week."

"Don't do me any favors."

"It isn't one." Rook put his hands on his knees. "There are three ways in. That's all anybody's ever found and I'd be glad to be wrong about it. There's a stone in your arm, which is Toren's, and it's the one everybody's heard of because it's the one you can *see.* There's a hand that does things to the air, which was Marta's, and it's the one they teach where I come from. And there's a body that has decided, over years, without anybody's permission, not to be moved."

Wyck did not say anything.

"They are three doors," Rook said, "onto the same room. That's not a nice way of putting it. It's the flat fact of the thing, and almost nobody believes it, and the only difference between the three is which one you happened to be standing in front of when you turned thirteen."

"You're saying —"

"I'm saying that something went into a tailgate at a run in a rock cut eleven days ago and you were standing where you were standing. And then the second one did. And then the third one. I looked at that ground afterward, after everybody else was up the bank, and there were two boot marks in four inches of ash and they had not moved."

"That's just —"

"It is not just anything," Rook said. "I have seen four people in my life who could do that and three of them had spent twenty years on it in a room with a teacher."

The canal made its small noises. Somewhere north a bird went over.

"You're Ember," Rook said. "You have been Ember since a tailgate in a rock cut eleven days ago and nobody has ever told you, because the third door hasn't got a ceremony and there's nothing to hand anybody and no metal to strap on, and most people who have it go their entire lives being called stubborn by their mothers."

Wyck lay on the cinder path with his arm over his eyes for a long time.

"Since the cut," he said.

"Yes."

"And you knew."

"I suspected it four days ago and I was sure this morning, about eleven seconds after that stone ticked on your arm."

Wyck took his arm off his eyes.

"Then why," he said, "did you let me sit against a wall in front of all of them and try to be *her*, and be sick on the ground, and get told it didn't work?"

Toren stopped breathing.

"Because I was going to make you find it," Rook said.

"What?"

"That's the answer and you're owed it straight. I was going to wait a week, and put you in three or four situations, and let you notice it yourself, because a thing you find is worth four of a thing you're handed." He was looking out at the water. "That's how I was taught. That is how I have taught every single person I have ever taught, and I have never once had cause to think it was wrong, and I had already decided this morning that you'd have it by the end of the week."

"And?"

"And you've got three broken ribs," Rook said.

He said it in exactly the voice he said everything.

"I was wrong. That's the whole of it and there isn't any more to it. I should have told you at eight o'clock this morning when you asked me for the loan of a thing you did not need, and I did not, because I was doing it the way I have always done it, and it cost you a week on a cart and a scar you'll have when you're sixty." He stood up. "You don't have to do anything with that. You don't have to say it's all right, and I'd think less of you if you did."

Wyck lay on the path and looked up at him.

"It isn't all right," he said.

"No," Rook agreed.

And he walked off up the towpath to get the cart, and Toren sat six feet away with a bloody cloth in his hands and watched him go, and could not decide whether what he had just heard was the best or the worst thing that had happened all day.

---

They camped at the bottom of the flight because Wyck could not be moved further.

Nobody talked much. Milo collected the ash out of the dry pound with the horn scoop, on his own, without being asked, and brought it up and it filled most of a flask and a bit.

"Flask and a half," said Rook, when he saw it. "That's what they're worth at Hobb's Wall, and it's a fair price and you should never take less."

He sat down against the lock wall with the flask beside him.

Toren lay awake a long time. The warmth under his breastbone was there, small and steady and not moving, exactly as it had been on the flight the night before, and he could not make himself be glad about it.

Across the fire Wyck slept poorly on his good side with his ribs strapped. Dessa had put herself between him and the wind without discussing it with anybody. Milo was curled up with both hands under his chin, the way he always slept, and his fingers were still shaking.

And the man sat against the stone with his hands open on his knees, upright, looking north, exactly as he had every night for eighteen days.

*I was wrong,* Toren thought. *He said I was wrong.*

He turned it over and over and could not get to the bottom of it, because there were two things in it and they did not fit in the same hand. A man who would say that out loud, to a boy, in front of the wind, was not a man who was going to leave them at a wall.

And a man who had just found out that the way he had done everything for eleven years put a hole in somebody was a man who now had to decide what to do about the other ten thousand things he had always done that way.

Toren fell asleep before he got any further with it.

Rook did not.
