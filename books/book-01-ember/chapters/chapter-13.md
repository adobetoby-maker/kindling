# Chapter 13 — Five Days

The ridge lane ran out on the twenty-fifth day, at about eleven in the morning, and what it ran out into was a crossroads with a stone trough in it.

Toren had been watching the hedges. There had been two of them for eleven miles, one on each hand, gone up into trees and grown together overhead in places, and for four days they had been the whole world. Then the left one stopped. Then the right one stopped. The lane came out of the tunnel it had been into a flat open place with a sky on it, and the sky was so large and so gray and so entirely without cover that Toren's shoulders came up around his ears before he knew he had done it.

"Stop there," Rook said.

They stopped. They stopped with the cart's front wheels still under the last of the trees, which nobody had been told to do and all four of them did.

"Good," Rook said. "That's the first thing any of you have done this week that I'd have paid money for."

He went out into the open on his own and stood there for a while.

---

It was a drovers' place. Toren worked that out before anybody told him. There was a pinfold of dry-stone walling with a gate, and a trough eleven feet long cut out of a single piece of stone. The ground for forty yards in every direction had been trodden to hardpan by a great many hoofs over a great many years, and it had never come back.

Four roads met there. The lane they had come out of, a track going west into rough pasture, a wide made road running north, and another going away east and slightly down, toward country Toren could not see the bottom of.

And there was a house.

It sat back from the north road behind a wall, long and low and built of the same stone as the trough, with a slate roof that had been patched in three colors. There was smoke coming out of it. There was a line of washing at the back of it with four things on the line.

Toren had not seen washing on a line since Hobb's Wall.

"Is that —" Milo started.

"Wayhouse," said Senna, from the cart, with her eyes shut. "Or it was. There'll have been a trade in that once. Beer and a floor and a byre, and you paid by the head of stock, not by the man."

"How do you know?"

"Because I am extremely old, Milo, and because there's a trough out there you could water thirty beasts at. Nobody cuts that much stone for a house."

Rook came back.

"Eighty yards," he said. "All of you, off the road, on the pinfold wall where you can be seen and can't be reached. Wyck stays on the cart. Dessa, you're calling. Milo, hands where they show. Toren, with me and one step behind."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one who's been asking me questions for a fortnight," Rook said, "and I'd rather you got a look at where the answers come from."

---

The man came out before they were halfway across the hardpan, which meant he had been watching them for a while.

He was seventy or thereabouts, narrow, with a coat on over a nightshirt in the middle of the day and a billhook in his right hand held down low and loose. He stopped at his own gate and did not come through it.

"Far enough," he said.

Rook put his hands out from his sides.

"Six of us. One old, one mending. Going north."

"You've come off the ridge lane."

"We have."

"Nobody comes off the ridge lane." The billhook did not move. "That's eleven miles of nothing and it's been that since I was a boy. A man who takes it with a cart is a man who doesn't want to be on the Marl. What's behind you?"

"Coldharbour's gone."

The old man's face did not do anything at all for about two seconds.

"Halloran's," he said.

"Four nights before we got there. Seven working the wharf. Five under stones behind the shed, and two that a woman called Cass Ordway dug half a day for and never found."

"Spawn?"

"No."

The billhook came up about four inches and went back down.

"Come in off the road, then," said the old man. "Bring the cart. There's water in the trough and it's clean, and I'd take it as a kindness if the young ones would stay out of the byre, because the byre's got a floor I don't trust."

---

His name was Bram Stell and the crossroads was Stell Cross. His people had held the wayhouse there for a hundred and ten years, and had stopped holding it about nine years ago, when the droving finished — because the droving finished when there stopped being anywhere south to drove to.

He told them all of that inside four minutes, standing at his own gate, in the flat hurried way of a man who has not had anybody to say it to.

There was a woman in the doorway behind him with a child on her hip. She did not come out, and Bram Stell did not introduce her. Toren noticed that Rook never once looked directly at her, which he understood about an hour later was a courtesy.

Milo filled the water can at the trough. Dessa sat on the wall where she could see the east road. Senna got two sentences out of the woman in the doorway about the child's teeth and got, in return, a piece of hard cheese the size of a fist, which she accepted without any fuss at all and divided into six that evening.

And Rook stood in the yard with his hands at his sides and asked Bram Stell four questions.

---

"How many were they?"

"Nine." Bram Stell said it immediately. He had been waiting five days to be asked. "Nine, and two of them women, and every one of them fed. That's the thing I keep going back to. They were *fed.* You don't see fed people on this road."

"Which way did they come in at?"

"East." He pointed with the billhook. "Up from the Ley. Came in at about four in the afternoon and went out north at about six. They watered at that trough and they paid me for the water. Nobody has paid me for that water in nine years."

"Paid you in what?"

"Coin. Real coin, four bits of it, station-struck, and one bit of brass that isn't money." The old man's mouth went sideways. "I asked about the brass. The one doing the talking said it was better than money on the right road, and to keep it, and not to be too quick about showing it to anybody."

Rook did not move.

"And did anybody count who came out?" he said.

Bram Stell blinked at him.

"Out of what?"

"Nothing," Rook said. "Ignore that. Wrong question."

It was the only time in twenty-five days that Toren had heard him take one back.

---

There was a fifth question and it was not really a question.

"They'll have asked you something," Rook said. "Before they went. A thing that didn't sound like much at the time and that you've been turning over since."

Bram Stell looked at him for a long moment.

"You've met them," he said.

"Tell me what they asked."

"They bought my book." He said it slowly, the way a man says a thing he has been trying to get to sound ordinary. "I keep a loss book. Kept. Eleven years of it. Every head of stock that went off this road between the Ley and the high pasture, and where, and what month, and what condition the ground was in when it happened. That's not a thing anybody's ever wanted. That's a thing a drover keeps so he can argue with a factor about a dead bullock."

"How much did they give you for it?"

"Three of the four bits." Bram Stell's jaw worked. "Which is fair. Which is more than fair. Which is about eleven times what anybody's ever given me for anything. They didn't haggle. The woman who took it off me held it in both hands and said thank you." He turned his head and spat on his own hardpan. "And then she asked me the other thing."

"Go on."

"She asked me who else keeps a book."

---

Nobody said anything in that yard for a while. Out at the trough, Milo was singing something to the child in the doorway under his breath and getting it wrong on purpose, and the child was laughing.

"And what did you tell her?" Rook said.

"I told her nobody. I told her the droving's finished and there's nobody left on this road to keep anything." Bram Stell's hands had started to shake, and he put the billhook against the wall so that he could stop holding it. "That was a lie. She knew it was a lie. She smiled at me like a woman letting a child keep a stone, and they went north."

"Who else keeps a book?"

"The Ridley outfit at Wenn's Ford keeps a book. Four families at the Stage keep a book between them. There's a woman at Marram Head who's kept a tally of every bad ground in this whole country for twenty-six years and she'd give it to anybody who asked her nicely, because she's ninety and she wants somebody to have it." He got his breath. "And I've been standing in this yard for five days doing nothing about any of it. Because I'm seventy-one. Because there's a woman and a baby in that house. Because the road goes both ways."

"Yes," Rook said.

"Don't you do that." The old man's voice went thin. "Don't you say *yes* at me like that's an answer."

"It's not an answer. It's the only true thing I've got and I'm not going to improve it for you." Rook shouldered his pack. "How far to Wenn's Ford on the north road?"

"Two days with that cart."

"Then we'll be at Wenn's Ford in two days and I'll tell the Ridley outfit myself. They can decide what they want to do with a book. None of that is your fault and none of it is your job." He looked at the old man properly for the first time. "You told me. That's the job. You looked backward and you said what you saw out loud, to a stranger, in your own yard, with a baby in the house behind you. That is the whole of what anybody can do."

Bram Stell stood in his yard and did not say anything.

"Water's clean," he said at last. "Take what you want of it."

---

They made six miles north that afternoon and it took them until the light went. The made road was better than the ridge lane in every way that mattered. Rook stopped them four separate times to look at nothing.

Toren did the sums on the shafts.

Coldharbour was nine days behind them and had been four nights old when they reached it. That made thirteen. Stell Cross was five days old. Nine men at Coldharbour, and nine at the Cross with two of them women, which was not necessarily the same nine and was not necessarily not.

Thirteen days and five days.

He had been carrying the number thirteen around since the towpath and it had felt like history. It had felt like a thing that had happened to other people in a place they had walked past afterward. Five days was not history. Five days was Wyck sitting up on his own for the first time. Five days was the second night in the wet hedge.

Five days ago these people had stood on hardpan and paid an old man for water.

---

The drilling changed that evening and everybody knew inside about nine seconds.

They had stopped in the corner of a walled field with a dead thorn in it. Rook had them up before the food, which was normal, and put them in the line, which was normal. Then he walked out in front of them and stood with his hands at his sides instead of picking up the hazel rod.

"Right," he said. "Everything I have taught the four of you for twenty-five days has been about a thing that cannot change its mind."

Nobody moved.

"A Husk has one plan. A verge-hound has one plan; it picks that plan early and cannot re-pick it. A stilt has a line and a stop, and the stop is the whole of your business. All of that is true. None of it is going anywhere. We're going to keep drilling it until you're bored." He let that sit for a second. "And it is all completely useless against a man."

"Rook —" said Dessa.

"A man has hands," Rook said. "A man changes his mind. A man watches what you do and does something else because of it, which is a thing nothing that has ever come out of a hole in the world has done or will do. So we start on it tonight, and we do a small amount of it every day from here on. Understand two things before we begin. You will be worse at this than you are at anything else, for years. Being worse at it is correct."

Milo put his hand up.

"Put it down," Rook said. "Ask."

"Why now?"

"Because the road's got people on it," Rook said, "and the ridge lane hasn't got anything on it at all, and we've come off the one and onto the other."

That was an answer. Toren thought about it for the rest of the evening and could not find a single thing in it that was a lie, and could not find the part of it that was true either.

---

"Eight feet," Rook said. "That's the first thing. Everything I've given you about eighty yards is for a person who wants to be seen coming. A man who means it doesn't need eighty yards. He needs eight feet and a reason to be standing there. He will have one. It will be a good one, and it will be about a wheel or a road or the weather."

He walked down the line.

"Second thing. He will talk. He'll talk the whole way in, and every single word out of his mouth in those eight feet is a hand pushing you somewhere. He is not making conversation. He is moving you. If you find yourself turning to answer a man, you have already been moved. You turned yourself. He didn't have to do it."

"So what do you do?" said Toren.

"You answer him without moving your feet and you keep answering him. Talking back costs you nothing. Turning costs you everything." Rook stopped in front of Dessa. "Third thing, and it's yours. You've been calling *how many* for three weeks and you're good at it. Stop counting people."

Dessa looked up at him.

"Count hands," Rook said. "People is the wrong number. Six men with their hands showing is a better afternoon than three men with one hand each behind a hip. You will not have time for both and the hands are the one that matters. Practice it on Milo tonight and get it wrong about forty times."

"And what am I doing?" said Milo.

"You throw at the face."

Milo's mouth opened.

"Not the body. Not the shoulder, which is what you've been doing all fortnight because it's the biggest thing on a man and you're a good enough shot to know where it is." Rook picked a stone up off the wall and put it in Milo's hand. "A Husk hasn't got anything to blink with. A man blinks. That's half a second where he can't see and doesn't know what he's missed, and half a second is enough time for somebody else to be somewhere better."

"That's —" Milo turned the stone over. "That's a rotten thing to do to somebody."

"Yes," said Rook.

He did not say anything else about it, and Milo, after a while, put the stone in his pocket.

---

Then he went to Wyck, who had got himself off the cart and onto the wall and had been sitting there with his arms round his own ribs saying nothing at all.

"You I'm going to tell the worst one," Rook said.

"Go on."

"A man will not run into you."

Wyck's head came up.

"It's the whole of the difference and it's the thing you'll hate." Rook crouched down so that they were level, which he did about once a week and never without a reason. "Everything that has come at you in twenty-five days has come at you because you were in the way and it could not want anything except forward. That is what you are for and there is nobody on this road who is better at it. A man looks at you standing in the road with your feet apart and an iron bar in your hands, and he goes *round.*"

Nobody said anything. Dessa had gone very still on the wall.

"He goes round, and he takes the person behind you," Rook said. "There is nothing whatever you can do about that by standing better."

"Then what do I do?"

"You stop being a wall and you start being a door." Rook stood up. "A wall is a thing men walk round. A door is a thing they have to come through, and you make a door by putting yourself where the ground has already done half the work — a gate, a gap, an arch, the inside of a bend, the end of a cart. You do not choose where you stand any more. You choose it about four minutes earlier than you've ever chosen anything in your life, and that's the whole of the next year for you."

Wyck sat on the wall with his hands loose between his knees and looked at the ground for a long time.

"The arch," he said.

"The arch."

"I didn't choose that. He did." Wyck jerked his chin at Toren without any particular feeling in it. "He shouted at me to get up the rubble and stand on a path."

"He did, and it was the best thing anybody did that day. He'll get it wrong nine times out of the next ten. So will you." Rook picked up the hazel rod at last. "That's the lesson. Everybody up. We're going to do the stop-and-close on a person for twenty minutes and then we're going to stop, because you'll all be worse at everything tomorrow if we don't."

---

They did it for twenty minutes and it was horrible.

It was horrible in a way Toren had no equipment for. The hazel rod had been a thing at the end of a man's arm. Now Rook walked at them, at ordinary walking speed, with his hands out and open and empty, saying ordinary things — *mind the wheel there, is that your mother in the cart, you've come off the ridge lane, have you.* Every single one of them turned their head at some point. Every single time they turned, he was closer.

Toren turned on the fourth pass and found the man two feet away.

He had not seen it happen. He had heard a sentence about the weather, and his face had gone toward it the way a face goes toward a voice. Then there had been a person inside his arms.

"That," said Rook. "Again."

They did it eleven times. Dessa was the best of them by a long way, because Dessa had spent her whole life not turning her head for people. She said so, flatly, as a fact about the world. Nobody laughed.

On the twelfth, Rook came in on Toren with his right hand open at his side and his left behind his hip, and Toren said "Left hand," out loud, without meaning to.

Rook stopped.

"Good," he said. "That's enough of that for one evening."

---

It was the first time in twenty-five days that he had stopped a drill before he had finished with it.

Nobody remarked on it. Milo went and did the water. Dessa got the slate out and wrote the rota on it with a nail. Wyck got himself off the wall and back onto the cart in the eleven separate small movements it took him now, and did not let anyone near him while he did it.

Toren sat with a bowl in his hands and did the thing he did, which was to put the day in order.

Nine men, fed, coming up from the east. Coin and a piece of brass that was not money. A book bought at eleven times its worth by a woman who held it in both hands and said thank you.

*Who else keeps a book.*

And a man in a walled field teaching four children that the person in front of them would talk about the weather all the way in.

Toren waited for somebody to say it. He was fairly sure it was going to be Dessa, and he had half an ear turned to her all evening in case it was, because Dessa said true things out flat and let people take them or not.

Dessa did not say it. Dessa wrote the rota and went to sleep.

Nobody said it. That was the part Toren could not get past, lying down at last with the fire a seam and a wall at his back. Five people had spent an evening learning where to hit a man in the face, and not one of them had asked which man.

Across the field, at the edge of the light, Rook sat against the wall with his hands open on his knees and did not sleep.

And eight feet from Toren, in the bed of the cart, Wyck Tanner lay on his good side with his ribs strapped and his eyes open, staring straight up at the canvas hoop, moving his lips.

He was doing what Toren did on the shafts. He was saying a thing over to himself until he had it in the order he wanted it.
