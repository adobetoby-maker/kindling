# Chapter 25 — What It Does First

They spent the night in the lee of a field wall two hundred yards off the road. Nobody slept, and Rook did not pretend anybody was going to.

He got them round in the dark with the cart on its side as a back wall and he laid it out the way he laid out a bill.

"Everything I know about a Barrel I can say in about forty seconds and then we'll be down to what we can see for ourselves. So listen to the forty seconds.

"One. It is one. Handful means a lot of them and the number is the danger. Flask means a pack or a good solo and you can plan against a pack. Barrel means one, on its own, and nothing else on that ground with it, and the reason there's nothing else on that ground with it is that there isn't any more.

"Two. It will come at worked power. Everything that has ever come out of a hole in the world comes at worked power. That is not a failing in them. That is what they are made of, and they are going toward it. I am the loudest thing in this basin by a very long way. That is going to matter tomorrow, and I want it said out loud tonight so nobody is surprised by it in daylight.

"Three." He stopped. "Three is that I have never seen one. The two men I ever heard describe one were describing different animals. I do not know one single true thing about how it fights. That's the end of the forty seconds."

"That's thirty," said Dessa.

"Then take the other ten as a gift."

---

Here is what Toren did instead of sleeping.

He lay on his side against a cart bed with the wheel above him and he put the day in order, the way he had put every day in order since he was nine.

A furrow a hundred and ten yards long, rolled turf, no curve in it, going through a wall instead of over it. A stop with nothing to stop against. A trunk cut off eight feet up, in one face, on a rising line.

*Within about a second,* Rook had said. *And I'd say less.*

Toren lay in the dark and did the thing he could not help doing, which was to put a number on the middle of it.

Four seconds. That was his own number, on flat ground, at rest, with nobody in front of him. Four seconds from a hook to a strut. Rook could not tell him how long his own changeover took because Rook did not do one.

And somewhere out on the floor of the Thirl there was something that had run a hundred and ten yards and stopped dead and cut a tree in half, and the whole of it had taken less than a second.

He did not sleep at all.

---

They went at first light, because Rook would not go in the dark and would not wait for the middle of the day.

"Two and a half miles," he said. "There is a made road running straight up the middle of this bottom to the gate of that place, and we are not going to put one boot on it."

"Why not? It's the fastest —"

"Because it's straight," said Rook. "Look at the furrow. Look at what that thing did to a wall it could have gone round. Straight is what it is *for*. A road is a line with an invitation on it."

He put the flat of his good hand down on the turf and drew it with his finger, and Toren watched a man who could not close his left hand draw a map in wet grass in the half-dark.

"Orchard. Twenty-two rows, north and south, eleven feet between them, and the trees are twelve feet apart in the row. That's four hundred yards of length and it is the only cover between us and that bank.

"Off the top corner of it there's a glasshouse range, seven of them, running east. Brick footings a foot and a half high, iron hoops standing up out of them, and all the glass on the ground.

"North of that it is half a mile of open field with the road in the middle and nothing else in it at all, and then the bank, and then the gate.

"So. We go up the inside of the orchard between the rows. We come out at the top corner into the glass. And then we do the half mile of open in one go, and whatever that costs us it costs us there, because that is the only place on this bottom where there is nowhere to hide and there is no arrangement that changes it."

"And you?" said Wyck.

"I go two hundred yards west of the orchard, in the open, on my own," said Rook, "because I'm the loud one, and because a thing that goes toward worked power is a thing you can put in a place you'd rather it was."

---

Toren said no.

He said it out loud in the grey with his heart going. He was astonished to hear it come out of himself, and more astonished that his voice was level.

"No."

Rook looked at him.

"Say the objection."

"You said nobody in this group is bait." Toren's mouth had gone dry. "You said it at a stone heap and you said it in an engine house. You made Milo say it back to you word for word about nine hours before he died. You said if you ever decided otherwise you'd say it out loud to our faces beforehand, and we could tell you what we thought of it."

The wall was very quiet.

"I'm saying it out loud to your faces beforehand," said Rook.

"That's not —"

"It's exactly what I told you I'd do, and you've just quoted it at me correctly." He did not raise his voice one degree. "Now tell me what you think of it."

"I think it's the arch again," said Toren. "I think it's you going up a road on your own at three in the morning at a third of the room, because it's the only plan. I think you're at a quarter this morning and not a third. And I think the last time we did this we came back down the bank with four people."

Dessa said, "Toren."

"No, let him," said Rook. "He's got it in order and he's saying it to my face in front of everybody. That's exactly what the arrangement was for." He crouched down on his heels, which cost him. "Here's my answer and you're owed it plainly. I'm not bait. Bait is a thing you leave somewhere. I'm the loudest thing in this basin whether I stand here or two hundred yards west, and there is no version of this morning where that thing does not know where I am. The only question available is whether I am standing beside that cart when it arrives, or two hundred yards away from it."

Toren stood in the wet grass with nothing left to say.

"And if you'd like a thing to hold," Rook added, getting up, "hold this. Milo was right on the Level and I was wrong. I have had eleven days to find the place where he was wrong, and there isn't one. That is not going to make me hand a road to a child. It has made me a great deal more careful about which of us is standing where."

---

They got four hundred yards.

That was the whole of the plan's life. Four hundred yards of grey morning, with the cart down the eleventh row of a dead orchard and the trunks going past on both sides at twelve-foot spacing, and Toren pulling the near shaft and counting the trees because the trees were there to be counted.

Then Dessa said, "Stop."

They stopped.

"West," she said. "Out past the last row. It's in the open."

"How far?"

"Three hundred. It's not coming. It's —" She had her head turned and her eyes half shut, and the fingers of her right hand had come up and spread. "It's going along. North to south. Then it stops. Then it goes again. It's *quartering.*"

"It's hunting," said Rook. "Keep going. Don't hurry and don't stop again unless I say. Wyck, that wheel's squealing and I want it not squealing in about four seconds."

Wyck put the last of the pear-fat on the hub with his thumb, walking, one-handed, and the squeal went out of it.

They made another hundred yards.

Then the sound came across two hundred yards of open ground. It was the first sound any of them ever heard that thing make, and it was not a noise an animal makes.

It was a long dry grinding, low down, like sand going down a chute.

---

It crossed the two hundred yards in about a second and a half.

Toren had time to turn his head. That was all. He had a shaft in his hands and his body half round and one boot still moving. There was a grey shape out past the end of the rows. Then the grey shape was at the mouth of the eleventh row, eighty feet away.

It was the size of a cart horse and it was nothing like the shape of one.

It went low and long, close to the ground, on four limbs that did not match each other. The front left one was longer than the others and heavier, and the joint in it was in the wrong place. There was no head. There was a wedge at the front of it with a seam down the middle and the seam did not open.

It was the colour of wet slate.

Toren stood in an orchard row with an ugly grey hook coming off the back of his own knuckles and looked at a thing the exact same colour, and something in him went a long way down and did not come back up for a week.

"*ROOK!*"

"I see it."

---

Here is what Rook did. It was the best thing anybody did that morning, and it very nearly worked.

He came in from the west end of the row at a flat run with the struts down his legs and a rib either side of his spine, and he did not go toward the thing.

He went between it and the cart.

Then he put his hands out and shut the road.

Toren had seen it once before at the south mouth of a rock cut on the worst morning of his life, and he had been too far away and too much on fire to take any of it in. This time he was forty feet away and looking straight down the row.

There was nothing to see. That was the whole of it. The air between two apple trees eleven feet apart did not shimmer or thicken or glow. It simply stopped being a place where a thing could go, the way a doorway stops being a doorway when somebody shuts the door, and the only way Toren knew it had happened at all was that his ears popped.

The Breach hit it at full speed.

The sound was enormous and completely wrong — not a crash and not a thud, but a flat hard slap, the way a wave hits a harbour wall. The whole grey mass of it came up off the ground and went over sideways into the second tree in the row and took the tree off at the root.

Rook went down on one knee in the grass with both arms out.

"*GO!*" he shouted. "*Wyck, straight up the row, don't look at it —*"

And the Breach got its feet under it, eleven feet from a shut road, and turned.

---

Toren's first model of that thing was wrong. It was a reasonable model, and he had been given it himself on a towpath above a dry canal by a man with a hazel rod.

*A stilt has a line and a stop, and the stop is the whole of your business.*

A stilt cannot re-pick its line. That is the entire trade. It goes dead straight at a speed nothing has any business going, and when it wants to go somewhere else it has to stop, in the open, exposed, for about four seconds, and choose again. Toren had drilled it for a fortnight. He had drilled it on bad ground and in the dark and standing behind a tree. He knew it the way he knew his own mile counts.

The Breach came off the shut road at a dead stop. It dropped its hindquarters, and its whole weight went *back* and *down*.

And there was a hard flat click, like a slate being set down on its edge.

And the long front limb went flat, and went hard, and got an edge on it.

---

It happened in a quarter of a second and Toren understood exactly nothing about it at the time.

What he had was Rook on one knee in the grass eleven feet in front of a thing that had just stopped, and every single thing he had ever been taught told him that a stopped one is a safe one for four seconds.

He was wrong and he did not have time to be wrong twice.

Rook went sideways off his knee before the click had finished. He did not stand up and he did not turn. He went flat along the ground with the struts still on, and the cut came through the place where his chest had been and went on through the trunk of the third tree without slowing down at all.

The top of the tree came down across the row.

"*NOT THE STOP,*" Rook shouted from the grass. "*The stop is the OTHER ONE — get the cart OFF this row —*"

---

They did not get the cart off that row.

The Breach came up out of the set. Its weight went forward. The grinding started.

It took the length of the eleventh row of a dead orchard in under a second, with eleven feet of trunk on either side of it and nothing in the way but a hand cart.

Wyck Tanner was between it and Senna Voss when it arrived, because Wyck Tanner was always between it and somebody, and because he had been standing in the place he had chosen four minutes earlier, which is a thing a man learns in a walled field and never afterward stops doing.

He got both hands on the tailgate and set his feet.

Toren saw him do it and understood, with perfect clarity and no time whatever, that a body which has decided not to be moved is the exact right answer to a thing coming at you in a straight line, and that it is the exact wrong answer to a thing that will stop in front of you and cut.

It did not stop.

It came into the cart at the run.

---

The cart went.

It went the way a thing goes when four hundredweight arrives at it at that speed — not over, but *apart*. The near shaft sheared at the socket. The off wheel came off the axle and went down the row on its own for thirty feet and fell over. The bed came up at the front and the whole body of it rotated round the standing wheel and went into the trunk of the sixth tree and stopped being a cart.

Wyck went with it. He went eleven feet and hit the ground with the tailgate still in his hands and rolled twice and came up onto one knee with his ribs audible from twenty feet away.

And Senna Voss went out of the bed sideways.

She did not go far. She went about six feet, into the wet grass between two rows, with the blanket still round her legs, and she made no sound whatever.

The Breach's own weight carried it four feet past the wreck. It dropped its hindquarters. Its weight went back and down.

The flat click.

---

Rook covered eleven feet in a time nobody counted.

He did not shut the road and he did not put out a blade. He went down on the grass on his knees over the top of an old woman, with his back to the thing and his arms round her head, and he stayed there.

He took the cut across the back of his left shoulder, from the top of the blade bone to the bottom of it.

It did not go deep. That was the thing Toren understood four days later, in a lit room, with a doctor explaining it. It was not luck and it was not mercy. The cut came on a rising line, the way it had come at the tree. A man on his knees is a low thing. The rising line took the meat off the top of the shoulder and went on into the air.

Rook made a sound.

In forty-five days, that was the first one.

---

"*OFF HIM!*"

That was Toren, and he had no memory of deciding it.

He went down the row with the hook out. He was thirteen years old. He weighed about a hundred and ten pounds. He had an ugly lopsided hook on the back of his knuckles that was blunt along the whole of its back. And he went at a Barrel-tier Riftspawn from behind at a dead run, because there was a man on the ground in front of it.

He did not reach it.

The weight came up and forward. The grinding started. And the thing was forty feet away up the row before his second stride had landed, and he ran four more steps at nothing at all and stopped in the wet grass with his chest heaving.

It had not been avoiding him.

That was what he could not get past, afterward. It had not even known he was there. It had gone because it was going, in a straight line, the way it did, and he had been off the line.

---

Dessa got them into the Long Lights.

She did it in about ninety seconds, and Toren did not see how. He was under Rook's good arm with Wyck under the other one and Senna across Wyck's chest, and the four of them went up the last sixty yards of the eleventh row at the speed of the slowest of them, which was every single one of them.

"Left," said Dessa, from in front. "Left and down. Over the brick. Don't step in the beds — the glass is all in the beds. *Left.*"

The glasshouse range ran east off the top corner of the orchard. It had been seven glasshouses. It was now seven parallel lines of brick footing a foot and a half high, with rusted iron hoops standing up out of them at four-foot intervals, and a hundred and twenty feet of broken glass lying in the bed between each pair.

They got over three of the footings and put themselves down in the fourth bed with their backs against brick.

Dessa did not sit down. She knelt at the wall with one hand flat on the brick and her head turned, and she stayed that way for a long time without moving.

"Dessa."

"East end of the range. Hundred and forty yards. Going away."

"You can't hear that far."

"I can hear that far today," said Dessa Cole.

---

Here is what it cost them to get four hundred and sixty yards into a dead orchard.

The cart was gone. Not damaged — gone, in pieces, in the eleventh row, with the spare cord and the second can and Amery's spade and the slate in it.

Wyck had his three ribs, and now had something else on the same side that made him hold his breath in threes.

Senna had come out of a moving cart onto wet ground at seventy-three years old. She was conscious and she was grey. When Toren got to her she had taken hold of his sleeve with both hands, and she had not let go of it since.

And Rook had eleven inches of open shoulder on top of an arm that had not closed in eleven days.

Dessa did the Reading at about nine o'clock, sitting in broken glass, with two fingers above his wrist.

She did not say the fraction out loud.

That was how Toren found out. He thought about it for years afterward and never once decided whether she had been right to do it that way.

---

"Say it," said Rook.

"An eighth," said Dessa.

"That's about what I've got as well. Good." He got himself up onto one elbow against the brick footing. "Now everybody stop looking at me, because I am going to be no use to any of you inside an hour and we have about that long to become a group that does not need me."

Nobody said anything.

"Toren."

"Yes."

"You went at it."

"I didn't get anywhere near it."

"I know. That isn't what I asked you." Rook's voice had gone back to exactly what it always was. "What did you see?"

---

Toren sat in a glass bed with his back to a brick wall and put the morning in order, because the order was the handrail, and because a man who could not sit up straight had asked him for it.

"It stopped," he said. "Twice. Both times it stopped first and then it cut. I went at it on a stop because a stop is four seconds and a stop is where you go. And it wasn't four seconds. It was —" He shook his head. "It wasn't anything. It was less than a blink."

"That's the first true thing anybody's said this morning," said Rook. "Go on."

"So the stop's not a stop. It's the wind-up."

"For which one?"

"For the cutting."

"And when it runs?"

Toren stopped.

He sat very still with his hands flat on his own knees and went back through it. The mouth of the eleventh row. Rook on his knee. The cart.

"It doesn't stop first," he said slowly. "When it runs it doesn't stop at all. It — " He put his own hands out and moved them. "It comes *up.* At the front. It picks its front up off the ground, the way a dog does. Then there's the grinding noise. Then it's gone. And when it cuts, it goes the other way. It goes *down* at the back. It sits down on its own back end, and the front comes up hard, and then there's the —"

"Click," said Dessa.

Everybody looked at her.

"There's a click," she said. "Every single time before it cuts. There is not one before it runs. I have had eleven of them this morning. There is a click before the cutting and a grinding before the running. They are not the same sound, and they do not come out of the same part of it."

---

"Then that's the tell," said Wyck.

"That's the tell," said Rook.

He said it flatly and without any satisfaction in it at all, and Toren noticed the lack of satisfaction, and did not understand it yet.

"Say it back," Rook said. "All three of you. In order, out loud, the way you'd say it at a gate. Wyck first."

"Weight goes back and down and there's a click, and that's the cut," said Wyck. "Eight or nine feet of it and it comes up at you from low, and it can't move its feet while it's doing it."

"Dessa."

"Weight comes up and forward and there's the grinding, and that's the run. Dead straight, no curve in it, as far as it likes." She had her eyes shut. "And it cannot turn while it's doing it. It hit a shut road at the mouth of that row and went straight through it into a tree. If it could have bent round that by a foot, it would have."

"Toren."

Toren sat in the glass and felt the thing arrive in him whole, the way the number of a distance arrives, without being asked for.

"It can't do both," he said.

---

Nobody spoke.

"It can't do both," Toren said again. "It's got to put one down before it picks the other one up. That's why the weight moves. The weight *is* the putting-down." He had his hands out in front of him and he was staring at them. "It came off the shut road and it wanted to cut. It had been running. So it had to get the weight off the front and onto the back before it could do anything at all. That's the click. And the click isn't the cut — the click is it *finishing changing over.*"

"How long," said Rook.

"I don't —"

"Dessa. How long."

"Half a second," said Dessa Cole, in the voice she used for reading out the number of crows in an elm. "It's half a second. I've had eleven of them and they're all the same and I'd put money on it in a yard."

---

Here is what Toren Voss understood sitting in a bed of broken glass at half past nine in the morning on the forty-sixth day, with an old woman's hands in his sleeve and a man bleeding into brick eleven feet away.

That thing had a four-second hole in the middle of it.

It was half a second wide instead of four, because it was not a person and it did not stop to look. But it was the same hole. It was the exact hole Rook had drawn for him in a lay-by on the Ninety with the light going: *there is a thing everybody does in the middle.* Put one down. Then go and find the warmth again and ask for the other.

A hook. Then nothing. Then a strut.

A run. Then nothing. Then a cut.

The same hole, in a boy on a white road line and in a thing the colour of wet slate, and the only difference between them was the size of it.

He looked up.

Rook was watching him, and had been watching him for some time.

"Yes," said Rook.

"You already had it."

"I had it at the mouth of the row when it came off my road and sat down." Rook shifted against the brick, and his face did something and went back. "I've had ninety minutes with it. I have not been able to make it into one single useful thing. I would very much like one of you to do better, because I am about out."

---

They tested it at ten o'clock. It worked. It did not help at all.

It came down the outside of the third footing at them because they were the only worked power in the basin and it had been quartering for them for an hour. Dessa called it at ninety yards. Wyck stood up behind the iron hoops where a running thing could not take a line, which was correct and which he had worked out for himself, and it came to fifteen feet of him and could not run.

So it set.

"*CLICK,*" said Dessa. It was not a shout. It was a flat statement, said at ordinary volume, about half a second before it happened.

Wyck was behind the second hoop before the cut came through.

It took the hoop off at the footing. It took the next one off as well. Wyck went backward over the third wall into the glass and came up with his hands cut to pieces and nothing else wrong with him at all.

Then the Breach's weight came up and forward. The grinding started. It went east along the outside of the range and was two hundred yards away in three seconds.

And they sat in the glass with their hearts going and knew exactly what it had been about to do, half a second before it did it, on both occasions.

And the cart was still gone. And Rook was still at an eighth. And Meridian was still half a mile of open field away, with a road up the middle of it that not one of them could put a boot on.

---

"Right," said Rook.

He said it the way he said it, which was the way a man says it when the bill has been added up and does not come out.

"We've got the tell and the tell is a good one and Dessa can call it in a voice that carries. That buys us one thing and I want everybody to be clear about what the one thing is. It lets us *not be hit.*

"It does not let us hurt it. It does not let us leave. It does not put a wheel back on a cart or half a mile of open field behind us.

"I can shut a road once more. Once, not twice, and probably not for long. It went through the last one hard enough to take a tree out at the root.

"Wyck can stop it running at him and cannot do one single thing about it cutting. That's exactly half the problem, and it's the half that doesn't get anybody to a gate.

"Dessa can tell us what it's about to do and cannot do one single thing about what it does.

"And Toren has a hook."

---

"That's the account," Rook said. "You're all owed it plainly and you've had it. Now here's what I'm going to do about it."

"No," said Toren.

"I've not said it."

"I know exactly what you're going to say." Toren got up off the brick footing. His hands were shaking and he let them. "You're going to say that it comes at worked power and you're the loudest thing in this basin, and that you can shut a road once, and that half a second is enough for one man to be somewhere if he already knows which somewhere, and that the four of us can do half a mile of open field while it's busy.

"And then you're going to say a number. It'll be about eleven minutes."

Rook looked at him for a long moment.

"Eight," he said.

---

"Say the rest of it," said Wyck.

"There isn't a rest of it," said Rook. "That's the whole plan and it's a bad one. I've said the words *it's bad* out loud to the four of you. I have done that twice before on this road, and both times I was right about it."

"And afterward?"

"There is no afterward in it," said Elias Marsh. "That's what makes it the only one."

Senna Voss made a small sound in the grass.

Nobody looked at her, because looking at her would have been unbearable, and because every person behind that wall had been on a brick platform eleven days ago and knew exactly what this was and where it went and how it ended.

And Toren stood up in a bed of broken glass with an ugly hook coming off the back of his knuckles, and put his own four seconds down beside a thing's half second, and found that he had been carrying the answer since a lay-by on the Ninety and had not known what it was for.

"No," he said. "I've got a better one."
