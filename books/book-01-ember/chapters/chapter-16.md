# Chapter 16 — Tonight

He knew by breakfast. Toren was certain of it and could not have said what gave it away.

It was not anything anybody did. Dessa gave nothing away by daylight and never had. Wyck said four words all morning. Milo was perfectly normal, which was the only part that could have gone wrong and did not, because Milo Faust had spent the whole of the previous night deciding to be normal and then had simply been it.

Rook was correct all day. That was the tell.

He drilled them at the halt. He did the eight-feet work, and the stop-and-close, and twenty minutes of Dessa calling hands from a hedge. He answered three of Milo's questions properly and at length. He checked Wyck's strapping at noon and told him he could pull the off shaft for one mile on the flat and not one yard of it uphill. He got Senna's blanket folded a different way that took the weight off her hip and was so obviously better that she was rude to him about it for a quarter of an hour.

He was, in short, entirely available to all of them, all day, about everything, and Toren understood on the shafts at about four in the afternoon that this was the most complete refusal he had ever been given by anybody.

---

Dessa picked the place, and she picked it two miles before they got there.

"That," she said, from the head of the shafts, and put her chin at a shape on the rise about a mile ahead. "Whatever that is. I want that."

It had been a steading. It was four dry-stone walls in a rough square with the house gone out of the middle of it, and the one thing standing was a byre gable with a slate roof on it big enough to put a cart under. The yard wall was chest-high and unbroken on three sides. On the fourth, where the gate had been, there was a gap eight feet wide with a socket stone on either side.

"There's no water," Rook said.

"There's a spring at the bottom of the rise and Milo's already been sent." Dessa did not look round. "And there's cover for the cart and a wall on the weather side, and Wyck can get from the tailgate to a seat in four steps instead of eleven."

Rook looked at the back of her head for a second.

"Fine," he said.

---

They came into the yard at about six and had it set up in a quarter of an hour. Rook knew what it was inside three seconds of sitting down.

Toren watched it happen.

The man sat down on the flat stone by the byre wall, with the fire in front of him and his back to the stone, which was where he always sat. Then he looked, without moving his head very much, at where everybody else was.

Wyck was on a crate four feet inside the gap in the wall, on the left socket stone, with the iron bar across his knees.

Dessa was on the other side of the fire with her bad leg out straight.

Senna was sitting up in the cart with her hands folded, awake, at six in the evening, which she had not been in nine days.

Milo was standing in the gap.

Rook's eyes went to him and stopped.

Toren, who had spent twenty-eight days learning to watch this man for the size of a thing crossing his face, saw one go across that was bigger than any of the others. It was not fast, either. It stayed.

"Milo," Rook said.

"Yes?"

"Come in out of the gap."

Dessa was already moving. She was up off the ground with her hand out and her face doing something Toren had never seen on it — not embarrassment, something worse and more useful, the look of a person who has made a mistake in a place where mistakes are the whole point.

"Milo, here. Sit by me. Now, please."

Milo came and sat by her, baffled and obliging.

"That was mine," Dessa said. "I put him there. I'm sorry."

"Leave it. It doesn't matter."

It plainly did matter. Nobody said so. The fire made its small noises. The light went off the top of the rise. Rook put his hands on his knees, palms open, and waited.

"Well," he said. "Go on, then."

---

Dessa did it. They had agreed she would, because Dessa said true things out flat and let people take them or not.

She did not accuse him of anything. She laid out the five things off the slate, in order, the way she had laid them out at first light, and at the end of them she said:

"And you went over sideways against a wheel at twenty past one, and you have not told us why you can't sleep. Those are the facts. We're not asking for an apology and we're not frightened of you."

"All right," said Rook.

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's me telling you I've heard it. It was correct. It took courage. I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

He got up.

"I'll take the first watch," he said. "Somebody else can have the last one. I'd like the walking about, and I'd rather not sit down."

And he walked to the gap.

---

Wyck did not stand up.

That was the part Toren thought about for the rest of his life. There was no scene in it at all. Wyck Tanner was sitting on a crate with a blanket on his knees and three ribs that had not finished, four feet inside a gap eight feet wide, and he simply did not move out of it.

Rook stopped.

"Get up, please."

"No."

"Wyck."

"I've got three ribs and I can't stand for ten minutes without it costing me," Wyck said, in the voice he used for reading out a number. "So I sat down four minutes early in the only place on this hill that's worth anything. You told me to do that. You told me at Stell Cross, in a field, with a hazel rod in your hand. You said a wall's a thing men walk round and a door's a thing they have to come through, and you said I'd have to choose the ground four minutes before I'd ever chosen anything in my life."

He looked up.

"That's four minutes," he said. "I've done my part of it."

Rook stood in the gap of a wall and looked down at a boy on a crate, and Toren could not see his face because the light had gone.

"That's correct," Rook said.

He went back and sat down on the flat stone.

---

"Right," he said. "Then I'll make you an offer, and I want you all to understand before I make it that it is not a trick and it is not less than it sounds."

He put his hands on his knees.

"Three questions about Cinder Company. Anything at all. How many there are and how they're organized. How they pick a place. What they do to a yard before they go into it and what they do to it after. What a chit is and what the mark under the number means and how a person uses one." He said it flatly, as information. "Whether they can be beaten, and by how many, and what it costs. What I would do if nine of them came up that rise at four in the morning and what each of the five of you would be doing while I did it.

"That's the most useful thing any of you will ever be given. It's worth more than what you're asking for, and it will keep you alive. What you're asking for will not keep anybody alive."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

It was, Toren thought, the most frightening thing that had happened all evening, because for about four seconds he wanted it. He wanted it badly. He had his mouth half open on *how do they pick a place* before he got hold of himself.

"No," said Dessa.

"Think about it before you —"

"I have thought about it. I thought about it this morning at first light. I told Senna you'd offer us something good, and she said you would, and that it would be a real thing and not a bribe." Dessa's chin came up half an inch. "It's a better offer than anything I've ever been made. And it's the same move."

"Explain that."

"It's the road," she said. "Everything you've ever given any of us is the road. Ground and spacing and where the mass is and what three men across a gap are for. You give us the road when we ask you anything else, and we take it, every time, because it's always worth having." She did not raise her voice one degree. "We're not asking about the road tonight."

Rook was quiet.

"And you'd turn that down," he said.

"I'd turn it down tonight. I'd like it tomorrow, please, all of it, and I'll write it on the slate."

Milo laughed, once, out of pure nerves, and clapped his hand over his mouth.

---

The third one was ugly. It was over in about fifteen seconds, and Toren was the one it happened to.

"Why do you want it?" Rook said.

He said it to Toren specifically, across the fire, and there was something in his voice that had not been on this road at any point in twenty-eight days.

"What?"

"You. Not her. She's told me what she wants and I believe her. What do *you* want?" He did not blink. "You've been counting me for a month. My watches, my noes, four seconds at a ford yesterday — and you think I didn't see you do it. So tell me what you're collecting."

"I'm not —"

"You want a story," Rook said.

The fire popped.

"You want the thing at the end of the counting. You want it to come out as a shape, with a beginning in it, because that's what you do with everything that frightens you — you put it in numbers and you make it hold still." His voice had gone flat in the wrong way, hard and level and fast. "And I haven't got a story. I've got a list of names in an order. You want me to hand you a list of names so that you can find out what kind of a shape it makes. It doesn't make one. It never —"

"Don't," said Milo.

That was all. He said it in a small voice from beside Dessa, and he did not even look up when he said it.

Rook stopped.

Toren sat on the other side of a fire with his face burning and his ears full of blood and discovered that the worst part of it was that about a third of it was true.

Nobody spoke for a while.

"That was beneath me," Rook said. "I'd take it back if there were any way of doing that. There isn't, and I'm not going to dress it up. I'm sorry."

"It's all right."

"It is not all right and you've just done the thing I told Wyck I'd think less of him for." Rook put his hand over his eyes for a second, the way he had in the dark the night before. "Ask me your question. You're owed one now and it's a poor exchange."

---

Toren stood up.

He had not planned it. He had planned about nine things over the course of the afternoon and none of them was this. What he did was put his hand inside his shirt and pull the cord over his head.

He heard Dessa's breath go in.

They had never seen it. Twenty-eight days and none of them had ever seen it, because a man had told him at a fire on a drove road not to take it out in a yard or at a gate or in front of anybody, and because before that his father had told him the same thing on a dark road with his arms folded.

He crouched down and put it on the flat stone at the edge of the fire, where the light got at it.

Nine fine channels running in from the rim, stopping a hair short of the middle. A small ring of nothing. A leather cord somebody had knotted through it in a house, at a table, in the light, four weeks and a lifetime ago.

"Toren," Rook said. "Put that away."

"No."

"Put it away."

"My father gave me that at my Kindling," Toren said. "On a road at night, an hour after he put an Ember on my arm. He told me it's been in my family five generations. He told me it will save the world or end it, and that nobody has ever known which. His grandmother called it a curse in a gift's coat."

His voice was not steady and he went on anyway.

"I haven't told one person on this road. Not Dessa. Not Milo. Not my *grandmother.*" He heard Senna make a small sound in the cart and did not look at her. "I've carried it under my shirt for twenty-eight days and I've taken it out twice. One of those times you stopped walking in the middle of a stride and counted the channels."

Rook did not say anything.

"So there it is," Toren said. "That's mine. It's the whole of what I've got. I've put it on a stone in front of everybody, and I'm not asking you to tell me what it is." He straightened up. "I'm telling you I'll go first."

---

Then Wyck said the thing that ended it.

He said it quietly and without any particular weight, in the way of a man who has had a sentence ready for some time and does not need it to be loud.

"Eleven days ago you sat down on a coping stone," Wyck said, "and you told me you were wrong."

Nothing moved.

"You said you'd had a way of doing things for eleven years. You said a thing a person finds is worth four of a thing they're handed, and that you'd taught everybody you'd ever taught that way and had never once had cause to think it was wrong." Wyck's hands were flat on the blanket over his knees. "And then you said you'd been wrong, and that it had cost me a week on a cart and a scar I'll have when I'm sixty, and that I didn't have to say it was all right."

He stopped.

"And I said it wasn't all right. And you said no."

Rook was completely still.

"That's the whole of it," Wyck said. "You knew a thing I needed and you kept it, because you'd decided it'd be better for me to find it myself. And you were wrong. You said so out loud in front of the wind. And eleven days later you are doing exactly the same thing to all five of us at once, only bigger."

The fire went down and nobody built it up.

"That's not fair," Rook said at last.

"No," Wyck agreed. "It's true, though."

---

Senna said the last of it, from the cart, in the voice she used for reading out the weather.

"I want to say one thing and then I'd like to lie down, because I've been sitting up for two hours and I'm going to pay for it in the morning."

"Senna —" said Toren.

"Hush, love." She got her hands settled in her lap. "Young man. I don't know your age and I'd put you at thirty-one or thereabouts. You have kept my family alive for four weeks. I have said thank you to you exactly once, and you left the room."

"You don't need to —"

"I'm not doing that. I'm doing a different thing." She looked at him across the fire. "I am not going to get to the end of this road."

Toren's whole chest went hollow.

"Senna."

"Be quiet, love. I'm not being brave at anybody. It's arithmetic and I've done it twice." She did not look away from Rook. "I am going to Meridian because there's a chance at Meridian, and because the five of you have decided to carry me to it. I am grateful past saying. I am also seventy-three years old, and I know exactly what my own chest sounds like at three in the morning.

"So here's my thing." Her voice did not change at all. "I have spent twenty-eight days lying in a cart looking at the back of your head. It is the last thing I am going to be looking at, in all probability, near enough the end. And I would like to know what I've been looking at."

Nobody breathed.

"That's all," Senna said. "It's not a claim and you don't owe me it. It's just that I've got the shortest road of anybody at this fire, and I'd rather not go down it with a stranger."

---

Rook sat on a flat stone in a ruined steading with his hands open on his knees for a very long time.

Toren started to put a number on it, because he could not help it, and then stopped halfway through and let the rest of it happen without one, which was the first time in his life he had ever managed that.

Then Rook leaned forward and picked the disk up off the stone.

He did it without asking and nobody stopped him. He turned it over once, in the firelight, with his thumb along the rim, and his lips moved slightly without any sound coming out.

He put it back down where it had been.

"Put that under your shirt," he said, "and don't take it out again until I've finished talking, because I want to say a thing about it and I'd rather not be looking at it."

Toren picked it up. The metal was warm from the stone and it had a small hard weight to it that was the same weight it had had on a dark road four weeks ago.

"All right," Rook said.

---

"Tonight. Here. Once."

He stood up.

"And I'll have some conditions. They're not negotiable, and you can decide afterward whether you still want it." He set them out on his fingers, flatly, the way he read out a bill. "One. All five of you, together, in one go. I'm not doing it in pieces to people who come and find me afterward.

"Two. Nobody asks me about it tomorrow. Not tomorrow, not the day after. When I'm finished, I'm finished. The morning after this is an ordinary morning, and we do the stop-and-close before we move.

"Three." He stopped. "There's a thing in it I'm not going to give you. There's exactly one, and I'm telling you now that it exists so that none of you spends the evening hunting for the hole. When I come to it I'll say *that's the one*, and then I'll go on."

"That's fair," said Dessa.

"It isn't, particularly. It's what's available." He put the flask cord off his shoulder and set it against the wall. "And there's a fourth thing. It's a cost, and I want somebody to argue with me about it."

"Go on."

"Build the fire up."

Nobody moved.

"Properly," Rook said. "All of it. Break the crate, take the dry stuff out of the byre roof, and put the lot on. I want a fire you could see from three miles off."

Toren was on his feet. "You've said it forty times. You said everything in the dark knows where you're standing —"

"I did say that. It's true, and it hasn't stopped being true." Rook looked round at all of them. His face, in what was left of the light, did not have anything on it Toren could read. "But say it back to me properly. It's not the fire that's the beacon. It never was."

Toren opened his mouth and shut it again.

Milo got there first.

"It's *you*," he said.

"It's me. Same as a light in a palm, only bigger and harder to switch off, and I have been standing on this hill since six o'clock." Rook picked up a piece of broken crate and turned it over in his hands. "Everything within a mile of here that hunts worked power has known exactly where I am and what I am for two hours. It knew it at the ford. It knew it on the ridge lane. It has known it every night for eleven years. A fire doesn't add one thing to me."

"Then what's the cost?"

"It adds something to *you.*" He said it flatly, as information. "Five people at a fire you can see from three miles off are five people who are visible for exactly as long as I am standing next to them. Tonight that's a trade I'm making on your behalf, without asking, which is the thing I told you at a stone heap I'd stopped doing. So I'm asking. Somebody argue."

"No watch?" said Wyck.

"No watch. Nobody on that gable. Nobody at the edge of the light with their back to us, and nobody sitting there getting half of it and pretending that's the same." Rook put the wood down. "I'll be the watch. I have been the watch since before any of you were born. I can do it and talk at the same time, and it is the one thing I have ever been genuinely good at."

Wyck thought about it for a while.

"All right," he said.

"Dessa."

"Yes."

"Say it properly. You're the one who'll be sorry if I'm wrong."

Dessa Cole sat on the ground with her bad leg out straight and looked at the man for a good four seconds.

"Yes," she said. "And I'd like it on the record that I'd rather have a fire I can see faces by, and that if that's a stupid reason it's the reason anyway."

"It's noted," said Rook.

And they built the fire up.
