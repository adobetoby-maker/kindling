# Chapter 21 — In Order

The water can had four inches in it at midnight and Dessa measured it with the nail and wrote the figure on the slate and put a ring round it.

"Say it out loud," said Rook.

"Four inches. Six people." She did not look up. "If nobody drinks anything at all, that's tomorrow. If Senna drinks what Senna needs to drink, that's tomorrow morning."

"And there's a whole country of water out there," said Milo.

"There is."

"How bad is it, actually? Actually, I mean. Not the version for children."

"It has had a drowned country standing in it for seventy years," said Rook, "and there's a farm four hundred yards west of us with the water halfway up its doorways and everything that was ever alive in it still in there. You would be ill by tomorrow night and you would be very ill by the day after, and then we'd have a sick woman and a sick boy and no road."

"Right," said Milo. "Thank you. I'd rather have it."

---

Toren sat against the inside of the engine house wall with his shoulder strapped and listened to the whole of the rest of it and did not say one word, because his arm hurt and because he had killed a man that evening and had not yet found anywhere to put it.

The inside of the engine house was forty feet by twenty with no roof on it. There had been floors once. The joist sockets went round the walls in two rows, and there was nothing in the sockets and never would be again. The bottom of the place was full of brick rubble, nettles, and one enormous iron thing lying on its side that had been the bottom of a beam or the top of a pump and was now a shape.

You could see the sky through the window holes. That was the worst of it. Three storeys of wall and no cover at all above eight feet.

"Say the plan," said Wyck, from the doorway.

"I've got one and it's bad," said Rook.

"Say it anyway."

---

"Right," said Rook. "The arch, in order.

"One. That wall is a place, not a thing. It is not a lid over eleven feet of road. It is the shape one woman is holding in front of her own two hands, and it is exactly as wide as she can reach and exactly as far forward as she can put it. Marta Faust could make one in front of her and she could hold it and she could make it smaller, and she could not take one single step in any direction without it coming down. That is not a weakness in that woman. That is what the thing is, everywhere, for everybody who has ever done it, including me."

"So it moves if she moves," said Dessa.

"It ends if she moves. Not moves. Ends." Rook put his hand flat in the air and took it away. "And she knows that better than I do, which is why she has been standing on the crown of that arch since eight o'clock this evening and why she will still be standing there at dawn. There is nothing on this bank that can make her want to be somewhere else."

"And two?"

"Two is that the dark does not help us and I would like all of you to stop hoping that it does."

---

"It's dark," said Milo.

"It's dark for us. She doesn't need to see. She needs to stand still in a place she has already measured, and she measured it five days ago in daylight." Rook went on. "The man with the bow does not need to see either, because he is not shooting at anything. He is shooting at eleven feet of road, and anything on that road tonight is us.

"And we cannot move the cart. Not three hundred and eighty yards of a bank fourteen feet high with an edge on both sides, in the true dark, with a woman in it who cannot be thrown out of it. Dessa can give us ten feet."

"Fifteen," said Dessa.

"Fifteen. And fifteen feet of clear round the cart puts an old woman and four children inside a circle that they can see and we can't see out of. It is worse than nothing." He shook his head once. "A clear thing is not a light. That's the whole difference between what you've got and what Milo's got, and tonight it is the difference that matters, and it is the wrong way round."

---

"So what's the bad plan?" said Wyck.

"The bad plan is that I go up there on my own at about three in the morning and I take her off that arch."

Nobody said anything.

"I can do it. I want that said plainly and not modestly, because modesty would be a lie and this is not the night for one." Rook's voice did not change at all. "She is very good and I can do it. There are four people round her and I can do that too, and I have done worse than four, and the arch is eleven feet wide which makes it a bad place for four people to stand and only a slightly bad place for one.

"And afterward I will be worth nothing."

"Say what nothing means," said Dessa.

"It means a man walking at four miles an hour who cannot put an edge out and cannot shut a road and cannot put his hands on Senna Voss at the halt. For two days. Possibly three." He looked at the doorway. "There are six more miles of this bank and then seventy miles of country I have never seen, and a company on the road behind us with a book in it that says exactly where everybody is."

---

Toren did the sums in the dark with his back on brick and did not like any of them.

Three hundred and eighty yards. Four inches of water. A woman on a crown of brick who did not have to do anything at all. Two days of a man worth nothing.

"There's another one," said Milo.

He said it the way he said everything, straight up, at the worst possible moment, out of a silence that had been going on for about a minute and a half.

"Go on," said Rook.

"You said nothing on this bank can make her want to be somewhere else."

"I did."

"That's not the same as nothing making her *look.*"

---

The nettles moved against the wall. Out east, four miles away, something went across the water and made a small sound and stopped.

"No," said Rook.

"I haven't said it."

"You've said it. You said it at a stone heap sixteen days ago and I crossed eleven feet of road and went down on one knee in front of you and told you no, and I will give you the same answer in the same words tonight, because it is still true." He did not raise his voice one degree. "Nobody in this group is bait. If I ever decide otherwise I will say it out loud to your face beforehand and you can tell me what you think of it. I have not decided otherwise. I am not going to."

"All right," said Milo.

"Say it back."

"Nobody in this group is bait." Milo's voice was perfectly even. "I've had it word for word since the stone heap. I'm not going to forget it."

"Good."

---

Here is what Toren thought about for the rest of his life.

Milo said *all right* and he meant it, because Milo Faust had never in fourteen years said *all right* about a thing he did not mean. He agreed with every word of it. He agreed with it completely.

And what Rook had said was that *he* would not decide it.

Milo went and did the water. There was no water to do. He went and stood over the can with the lid off and looked into four inches of it for about a minute. Then he put the lid back on. He came and sat down against the wall in the dark next to Toren, and put both hands round his knees.

"Toren."

"Mm."

"What's it like?"

"What's what like?"

"Having one." Milo said it without any weight on it at all. "A word. You've got Ember. Dessa's got Ember. Wyck's had it since the cut and didn't know. I'm the only one on the bottom rung and nobody's said so for four weeks, which I think is the kindest anybody's ever been to me and I'd quite like it to stop."

Toren's throat went.

"Milo —"

"Don't. I'm not being sad about it, I'm asking a question." He turned his head. "Is it different? Actually different, inside? Or is it just a word somebody says at you?"

Toren sat in the dark in a ruined engine house and thought about it properly, because Milo deserved the true answer and not the kind one.

"It's different," he said. "It stopped being a thing that was happening and started being a thing that was. That's the closest I can get."

"Right," said Milo. "Thank you."

He put his chin on his knees.

"Mine's always been a thing that is," he said.

---

He did the bread at about two in the morning.

There was very little of it. It had gone hard and it was the end of the Wenn's Ford loaf, and he cut it on his knee with the small knife in the dark, poorly, the way he always did.

He did it in the order.

Senna first. She was awake. He put it in her hand and said her name, and she said, "Thank you, love," and held on to his fingers for a second longer than she needed to, and let go.

Then Rook. Rook was on the threshold with his back against the jamb, and Milo went and stood at six feet and said his name out loud from there, which is the rule and has been the rule since before any of them were born, and waited to be told to come in.

"Come on, then," said Rook.

Milo came in and put it in his hand and said his name again, properly, the way he did.

Then Wyck, on the other side of the doorway. Then Dessa, on the rubble with her leg out straight. Then Toren.

Then himself, last, sitting down against the wall, with the end crust and whatever the knife had ruined.

---

Toren had a sentence.

He had been carrying it for thirty-four days and it was nine words long and it had been given to him on a shale fan above a rock cut by a woman who was dying, and he had not been able to find a single hour in thirty-four days that was the right hour for it.

He got as far as "Milo."

"Mm."

And then the whole of it turned over in his chest, and what came out instead was:

"You did a good thing today. With the stone."

"I know," said Milo. "That's the part I keep having to sit down about."

---

Rook went at ten past three and he went alone, and the last thing he said in that doorway was to Wyck.

"You do not come off this threshold. Not for me. Not if you can hear me. You will be able to hear me and it will be the hardest thing you have ever done in your life, and if you come off that stone there is an old woman in a cart with nobody in front of her."

"I know."

"Say it."

"I don't come off the threshold," said Wyck.

And then he was gone up the road, and there was no sound of him going at all, which Toren had stopped finding strange about three weeks previously and started finding strange again that night.

---

Toren got up onto the low wall at the east side of the platform where he could see north. Dessa came and got up beside him. Neither of them said anything for eleven minutes.

The bank went away into the dark and stopped being anything after about forty yards. The sky had cleared a little. There were stars in the water, which Toren had not expected and which he never got over.

"He won't do it," said Dessa.

"He said he could."

"He said he could when he was full. He's at a third and he's been at a third for four days and he hasn't slept since before the rock cut." Dessa's voice was flat. "And he told us the plan was bad, in those words, twice. He's never said that about a plan in thirty-three days."

"Then why's he doing it?"

"Because it's the only one," said Dessa. "That's what a bad plan is."

---

Milo came up on the wall beside them at about half past three.

He did not say anything. He sat on the coping with his boots hanging down the outside and his hands flat on the stone either side of him. He looked north up the bank. After a while he looked west, out along the sluice wall.

Toren looked where he was looking and saw nothing, because there was nothing to see. Eighty yards of stone going out into black. The three gate frames were three slightly different blacks. The end of it was not visible and had not been visible since eight in the evening.

"How far out does it go?" said Milo.

"Eighty yards."

"And how far is that from the arch?"

Toren did the sums without meaning to, because the sums were the handrail, and because he had walked the platform twice and had paced the road that afternoon.

"The end of the wall to the crown of the arch," he said. "Call it a hundred and ten yards. A bit more."

"Right," said Milo.

---

Three things happened at nineteen minutes to four and Toren could never afterward put them in an order he trusted.

Somebody shouted, a long way up the bank, and it was not Rook.

There was a noise from the arch that was not a noise so much as a change, the way a room changes when a door opens in another part of the house.

And Milo Faust stood up on the coping of a low wall in the dark and said, in a completely ordinary voice, at conversational volume, to nobody in particular:

"I'm doing the light. I'm telling you first."

---

He was off the wall and going before Toren's feet were under him.

Not running. That was the part Toren could never get past. Milo did not run one single step of it. He went across the platform and out onto the sluice wall at a fast walk, with his arms slightly out for the three-foot width of it, the way a boy goes along the top of a garden wall on an ordinary evening.

"*MILO —*"

"*Don't follow me,*" said Milo, without turning round, "*because then there's two of us on it.*"

He was ten yards out.

Then twenty.

Toren got to the end of the platform and got one boot up on the stone and Dessa's whole weight came onto his arm from behind, on the strapped shoulder, and the pain of it put him on his knees.

"*Dessa!*"

"He's right," said Dessa Cole.

Her voice was not steady and her hand did not move.

"He's right, Toren. Two of you is two."

---

At forty yards Milo Faust stopped, and turned round, and looked back at the platform for about a second and a half.

Then he turned north and put his right hand up, palm open, and made a light.

---

It came up off his palm about the size of an apple. It was white with a blue edge to it, and it was cold. It did not flicker at all.

It lit him from below, poorly, the way it always did.

It lit about eight feet of stone wall under his boots and the black water on either side, and out past that it lit nothing whatsoever, because there was nothing out there for it to reach.

And from the crown of a brick arch, a hundred and ten yards away, across four miles of flat dead water at nineteen minutes to four in the morning, it was the only lit thing in the world.

---

Toren was on his knees on a stone platform and he heard the whole arch turn.

That was what it sounded like. Not a shout. A *turn* — boots on brick, all at once, six or seven people moving their feet at the same instant in the same direction, the sound a congregation makes when somebody comes in late.

Somebody up there said a word with a question in it.

Somebody else said, "*West* — on the *wall* —"

And Dessa said, in a voice Toren had never heard out of her and never heard again:

"She's come off it."

"What?"

"The woman on the crown. She's turned round." Dessa's hand was on his shoulder and her fingers had gone right into the strapping. "She's turned round to look and she's put her hands down and *she's come off the wall.*"

---

Out on the stone, forty yards over black water, a fourteen-year-old boy stood in the dark with his arm up and a cold white light in his palm and did not move at all.

And two hundred yards north up the bank a man who had been going up that road in the dark for half an hour, without a sound, at a third of the room, found eleven feet of open brick in front of him where a wall had been.

---

Toren counted it.

He did not decide to. He was on his knees on the edge of a brick platform with a girl's hand in the strapping on his shoulder, and the counting started the way it always started, which is to say without being asked.

He got to eleven and Milo had not moved.

He got to thirty and Milo had not moved.

At about forty the light went up.

Not brighter. Higher. Milo put his arm up straight above his head, the whole length of it, the way a person holds a lamp out of a doorway for somebody coming up a path, and the cold white thing sat in his open palm eight feet above eighty yards of stone and did not flicker.

Toren heard somebody on the arch swear.

"What's he doing," he said. "Dessa, what is he *doing* —"

"He's making sure," said Dessa.

---

*Here is what it costs to stand still.*

There is a thing that happens to a person's body when it is being looked at, and Toren found out about it on his knees on a brick platform at nineteen minutes to four in the morning. It is not fear. Fear is a thing that moves you. This was the opposite of that. It went into him at forty yards' distance from somebody else. It was the single most difficult thing he had ever watched anybody do.

Milo Faust stood on three feet of stone over black water with his arm straight up and a light in his hand, in the open, with nothing in front of him and nothing behind him and nothing on either side.

He did not shift his feet.

He did not put the arm down and change hands.

He did not look back at the platform, and Toren understood a long time afterward that this was deliberate, and that it was the most careful thing in the whole of it — because a boy looking back over his shoulder is a boy who might be about to do something, and a boy standing still with his arm up is a thing you have to keep watching.

He stood there for a minute and eleven seconds.

Toren had the number. He had it for the rest of his life, and he was never once able to decide whether having it was a mercy or the other thing.

---

The cart went through the arch at about eighteen minutes to four.

Toren did not see it. He heard it — the hard iron rattle of a hand cart going over brick at a speed a hand cart is not built for, and a woman's voice shouting one word over and over, and then nothing.

Wyck told him afterward, and told it once, and never again.

"He came back down for us at a dead run and he did not say anything at all. He just took the off shaft out of my hands and put it in Senna's and then he had the cart. I don't know how he did the hump. I was at the tailgate with the bar and I was doing the backward job and I was saying what I could see out loud even though it was nothing, because that's the job, and there was nobody behind us. There was nobody behind us the whole way."

"Because they were looking west," said Toren.

"Because they were looking west," said Wyck.

---

Toren got to his feet on the platform and got one boot on the sluice wall and Dessa took her hand off his shoulder, because the cart was through and there was no longer any arithmetic in it.

He got twelve yards out.

Then, from a long way north, across a great deal of flat water, there came a sound like somebody dropping a plank on a floor two rooms away.

And the light went out.

---

It did not gutter and it did not shrink. It was there and then it was not there, the way it went when Milo shut his hand, and for about a second and a half Toren's whole body believed that was what had happened.

Then he understood what the sound had been.

Then the dark came down on eighty yards of stone.

It was total. Toren stopped where he was, because his feet stopped, on three feet of wall with four feet of black water on either side.

He could not see his own hands. He could not see forty yards. He could not see one single thing anywhere in the world except stars in flat water.

"*MILO.*"

Nothing.

"*MILO!*"

He went forward. He went forward on his hands and knees, because standing up had stopped being available, and he went twenty yards like that with his palms flat on wet stone and his shoulder screaming and his face six inches off the wall.

He heard somebody coming out behind him.

Step. Drag. Stop.

Step. Drag. Stop.

---

*Here is what the difference is, and nobody had ever needed it to be true before that night.*

A light is a thing you make. It goes out from you and it lands on things and it tells everybody where you are standing.

What Dessa Cole did on eighty yards of stone in the dark at a quarter to four in the morning was not that.

Toren's ears popped.

And the dark got thinner.

---

It did not get brighter. He wanted, for years afterward, to be able to say it got brighter, because that is a thing people understand, and it would have been a lie.

The wet on the stone got easier to see. Then the joint lines between the blocks. Then the weed in the joints, and the edge of the wall, and the black water lying flat eighteen inches below it with not one thing moving on it.

It came out to about fifteen feet, all at once, everywhere, and it was entirely steady.

And in the middle of it, forty yards out from a platform on the only road across eleven miles of drowned country, Milo Faust was lying on his side on three feet of stone with one arm out in front of him and his hand open.

---

Toren got to him first.

He was breathing. That was the first thing and Toren had it in his hands like something he had been given and was not going to be allowed to keep. He was breathing fast and shallow and wrong, and he was very cold already, which Toren did not understand and which was the worst of all the things in that night.

There was a crossbow bolt through him below the collarbone on the left side.

Toren did not look at it twice. He put his good hand where Rook had taught him to put his hands eleven days ago in a dry canal, flat, either side of it. He did not move it. He did not look at what was underneath. He started shouting.

Dessa came the last ten yards on her knees.

She did not say anything at all. She lay down on the wall on Milo's other side, full length, with her face about a foot from his. Then she held the dark thin. She held it for eleven minutes and she did not let it slip once.

---

Rook came out that wall in eight seconds.

Toren heard him hit the stone at the platform end and heard nothing after that until he was there, on one knee, on three feet of wet stone in the middle of the water, with both hands already going.

"Milo."

"He's —"

"Quiet." Not unkindly. Not kindly either. "Both your hands where they are. Don't move them. Dessa, hold that exactly where it is and do not move one inch closer."

"I've got it."

"I know you have."

He put his hands on.

---

Eleven seconds.

That was how long it took, every time, in a rock cut and on a towpath and in a dry canal. Eleven seconds of a man kneeling with his hands on somebody and absolutely nothing happening. Then a small hard pressure change. Then Toren's ears would pop. Then it would be all right.

Toren counted.

He got to eleven.

He got to twenty.

He got to forty. His ears did not pop.

And he understood.

---

"Rook."

"Wait."

"Rook, your ears — my ears haven't —"

"*Wait.*"

The water lay flat. Dessa did not move. Somewhere four hundred yards north a cart stood on a road with an old woman in it and a boy in front of it with an iron bar, and there was no sound from up there at all.

Rook took his hands off.

He did it the way a man puts down something he has been carrying a long way. He sat back on his heels on three feet of stone in the middle of eleven miles of black water and he looked at his own two hands, palms up, in the clear that Dessa Cole was holding around all four of them.

"No," he said.

---

"Do it again," said Toren.

"Toren."

"You did it for Wyck. You did it for my grandmother, you did it for her every night for a *fortnight* —"

"I have a third of the room and I have had a third of the room for four days and I spent most of it going up that road." Rook's voice did not change at all and that was the worst thing that happened on the Level. "And it would not be enough at full. I want you to have that and I want you to have it now, tonight, from me, while you can still hear it. It would not be enough at full. I can hold a door shut. I have never been able to build the wall."

"Then —"

"There isn't a then," said Rook.

---

Milo opened his eyes.

It took him some time to find anybody. When he did it was Rook, which Toren thought about afterward and was glad of and never once resented.

"Did they get through?"

"They got through."

"All of them?"

"All of them. Cart, woman, Wyck on the tailgate doing the backward job." Rook had hold of his hand. Toren had never seen him take anybody's hand in his life. "A hundred and fifty yards clear on the north side and nobody behind them. It worked, Milo. It worked exactly the way you had it."

"Good," said Milo. "That's good."

He shut his eyes and opened them again.

"I told you first," he said.

"You told me first."

"That was the rule. I checked it about nine times. I didn't want — " His breath caught and came back. "I didn't want anyone saying afterward that I'd broken it."

"Nobody is ever going to say that," said Rook. "There is not one person alive who could."

---

"I'm no good at this," Rook said.

Toren heard it start and knew what it was about a half second in, because he had been nine feet away on the worst morning of his life at the south mouth of a rock cut with a hundred and forty yards of scree behind him and thirty-one trips in his legs.

"I'm no good at this. It isn't going to sound better in a —"

He stopped.

He had been saying it into Milo's face and he stopped in the middle of the word and he did not start again.

Toren had watched that man say hard things for thirty-four days. He had watched him say *your father is dead and I was late* to a boy on the ground, and *she will not get to the end of this road* to that boy's grandmother, and *forty-nine people out of sixty* to a fire. He had never once seen a sentence stop in him.

Rook put his forehead down for a second against the back of Milo's hand and then lifted it again. When he spoke, his voice had gone back to exactly what it had been. Toren understood that this was going to cost him something for the rest of his life.

"Say it for me," Rook said. "Somebody."

---

"The order's wrong," said Milo.

"What?"

"The bread." He was somewhere else and coming back. "I've been doing it wrong for weeks and I worked it out about three days ago and I kept not saying it because it's daft."

"Say it," said Dessa, lying a foot from his face with the dark held thin all round them.

"Oldest first. That's how I've done it. Senna, then him, then Wyck, then Dessa, then Toren, then me." Milo's hand moved in Rook's. "That's wrong. He's the one who's up longest. He's up all of it, every night, all the way through, and if you give him his at the start he's had it and there's nothing left for the end."

Nobody said anything.

"So it's him last," said Milo. "Not me. Him. Senna, Wyck, Dessa, Toren, me — and him last, and he's to actually eat it, and somebody's got to make him, because he won't."

"All right," said Dessa.

"Say it back so I know it's had."

"Senna, Wyck, Dessa, Toren, Milo," said Dessa Cole, in exactly the voice she used for reading out the number of crows in an elm. "And him last."

"That's it," said Milo. "That's right now."

---

Toren said it then, because there was not going to be another hour, and because he had been carrying it for thirty-four days and had been wrong about every single one of them.

"Milo. Your mother said a thing."

Milo's eyes came round.

"On the fan. At the top of the cut, after. I was the last one there and she was talking and most of it was about the wheel." Toren's own voice had gone somewhere he could not reach it. "And the last thing she said was *tell him eyes forward.*"

It went into him. Toren watched it go in.

"She didn't."

"She did."

"She *always* said that." Milo's face came apart and went back together. "She said it at me about four hundred times. She said it when I was being — when I was going on about something, or looking at a thing I'd done wrong, she'd put her hand on the back of my neck and she'd say —"

"Eyes forward, love," said Toren.

"*Eyes forward, love.*"

He laughed. It was a small wet laugh and it went wrong at the end of it, the way his laughs always did.

"Thirty-four days, Toren."

"I know. I know. I couldn't find —"

"It's all right," said Milo Faust. "You've found it."

---

He went at about eleven minutes past four.

There was no moment in it. Toren had expected a moment and there was not one.

The hand in Rook's stopped doing the small thing it had been doing. The breathing got further apart. Then there was a gap that was slightly too long.

Then that gap did not end.

Dessa held the clear for four minutes afterward. Nobody asked her to and nobody told her to stop.

Then she put her face down on the wet stone next to his. The clear went out. The dark came back over eighty yards of sluice wall and four miles of drowned country and the whole of the rest of it.

---

Rook stood up.

He did it slowly and he did it in the dark, and Toren, kneeling on stone with both hands still where they had been put, felt rather than saw him go up.

"Dessa."

Her voice came up off the stone. "Yes."

"Can you get him back along that wall between the two of you?"

"Yes."

"Do it now and do it slowly and do not either of you stand up until you're on the platform." A pause. "Toren. Your shoulder's out again and I have not got anything to do about it. Use the good one and use your knees."

"Where are you going?"

There was a long silence on the sluice wall.

"North," said Rook.

---

Toren got up onto his knees.

"Rook —"

"There is a man on the north end of that arch," said Rook, "who has been standing on it since eight o'clock this evening with a bow, and who did not shoot at the cart when it went under him, and who did not shoot at me when I came back down the road, and who has not, as far as I can tell, moved off that spot all night.

"He shot at a light.

"He put out a light on a wall in the dark at a hundred and ten yards over water with one bolt, first time, from a standing start, because it was lit and it was moving away from the road and it was not accounted for."

Toren could not see one single part of him.

"And there is exactly one job in a crew of nine," said Rook, "that a man like that is for. I have been asking the wrong question about it for six years and I have taken it back every single time, in a yard at Coldharbour and on a hardpan at Stell Cross and in an old man's garden two days ago, because it is not a question you are allowed to ask about people who are already dead.

"I am going to go and ask it now."

---

He went off the sluice wall and up the bank at a run, and it was the first time in thirty-four days that Toren Voss had ever heard that man's boots on a road.
