# Chapter 4 — After

The ground at the south mouth was not rock, but it was not much better than rock, and by the third foot they were hitting shale and by the fourth they stopped pretending.

So it was cairns.

They carried the stones from the scree at the foot of the west wall, two hands at a time, a hundred and forty yards each way. Toren made thirty-one trips. He knew the number exactly, and after the eleventh trip the number was the only thing keeping his legs going.

Nobody organized it. That was the strange part. The man went down to the scree, came back with two stones, and put them down. Then Dessa went. Then Wyck. Then Toren. After a while Milo came down off the bank and joined the line without looking at anybody, and that was the entire discussion.

They built three.

Garrick Voss, forty-four years old, of Sallow Creek.

Marta Faust, who had mended everybody.

And a gray mule, because Milo said in a small flat voice that he was not going to leave him lying in the traces. Nobody argued. It took four extra trips.

---

Senna sat against the bank with the blanket around her and gave directions about the stones, which was the only work anyone was going to let her do and which she did with total seriousness.

"Wider at the bottom," she said. "That's a cairn, not a chimney. Wider at the bottom or it's down by the first frost."

"Yes, Grandmother."

"Your father built one for your grandfather that stood eleven years and it was wide at the *bottom.*"

"Yes, Grandmother."

She stopped. Toren, kneeling with a stone in both hands, felt her stop, and did not look up, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"Wider at the bottom," Senna said again, in a completely different voice.

"I know."

"I know you know."

---

The man worked with them and did not talk. When the three cairns were done he went back to his pack, and Toren assumed that was that, and began to think about whether there were words and whose job it was to say them.

Then the man came back with a piece of bread.

It was hard bread, the kind that travels, dark and dense and about the size of a fist. He broke it in half. He put one half back inside his coat. Then he crouched at the head of Marta's cairn and set the other half on a flat stone there, square in the middle of it, and adjusted it slightly so it sat level.

Milo watched him do it. "What's that for?"

The man did not answer that.

Instead he stayed crouched, with his forearms on his knees, and looked at the stones for a while, and then he said:

"I didn't know her."

Nobody moved.

"She held a road for six minutes so five people could get up a hill. She knew the whole time she wasn't going to be able to walk off it afterward. That's not a guess. She'd have known the geometry of it better than I do." He turned the bread a quarter turn on the stone, which made no difference to anything. "That's the entire sum of what I know about her, and I'd say it's plenty. I'd say most people go the whole way and don't leave that much behind them."

He stood up.

"I'm no good at this," he said. "It isn't going to sound better in a year."

Then he walked off down the road toward the cut, and left them there, and Milo Faust put his hands over his face and finally, four hours late, made a sound.

---

They found out what he was doing about twenty minutes later, and it was Wyck who saw it first, because Wyck had gone to the cut to be away from people and had come straight back.

"He's collecting it," Wyck said.

"Collecting what?"

"Them."

The man was halfway up the cut with a horn scoop in one hand and a leather flask braced against his hip, going along the west gutter where the ash had drifted deepest, working it in short strokes like a man sweeping out a stable. He had two more flasks on a cord over his shoulder. One of them was already full.

Toren walked up the road until he was ten feet away and stood there and could not think of one single thing to say that was not going to come out poorly.

The man did not stop working. "Say it."

"My father's up that road."

"Your father's under a pile of rocks at the south mouth with a boy's thirty-one trips on top of him. I counted." The scoop went into the drift, came up, tipped. "What's on the road here isn't your father and it isn't anybody. It came out of a hole in the world about four hours ago with nothing inside it and it went back to being nothing the minute somebody interrupted it."

"You're doing it *now.*"

"In two days this'll be off the road and up in the scrub and gone. In two days it's worth nothing at all." He moved four feet up the gutter. "Today it's worth about nine weeks of what your grandmother's going to need at the far end of this road, and I am not going to stand in a gutter and pretend otherwise so that a thirteen-year-old can have a better afternoon."

Toren's hands were shaking. He put them under his arms.

"Is that why you came?"

The man stopped.

He straightened up and looked at Toren properly for the first time, and Toren discovered that he did not much care for being looked at properly by this person.

"I was on the ridge," the man said. "About half a mile that way, on the high ground, because the high ground is where you walk if you want to see a road before the road sees you. I saw ash come up out of a rock cut in a column about sixty feet high, which means something is dying in numbers down there, which means people." He picked the scoop back up. "And then I came down the fastest way there is, and I got here, and I was late. So no. That is not why I came. But I'm here, and it's on the road, and it'll be gone tomorrow."

He went back to work.

Toren stood in the ash until his legs decided for him, and then he went and sat down against the wall about thirty feet away, and stayed there, and watched.

After a while the man filled the third flask, capped it, and walked over and held it out.

"Yours," he said.

"I don't want it."

"I didn't ask you that." The man kept holding it out. "Your father put eleven of them down in a gap eight feet wide. That woman held off about forty by herself. The boy with the chain took a leg off one and the one with the bar broke six that I can read from the pattern on the ground." He pushed it into Toren's hands. "That's not mine. It was never going to be mine. Don't lose it, and don't let anybody at a gate see how much of it you've got."

---

They ate at dusk, at the south mouth, at a fire they built out of thorn scrub because there was nothing else.

Nobody had asked his name. Toren realized it at the same moment he realized nobody had said one word directly to the man for two hours, and that the man had made no attempt whatsoever to fix this.

Dessa fixed it. Of course she did.

"What do we call you?"

The man was cleaning the horn scoop with a rag. "Rook."

"Is that your name?"

"It's what I'm called."

Dessa waited. She was very good at waiting. She let it go on well past the point where most people would have filled it in, and when it became clear that this particular silence was going to last the rest of the night if she let it, she gave a small nod, as though a form had been filled in wrongly and she had decided to accept it anyway.

"I'm Dessa Cole. That's Milo Faust. Wyck Tanner. Toren Voss. Senna Voss."

"I know," Rook said. "You've all been saying each other's names at each other for four hours."

---

He laid it out after the food, flatly, the way a man reads a bill.

"Two roads. South is nine days back to where you came from." He held up one finger and then folded it down again, immediately, which told them exactly what he thought of it. "Nine days with no cart and no animal, carrying her, through the same country that just produced whatever that was in the cut. That's not a road, that's a way of dying more slowly. Forget it."

"Then —"

"North is four days to a place called Hobb's Wall."

Toren said, "That's not where we're going. We're going to Meridian."

"You're going to Hobb's Wall," Rook said. "It's on the way and it's four days instead of thirty-something, and it has a wall, which is the entire point of it."

"My grandmother needs —"

"Your grandmother needs a roof and a person who knows what they're doing, and she needs both of those things inside a week, and the only place inside a week is Hobb's Wall." Rook stood up and went to get more thorn for the fire. "You can argue with me about Meridian on the road. You'll have four days."

Wyck spoke for the first time in hours.

"Are you taking us?"

Rook came back with an armload of thorn and dropped it on the fire. The fire spat. For a second nobody could see anybody's face.

"I'm going that way," he said.

---

In the night Toren tried it.

He had waited until the fire was down and Milo had stopped crying and Wyck was on his back with his arm over his eyes. Then he lay on his side facing the stones, and pushed his sleeve up, and looked for a long time at the flat dark oval strapped to the inside of his forearm.

*One day,* he thought. *I had it for one day.*

He reached for the place under his breastbone.

He knew where it was now. That was the awful part — he could find the spot exactly, the way you can find a tooth that has come out, and there was nothing in it. Not cold. Not dead. Just a small quiet emptiness where, thirty-two hours ago, there had been a warm coin.

He reached anyway. He pulled at it with everything he had until his eyes went hot. Nothing happened. Nothing kept on not happening for a long time.

"It comes back."

Toren went rigid.

Rook was sitting eight feet away with his back against a stone. He had not been there five minutes ago. Toren had not heard one single thing.

"How long?"

"For you? Two days. Maybe three."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're thirteen and you emptied yourself out in a rock cut this morning and you've eaten since." Rook was looking at the fire, not at him. "It fills. That's what it does. It fills slowly and it empties fast, and for the rest of your life the entire question is going to be which of those two things you're currently doing."

Toren pulled his sleeve back down.

"It went out," he said. "On my father. At the end. It went out and it didn't come back and he —"

He stopped, because his throat had shut.

Rook did not say anything for a while. The fire moved.

"Yes," he said.

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"You asked me a question about a thing that happens and I told you it happens." Rook's voice had not changed at all. "If you want me to tell you it wasn't his fault, it wasn't. If you want me to tell you he made a mistake, he didn't. He made the trade every one of us makes about forty times an hour, and he made it right every single time, and at the end of it he was a man standing in front of a hill with nothing left. That's not a lesson. There isn't a lesson in it." He shifted. "That's the part people won't tell you and it's the only part that's true."

Toren lay there with his eyes stinging.

"I hate you," he said, conversationally, to the stones.

"That's all right."

"You were *late.*"

"I was."

"Say something about it."

"No," Rook said, and did not.

---

Toren woke again later, before the moon was down, because somebody was moving.

He kept still and opened his eyes a slit, the way you do when you are thirteen and something is happening that was not meant for you.

Rook had his pack on.

He stood about twenty feet out from the fire, on the road, facing north, with the straps done up and the flasks on their cord and his coat buttoned. Nothing in the way he stood suggested a man who had got up to look at the dark. He was ready to walk. He had been ready to walk for some time.

Toren did not move. He lay with his cheek on his arm and watched a man decide something.

It took a while. Longer than Toren would have guessed, if he had ever had cause to guess at anything like it. Rook stood on that road with five people behind him and the whole north in front of him, and he did not take one step in either direction for four or five minutes.

Then he turned his head a little and looked back at the fire.

At the drag, with an old woman asleep in it. At Milo, curled on his side with both hands under his chin. At the three cairns a hundred yards south, which could not be seen in that light, and which he looked at anyway.

He took the pack off.

He did it slowly. He set it down against the stone he had been sitting on, and sat down beside it, and put his forearms on his knees, and looked at the fire with an expression Toren could not read at all.

If he said anything, Toren did not hear it.

---

In the morning he showed them how to build a drag.

He did not explain it and he did not call it a lesson. He simply walked back into the cut, alone, and came out an hour later with the cart's two side-boards, the broken tongue, a length of trace-chain, and four feet of canvas off the hoop, and dropped the lot on the road in front of them.

Then he crouched and started putting it together, and looked up once, and said: "Well?"

They crowded in.

It took the five of them two hours, and about forty minutes of that was Rook letting them do it wrong. He let Wyck lash the crosspiece at the wrong height and said nothing until Milo worked out that the whole thing would ride on Senna's shoulders. He let Toren tie a knot that would have come apart inside a mile. Then he put his boot on it and pulled, once, and let Toren watch it fail.

"Again," was all he said.

By the end of it they had a drag that one person could pull and a second could steady, with the canvas slung between the two boards and the chain making a wide loop for a chest-harness. Senna sat in it. It held.

"This is undignified," Senna said.

"It is."

"I want that noted."

"It's noted," said Rook.

He set off north at a pace Toren recognized about two hours later as deliberate. It was exactly as fast as the slowest of them could manage while still being able to breathe, and not one step faster. Rook never once looked back to check whether it was working.

---

It took four days. They were the four longest days of Toren's life, and almost nothing happened in any of them.

That was the part nobody had prepared him for. He had expected the world to have changed. The world had not changed. The road went north. The sun came up and went over and went down. They walked. The drag made the same two sounds all day — a hiss on packed dirt, a rattle on stone — until the two sounds stopped meaning anything at all.

They took the harness in turns. Wyck took the longest turns and said nothing about it, split his knuckles open again on the second day getting the drag out of a wash, and would not let Dessa look at them. Dessa walked point. Rook put her there on the first morning without any discussion, kept her forty yards out in front of him for four days, and never once told her what she was looking for.

Milo talked. That was the surprise. He talked more in those four days than in the whole month before them — about his mother, about Sallow Creek, about a dog somebody had owned when he was six. Nobody asked him to stop. Rook, who plainly hated it, never said a word about it either.

Senna slept, and did not eat enough, and got quieter each day in a way Toren watched and did not mention to anybody.

On the third afternoon it came back.

He was in the harness with his head down when he felt it, and he nearly went over sideways. It was smaller than it had been — a warmth about the size of a fingernail, not a coin, low and central and unmistakably *there.* He stopped dead in the road. The drag's shafts came up against his hips, and Senna asked him what was wrong.

He put the hook out.

It came. It was thin, and it was ugly, and it lasted about two seconds. It made him feel sick afterward. It was also, without any argument at all, the single best moment of that entire terrible week.

Behind him, eighty yards back, Rook did not say anything.

But Toren was watching for it by then, and he saw the man's chin come up half an inch at exactly the moment the metal ran, and go back down again, and nothing else about him move at all.

---

On the fourth evening the road came up out of the low country and ran straight across a shallow, dry, open plain, and at the far end of it, small and gray on a rise, there was a line.

"Wall," Dessa said.

"Hobb's Wall." Rook shifted the pack on his shoulder. "About two hundred people. A factor who keeps a ledger. A yard where hunters sell what's in these flasks, and a woman who sets bones."

Toren was in the chest-harness again. His shoulders had gone past hurting into a kind of hot dullness, and he had stopped counting paces two days ago because the number had become upsetting.

"And what happens there?" he said.

Rook did not break stride.

"There's two hundred people behind that wall," he said. "One of them will take you."
