# Chapter 5 — The Waypoint

Hobb's Wall was not a wall. It was three walls, and none of them had been built to be one.

The oldest and best of them was a pre-Fall retaining bank of poured stone, sixty feet of it, put there a century and a half ago to stop a hillside coming down onto a road that no longer existed. Somebody had looked at that and had the single practical thought of their generation, and the settlement sat behind it with its back to the hill. The second wall was earth and thorn and ran east from the stone for about two hundred yards. The third was a stockade of old rail sleepers set upright, and it was newer than the other two and worse than both.

The gate was in the sleepers. It had a man on it.

"Stop there," the man said, at forty feet, before any of them had done anything. "State it."

"Ash," Rook said. "Three flasks and a bit. Off the Meridian road, four days south, in the cut."

"You went through the cut?"

"There was a party in it."

The gate man looked past Rook at the party, and Toren watched him arrive at a number and not like it. He was about fifty, with a jaw that had been broken and set poorly, and a crossbow that he did not raise and did not put down.

"How many went in?" he said.

"Seven and a mule."

Nobody said anything else. The gate man looked at the drag for a while, and at the old woman in it, and then he did something Toren did not expect, which was to lower the crossbow entirely and come down off the step and open the gate himself.

"Corb Ives," he said. "Yard's left. Quill's in the yard. Ovey's the third door past the well and she'll be asleep and she won't mind." He held the gate. "That's a bad road and it's been getting worse for a month, and I've told the factor so, and it's in the book that I told her."

---

The weigh-yard was a square of packed earth with a scale in the middle of it under a roof on four posts, and everything about it said that this was the actual reason Hobb's Wall existed.

The scale was old and enormous and cared for. Somebody had kept its beam oiled through seventy winters. Around the yard ran a low shed with bins in it, and the bins had gray in them. Over the bins, on a board, somebody had painted a set of prices and then painted them out and painted them again, four times, in four different hands.

Hesta Quill came out of the counting-house with the ledger already under her arm.

She was perhaps sixty and very upright, in a coat that had been good twenty years ago and was still brushed, and she looked at the five of them and the drag before she looked at Rook, which Toren noticed and did not understand until much later.

"Ash first," she said. "Then the rest of it. I can only do one thing at once and I'd rather do the one that has a number on the end."

She weighed it herself. She did it slowly. She let Rook watch the beam, and she said the weight out loud before she wrote it down.

"Nine and a quarter," Hesta said. "That's a good take. That's a very good take. Where?"

"The cut, four days south."

"How many?"

"I stopped counting at sixty."

The pen stopped.

"Sixty," Hesta Quill said.

"That's low. Sixty is what I'd swear to in a room." Rook capped the empty flask and put it on the cord. "They were coming out of a drain and over both banks at the same time and they were still coming when it finished."

Hesta wrote for a while. Then she blotted it, and closed the ledger on her finger, and said, in a completely different voice: "Corb has been telling me that road is turning for five weeks."

"Corb's right."

"Corb is frequently right and it is frequently inconvenient." She opened the book again. "Nine and a quarter at the board price is eleven and two. I'll give you twelve because of where it came from and because I want people to keep telling me things like that. Now. The children."

---

Here is how it went, and Toren could never afterward decide whether he had been brave or simply thirteen.

"They came off that road with him," Hesta said. "Are they his?"

"No," said Rook.

"Are they anybody's?"

"No."

"Right." Hesta Quill turned a page in the ledger, and turned another, and put her finger on a line. "Then I'll tell you what I have, and you'll tell me what you think of it, and neither of us will waste the other's afternoon.

"I have three places. Not five. Three, and I'll tell you exactly why there are three, because you're going to be unhappy about it and I'd rather you were unhappy accurately." She did not look up. "We are two hundred and four people behind a stockade made of railway sleepers. We put up eleven tons of grain this year, which is a hundred and forty short of comfortable. Every name I write in this book is a person this wall has agreed to feed through a winter, and every name I write is a person I have to justify to the other two hundred and four when the bins are low in the third month. Three is what I have. Three is not a negotiation and it is not cruelty, it is eleven tons of grain."

"Which three," Rook said.

"That's not the shape of it." Hesta put the pen down. "The yard can use one pair of hands. That's a place. Fen Roulet takes a cart east to the river settlements on the fourteenth and he's asked me twice for a boy for the winter and he'll feed him properly and I've known him thirty years — that's a place, but it's east and it leaves on the fourteenth. And there's a household at Marrow Ford, two days west, that lost a daughter in the spring and has asked me to look out for a girl. That's a place." She let it sit. "Three places. Three different roads. I cannot make them into one place by wanting to, and anybody who tells you otherwise is selling you something."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind the counting-house a goat made a noise.

"And her," Hesta said, more gently, nodding at the drag. "Senna Voss goes on the almshouse roll and that's not one of the three, because the almshouse is a different line in a different part of this book and it is not a question of grain, it's a question of a bed and a fire and Nan Ovey's time. She can have that today. She should have had it an hour ago."

"That's the piece that matters," Rook said.

"I know it is."

"Then do that one."

"I can't do that one on your word," Hesta Quill said, "because you're not her family, and you're not registered anywhere, and I have never seen you before in my life. The almshouse line needs a mark from the household. Blood, or the nearest thing standing."

Everybody looked at Toren.

---

The ledger was enormous. That was the thing he had not expected.

She turned it around on the counting-house sill so that it faced him. It was two feet across and four inches thick, bound in something that had been re-backed twice, and it smelled like dust and old ink and rain. The page she had open was ruled in five columns, and the columns had names in them going back further than Toren could see.

She put the pen down on the page, next to the line, and turned it so the handle was toward him.

"Your grandmother's name, the settlement you came from, and your own mark under it," Hesta said. "That's all it is. Then she gets a bed tonight, and we can argue about everything else tomorrow with her indoors."

Toren looked at the pen.

He was aware of every single person in that yard. Wyck at the gate-side with his arms folded. Milo close to the drag, because Milo had not been more than eight feet from Senna in four days. Dessa half a step behind Toren's shoulder, absolutely silent, which from Dessa meant *I am watching this and I will remember it.*

And Rook, six feet to his left, with his hand already halfway out — not to stop him. To help. To steady the book.

"What happens to us," Toren said.

"Pardon?"

"After. Once I've signed. What happens to the four of us."

Hesta Quill did not soften it, and Toren was grateful to her for that.

"The yard boy starts tomorrow. Roulet's boy leaves on the fourteenth, which is nine days. The Marrow Ford girl goes west when the next party goes west, which is generally a week. And the fourth of you —" she paused, and made herself finish it — "the fourth of you I have no place for, and I will feed him for as long as I can, and then he will have to go somewhere, and I do not know where that is. I would like to be able to lie to you. I've been doing this for nineteen years and I have found that lying only moves the bad afternoon somewhere further along."

"So we split up."

"Yes."

"All of us. Four roads."

"Yes."

Toren picked the pen up.

He held it for what felt like a very long time. He was thinking about a great many things at once and could not have sorted them into an order. The one that finally arrived at the front was not an argument at all.

It was his father, at the bottom of a gully, with nothing in his hands, stepping sideways across the mouth of the fan.

*He didn't pick which four,* Toren thought.

He put the pen back down on the page.

"No," he said.

---

"Toren." Rook's voice went very quiet, which was much worse than loud. "Pick it up."

"No."

"Your grandmother has a hole in her side that I stopped and did not close. She is four days worse than she was on that road and she needs a fire and a roof tonight, and this woman is offering you both of those things, for nothing, in the next ten minutes." Rook had not raised his voice one degree. "Pick up the pen."

"Then put your own mark on it," Toren said.

Silence.

"You're the one who brought us in," Toren said. His voice was doing something unreliable and he kept going anyway. "You told the gate. You sold the ash. You've been giving orders for four days and everybody in this yard thinks you're in charge of us, and if you say you're responsible for us she'll take it, won't you — you'll take his mark —"

"I would take his mark," Hesta Quill said carefully.

"There. So do it." Toren turned round and looked at him. "Sign it. Say you're responsible for us. Then you can walk out that gate tomorrow and never see any of us again and it'll all be exactly the way you want it."

Rook did not move.

Toren had expected shouting. He had braced himself for it the whole way through the sentence, and what he got instead was a man standing absolutely still in a weigh-yard with his hand half out toward a book, not moving.

"That's a lie on a roll," Rook said at last. "I'd be signing that I'll answer for five people I met on a road eight days ago."

"Yes."

"If I put my name on that line and then I'm four hundred miles away when one of you is turned out of somewhere, that's not a piece of paper anymore. That's a thing I did."

"Yes," Toren said. "I know. That's why I'm asking."

And there it was, out in the open air of a weigh-yard in front of a factor and a goat and four children: the entire thing, said poorly, by somebody who was thirteen.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"My father held the bottom of that hill," Toren said, "so that five people could get up it. He didn't get to pick which five. He got Wyck, who he'd have swapped for a better mule, and he got me, and he got Marta's boy, and none of that mattered, because the hill doesn't care who's on it." His eyes had gone hot and he ignored them. "He spent everything he had, and he did it for a *group,* and then four days later I'm supposed to stand in a yard and sign a paper that cuts the group into four pieces because there's not enough grain."

"Toren —"

"Hold on," Wyck said. "Nobody's asked —"

"I'm not doing it." Toren went straight over the top of him without once turning round, and put both hands flat on the sill either side of the book. "Take her. Take her today, she needs it, and I'll say so out loud in front of anybody. But I'm not putting my name on a line that splits us up, and I'm the only Voss standing, so it doesn't happen without me."

Hesta Quill looked at him for a long moment.

Then she closed the ledger.

"Well," she said. "That's a stalemate, and I haven't had one of those since the spring."

---

She gave them until morning. She was sorry about it, and she did not pretend the sorrow changed anything. Toren thought she was probably the fairest person he had ever met, and he still wanted to put the ledger down the well.

They took Senna to Nan Ovey anyway, because Nan Ovey did not keep a book.

She was ancient, and she was not asleep. She looked at the wound for a long time with a lamp held close, and then said a short flat word that Toren had not expected from a woman of that age.

"Who stopped this?"

"He did," said Dessa.

Nan Ovey turned around and looked at Rook with an expression of frank, professional, deeply irritated interest.

"That's a good job," she said. "That's a *very* good job, and it's four days old, and whoever taught you that taught you properly and then didn't finish." She turned back. "Right. This is going to take three days and it's going to cost you, because I am eighty-one and I don't do favors on credit."

Rook put two measures of ash on the table.

"That's too much," said Nan Ovey.

"Then do it for three days and be generous with the second one."

She grunted, which appeared to be acceptance, and shooed all of them out except Milo, who had somehow become the person who stayed.

Outside in the dark, Wyck said, to nobody in particular: "So we've got till morning."

"Yes," said Dessa.

"And then what?"

Nobody had an answer, and they all went and sat against the stone wall, the real one, the old poured one, which held the heat of the whole day and gave it back for hours.

---

Rook came and stood in front of them about an hour later.

He looked at the four of them sitting in a row against a hundred and fifty years of somebody else's concrete. He did not sit down. When he spoke, his voice had gone to the flattest Toren had ever heard it.

"Nobody's taking you," he said.

Toren braced.

"I have spent four days working on this," Rook said. "I had it done. It was a good outcome — a yard place, a carter I could have checked, a household that wants a daughter, and a bed and a fire for a woman who is going to die without one. That was better than anything any of you had a right to expect on a road like that, and I got it inside two hours, and a thirteen-year-old put a pen down and undid the whole of it in four seconds."

"I —"

"I'm not finished." Rook's jaw worked once. "And I could have signed it. She'd have taken it. I stood there and thought about it and I did not do it, and I am not going to stand here and explain that to you, tonight or ever, so don't ask."

Nobody asked.

"So." He took a breath. "Here is where we are. I can't give you away, because you won't be given, and because apparently I won't do it over your head either. That's the whole of it. That's what happened today."

Dessa said, very quietly: "Does that mean you're keeping us?"

"No," Rook said. "It means I've run out of other ideas."

He turned around and looked out across the yard at the gate.

"Get some sleep," he said. "North gate, first light. All four of you."

"Why?" said Wyck.

"Because the five of you got off that road by accident," Rook said, "and I have watched accidents for eleven years, and they don't happen twice."

---

He had them outside the north gate at first light and kept them there for six hours. Not one minute of it was what any of them had imagined.

He did not teach them to fight. He said so at the start, flatly, in case anybody had come out with hopes.

"You're not going to learn to fight. Fighting takes years and you don't have years and three of you don't have anything to fight with. You're going to learn not to be standing where the thing is. That's it. That's the whole lesson and it will take you the rest of your lives to get good at it."

Then he made them walk.

Six hours of walking, on ground he chose specifically to be bad: loose scree off the hillside, a dry gully with round stones in the bottom, a slope of dead grass with frost still in the shade of it. He made them walk it fast. He made them walk it backward. He made them walk it carrying each other, and carrying the drag, and carrying nothing, and every time one of them took a full stride on loose footing he stopped the whole exercise and made them do that stretch again.

"Short steps. Weight between your feet. Your back foot is not a place you're leaving, it's a place you're keeping." He pushed Milo, hard, without warning, and Milo went over on his side. "That. That is what a full step costs. Again."

By the third hour Wyck had stopped complaining and started keeping time, out loud, under his breath, in a rhythm. By the fourth hour all four of them were doing it, and none of them had discussed it.

Then he taught them the other half, which was the half that mattered.

"When a group of them comes at you, you get about two seconds to read it, and you only need one thing out of those two seconds." He crouched and drew in the dust with a stick — a rough scatter of marks, and a line for the road. "Where's the mass? Not where's the closest one. Where's the *weight.* Where are the most of them, moving the fastest, making the most noise. That's your problem. Everything else in that field is a distraction from your problem."

"What if —" Dessa began.

"Find the mass. Put your back to something. Move away from the mass at an angle, never straight back, because straight back is how you end up in the second lot." He looked around at them. "Say it."

"Find the mass," they said.

"Again."

"Find the mass."

"It'll keep you alive against ninety-nine Riftspawn out of every hundred you meet on that road," Rook said. "You had sixty of them come at you in a rock cut four days ago and every single one of them was in the mass and every single one of them was going as fast as it could go and making as much noise as it could make. That's what a Husk is. That's what most of them are."

Then he took them two miles out past the wall and made them do it for real.

He had known the thing was there. Toren worked that out afterward. Rook had walked a wide loop of the country north of Hobb's Wall at dawn, before any of them were up, and he had picked his ground the way a man picks a table in a room.

It stood in the middle of a fallow field, about ninety yards out, doing nothing at all. Husks did nothing at all until they saw you. It was a smaller one, thin, gray, half-turned away, with its weight down on the outsides of its feet.

"One," Rook said. "You're four. Off you go."

Dessa said, "You're not coming."

"No."

"What if —"

"Then it'll be a very short lesson and I'll be extremely annoyed." He sat down on the field wall. "Find the mass. There isn't one. That's your advantage and it is the only one you are ever going to be handed for free. Use it."

They stood there for about ten seconds.

Then Dessa said, "Right," and started giving directions, because somebody was going to and she got there first.

It took them a little over a minute, and it was ugly, and it worked.

Dessa put Wyck on its blind side at forty feet and made him wait. She took Milo wide to the left with the chain and had him make noise, which was the thing Milo could do best. She kept Toren back on the diagonal.

The Husk came at Milo, because Milo was loud. And Milo did exactly what he had been told. He did not stand and meet it. He went away from it at an angle, on short steps, over broken furrow, and he did not fall down.

Toren came in behind it.

He did not reach for the warmth and he did not think about the shape. He put his arm out where the trouble was. The metal ran. The hook came off his knuckles ugly and heavy and entirely his own, and he caught the thing behind the knee at a dead run and put his hips into it.

It went over. It went over *hard,* face-first into a furrow.

Wyck was there before it stopped moving.

Afterward the four of them stood in a fallow field breathing like bellows, with a spreading patch of gray ash in the dirt. Nobody said anything for a bit.

Then Milo said, "I was *very* loud," in a voice of pure amazement, and Dessa put her hands on her knees and laughed until she had to sit down.

Toren looked back at the wall.

Rook had not moved off it. He was sitting exactly where he had been sitting, with his forearms on his thighs, looking out across the field at the four of them.

He did not clap and he did not call out anything. But he was still sitting like that a minute later, when Toren looked again.

---

They walked back in the last of the light with a half flask of ash between them and an argument going about who had been loudest.

Dessa dropped back beside Rook. She did not let things go. She never had.

"The other one," she said. "Out of the hundred. You said ninety-nine out of a hundred are the mass. What's the hundredth?"

Rook knocked the stick he was carrying against his boot until the dust came off it.

"Not today," he said.
