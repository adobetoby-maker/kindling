# Chapter 6 — What He Saw

On the third morning Nan Ovey finished with Senna, took one more measure of ash for the trouble, and told all of them to get out of her doorway.

"She'll travel," she said. "She'll travel poorly. If it opens again you're a week from anybody who can close it and I want you to hold that number in your head the whole way — a week. Say the number."

"A week," said Milo.

"Good boy. You're the one who'll remember it." Nan Ovey put a hand flat on Senna's shoulder for a second, and neither of them said anything, and then the door shut.

Rook had spent the morning in the weigh-yard doing something with a great deal of the ash money, and what he came back with was a handcart.

It was not much. Two wheels, a flat bed, high rails on three sides, a pair of shafts sized for a person rather than an animal. The near wheel had a repair in it that somebody had been proud of.

"That's a cart," Milo said.

"It is."

"For her?"

"For her, and for water, and for everything the four of you are currently carrying on your backs like a string of very bad donkeys." Rook dropped the shafts. "Two of you on it at a time. Swap every mile. Anybody I catch pulling it for two miles to prove something gets to pull it for six."

Wyck, flatly: "How much of that was ours?"

Rook stopped and looked at him for a moment, and Toren saw, with interest, that the question had actually landed.

"All of it," Rook said. "Every measure of it came off a road your lot paid for. If any of you wants an accounting I'll sit down and give you one tonight, line by line, and it'll take an hour and it will be extremely boring."

"I want it," said Wyck.

"Fine."

"I'm not saying you took anything."

"I know what you're saying," Rook said, and went to get the water.

---

They moved out a mile and a half north of the wall to a stone sheep-fold that still had three walls. Rook drilled them there for two days, and the drilling changed.

The first day had been footwork. The second day was worse and the third was worse than that. He made them do the whole footwork course while Milo shouted wrong directions at them, on purpose, to teach them who to listen to. He made them carry the cart over ground that no cart should go over. He put Wyck and Toren on opposite ends of a rope and made them move a weight across a slope together without talking, four times, until they stopped trying to out-pull each other and got it right.

He taught them where to stand relative to one another, which was the thing none of them had known they were doing wrongly.

"You're bunching. All four of you, every time, you bunch." He walked in among them and put a hand on each of their shoulders in turn and shoved them apart to a distance that felt absurd. "That. Now nothing can reach two of you in one movement. Now you can all see past each other. Now when one of you goes down the others aren't standing in the way of the people getting to him."

"It feels wrong," said Dessa.

"It's supposed to feel wrong. Standing close feels safe, which is the single most expensive lie the human body tells."

He gave them jobs. That was the other change. Dessa saw and called. Milo carried the noise and the light work and the water and, it turned out, had an unreasonable gift for throwing things accurately. Wyck held ground — Rook did not put it any more elaborately than that, and did not explain why he had looked at Wyck for four seconds before saying it. Toren did whatever the shape in his hand was good for, which was catching things and pulling them.

He did not tell any of them that they were good at any of it.

---

The thing with Wyck happened on the second afternoon and it took about ninety seconds.

Rook had set them a problem — four of them, a gap in the fold wall, a sack of grain on the wrong side of it — and had then walked off forty yards and sat down and refused to answer any questions about the rules.

Toren started talking.

He did not think about it. He simply started, the way you start walking when a road is in front of you. "Right — Milo, you're on the wall, you can see over. Wyck, if you take the low end we can —"

"Why you?"

Toren stopped. "What?"

Wyck was standing with the sack between them, not moving, with his wrecked knuckles hanging loose at his sides.

"Why is it you," Wyck said. "Every time. Why is it you saying who does what."

"I'm not — somebody has to —"

"Somebody has to. Right." Wyck nodded slowly. "You said 'we' in that yard. You put your hands on that book and you said *we,* about four times, and you never once turned around."

The whole thing went very quiet. Milo came down off the wall.

"There was a man going east on the fourteenth," Wyck said. "With a cart. Who wanted a boy for the winter and would have fed him and was known to the factor for thirty years. That was a real thing that was sitting there on a table, and it was for one of us, and it might have been me, and I'd have been fed and I'd have earned it and I wouldn't have owed one single person anything at the end of it."

"Wyck —" Dessa started.

"I'm not finished. I've been not finishing this for two days." He looked back at Toren. "And instead I'm here. On a hill. Doing a man's exercises for free and eating a stranger's food, and every time anybody looks at this group they look at *you* first, and I never said yes to any of it and nobody ever asked me."

Toren's face was burning. "Would you have gone? East?"

"That's not the point."

"It's exactly the point. Would you have gone?"

"I don't *know,*" Wyck said, and his voice cracked straight down the middle on the word. That — not the anger, the crack — was the part that got through. "I don't know, because I never got to stand in front of it and decide, because you decided at the counter with your back to me. And I'd rather have said no myself than have somebody say no on my behalf and then be thanked for it."

Nobody said anything.

Forty yards off, Rook sat on a rock and watched the whole thing and did not get up, and did not say one word about it then or afterward, which Toren found, on reflection, was the single most interesting thing about it.

Wyck picked up the sack and carried it over the wall by himself.

"There," he said. "Solved."

---

They met the verge-hounds the next evening, an hour before dark, four miles north of the wall on the old drove road.

It was good country for a cart and bad country for everything else. The road ran along the top of a low raised bank with a drainage ditch on the east side of it, deep and dry and full of last year's reeds. On the west side the ground dropped into rough pasture gone to brush, with a long spoil-heap of gravel along it where somebody had once dug something out and never put it back. A line of dead poplars ran the whole length of the bank, and forty yards ahead the ditch went under the road through a stone culvert with a slab top.

Dessa stopped walking.

"Something's in the brush," she said.

Rook came up past the cart without hurrying. He looked west for about two seconds.

"Verge-hounds," he said. "Cart on the culvert slab. Now, please."

They put the cart on the slab. It took eleven seconds, and it was the best eleven seconds of work any of them had done all week. Senna held the rails and said nothing whatsoever, which was her own kind of help.

Then Toren looked west and saw them, and the whole of the last three days came together in his head like a key going into a lock.

Three of them were in the brush. He could see two and hear the third. They were not hiding — they were *coming,* at a long loose trot, thirty yards apart, and they were making an astonishing amount of noise about it. The gravel of the spoil-heap cracked and slid under them. Brush broke. One of them went through a stand of dead thistle and took the whole stand with it.

Verge-hounds were bigger than he had expected, long in the body and low, the same wet-ash gray as everything that came through, with too many joints in the back legs.

*Find the mass.*

"West," Toren said.

It came out of him with no gap at all between seeing and speaking. He had said it three hundred times on a hillside in two days. "West — all of you, off the ditch, angle back along the bank —"

"There's two on the right," Dessa said.

He looked.

There were. Two of them, in the reeds in the dry ditch on the east side, about twenty-five yards off. And they had stopped.

They had come about ten yards down the ditch and then simply stopped, side by side, half-crouched, absolutely motionless, with their heads low. They were not creeping forward. They were not doing anything at all. One of them had its head turned slightly away.

Everything Toren had been taught in the last three days went through him at once. It all pointed the same way. It pointed wrong.

*Where's the weight. Where are the most of them, moving the fastest, making the most noise.*

Three, moving, loud. Two, still, quiet, one of them not even looking at him.

"They've stopped," Toren said. "Leave them. They're hanging back. *West* — Wyck, Milo, angle west off the ditch, Dessa, come *off* the —"

"Toren —"

"*Come off the ditch!*"

And Dessa Cole, who had seen it and said it and been overruled, did exactly what she was told, because that was the other thing they had spent three days drilling.

She came off the ditch. Which meant she turned her back to it.

---

The two in the reeds came out of the stillness at full speed with nothing in between.

That was the part Toren could never afterward describe to anyone who had not seen it. There was no gather. There was no crouch, no dip, no wind-up, no warning at all. They had been stopped. Then they were at a dead run. The ten yards between the stop and the top of the ditch bank took less than a second and a half.

They did not make a sound until they were on the road.

"*DESSA!*" Milo screamed.

She turned. She got most of the way round.

The first one hit her low and took her off her feet, and both of them went down together on the road stones. Toren heard something he refused for a year to believe he had heard. Dessa did not scream so much as stop.

The second one came past her going for the cart.

Toren was eleven feet away and every single thing he had learned in eight days arrived at once and in the wrong order.

He did not reach for the warmth. He put his arm out where the trouble was, and the hook came. He swung it at the second hound's back leg as it went past him.

He caught it. The curve bit above the hock, and the weight of the animal did the rest. It went over on its side on the road with a crash that jarred all the way up Toren's arm and into his shoulder.

It was up again in under a second.

That was the other thing about verge-hounds, and nobody had told him that either.

"*Wyck!*"

Wyck Tanner was already there.

He did not come running and he did not dive. He walked three steps and stood over Dessa Cole with his boots apart and the iron bar low in both hands, in front of a hound that had a hundred pounds on him, and the hound came into him at full speed.

Wyck did not move.

The hound bounced. It went down onto its shoulder, scrabbling, and came round for a second try, and Wyck was exactly where he had been standing.

Milo threw a rock and hit it in the head from fifteen feet, which did nothing at all except make it look at him, which was, as it turned out, exactly the right amount of help.

Then Rook came down the bank.

---

He had been forty yards west with the loud three, and that was where he had been all along. Toren understood in one flat cold instant that the noisy ones had been a way of buying exactly those forty yards.

He took the one on Wyck first. Then the one Toren had tipped. Then he went up the road and met the remaining three coming through the poplars, in the open, in the last of the light, and it was not a fight — it was a man doing a job he had done a great many times, on ground he had chosen the second he saw the ditch.

And not one of those hounds broke off. Not one. There were four of them down and the fifth came straight on through its own pack's ash, which was the worst thing Toren saw all evening, and he had seen a great deal.

Then it was over. The road went quiet. And there was a sound coming from the stones by the culvert.

---

Dessa's leg was bad.

Toren got to her first and did the only useful thing he did in the entire hour, which was to put both his hands where Rook told him to put them and keep them there without moving them, for a very long time, while Rook worked.

She was conscious the whole time. That was the worst of it. She swore, which nobody had ever heard her do, and apologized to Milo for it, and then went on swearing.

Somewhere in the middle of it Rook did the thing he had done for Senna. Toren's ears popped, and Dessa's breathing changed.

Afterward, Rook sat back on his heels in the dark and wiped his forearm across his mouth.

"Right," he said. "She'll walk in a fortnight and she'll limp for the winter."

"The *winter?*"

"That's a good outcome. That is a very good outcome and I want all four of you to understand it, because if that had been two inches further round she'd have been on that road for the rest of her life." He got up. "Milo. Water, and the clean cloth in the top of my pack, and then sit with her and don't let her get up for anything."

"Yes."

"Wyck." Rook did not soften it at all. "You stood over her."

"She was on the ground."

"Yes." Rook looked at him for a second longer than he needed to. "Yes, she was."

Then he turned around and looked at Toren, and Toren, who had been waiting for it, discovered that he had not been waiting nearly hard enough.

---

He did not shout. Toren almost wished he had.

"Sit down," Rook said. "All of you who can. This is the part that costs, and I'd rather you paid for it once."

They sat on the road in the dark.

"Verge-hounds hunt in a pack and they only ever have one plan," Rook said. "They pick a flank and they commit to it, all of them, everything, and once they've committed they can't change it. That's their weakness. That's the whole weakness. They cannot re-choose."

"Then why —"

"Because they pick the *quiet* flank. Always. Every time." He let that sit in the dark. "The ones that make the noise aren't the attack. They're the reason you're looking the other way. The ones that go still are the ones that are coming, and the stillness isn't hesitation and it isn't hanging back and it isn't a hurt one falling behind the pack. It's the wind-up. When one of those things stops moving, that is the single most dangerous second of your evening."

Toren had his arms around his knees and his face on them.

"You said find the mass," he said.

"I did."

"You said it for two days. You made us *say it back.*"

"I did."

"It's *wrong.*"

"It's right for ninety-nine things out of a hundred," Rook said, "and it's the reason you're alive after that rock cut, and if you'd had it eight days ago some of you would have come out of there with fewer holes in you. It's a good rule. It is not the only rule." He shifted. "And there's a second half to it that I did not give you, and I want you to hear the second half now, because it's the part that would have cost you nothing this evening.

"A called flank can be countered. If somebody *names* it before contact — says it out loud, says *right side, two, quiet ones* — then everybody turns, and they're committed to a flank you're already facing, and they come into a line that's ready for them, and you take them one at a time as they arrive." He paused. "It was named. Somebody in this group named it correctly, out loud, four seconds before it happened."

Nobody said anything.

"It was," Dessa said, from the ground, in a voice like gravel. "I saw them. I said it."

"You did."

"And then I did what he said."

She did not say it cruelly. She was not even angry. She said it the way she said the number of crows in an elm, as a true thing that ought to be in the room, and it went into Toren like a nail.

"Why didn't you say?" he said.

He was not looking up. His voice came out ruined.

"Why didn't you *say?* You knew. You've known this whole time. You were standing right there when I called it and you knew exactly what was about to happen and you turned west and you *let me* —"

"Yes."

"Then *why?*"

"Because you'd have known it," Rook said, "and you wouldn't have learned it."

Toren got up off the road so fast he nearly went over.

"She's got a *leg —*"

"I know exactly what she's got, I'm the one who just spent forty minutes on it." Rook did not get up. He stayed sitting on the road with his forearms on his knees and looked up at a thirteen-year-old shaking with rage in the dark, and he did not raise his voice once. "And you will never make that mistake again for the rest of your life. Not once. Not at fifty. Somebody will stand still in front of you when you're an old man and everything in your body will go cold before you've worked out why. That's what it bought."

"That's not *yours* to buy."

"No," Rook said. "It isn't."

And then he did not say anything else. He did not defend himself. He did not take it back. The not-taking-it-back went on for a very long time.

---

Later, when Milo had got a fire going in the lee of the cart and Dessa had finally gone under, Toren sat apart with his back against a poplar and took the disk out from under his shirt.

He had not looked at it since the cut.

He turned it over in the lamplight. It was exactly as heavy as it had been on a dark road eight days ago, and exactly as uninteresting — nine fine channels running in from the rim, stopping a hair short of the middle, making that small ring of nothing he still did not enjoy looking at. There was a scuff on the rim that had not been there before. The cord his father had knotted through it in a house, at a table, in the light, had held.

He put his thumb in the worn place and sat there and did not cry, quite.

He heard Rook stop walking.

It was about fifteen feet behind him and slightly to the left, and it was not the ordinary sound of a man arriving. It was the sound of a man who had been walking at a normal speed and had stopped in the middle of a stride.

Toren did not turn round.

"Where did you get that," Rook said.

His voice had something in it that had not been in it at any point in eight days — not on the road, not in the cut, not in the weigh-yard. It was not loud. Toren could not have named what it was. He turned round with the disk still in his hand and found the man standing exactly where the sound had stopped, not coming closer.

"My father gave it to me," Toren said. "At my Kindling."

Rook came three steps and crouched down, out of arm's reach, and looked at it.

He did not ask to hold it. He did not put his hand out for it. After a moment he turned his own palm up and moved it slowly toward the disk and stopped it about four inches away, and held it there, and did not touch it.

His eyes went round the rim.

Toren watched him do something then that made no sense at all: his lips moved, slightly, without sound, the way Toren's own did on a road when he was counting paces.

He was counting the channels.

He got to the end of them and stopped and did not blink for a while.

"How long has your family had it?" Rook said.

"Five generations."

Something happened in the man's face. It was not large. It was about the same size as the thing that had crossed his face at the top of the gully when Dessa said *no.* It went through and was gone. Toren still had no idea at all how to read it.

"What did he tell you about it?"

"That it'll save the world or end it, and nobody's ever known which." Toren's voice was flat with exhaustion. "And that his grandmother called it a curse in a gift's coat."

Rook closed his eyes.

He kept them closed for about three seconds, which is an extremely long time for a man to have his eyes closed while crouching in front of a stranger in the dark.

Then he stood up.

"Put it away," he said. "Under your shirt. Don't take it out in front of anybody at a gate, don't take it out in a yard, and don't take it out in front of me again unless there's a reason."

"What is it?"

"I don't know."

"You *do.*" Toren was on his feet without any memory of standing. "You stopped walking. You counted them — I *watched* you count them —"

"Put it away, Toren."

"Have you seen one before?"

Rook did not answer that.

He looked at the disk in the boy's hand for one more second, in the light of a small fire, four miles north of a wall, with an injured girl asleep behind him and four days' walking behind that. Then he turned around and walked back to the cart and sat down against the near wheel, facing out into the dark, with the repaired spokes at his shoulder.

---

Toren gave it half an hour. Then he went over and sat down against the same wheel, a yard along, because he did not know what else to do with himself and there was nowhere on that road to go.

Rook did not tell him to leave.

They sat there. The fire got low. Out in the black somewhere north, something called twice and stopped.

"Hobb's Wall," Toren said eventually. "If we went back. She'd write us in the book, some of us. She said we could argue about it tomorrow."

"We're not going back to that ledger."

It came out immediately and without any weight on it at all, the way a man answers a question he settled days ago.

"Ever?"

"Ever." Rook shifted against the spokes. "I've stopped looking for somebody to take you. I'm not going to say it a second time and I'm not going to be thanked for it, and if any one of you brings it up again I'll put you on the shafts for a day."

Toren sat with that.

"Then what happens?"

"Then everything gets harder starting tomorrow," Rook said.

He said it the way he said everything, flatly, as information. Then he did not say anything else, and he did not lie down. After a while Toren's eyes started to close on their own.

The last thing he saw before they went was the shape of the man against the wheel, upright, both hands open on his knees, looking north into a country neither of them could see.

He was still sitting like that when the fire went out. He was still sitting like that when Toren woke in the small hours. He was still sitting like that at first light.
