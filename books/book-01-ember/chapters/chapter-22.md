# Chapter 22 — Off the Books

They got him back along the wall in eleven minutes and it took both of them and Toren never told anybody how it was done.

At the platform end Dessa sat down on the stone with her back against the last gate frame and put Milo's head on her thigh and arranged his arms, which took her some time, because one of them did not want to go where she wanted it.

"There," she said, to nobody.

Then she looked north.

---

"Toren."

"I know."

"You don't know yet, because I haven't said it." Dessa's voice had gone completely level, which from Dessa meant the exact opposite of what it sounded like. "There's two of them in the water on the west side and they went in about a minute ago and they are not coming this way."

Toren got up on the low wall.

He could see nothing. The dark was still absolute over the drowned country, and the arch was a hump against a sky that had begun, in the last few minutes, to be very slightly less than black at the bottom edge.

"How do you know?"

"Because I have been listening to that water for nine hours," said Dessa, "and it does one thing, and just now it did a different thing twice, forty feet apart, going north."

"Wyck's on the cart."

"Wyck's on the cart with a bar and three ribs and there's two of them," said Dessa. "Go."

"Dessa —"

"I can't run. That's not an opinion, it's a fact about today." She had both hands on Milo. "*Go,* Toren."

---

He went four hundred yards up a bank in the dark with one arm strapped across him and he never remembered any of it.

That is the honest account and he gave it that way for the rest of his life, to anybody who asked, without embroidering one part of it. He got up off the platform, and then he arrived at the foot of the hump. In between there was a road going under him, and a noise coming out of his own throat that he was not aware of making.

What he remembered was arriving.

---

A man sat on the road at the south end of the hump with his back against the parapet and his right arm held against his chest in his left hand. It was the young one with the straight blade, the one who had been staring at Toren since the brick. He had his teeth shut and his eyes open and he was rocking very slightly. He looked up at Toren going past him and did not do one single thing about it.

There was a woman on her knees ten feet further on, on the south side of the crown, with her palms flat on the brick and her head down, breathing the way a person breathes at the top of a long hill. Her hands were not doing anything. Toren understood a year later that this was a woman who had held a wall for eight hours and had come off it and could not get back on.

There was a man lying face down on the crown itself who was not moving.

And there was nobody stopping anybody, because every person on that arch who could still do anything was doing it four hundred feet away in the water.

---

Toren came over the top of the hump and down the north side and stopped.

There were three people on the road in front of him in the last of the dark.

Hoyle was standing to the left, at the edge of the parapet, with her arms at her sides and her coat open and nothing in her hands.

A man was on the road on his face with his right arm up behind him at an angle it did not want to be at, and a knee in the middle of his back, and his cheek against the brick.

And Rook was over him.

---

Toren had watched that man for thirty-four days.

He had seen him come down a shale fan in eleven seconds. He had seen him take a chit out of an open palm faster than a boy could shut his hand. He had seen him stand in a rock cut and shrink a wall to eight feet and hold a road for a family he had never met.

He had never, in thirty-four days, seen him look like anything at all.

Rook knelt on a brick road with his left hand flat between a man's shoulder blades. There was no blade out. His face in the grey had nothing on it.

Toren stopped nine feet away with his heart going and found that he could not make himself go one step closer.

"What's your job," said Rook.

---

The man on the road did not struggle.

That was the first thing. He had stopped struggling, if he ever had. He lay with his cheek on the brick and his eyes open and his breathing going in and out. He answered in the voice of a man who has answered questions from people with the right to ask them for a very long time.

"I count."

"Count what."

"What's asked." A pause for breath. "Days worked. Gates. What goes into a place and what comes out of it. That's what I'm for. I've done it nineteen years."

"Say your name."

"Tull."

---

There was a small sound from the parapet.

Hoyle had not moved. She stood with her hands at her sides, eight feet away, watching Rook's face and not the man on the ground. Toren, in the grey, saw something go across her that he could not read.

"You shot at a light," said Rook.

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because it was lit," said Tull, "and it was moving off the road, and it wasn't accounted for."

Rook did not say anything for about two seconds.

"Say that again."

"It wasn't accounted for," said Tull.

---

Here is the thing that Toren Voss took away from the Level and carried for the whole of the rest of his life, and it was not the sluice wall and it was not the dark.

It was that the man was not lying.

He was not being cruel and he was not being clever and he was not, as far as Toren could tell from nine feet away in the last of the dark, frightened in the ordinary way. He had done a thing his whole trade was arranged around, correctly, from a standing start at a hundred and ten yards. When he was asked why, he gave the true reason.

And the true reason was a piece of bookkeeping.

---

"Eleven years ago," said Rook.

Nothing happened on the arch.

"The eleventh of the ninth month. A valley two hundred miles north of a river with a kink in it, with a cliff at the top end and a station in the bottom of it and a wire round the whole."

Tull's eyes moved.

"You came over the west wire at two in the morning. You went to the gate first, before anything, before one hand was laid on one person." Rook's voice had not changed by a single degree and Toren wanted, suddenly and violently, for it to. "There was a long shed at the west end with one door in it. There were children going out of the low end of it and up a bank all night. Somebody stood at that door.

"Did anybody count who came out?"

---

The dark had got thin enough by then to see a face.

Toren watched the man on the road understand which question he had just been asked, and watched him take about a second and a half to decide, and watched him not lie.

"Eleven," said Tull.

---

Nobody on that arch said anything.

Out east, four miles away, the flat water had started to have a colour.

"Eleven," said Rook.

"Eleven out over the west wire. Nine of them children and the smallest about six, and a young one in front carrying one of them." Tull's cheek was on the brick and he did not shut his eyes. "I put it in the book because the book is what I'm for. It got read out in the morning and nobody said anything about it and nobody has said anything about it since, and I have carried the number nineteen years."

"You carried the number."

"I carried the number," said Tull. "I'm not asking you for anything about it. I'd not know what to ask for."

---

"Lowry Bend," said Rook.

Toren saw it hit.

It was not the valley that did it. The valley had gone in like a thing a man had already been carrying and could not be made heavier by. This one went in somewhere else.

"I don't —"

"Ninety people. A river with a kink in it and a bridge with a hole in it. Nine of you came in on a Tuesday to hold bad ground above a hand village, and you were competent, and you were good, and on the fifth night you took the top lane in the dark at a run."

"Don't," said Tull.

"There were three people in that lane. That is where the hand-tradition people go when there is bad ground above a village. It is the whole of the trade. You go toward it and you hold." Rook's hand did not move. "A woman and two boys. One of them was seventeen."

The man on the road had shut his eyes.

"It was dark," he said.

"I know it was dark."

"It was dark and it was fast and we were four days in and nobody had told us there'd be anybody up there. Nobody told us. That's not — " His breath went. "I'm not saying that as an excuse. I've had nine years to find out whether it's an excuse and it isn't one. It's just the thing that happened."

"The company paid compensation."

"The company paid compensation," said Tull. "Eleven bits of station coin and a written apology, and a man read it out in the square, and I was put off the books."

---

Rook said it then.

"I took the eleven bits."

The arch went entirely quiet.

"There were four families in that village who needed them and I was not going to make a point with somebody else's winter." Rook's voice did not go anywhere at all. "I have had them for nine years. Two of them are in my coat and the other nine went where they were needed, and I have never once been able to decide what I did when I took them."

The man on the road under his knee opened his eyes.

"You," he said.

"Yes."

"Who —"

"You don't get that," said Rook.

---

The book was lying on the road.

Toren had not seen where it came from. It was on the brick about a foot from Tull's out-flung hand, face down, and it was not what he had spent a day and a half imagining. It was a fat soft-covered notebook the size of a hand, swollen with damp and re-sewn twice down the spine, wrapped in a piece of oilcloth that had come half off it, with a pencil stub tied to it on a bootlace.

Rook picked it up left-handed without taking his knee off the man's back.

"Don't," said Hoyle.

"You're eight feet away and you've had eight feet for two minutes and you haven't come," said Rook. "So we both know how this part goes."

He put his thumb in it.

---

And Toren, standing nine feet up the road with one arm strapped across his chest, heard it start.

It came from the north.

It was a hundred and fifty yards away and it was iron on wood, three times, fast, and a boy's voice saying one word twice and then not saying it a third time.

"*Wyck!*"

---

Everything after that took about four seconds and Toren had every single part of it for the rest of his life, in order, and never once got any of it out of order.

"Two of them," said Hoyle. "Shaw and the other. They went in the water when you came up the road and I did not send them and I could not have stopped them, and I would not have stopped them at eight o'clock last night and I would now."

Rook was looking north.

He had the book in his left hand, open, with his thumb in it. His right hand was empty. His knee sat in the middle of a man's back, and the man under it was not going to get up.

"Say the number," said Rook.

"What?"

"The number. What you want for it."

"The book," said Hoyle, "and I call them off. Now. This second. I say one word off this arch and they come out of that water and they stand still and they stay standing still, and you know I can do it because you have been watching me for two days and there is nothing else on this bank that either of us has got."

---

"I have got one thing left in me," said Rook.

He said it out loud, on an open road, in the grey, to a woman standing eight feet away.

"I want that said, because in about two seconds I'm going to do something and afterward people are going to make a story out of it, and the story will be wrong.

"It isn't that I can't. I can. He's under my knee and it takes about a second and there is nobody on this bank who could reach me in a second, and I have wanted it for six years, and I have got six years of the only reason anybody ever has in my left hand.

"What I have not got is two of them.

"I have one thing left. Not two. And the cart is a hundred and fifty yards north of here."

---

He held the book out sideways.

Hoyle came two paces and took it out of his hand.

"*Shaw!*" she shouted, up the road, in a voice Toren would not have believed came out of a woman of that size. "*Off it! Off it now and stand where you are!*"

And Rook took his knee off Tull's back and stood up and went north.

---

Toren had never seen a stride.

He had seen his father describe one, on a road at night, an hour after a ceremony, with two fingers on a strap. He had had it explained to him four times by four people. He had watched a man with three doors do three things at once on a hillside above a rock cut and had been too far away and too much on fire to take any of it in.

This was nine feet from him.

The metal came up out of the seam and went the wrong way — down instead of out, along the outside of the leg from the hip to the ankle, two struts a side, and up the back with a rib either side of the spine.

It did not look like a weapon. It did not look like armour. It looked like a thing built for a job.

Rook took the first step and Toren felt the road through his boots.

The second one put him twenty feet up the bank.

---

He covered a hundred and fifty yards in a time Toren had no number for, because Toren was running after him with one arm strapped and the whole world had gone sideways.

What Toren got, arriving, was the end of it.

There was a cart across the road with an old woman sitting bolt upright in it holding both rails. There was Wyck Tanner on the ground at the tailgate on his hands and knees with the bar still in his fist and his mouth open, trying to get up, on the third or fourth attempt.

There was a very big man with a boarding axe, standing in the road, turning round.

And there was Rook, arriving, and he did not slow down.

---

It was not a fight. Toren wanted it to have been a fight for about a year afterward, because a fight would have been easier to hold.

The big man got the axe round and he got it round fast and well and he was not slow and he was not a fool, and he put it where a man coming at that speed was going to be.

Rook went under it.

He went under it on the struts, low, at a speed that nothing weighing what a person weighs has any business doing.

The axe came down through the place he had been. It took him along the left forearm, from the wrist to halfway to the elbow.

He did not stop.

He hit the man across the thighs.

That was all. Toren saw it happen and could not make anything more of it. There was no blow. A hundred and eighty pounds of man arrived at another man's legs at a speed that had a whole bank of earth and two struts and a rib either side of a spine behind it, and the big one went over the top of him and onto the road and did not get up quickly.

And then Rook was down on the road on his hands and knees, four feet past him, and the struts ran back into the seam and closed with a small hard tick.

And he stayed there.

---

Shaw did not come back at him.

He got up onto one elbow in the road and looked at the man on his hands and knees four feet away. Toren stood eleven feet off with his hook half out and no idea in the world what to do with it, and watched a man who could have finished it decide not to.

"*Off it!*" came up the bank behind them, again, from four hundred feet away. "*Shaw! Stand where you are!*"

"I heard you the first time," said Shaw, to nobody, and sat back down on the road.

---

Hoyle came up the bank at a walk with the book under her arm.

It took her a long while, because she was fifty-five and the light was bad, and nobody on that road did anything at all while she came.

Wyck had got to the cart rail and got himself up it. Senna had not let go of the rails. Toren stood between the tailgate and a man sitting in the road, with a hook on the end of his arm that he could not put away, because he had never in his life tried to put one away when there was still somebody there.

And Rook stayed on his hands and knees on the road, with his head down, for a long time, and then sat back on his heels and looked at his own left forearm.

The sleeve had gone. The strap had been cut through at the tongue end, and the small dark oval hung off his arm by nothing at all. He caught it left-handed, poorly, on the second try, before it went onto the road.

He held it in his fist.

"Right," he said.

---

"Four of us can walk," said Hoyle.

She stopped at fifteen feet, which was where she had stopped the first time, and she put her hands out from her sides, palms showing, which nobody had asked her to do.

"Lask is dead. Brann's arm is broken in two places and he's nineteen and he'll mend. Odle's been no use since your boy hit him and Teague will be no use for a week. I've a two-wheel cart with a cracked axle on a platform four hundred yards back with four books in it and a pot I'd like.

"I'm going south off this bank and I am not coming north again. I'll say that once and you can believe it or not, and I would not believe it either."

Rook did not get up.

"Take him," he said.

---

Tull came up the road on his own.

He came past Toren and did not look at him. He held his right arm the way the young one had been holding his, against his chest, in his other hand, and he walked poorly. He was about fifty and grey and there was nothing whatever remarkable about him.

Toren looked at him for eleven straight seconds and made himself learn the face.

He stopped in front of Hoyle at the arch end of it.

"Hoyle."

"Get behind me," said Hoyle.

He got behind her.

And that was the whole of it. There was no other part.

---

"One thing," said Hoyle.

She had the book under her arm and she had not opened it.

"The one on the wall. With the light."

Nobody answered her.

"I keep a book," said Hoyle. "That's the whole of what I am and I've never once pretended to be anything else. It has got every day anybody has ever worked for this company in it, and it has got what those days cost, because a day that costs something is still a day and it goes in the same as the others.

"And there is going to be a line in it for last night whether anybody likes it or not. I'd rather it had a name on it than a number." She did not soften it. "So. What was his name?"

---

Toren opened his mouth to say something that would have been wrong.

"Milo Faust," said Rook, from the road.

Hoyle got the book out from under her arm. She untied the pencil stub off the bootlace and she stood in the middle of a raised bank over eleven miles of drowned country in the grey before sunrise and wrote two words in it, slowly, with the book braced on her own forearm, the way a person writes standing up.

Then she tied the pencil back on and put the book inside her coat and did the button.

"Fourteen?" she said.

"Fourteen," said Rook.

Hoyle nodded once.

"Then that's a bad one," she said, "and I've had bad ones before and I'll have them again, and I'm not going to stand on a road and tell a man it grieves me, because it would and it wouldn't be worth anything to you."

She turned round and walked south with her people, and Toren watched Cinder Company go back over the arch in the first of the light, seven of them, two of them poorly, carrying nothing.

---

Nobody moved for a while after they had gone.

Wyck got down off the cart rail eventually and came along the road in the eleven separate movements it took him and sat down in the road next to Rook, which took him four more, and did not say anything.

Senna let go of the rails.

"Where is Milo?" she said.

Toren could not do it. He got about two words into it and his whole chest shut.

"He's on the platform," said Wyck. "With Dessa. Dessa's got him."

Senna Voss put both her hands flat on the blanket over her knees and looked at them for a long time.

"Right," she said. "Then somebody is going to have to go back down that bank and get him, and I am going to be no use to any of you at it, and I would like it very much if one of you would take my hand for a minute and then we'll say no more about it."

Wyck got up and did it.

---

Toren asked it at about six o'clock in the morning, sitting in the road, because if he had not asked it then he would never have asked it at all.

"You had him."

"Yes."

"Say it properly."

Rook sat on the road with his back against the cart wheel, his left forearm bound in a strip off his own shirt and a small dark oval lying on his knee with a cut strap. He did not look up.

"I had him on the ground under my knee and he was not going to get up," he said. "There was nobody on that bank who could have reached me and there was a second in it and I have had six years of wanting it. And I had eleven years of their own book in my left hand, open, with my thumb in it, and somewhere in that book there are nine lines with nine names against them and a date at the top that I have been carrying since I was nineteen.

"And I had one thing left in me and not two.

"I put the book in that woman's hand and I took my knee off that man's back and I went north. Nobody made me do it. There was no part of it I did not see."

He turned the Ember over on his knee.

"And I will not get either of them back," said Rook. "Not the man and not the book. She will not come north again and she was telling the truth about that, and in a fortnight that crew will be four hundred miles from here and there will be no line in any book anywhere saying where.

"That's the account. You asked for it properly and you were owed it."

---

Toren sat in a road on an eleven-mile bank across a drowned country with the sun coming up out of the water on the east side, and found that he had one more question, and that he already knew the answer, and that he was going to have to ask it anyway.

"Was it worth it?"

Rook was quiet for a long time.

"Ask me that in about four years," he said, "and I will still not lie to you about it. Not this morning."

He got up off the road, which took him three goes.

"Let's go and get him," he said.
