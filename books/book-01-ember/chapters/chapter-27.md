# Chapter 27 — Intake

Meridian came out to get them, and the sound it came out with was the worst thing that happened to Toren Voss all day.

He heard it at about eleven o'clock, lying on his back in four inches of Riftspawn ash with Dessa Cole sitting beside him being extremely dull on purpose. It came up out of the north across half a mile of open pasture. It was not a sound the world had.

It was low. It was even. It did not vary.

"Dessa."

"I hear it."

"What is that?"

"I don't know," said Dessa, "and I have decided that I am going to find out standing up."

She got herself onto her feet in the glass. She put a hand on the top of the sixth footing and looked north.

Then she sat back down and put her hand on Toren's chest again.

"It's a cart," she said. "It's a cart with nothing pulling it."

---

It was a flat four-wheel truck about twelve feet long, with a rail round the bed and a man driving it from a seat at the front. It came up the road at the speed of a man walking fast, and it made that noise the whole way.

Toren, who could not get up, lay in a glasshouse bed and watched a thing move along a road with nothing in front of it.

"Milo," he said, out loud, in the ash.

"I know," said Dessa. "He'd have gone and got in it."

---

There were four of them.

Two were stretcher-bearers in grey canvas with a folded frame between them, and they came over the footings at a jog and did not stop to look at the ash or the walls or the boy in it. The third was a woman of about fifty with a short coat and a lamp on a strap and a slate board under her arm, and she came over the brick at a walk and stopped eleven feet away and looked at the whole bed before she looked at anybody in it.

"Who's the arm?" she said.

"Me," said Toren.

"Right. Don't sit up, don't be helpful, don't tell me you can walk." She crouched at six feet. "My name's Nesbitt and I'm the south warden. This is the first time I have ever come out of my own gate for anybody, so you can take that as either a compliment or a piece of information about how the day's going. Is there anybody else on this ground?"

"No."

"Dead?"

"No," said Dessa.

"Good. Now say the word for what did that, because a boy with a broken arm in four inches of Barrel-grade ash is a thing I would like to have the right word for before I put it in a book."

---

Dessa Cole looked at her for about two seconds.

"A Breach," she said.

Nesbitt did not write it down.

"Say that again."

"A Breach. It carried an Edge and a Stride at the same time and it couldn't do both at once. It telegraphed which one it was going to do about half a second before it did it, every single time." Dessa's voice did not change by one degree. "The tell's in the weight. Back and down is a cut. Up and forward is a run. There's a click before the cut and a grinding before the run. You can hear it at a hundred and forty feet in still air.

"I would write that down," said Dessa Cole, "because somebody is going to want it."

Nesbitt wrote it down.

---

Here is what happened in the next four hours, and Toren was conscious for about half of it and never got the order of the rest entirely straight.

They put him on a frame and carried him over four brick footings and half a mile of open field. The sky went across above him in a long slow slide. Somewhere in the middle of it he found out what an arm feels like when a stranger sets it against a board and ties it.

They went up a ramp cut into the face of an earth bank twenty-five feet high.

He saw the poles go past above him. There were lamps on the poles. He had been right and Rook had been right, and it did not matter, because the sky was still light and they were not lit.

He went through a cut in the bank about eleven feet wide with a counterweighted pole barrier and a gatehouse on the left and a painted line on the road surface, and he heard a woman's voice behind him say, "Two in, one frame, one walking, eleven fifty-four," and heard a pencil.

And then he was inside, and he could not see any of it, because he was on his back.

---

He saw one thing on the way through.

They carried him past the end of a row of long low buildings. Between two of them there was a yard. In the yard there were about thirty people standing in a line with basins under their arms, waiting for a tap.

And the tap was running.

Nobody was watching it. Nobody had a scoop. There was a brass tap on a standpipe in a yard, running water into a basin while a woman held it and talked to the woman behind her about somebody's roof, and the water went into the basin and over the top of it and away down a drain in the ground.

Toren Voss, who had carried water in two cans across a dying country, went past that yard on a stretcher with a broken arm and cried so hard that one of the bearers stopped and had to be told to keep going.

---

They took Senna Voss through a door at eleven minutes past twelve and it took ninety seconds and it cost nothing at all.

That was the part none of them ever got over.

There was no argument. There was no explaining who she was or where she had come from or what had been spent to get her there. A young man in a grey coat put two fingers on her wrist at the intake step, looked at her for about four seconds, and said, "This one now." She went through a door on a wheeled trolley. The door shut.

Toren was eleven feet away on a frame.

"Wait," he said. "Wait — she's — somebody has to —"

"Somebody has," said Nesbitt.

"But we haven't *said* anything about her. We haven't — there's a whole — she's been on a road for forty-six days. She's got a *thing*. It's a lung thing — she was in a reactor when she was —"

"And the doctor's going to find all of that out in about an hour, and she'll find it out better than you can tell her." Nesbitt put the slate board down on her own knee. "Listen to me, because this is the part nobody from outside ever believes and it is the truth and I have said it four hundred times.

"Medicine at this station is not a thing you trade for. It isn't conditional and it isn't reviewed, and there is no clerk anywhere inside that bank who has the authority to stop it. Not for a name, not for a number, not for anything.

"Everything else here is a great deal more complicated than you are going to like. That isn't."

---

Wyck was sitting on a bench outside the medical block with his hands in two basins of water.

He had been there since noon. Nobody had made him move and he had not moved. When Toren came past on the frame, Wyck got up, and the water went everywhere. He said one word.

"Toren."

"I'm all right."

"Say a true one."

"My arm's broken and the Ember's out," said Toren, "and I don't know how to say either of those and I'd like not to yet."

Wyck sat back down on the bench.

"Right," he said. "Then we'll do it later. I'll be on this bench."

---

They set the arm at two o'clock and it was the worst hour of Toren's life that did not have anybody dying in it.

The room had a light in the ceiling.

That was the thing. He lay on a table with his left forearm out on a board and a woman of about forty putting her thumbs where they had to go, and above him, screwed to the ceiling, there was a glass tube the length of his arm that was full of light.

Nobody had lit it. Nobody was tending it. It had been on when he came in and it would be on when he left, and it did not flicker. There was no fire anywhere in the room and no smell of anything burning.

"Look at me, not at that," said the doctor.

"What is it?"

"It's a lamp."

"It hasn't got a —"

"It hasn't got a flame in it, no." She had his elbow in one hand. "You've come off the south approach, so I'm going to guess you've not seen one. There are about fourteen hundred of them in this station. About nine hundred of them still work, and we cannot make any more. Now stop looking at it, because I'm about to do the bit you'll remember."

---

Afterward, with the arm in a splint and the whole of him grey, he lay on a bed in a room with four beds in it and looked at the ceiling.

The Ember lay on his left forearm, above the splint, where his father had put it on the inside of the wrist with the flat of his thumb on the night of his thirteenth birthday.

It was a pebble.

Toren went in after the warmth about nine times in the first hour and every single time he came back with nothing at all. Not a flicker. Not a cold seam in a bed of ash waiting for a handful of thorn. There was no seam. There was a place under his breastbone, low and central and slightly behind, that he had known about since the evening he was thirteen, and there was nothing in it.

Dessa came at four o'clock and sat down and did not say anything for a while.

"Have you told anybody?" she said.

"No."

"Are you going to?"

"No."

"Toren."

"Not yet." He did not look at her. "Not today. Everybody in this place has got one thing left in them and they've all spent it. If I say it out loud today, then Rook has to get up off whatever they've put him on. And he will. And he cannot."

Dessa put two fingers above his right wrist.

"How much of the room's lit?" she said.

"Nothing."

"Say a fraction."

"There isn't one. That's not modesty, Dessa, I'm not being modest about it. There is no fraction. It's a room with the door shut."

Dessa Cole took her fingers off his wrist and sat looking at the floor for a long time.

"All right," she said. "Then I'll carry it with you and I won't say it to anybody. And I want that logged as a thing I am doing on purpose and not a thing I forgot."

---

Intake was at six.

They did it in a long room with a counter down one side and eleven chairs against the wall. There were four people ahead of them, and they waited, which after forty-six days was an almost unbearable luxury.

The clerk was about twenty-two. He had a bad haircut and a very good pen and a stack of card forms in three colours, and he was polite in the way of a man who has been told nine times that he is polite and has decided to keep it up.

"Purdy," he said. "I'll be doing your intake. It takes about forty minutes and most of it is dull. Household?"

Nobody answered.

"I'm sorry — are you a household? Travelling together, sharing an allocation, one line or five?"

Rook was in the second chair with his shoulder dressed and his left forearm dressed under it and his face the colour of the wall.

"One line," he said.

Toren looked at him.

"One line," said Rook again. "That's the answer and it's the true one and I'd like it written that way."

Purdy wrote it that way.

---

"Names, then. Eldest first."

"Senna Voss. She's through in the medical block."

"I've got her." He turned a card. "Next."

"Toren Voss. Thirteen."

"Wyck Tanner."

"Dessa Cole."

"And yourself?"

The room went on being a room. A lamp buzzed very faintly somewhere over the counter. Out through the window the light had started to go orange on a made road with a kerb on it.

"Elias Marsh," said Rook.

---

Purdy wrote it without looking up, the way he had written the other four, and then his pen stopped about halfway through the second word.

He did not say anything. He finished writing it. Then he put the pen down, and got up, and said, "One moment, please," and went through a door at the back of the room.

Toren looked at Rook.

Rook was looking at the door.

"You know what that is," said Wyck.

"I've an idea."

"Say it, then."

"I came through this gate eleven years ago," said Rook, "in the winter, in the back of a cart, with nine other people and a girl of six called Dee Wren. I was nineteen and I had been on a road for two months. I do not remember one single thing about it, except that somebody gave me a blanket and asked me my name, and I gave them the one I was still using."

---

Purdy came back with a cloth-bound ledger about four inches thick and put it on the counter and opened it at a place he had marked with his finger.

"Would you confirm a date for me," he said.

"I can't."

"Winter, you said."

"I can give you the month it started," said Rook. "The eleventh of the ninth. Whatever it took ten of us to walk here from a valley two hundred miles north of Lowry Bend, with children in it — put two months on that and you'll be inside a week."

Purdy turned the ledger round on the counter so that it faced the chairs.

Toren could not see the page from where he sat. Nobody in that room moved.

"*Marsh, E.,*" Purdy read out, in a completely ordinary voice, because it was a completely ordinary line in a book. "*Nineteen. Party of ten off the south road. Conditional, reviewed, cleared the fourth of the twelfth.* And there's a second hand under it, later, in pencil." He looked up. "*Went out again on his own in the spring. Would not take the allocation.*"

Rook sat in a chair in a lit room and did not say one word.

"So you're already a line," said Purdy, and turned the book back round. "That makes this considerably shorter. Thank you."

---

The bench was after the forms, and Toren did not know there was going to be a bench.

They took them along a corridor to a room with a long scrubbed table in it, and on the table there was a wooden box about the size of a bread crock with a brass plate on the top of it and a needle behind glass on the front, and a man of about sixty sitting beside it with a notebook.

"Sowerby," he said, without getting up. "This is a register bench. Every Kindled person inside this bank is on it. It's done at intake and once a year after. Palm flat on the plate, count of five, I write a number. That's the whole of it."

"What number?" said Dessa.

"Depth. It's a scale of nine. It's not a very good scale and I've said so in writing four times." He turned a page. "Who's first?"

---

Toren watched Rook go very still.

It was not much. Toren had watched that man for forty-six days and he knew every degree of him by then, and this was about two degrees.

"How old is that bench?" said Rook.

"Older than me. They were made in a batch." Sowerby scratched his ear. "There's four of them in this station, and one of them's in a cupboard because nobody can get the glass out of the front to clean the needle. Why?"

"Where were they made?"

"I've no idea. Sit down or stand up — it's all the same to me — but we've eleven minutes before I'm meant to be somewhere else."

---

Dessa went first, because Dessa always went first when a thing was unpleasant and unavoidable.

She put her palm on the brass plate. The needle came off its rest and went up and settled about a third of the way along the scale and sat there entirely steady.

"Two," said Sowerby, and wrote it. "Two's an Ember and a good clean one. You've held that a while."

"A month and a half."

"Then you've got a steady hand." He said it without any particular warmth and it was, Toren thought afterward, the only compliment anybody at Meridian paid any of them for a fortnight. "Next."

Wyck went second and read two, which made him blink.

And then it was Toren.

---

He put his right palm on the brass plate, because his left arm was in a splint, and Sowerby counted to five.

The needle did not come off its rest.

It did not twitch. It did not lift and fall back. It sat exactly where it had been sitting when the room was empty, in a wooden box on a scrubbed table under a lamp that did not flicker.

"Again," said Sowerby, not unkindly.

Toren put his hand back on the plate.

Nothing.

"Right." Sowerby wrote something. "Nought. That's Kindled — door open, nothing through it. It's not a failing, and it's not permanent for most people. About one in nine never reads at all and lives a perfectly ordinary life. Next."

"He's an Ember," said Wyck.

"He's a nought on my bench today and that's all my bench does." Sowerby did not look up. "If it's different next year it'll say different next year. Next."

---

Toren sat down on a chair in a corridor at Meridian with a card in his hand that said *Kindled* on it in a clerk's handwriting.

That was the rung Milo Faust had.

He held it and thought about a boy sitting against a wall in a building with no roof at two in the morning, saying *I'm the only one on the bottom rung and nobody's said so for four weeks, which I think is the kindest anybody's ever been to me and I'd quite like it to stop.*

Nobody said one word to Toren about that card, then or ever.

---

They put Rook on the bench last.

He put his palm on the plate the way he did everything, without any ceremony, and Sowerby counted to five.

The needle went up the scale and did not stop.

It went all the way to the end of the brass and struck the pin at the top and stayed on the pin. There was a small hard noise from inside the box. Sowerby said a word under his breath, and lifted the whole bench off the table with both hands, and looked at the underneath of it.

Then he put it down and sat back in his chair.

"Get your hand off that, please," he said.

"It's off."

"So it is." Sowerby looked at the needle, which had come down off the pin and was going back to its rest in a series of small unwilling steps. "That's not a number. That's the bench telling me it hasn't got a number."

"I know."

"Have you done that before?"

"Three times," said Rook. "Four with this one. Once at nineteen, on a bench in this station, which somebody will have written down in this station's own book eleven years ago if anybody ever goes and looks."

Sowerby got up and went to the door and put his head out of it and said something to somebody in the corridor.

Then he came back and sat down and wrote in his notebook for about four minutes without saying anything at all.

---

Registrar Ambrose came at half past seven. She was not unkind, and that was the whole difficulty.

She was about fifty-five and she had a folder. She pulled a chair round and sat down in front of the four of them in the corridor, so that she was not standing over anybody, which Toren noticed and was obscurely furious about.

"I'm going to say four things and then you can ask me anything you like," she said. "The first one is the good one. Senna Voss is admitted. She's being treated as of about two o'clock this afternoon, and nothing in the next three things touches that in any way at all. I want that said first because in about ten minutes you're going to want to check, and I'd rather you didn't have to."

Nobody said anything.

"Second. Every one of you is admitted tonight. There's a bed, food, a bath. There's no arrangement attached to any of it, and that isn't conditional either."

"And the third?" said Rook.

"The third is that you are admitted *conditionally, pending assessment*, which is a phrase, and I'm going to tell you what the phrase actually does rather than let you find out."

---

"There are eleven hundred and four people inside this bank," said Ambrose. "Between two and three hundred of them are Kindled, depending on what you count, and every single one of them is on a register with a bench number against their name, and has been since before I had this job.

"That register is not a punishment and it is not a suspicion. It's a list of who can do what, in a place that has four working pumps, one hospital, nine hundred lamps, a berm that has to be walked every night, and a country outside it that has got worse every year for eleven years."

"And what does it *do*," said Wyck.

"It sorts you." Ambrose did not soften it. "A Kindled resident at this station is assessed on what their door qualifies them for and is expected to be available for the work that follows from it. Not tonight, not this month, not while any of you are in a splint. But it follows, and I am not going to stand here and let you find that out in eleven weeks from a man in a yard."

"And if we say no?"

"Then you're welcome here as long as you like and you'll be reviewed as a household with nothing to contribute, which is a different queue and a slower one." She looked at them steadily. "I am telling you the honest shape of it on your first evening, in a corridor, while you're all in bandages, and I know exactly how that lands. I'd rather it landed from me."

---

"Say the fourth," said Dessa.

Ambrose opened the folder.

"There's a Barrel in the Thirl orchard," she said.

Nobody moved.

"There is four hundredweight of reserve-ash in a glasshouse bed a mile and a half from this gate. There has not been a Barrel inside forty miles of Meridian in nine years, and there is no line anywhere in this station's book that predicted one. I have had six people on that ground since three o'clock this afternoon, and one of them is a man who has been arguing with me about the north sag field since before I was appointed.

"So I have to ask the question and I have to write down the answer.

"Who put it down?"

---

Toren opened his mouth.

"He did," said Rook.

Ambrose looked at the boy in the chair with his arm in a splint and a card in his hand that said *Kindled* on it.

"He's thirteen," she said.

"He is."

"And my bench read him at nought this afternoon. I've got the sheet in front of me."

"Then your bench is a scale of nine with a brass plate on it, made before either of us was born, by people who were guessing," said Rook. "And I would take the fourth one out of the cupboard and clean it, if I were you. You are going to want them to be better than they are inside about two years."

---

Ambrose wrote for some time.

When she had finished she closed the folder and put both hands flat on it.

"Right," she said. "Then here is where we are, and I'd rather give it to you straight than have you piece it together.

"You're in. Tonight, unconditionally, all of you, and the old woman's being treated and that isn't going to change whatever else does.

"You're on the register. All four of you, with numbers against your names, and one of those numbers is a needle on a pin and I do not have a box on my form for it.

"And you are on marked ground until you're assessed, which means the south quarter and the medical block and the yards, and it means somebody knows where you are. That's not a guard. Nobody is going to follow you. It is a line in a book and you should be under absolutely no illusion that it is anything less than that either."

She stood up.

"And somebody senior is going to want to talk to all of you about that orchard. I don't know who yet. I'd say a fortnight."

---

She got to the end of the corridor and stopped and came back four paces, which cost her something, and Toren watched her decide to spend it.

"For what it's worth," said Registrar Ambrose, "and it is worth nothing at all, because I've just spent twenty minutes telling you what this place is going to do to you.

"I have been doing intake on that counter for nineteen years. I have put about four thousand people through that door and I have got very good at knowing, inside a minute, which of them arrived as a group and which of them arrived as people who happened to be on the same road.

"You four came in as one line because a man in a chair said so before anybody asked him to. Nobody does that. Everybody haggles about the allocation."

She put the folder under her arm.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Somebody will bring you bread at nine."

---

Nobody brought them bread at nine.

At about twenty past, Wyck Tanner came back down the corridor from the kitchens with a covered basket and a look on his face that dared anybody to say anything about it, and put it down on the floor in the middle of the four of them.

There was a loaf in it. It was warm.

None of them had touched warm bread since Sallow Creek.

Dessa took it out and put it on the cloth and got the small knife out of her coat — not the station's knife, her own, the one she had been cutting with since the platform — and cut it into six, poorly, which was not an accident.

"Senna's through there," she said, "so hers waits. Somebody takes it to her in the morning. If they won't let us in, we'll argue about it then."

She put the first piece on the cloth on its own.

"Wyck."

"Me," she said, and took her own.

"Toren."

She stopped with two pieces in her hand in a corridor at Meridian with a lamp on over her head that nobody had lit.

"Milo," said Dessa Cole, and put one down on the floor beside the basket, and turned it a quarter so the flat side faced the ceiling.

Then she turned round.

"And him last," she said.

And Elias Marsh got up off a chair in the south quarter of a tech-zone station with his shoulder dressed and his name in a book from eleven years ago, and came and took it out of her hand, and ate the whole of it standing up, because a boy had told her to make him.
