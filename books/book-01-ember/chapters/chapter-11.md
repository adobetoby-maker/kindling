# Chapter 11 — Coldharbour

The cart had been built for one old woman and a water can, and it now had an old woman, a water can, a boy with three broken ribs, and — for about an hour out of every four, under protest — a girl with a leg that was knitting poorly.

"Move your elbow."

"That's not my elbow."

"Then move whatever it is."

"It's the water can, Senna."

"Then move the water can, Wyck Tanner, and stop lying on my legs, because I can't feel them properly and I need to be able to tell you the truth about whether you're on them."

It was, Toren thought, on the shafts, in the rain, the single most cheerful thing that had happened in two days.

---

They did the stop-and-close every morning before they moved and every evening before they ate, and it was as boring as Rook had promised.

He cut a nine-foot hazel rod and tied a rag to the end of it. He stood at one end of a flat stretch and one of them stood at the other. He held the rod out level and still. The moment it stopped moving they had to be going *at* it. If they hesitated for longer than a breath, the rag came round and touched them, and they did it again.

They did it forty times a session. Toren got touched nine times out of the first ten and four out of the second ten and then, sullenly, steadily, less.

"It's not the same," he said on the second morning. "You're a man with a stick. It's not the same thing at all."

"It isn't," Rook agreed. "It's about a fortieth of the same thing. Do it again."

Milo, from the side: "How long are we doing this for?"

"A month."

"A *month?*"

"And then twice a week until one of us is dead," Rook said. "Go."

---

They met the hunters on the third afternoon, a mile south of the aqueduct, and neither party liked it.

There were two of them, coming south fast on the towpath with packs and flasks and a hand cart of their own. They stopped at eighty yards and stood there. The older one put a hand back, and the younger one stopped dead.

"Far enough," she called.

Rook put both hands out from his sides, palms forward, and stopped.

"Six of us," he said. "Two hurt, one old. Going north."

"You're not going north."

"We'll talk about that. What's behind you?"

The woman came forward about twenty yards and stopped again. She was perhaps forty-five, small and weathered, in a coat that had been patched at both elbows by somebody good at it, with a scoop on her belt and a crossbow across her back that she did not touch. The boy behind her was sixteen or so and had not stopped looking at the cart since they arrived.

"Cass Ordway," she said. "This is Pell. What's behind *you?*"

"Verge-hounds on the drove road eleven days back, five of them, all down. A stilt at the eight-lock flight four days ago, down. We took a flask and a half off it." Rook did not move his hands. "The flight's clear, the pounds are dry from the top bar to the arch, and there's good water at the third lock."

Cass Ordway thought about that.

"A stilt," she said. "You took a stilt with two hurt and an old woman."

"They were the hurt ones afterward."

"Hah." It was not a laugh. It was the noise a person makes when a thing adds up. She came the rest of the way in and the boy came with her, and Toren noticed that she stopped eight feet out and slightly to one side, which was almost exactly where Rook made them stand. "Right. Then you'll want this and you won't like it. Coldharbour's gone."

"The wharf?"

"The wharf, the kiln, Halloran's whole outfit." She said it flatly, the way you say a thing you have already said four times. "Four nights back. Seven of them working that wharf, six grown and a girl, and there's five under stones behind the shed because me and Pell put them there."

Milo made a small sound. Cass looked at him without any unkindness at all.

"Sorry, love," she said. "It's on the road and you'd have walked into it."

"Riftspawn?" said Rook.

"No."

Nobody said anything for a second.

"No," Cass said again. "That's the whole of why we're doing twenty-two miles today with a hand cart. It wasn't spawn. Spawn come in a mass and they come from the water side and they don't take the gate off its hinges first and they don't go through a shed and leave nine weeks of ash sitting in the bins." Her mouth went thin. "It was men."

"Which men."

"The ones that call themselves Cinder Company," said Cass Ordway.

---

Toren, afterward, could not say what he had expected. Nothing, probably. It was a name, and the world was full of names, and he had heard about nine of them since Hobb's Wall and had retained perhaps two.

What he noticed was the cart.

He had the near shaft in his hands, and the cart had been rolling, and it stopped. That was all. It stopped because his own arms had stopped, and his arms had stopped because eight feet away a man who never moved suddenly had not moved in a different way.

Rook was standing exactly where he had been standing. His hands were still out from his sides with the palms forward. Nothing on him had changed.

The bird noise, Toren thought later, was what did it. There had been a great deal of ordinary noise going on — the boy Pell shifting his pack, water in the canal, a wren going off in the hedge — and all of it went on exactly as before, and the only thing in the whole afternoon that had altered was that one man had stopped being a man standing in a road and had become something with its weight forward.

"How many were they," Rook said.

"Nine or ten. Pell made it ten from the ridge and he's got a good eye."

"Which end did they come in at?"

"The road end. Up from the lane, not off the water."

"Did they burn it or did they open it?"

Cass Ordway's eyes narrowed. "What?"

"The shed," Rook said. "And the kiln house. Did they put them to the torch or did they go through them."

"They went through them. There's no burn on that place at all, bar the stove." She was looking at him differently now. "They went through everything in that shed like a woman looking for a lost ring, and they left the ash. Nine weeks. Four hundredweight of the stuff sitting in open bins with the lids off, and they walked past it."

"And did anybody count who came out?"

The question landed in the middle of the towpath and sat there.

It was not the words. Toren replayed it for years and it was never the words. It was that Rook asked it in a voice with the flat gone out of it — not raised, not hurried, but with a thing underneath it that had not been there in the previous three questions and had not been there at any point in nineteen days.

Cass Ordway did not answer straight away.

"Seven working that wharf," she said slowly. "Five under stones. Me and Pell dug for the other two half a day and never found them and I have chosen to think they got up the lane."

"Then somebody should have counted."

"There wasn't anybody left to count, friend."

"No," Rook said. "There never is."

He put his hands down.

Cass Ordway stood on a towpath in the rain and looked at him for about four seconds, and then she said the thing that Toren had felt coming and had not had any words for.

"You've asked me four questions," she said, "and not one of them was *is there anything left worth taking.*"

Silence.

"That's what everybody asks," Cass said. "Every single person I've told this to in three days. It's not even greed, it's just what a person's mouth does. And you've asked me which end they came in at."

Rook shouldered his pack.

"Water's good at the third lock," he said. "Under the beam, not out of the pound. Safe road."

And he walked north past both of them without one further word.

---

Toren looked back once from a hundred yards.

Cass Ordway was still standing on the towpath watching them go, with her hand on her nephew's arm, and the boy was saying something to her and she was not answering.

---

They came to Coldharbour in the last two hours of light and Rook did not slow down and did not stop anybody, and it occurred to Toren only afterward that at no point had anyone discussed whether they were going to go there.

The canal came round a long slow bend that the surveyors of a hundred and sixty years ago had apparently found necessary, and opened into a basin — a widening of the cut where two boats could pass and turn — with a stone wharf edge down one side and a lane coming in from the fields above.

There was a lime kiln. It stood at the top of the wharf, a brick cone thirty feet high going up out of a stone base, with an arched draw-hole at the bottom and grass growing out of the top of it, and it had not burned lime in a century.

There was a long open-fronted shed with a weighing beam under it and a row of bins.

The bins were full.

Toren stood on the wharf edge in the wet gray and looked at four hundredweight of Riftspawn ash lying in open bins under a roof, with the lids stacked neatly against the wall where somebody had put them down, and understood the size of what he was looking at better than any amount of explaining could have given him. He had carried three flasks of that stuff to a factor's scale and got twelve measures for it and been told it was a very good take.

"They walked past it," he said.

"Yes," Rook said.

---

He went over the ground for half an hour and he did it out loud, which was the other new thing, and Toren did not think any of them understood at the time how much they were being given.

He started at the lane.

"There was a gate here." The posts still stood; the gate itself lay flat in the mud eight feet inside. "Off the hinges and inward, so it was pushed, and it was pushed from the lane side. That's the first thing that happened and it happened before anything else, because there's nothing on top of it and everything else that's been dropped in this yard has been dropped on the mud beside it."

"They came in the front," said Dessa.

"They came in the front and they shut the front." Rook crouched at the post. "Look at the ground here. There's three sets of boots that stood on this spot and stayed on it, in a line across the gap, and they stood here long enough to go from mud to puddle. Everybody else in this yard was moving. These three weren't."

"Why would you put three men on an open gate you've already come through?"

Rook did not answer that.

He got up and went along the front of the shed.

"They went through this end properly. Everything's off the shelves and it's on the floor and it's *sorted* — there's a pile of what they didn't want and it's a tidy pile, which means somebody was doing this slowly with somebody else holding a light." He put his hand flat on the plank wall at head height. "Tally-boards were here. Four of them, by the nail holes and the clean squares. Gone."

"They took the boards?" said Milo.

"They took the boards, and they took whatever lived in that." Rook nodded at a shelf under the beam, where a rectangle of clean dry board sat in the middle of ten years of lime dust. "That's a book. That's a book that came out and went back every working day for a long time."

Toren looked at the rectangle on the shelf and thought about a counting-house at Hobb's Wall, and a woman turning a ledger round on a sill, and about how enormous it had been.

"What would be in it?"

"Where they hunted," said Rook. "What came out of which ground, on what day, in what numbers. A hunting outfit's book is nine years of where the country is turning and where it isn't, and there is no other way to get that information except to go and stand in it for nine years." He wiped the dust off his hand. "It's worth more than the ash and it doesn't weigh anything."

"So that's what they came for."

"They came a long way," Rook said, "and they took a book, and left four hundredweight of money in open bins with the lids off."

He stood in the middle of the yard for a while.

"They did this before," he said, "and they'll do it again, and whoever's running them knows exactly what they're for."

---

The graves were behind the shed, where Cass Ordway had said they would be. Five of them, in a row, under stones, with the turf cut and laid back over the way a person does it when they have done it before and are trying to do it properly with no time.

Rook stood at the end of the row and looked at them for a while.

Then he took the bread out of his coat.

Toren felt Milo go still beside him.

It was hard bread, the kind that travels, dark and dense and about the size of a fist, and he broke it in half and put one half back inside his coat. Then he crouched at the head of the nearest stone and set the other half on the flat of it, square in the middle, and adjusted it slightly so it sat level.

Nobody said anything at all.

He had done it in ash and gray light at the south mouth of a rock cut, nineteen days ago, for a woman he had never met, with four children watching him who had no idea what they were looking at. Toren had spent nineteen days assuming that it was something a man had thought of on the spot because he had not known what else to do.

He watched the quarter-turn of the bread on the stone.

It was the same quarter-turn. It made no difference to anything. It had made no difference to anything on that road either. It was the movement of a man doing a thing he had done a great many times in a great many places, in the same order, with the same hands.

"I didn't know them," Rook said.

He did not say anything else. He stayed crouched with his forearms on his knees for a while, and then he stood up, and turned around, and found five people looking at him.

Milo was crying without making any noise about it, which he had got very good at.

"How many times have you done that?" Toren said.

Rook walked past him and went to get water.

---

Toren found the chit twenty minutes later in the silt at the mouth of the kiln.

He was not looking for anything. He had gone up to the draw-hole because it was out of the rain and because he wanted three minutes in which nobody was looking at him. There was a flat place of wet silt under the arch where the runoff came down. Something in it was not the color of anything else.

It was a piece of brass about the size of a thumbnail, thin, square, with the corners taken off. There was a hole punched at one edge for a cord and a number struck into one face — 4 and then 1 and then something the mud had, and a small mark under the number.

The mark was a chevron with a line through it. It had been struck poorly, off center, by somebody in a hurry a long time ago, and it had been rubbed shiny at one corner by years of somebody's pocket.

He came down the wharf with it in his palm.

"Look at this — I think it's —"

Rook took it out of his hand.

He did not ask for it and he did not put his hand out and wait. He crossed nine feet of wet stone and took it out of a boy's open palm, and Toren's hand was still open and still up in front of him a full second after the thing had gone out of it.

Toren had watched this man for nineteen days. He had watched him come off a wall eighty feet above a road and land on one knee. He had watched him go up a shale fan in eleven seconds and take a girl's leg apart with his fingers in the dark on a road and never once hurry.

He had never seen him move fast at anything that was not going to hurt somebody.

Rook turned the chit over once. The rain came down on the wharf. Down the basin a moorhen went across the open water and made its noise, and Milo said something to Dessa about the shed, and none of it reached the nine feet of stone Toren was standing on.

Then Rook put the chit inside his coat and did up the button.

"Set up above the kiln," he said. "Not in the yard. Not in the shed."

"What is it?"

"Above the kiln, Toren."

"You put it in your *coat.*"

"I know what I did."

"It's —" Toren's voice came out higher than he wanted it. "That was on the *ground.* I found it. What is it, is it theirs, is it a —"

"Put the cart above the kiln, please," Rook said, "and then come back down and help me with the bins, because we are not leaving four hundredweight of ash standing in a yard for the next people down this road, and I would like it under a tarpaulin before dark."

---

They did it in an hour and it was miserable work and nobody complained once.

Afterward they sat above the kiln in the lee of the brick with a small fire that Rook had allowed on the condition that it went out at dark, and ate, and did not talk about very much.

Wyck was propped against the cart wheel with his ribs strapped and a blanket over his legs. He had not said one word since the towpath.

He said one now.

"That's not a name you were hearing for the first time."

The fire made its small noises.

"Cinder Company," Wyck said. "You asked her four things about it and you didn't ask her one thing about where they went, which is what a man asks about people he's frightened of, or what they're worth, which is what a man asks about people he's not. And you took a bit of brass out of Toren's hand like it was going in the fire."

Rook looked at the fire.

"So I'd like to know," Wyck said. "Not because I'm owed it. I'd just like to know whether the thing we're walking toward is a thing you're walking toward."

Dessa had gone absolutely still, which from Dessa meant *I am watching this and I will remember it.*

Rook did not answer for a long time.

"We're going up the lane in the morning," he said at last. "Off the canal. There's an old road on the ridge that runs north-east and it's slower and it's harder on that cart and it's got cover on both sides of it for eleven miles."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

"Is it toward them or away from them?" said Dessa.

Rook got up and put the fire out with his boot, which was two hours before he had said he would, and stood in the dark above a wharf where seven people had worked and five were under stones, looking down the lane at the gate that had been pushed inward off its hinges by somebody standing on the outside.

"North-east in the morning," he said. "Everybody sleeps. I'll take the whole watch."

---

Toren lay awake in the dark and made himself go through it the way Rook had taught them to go through a piece of ground.

*Three men stood across the gate and did not move. Everybody else was moving. Why would you put three men on an open gate you had already come through.*

He got it at some point after midnight, lying on wet turf beside a hundred-and-sixty-year-old brick kiln, and when he got it he went cold from the top down.

You would not put three men across a gate to keep anybody out. There was nothing left outside to keep out.

You would put three men across a gate so that nobody in the yard could use it.

He turned over and looked at the shape of the man sitting against the cart wheel twelve feet away — upright, hands open on his knees, facing down the lane, not moving.

*He knew that,* Toren thought. *He knew it standing at the gatepost this afternoon, and he told us about the boots and the puddle and the mud, and he told us everything except that.*

And underneath that, colder, arriving all at once the way the worst ones do:

*He didn't work it out. He recognized it.*
