# Chapter 12 — Carried

The lane out of Coldharbour went up four hundred feet in a mile and a half, and the cart did not want to do it, and they did it anyway before the light was properly up.

Nobody arranged it. That was the thing Toren noticed at the top, standing bent over with his hands on his knees and the rain coming sideways off the ridge.

Milo had gone to the near wheel without being told, because the near wheel was the one that dropped into ruts. Dessa had put herself at the head with the water can, calling the ground eight feet ahead of the shafts, which was the only job on that hill she could physically do and which she had found for herself inside the first hundred yards. Senna had got herself over to the tailgate end without any fuss at all, to put her weight where it helped, and had done it in about nine separate movements and had not mentioned any of them.

And Wyck had spent the whole climb lying propped on one elbow facing backward, with three broken ribs, saying what he could see behind them every two minutes, out loud, even when it was nothing.

*Especially* when it was nothing. Toren heard him do it the whole way up.

*That's the whole job. You look backward and you say what you see out loud, even if it's nothing, especially if it's nothing.*

His father had said that to Wyck Tanner on a gray morning at the mouth of a rock cut, nineteen days ago, and Wyck had not been given the instruction since, by anybody, and had gone on doing it.

---

The ridge road was a green lane between two old hedges that had gone up into trees. It was wet. It was slow. It was eleven miles of somewhere a person could not be seen from.

They did nine miles that day and nine the next.

The drilling did not stop. It got, if anything, more particular, and it started going to places that had nothing to do with anything that came out of a hole in the world.

On the first evening Rook made them practice meeting people on a road.

"Eighty yards. That's where it happens and it's further than you think, so pace it out and look at it, because you'll want to let them come to forty and forty is too late for everything." He put Milo and Dessa a long way off down the lane and stood the rest of them in it. "Hands out from your sides, palms where they can see them. One person talks and it's the same person every time and it is not the youngest one. You say three things: how many of you there are, how many are hurt, and which way you're going. You say them in that order and you don't embroider any of them."

"Why those three?" said Milo.

"Because those three are what a frightened person wants to know and it costs you nothing to give them. Everything after that is a trade." Rook walked back down the line. "You do not say what you're carrying. You do not say where you've come from until they've told you where they've come from. And you never, ever tell anybody how many of you can fight."

They did it eleven times. On the fourth go Rook made Wyck do the talking from the cart, flat on his back, and afterward said that it had been the best one, which made Wyck look at the sky.

Then he changed it.

"Again. This time there's five of them and they've stopped and they're not saying anything."

"What do we do?"

"You go past. Off the road on the far side, at an angle, nobody turning their back, and Wyck stays exactly where he is because you are not carrying him at a run and pretending otherwise gets people killed." He looked round. "Again. This time three of them have gone round behind you and put themselves across the lane."

Dessa said, "Why would anybody do that?"

"Again," said Rook.

"No — I'm asking. If they wanted the cart they'd come at the cart. Why would three people go past you and stand in the road behind you and not do anything?"

"They wouldn't," Rook said. "Go again."

They went again. Nobody believed him, and nobody said so, and all four of them practiced going through the three men in the road behind them until it was too dark to see the lane.

---

Toren did the sums that night and did not like them.

Twenty days ago a man had stood outside a north gate at first light and said: *You're not going to learn to fight. Fighting takes years and you don't have years and three of you don't have anything to fight with. You're going to learn not to be standing where the thing is.*

Everything for a fortnight after that had been about ground, and spacing, and short steps, and where the mass was, and none of it had one single thing in it about people.

He lay in a wet hedge on a ridge above a canal and went back through the last three days. How to look at a yard and tell what order things happened in. How to tell a pile somebody sorted from a pile somebody knocked over. Which end of a place gets taken first and why. What three men standing across a gap are for.

None of that keeps you alive against a Husk. A Husk does not sort anything.

---

Dessa told him on the second night and made him promise before she told him.

"You can't say it to Milo, because Milo will be encouraging about it, and I'd rather be dropped down a well."

"All right."

"Or to him."

"All right."

She was sitting with her bad leg straight out in front of her and her boot off, and she had her thumbs working at the muscle above the knee in a way that Toren could tell had become a habit and that he did not think she knew she was doing.

"I've tried eleven times," she said. "Since the beck. Every night. Twice in the middle of the day."

"And?"

"And nothing. Not one thing. Not a flicker of anything." She pressed her thumb in hard and her breath went out through her teeth. "And the awful part is that I can't even tell whether I'm doing it right, because the whole of doing it right was not doing nine things, and now there's a tenth thing, which is *checking whether it's working.*"

Toren, who had spent four days learning about exactly that, said nothing at all, which was the single best thing he did that week.

"He told me it'd be like this," Dessa said. "He told me the same morning. He said everybody who's ever done it once spends the next bit of their life being worse at it than they were the first day, and I nodded at him, and I thought, *well, not me, because I'll work.*" She laughed, once, with no humor in it. "And I've worked. Eleven times."

"It happened, though."

"I know it happened."

"No — that's not —" Toren struggled with it. "I was there. I watched it. It's not a thing you're remembering wrong or a thing you've made better in your head. The *dark got thinner.* I was twelve feet away and I could see the stones on the far bank."

Dessa was quiet.

"Say that again in about a month," she said, "when I've stopped being able to believe it. Would you? Just exactly like that. The stones on the far bank."

"Yes."

"Good." She put her boot back on, which took her some time and which she did entirely by herself. "That's the whole of the conversation and we're not having it again."

---

The watch rota changed on the third night and nobody discussed that either.

Dessa wrote it out on a bit of slate with a nail. She had done every rota since the flight, on the grounds that she was awake at strange hours anyway and might as well be useful at them, and Rook had looked at the first one and said "fine" and had not looked at any since.

Toren, who was on it, noticed the thing about it on the third night.

Rook's own watch had always been the last one before light. It was the worst one and he had taken it every night without being asked. On Dessa's slate it was still his, and it was still the last. It was now one hour instead of two. The hour before it had been quietly cut in half and given to two different people.

Toren looked at the slate for a while.

"He'll notice," he said.

"He's noticed. He looked at it this morning." Dessa put the nail in her pocket. "And he put it back down on the stone and didn't say anything, which from him is a signature."

Nobody mentioned it. Milo, who could not have kept a secret about a birthday, kept this one without ever having been told it was one — Toren watched him hand the man a bowl one evening and then hand him a second one about ten minutes later, in the dark, as though it were the first, and go away again before there could be any conversation about it.

He did not sleep. That was the part none of them could do anything about. He sat against the wheel or against a tree with his hands open on his knees, and on the fourth night out of Coldharbour Toren woke at some hour with no name and found him standing thirty feet down the lane in the dark, absolutely motionless, facing back the way they had come.

Toren lay still and watched for a long time. Nothing came up the lane. Nothing was going to come up the lane.

After a while the man came back and sat down against the wheel, and put his hands on his knees, and did not sleep.

---

Senna said it on the fifth evening, to Toren, in the cart, with the rain off and the light going green over the ridge.

"Come here. Sit on the tailgate where I can see you and stop hovering, you get it off your father and he got it off me."

He sat on the tailgate.

"How's the hand?"

"It's fine."

"It is not fine, you've had it under your arm twice in ten minutes." She waited him out until he gave her the hand, and turned it over, and looked at the blisters across the base of the fingers where four days of shafts and a hazel rod had been. "Hm. Well. Hands do that and then they stop doing it." She let go. "Now tell me about Coldharbour. Not what happened. What you saw him do."

Toren told her.

He told her all of it, the way he had not told anybody, because there had not been anybody to tell — the four questions and the fifth one with the flat gone out of it, and Cass Ordway on the towpath saying *you've asked me which end they came in at*, and the bread turned a quarter-turn on a stranger's stone, and a piece of brass going out of his open hand faster than he could close it.

Senna listened the whole way through without one interruption, which she had never once done in his life.

"Yes," she said at the end.

"Yes what?"

"Yes, that's what I thought it was." She folded her hands on the blanket. "I've had nineteen days of lying in a cart looking at the back of that man, Toren, and there is one thing about him that I have never been able to make fit, and it's not the strangeness. It's the opposite. He is the least strange person I have ever met. Everything he does has a reason and if you ask him he'll usually tell you the reason, and he's honest to the point of being unpleasant about it."

"So?"

"So a man like that does not take a road he cannot explain." She looked out at the green lane going north-east under the trees. "He's not running. I want you to understand that I have watched men run and this is not it, and I have watched men wander, and it is not that either." She said the rest of it quite gently. "That man is walking toward something. He's been walking toward it since before he came down off a bank into a rock cut, and he has picked up five people on the way, and he has not once turned round and gone back for any of them, which means he cannot."

Toren's mouth had gone dry.

"Then why's he teaching us?"

"Ah," said Senna Voss. "Now that's the question, isn't it."

---

The thing with Wyck happened on the sixth evening and it took about nine seconds and it did not happen at all.

Toren was doing the water. Wyck was sitting up against the cart wheel — he had got himself sitting up, on his own, over four days, in small increments that nobody was allowed to help with — and he had the iron bar across his knees and a stone, and was taking a burr off the end of it.

Toren put the can down and did not go away.

He had it ready. He had had it ready for six days and had rebuilt it about forty times, and it was short now, which he thought was probably better.

"Wyck."

"Mm."

"On the towpath. When I said hold and back off. I want to —"

"Don't."

It came out immediately and without any weight on it at all, the way a man answers a question he settled days ago.

"I'm not —"

"I know what you're not doing." Wyck turned the bar over and went at the other side of the burr. "He already did that. He stood there and he did the whole thing in front of everybody and it was correct and I heard all of it and there's nothing left in it for me."

"It isn't about him."

Wyck's hands stopped.

He looked up, and for about a second and a half there was something on his face that Toren had seen exactly once before, on a hillside above Hobb's Wall, when a boy's voice had cracked straight down the middle on the word *know.*

"No," Wyck said. "It isn't."

He went back to the bar.

"Go and do the water," he said.

---

On the seventh night out of Coldharbour they camped in a half-collapsed field barn on the ridge with the whole of the country below them going dark, and Milo asked his question.

He asked it the way he asked everything, which was straight up, at the worst possible moment, out of an entirely comfortable silence.

"If they've got a book with nine years of where the hunting is," Milo said, "then they know where people go. Don't they."

Nobody answered.

"That's what it's for. That's why you'd take a book instead of the money." He was looking at the fire with his arms round his knees. "It's a list of places where people are going to be standing."

"Milo," said Dessa.

"I'm not being — I'm just saying it out loud, because everyone's thinking it and nobody's saying it and I don't like it in my head on its own." He rubbed his face. "That's all. I'd rather it was in the room."

Rook was sitting in the barn door with his back against the post, half in and half out of the light.

"It's a good question," he said, "and it's the right one, and I'm not going to answer it tonight."

"Will you answer it?"

There was a pause that went on slightly too long.

"Yes," Rook said.

Toren's head came up.

It was not much. It was one word, and it was the shortest possible version of one word, and Rook had said *no* to that kind of question about eleven times in twenty days and had never once said anything else.

Milo did not notice. Milo said, "All right," and let it go, and went to see about the water.

Dessa noticed. Toren looked across the fire and found her already looking back at him, absolutely level, with her chin down, and she did not move a muscle of her face, and he understood perfectly.

---

He lay awake a long while after that with the rain starting again on what was left of the barn roof.

He went through it the way he went through everything, which was in numbers, because the numbers were the handrail.

Twenty-four days since a rock cut. Seven since a name on a towpath. Six since a boy had gone off a path into a dry canal because Toren Voss had said the wrong four words at the wrong second. Nineteen nights, at the very least, that a man had not lain down.

One piece of brass in a coat pocket, with a number on it and a mark under the number, that had been on the ground at Coldharbour for four days waiting for somebody to pick it up.

And a road going north-east under trees for eleven miles, which was slower and harder and had cover on both sides of it, and which nobody had been told the reason for.

The fire was down to a seam. Out in the doorway the shape of the man sat against the post with his hands open on his knees, upright, looking down the lane at a country neither of them could see.

Toren turned his head.

Six feet away, Wyck Tanner lay on his good side with his ribs strapped and his eyes wide open in the dark, and he was not looking at the fire, and he was not looking at the roof.

He was looking at the back of Rook's head, and he had been for some time.

And Toren understood, lying in a wet barn on a ridge with his hands blistered and a small steady warmth under his breastbone that had not gone out in six days, that this was not going to keep.

Somebody was going to say it. Soon, and out loud, and in front of everybody.

And it was not going to be him.
