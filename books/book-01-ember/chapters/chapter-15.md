# Chapter 15 — Forty Minutes

They came down off the high ground on the twenty-seventh day and the country changed under them in about four miles.

It went green. That was the whole of it, and it was enough to make Milo stop walking twice. The sheep pasture and the stone walls fell away behind them. The road came down through a long shoulder of bracken into a valley with a river in it, and there were trees down there with leaves on them, and a mill that still had half a wheel.

"Water," said Senna, from the cart, with her eyes shut. "Smell it. That's proper water. That's not a canal."

"How can you tell?"

"Because a canal smells like a bucket and a river smells like a road, Milo. Put me up. I want to see it."

They put her up. It cost her eleven separate movements and she did not mention any of them. Afterward she lay back and said nothing for a mile and a half, which was how Toren knew she had spent more on it than she had.

He had started tallying her coughs again. He had stopped for a fortnight because the number had been going down and he had not wanted to know when that stopped being true.

It had stopped being true four days ago.

---

Wenn's Ford was a hard gravel bottom across the river, thirty yards wide, with a made stone causeway under it that somebody had laid a very long time ago. There was a hamlet on the north bank of eleven houses, four of them lived in. There were fish traps in the fast water below the crossing. There was a drying rack on the shingle with Riftspawn ash in shallow trays under a canvas, which none of them had ever seen done before, because at Hobb's Wall you sold the flask and stopped thinking about it.

And there was a hunting outfit camped on the shingle, five of them, with two hand carts and a dog.

Rook stopped the cart at eighty yards and put his hands out from his sides and did the three things in the right order.

Then he went in and gave them the book.

---

Toren went with him. It was not discussed. It had stopped being discussed somewhere between Coldharbour and the ridge lane, and he had noticed the change and had decided to say nothing about it in case saying something ended it.

The Ridley outfit was a family — a man of sixty, his daughter, her two boys of nineteen and twenty-two, and a cousin. The man's name was Hask Ridley and he had one good eye and a way of standing with his weight on the wrong foot.

He listened to the whole of it without interrupting. Coldharbour. Nine weeks of ash left in open bins. Four tally-boards off the nails and a clean dry rectangle on a shelf. Stell Cross five days ago, and a woman who paid eleven times the worth of a loss book and said thank you with both hands.

*Who else keeps a book.*

"And Bram Stell told her nobody," Rook said. "She knew it was a lie. She went north up this road, which brings her to this ford."

Hask Ridley scratched under his jaw.

"That's a lot of telling for a man who wants nothing," he said.

"I want nothing."

"Everybody on that road wants something. That's not an insult, it's arithmetic." He put his head on one side to get his good eye onto Rook properly. "You've walked in here off the south road with a cart and a sick woman and four children and you've given me a warning for free. So I'll ask you the way I'd ask anybody. What's it for?"

"It's for you getting your book out of that cart."

"And putting it where?"

"Anywhere that isn't with the book." Rook's voice did not change. "Copy it. Split it. Give half to the woman at Marram Head and keep half. Put it in a wall. I don't care what you do with it as long as the thing and the people who made it aren't standing in the same yard."

"That's nine years of hunting ground you're asking me to hand round like bread."

"Yes."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you don't," Rook said, "and I'll have told you. That's the end of what I can do about it."

---

It was the daughter who took it.

Her name was Ferrin. She was about thirty, and she had stood through the whole of it with a scoop in her hand. At the end she looked at her father for a long moment, and then walked away up the shingle without saying anything at all.

The twenty-two-year-old laughed.

He did not mean anything by it. Toren was certain about that afterward, and it made no difference at all to what happened next.

"Cinder lot," the boy said. "They come through Marram in the spring. They paid old Sessy four bits for a barrel she couldn't shift and drank with her two nights and mended her door." He grinned round at all of them, pleased to be contributing. "They pay, don't they. That's more than the factor does."

Nobody answered him.

Toren was standing three feet behind Rook's right shoulder and could not see his face, which meant that what he had afterward was the back of a man and the sound of the river and a dog scratching itself on shingle.

What he had was this. Rook's right hand, which had been open at his side, closed.

It closed slowly, the way a thing closes when nobody has told it to. The thumb came over the first two fingers. The whole of the forearm above it went hard from the wrist to the elbow.

And it stayed like that for four seconds.

Toren counted them. One. Two. Three. Four.

Then the hand opened again.

"They pay," Rook agreed. "Water's clean above the traps?"

"It is."

"Then we'll take some and get out of your camp," Rook said, and turned round and walked past Toren without looking at him.

---

The fork was on the north bank, a hundred and forty yards past the last house, and Toren saw it before anybody said anything about it.

The road came up off the causeway and split at a stone. One arm went north, up out of the valley, into the long empty green. The other went east along the water, downstream, wide enough for carts and rutted in a way the north road was not.

The stone was a marker, four feet of it, with a lot of very old lettering on the north face that nobody had been able to read for eighty years.

There was something cut into the east face that was not old at all.

It was a chevron. It had a line struck through it. It had been cut into the stone with something narrow — a nail, a punch, the corner of a chisel — by somebody working fast, and the cuts were bright and pale where the weather had not got into them yet. Under the chevron there were four marks that might have been a number.

Toren stood in the road with his hands on the shafts and went cold from the top down.

He had seen that mark once, in wet silt, under a kiln arch, on a piece of brass the size of a thumbnail, with a rubbed-shiny corner where somebody's pocket had been for years.

"Rook," he said.

"I've seen it."

---

He stood at that stone for four or five minutes.

Toren put the shafts down and did not go closer. Milo came up and opened his mouth. Dessa put her hand flat on his chest without a word, and he shut it again. Wyck got himself up onto one elbow in the cart. Nobody spoke.

Rook did not touch the stone. He stood in front of it with his hands at his sides and looked at it, and then he turned his head and looked east along the water, down the rutted road, for a long time. The river went past. Somewhere behind them the dog barked twice on the shingle and stopped.

Toren watched him and thought about a road above a rock cut, twenty-seven days ago, where a man had stood with five people behind him and the whole north in front of him and had not moved in either direction for four or five minutes.

It was the same standing. It was exactly the same standing, and Toren had not understood it the first time either.

Then Rook came back and took the near shaft out of Toren's hands, which he had never once done.

"North," he said.

He pulled it himself for the first two miles. Nobody suggested otherwise.

---

He got the drill wrong that evening.

That was the thing Toren could not put down afterward. It was small, and nobody else even registered it as anything. It sat in his chest like a swallowed stone for the rest of the night.

They were doing the eight-feet work in a hollow above the road. Rook came at Dessa with his hands out and talking about a wheel, and Dessa called it — "Left hand" — correctly, early, the way she called everything.

And Rook said, "Good. Again, and this time count the hounds, not the —"

He stopped.

It was about a quarter of a second. He put his hand flat over his own mouth for a moment, which was a gesture Toren had never seen him make, and took it away.

"Count hands," he said. "Not people. Again."

They went again. Dessa did it four more times and got it right four more times. Nobody said one word about hounds.

Afterward Toren caught Dessa's eye across the fire and found her already looking, absolutely level, with her chin down.

She had heard it. Of course she had heard it. Dessa Cole heard the number of crows in an elm.

---

The forty minutes happened on the twenty-seventh night, between about two and three in the morning, and it ended everything.

Toren was not on that watch. He woke because Milo's boot went past his head on the turf, going fast and quiet, which meant Milo was doing the thing he had been taught to do — not shout, go and put your hand on his shoulder and say what you saw.

Toren rolled over onto his elbow.

The fire was a seam. The cart was a black shape with the hoop over it. And against the near wheel, where the man sat every night with his hands open on his knees, upright, facing the road —

He was lying down.

He had not lain down. Toren understood that immediately and with total clarity. He had gone over sideways against the wheel with his shoulder on the ground and one arm still up across his knee, in the exact posture of a man who had been sitting and had stopped.

Toren had been awake with him at that wheel eleven times in twenty-seven nights. He had never once seen his eyes shut.

Milo was three steps away and slowing down.

Toren opened his mouth.

He was never entirely sure what he had been going to say. He never got any of it out.

Milo Faust — who could not keep a secret about a birthday, and who had the single kindest instincts of anybody Toren ever met — crouched down and put his hand gently on a sleeping man's shoulder from behind.

---

It was not fast.

That was the part that stayed. Toren had seen this man come down a shale fan in eleven seconds. He had seen him take a piece of brass out of an open palm faster than a boy could close his hand. He had a whole shelf of memories of a person moving at a speed that did not look like speed.

This was not that. This was slow. It was wrong, and it was worse.

The shoulder came off the ground first, rolling away from the hand. The near arm came up across the body. The far one went round underneath and put a fist on the turf, and the whole shape of the man rose off the ground in one piece and turned. It looked like a thing a body does in water. It took perhaps a second and a half. Toren was on his feet without any memory of standing.

The seam on the Ember opened with a hard small tick.

Milo went over backward and sat down.

And it stopped.

---

It stopped in the middle. That was all.

There was a man on one knee in the dark, eighteen inches from a fourteen-year-old boy sitting on the ground. Metal had come up over the back of his left hand and out past the knuckles about four inches, and it had stopped there. It was the beginning of an edge. It had not been allowed to finish.

Nobody made any sound at all for about two seconds.

Then Rook said, "Milo."

"I'm —" Milo's voice had gone up an octave and stayed there. "I'm here. It's me. It's only me. It's the hour — you said the hour —"

"I know who it is."

The metal ran back down and closed.

Rook sat down on the turf where he was. He put both his hands flat on the ground on either side of him, which was not a thing Toren had ever seen a person do, and kept them there.

Behind Toren, in the cart, Senna Voss said, very quietly, "Dessa. Don't get up."

Dessa did not get up.

---

"How long was I down?" Rook said.

"I don't —" Milo was shaking. "I don't know. I came on at two. Wyck had the hour before me and he handed it over at two, and you were sitting up then. I've been out at the edge of the light since. I didn't look over till the fire needed doing."

"Forty minutes," said Wyck.

He was sitting up in the cart with his back against the rail. He had not made a sound in any of it, and Toren had no idea how long he had been awake.

"You went over at twenty past one," Wyck said. "I saw you go. I was on till two, and I let you."

"You let me."

"I let you," Wyck said.

Rook put one hand over his eyes for a moment.

"Right," he said. "Everybody sit down. Nobody is going anywhere and nothing has happened. I'd like all of you sitting, because I'm going to say a short thing and then walk off for a while, and I don't want any of you following me."

They sat down.

"Milo. Come here."

"I'd rather —"

"I know. Come here anyway, because if you don't do it tonight you'll not do it for a month, and I've watched that happen to people." Rook waited. Milo came, on his hands and knees, about four feet, and sat.

Rook looked at him.

"You did the job exactly as I taught it," he said. "You didn't shout. You came quietly and you put your hand where I told you to put it, on my shoulder, from where I'd said. Every single part of that was correct and I want it said out loud in front of everybody, tonight, before anybody goes back to sleep and starts rebuilding it."

"You had the —"

"Yes."

"You were *going to —*"

"I don't know what I was going to do," Rook said. "That is the truth. It is also the worst answer available. You're having it because you're owed it, and not because it's any good."

Milo's face did something complicated in the dark.

"So what do I do?" he said. "Next time. When it's the hour."

"You say my name from six feet off and you do not touch me," Rook said. "I am sorry that's the rule. I'm sorrier that nobody told you it was one. It has been the rule since before you were born."

Then he got up and walked out of the light.

---

Toren followed him twenty yards and stopped, because he had been told not to and because he had discovered somewhere around the tenth yard that he did not actually want to arrive.

Rook was standing at the edge of the field with his back to the camp.

"Go and lie down, Toren."

"I'm not —"

"I know what you're not doing." A pause. "Go and lie down."

Toren stood in the wet grass in his socks.

"Was it a dream?" he said.

The night went on making its noises. Down in the valley behind them the river said the thing rivers say, which is nothing, over and over.

"Tell Milo it wasn't him," Rook said. "Not tomorrow. Tonight, before he sleeps, in those words. It wasn't him. He needs it from somebody his own size and it'll be worth more off you than off me."

"Was it a dream?"

"Go and lie down," said Rook.

---

Toren told Milo. It took about a minute. Milo cried for some of it and then stopped. Afterward he lay with both hands under his chin the way he always slept, and his fingers were shaking. Toren stayed where he was until they stopped.

Nobody slept much.

At first light, with the fire cold and the man still out somewhere in the field, Dessa Cole got up and walked round the camp and touched four people on the arm, one at a time, without saying anything, and then sat down on a stone.

She had the slate in her hands. She was not writing on it.

"Right," she said. "We're going to talk about it now, because in about twenty minutes he'll come back over that wall and then we won't."

"Dessa —" Milo started.

"You first, then. You've got the most right to it."

Milo sat with his arms round his knees.

"I'm not frightened of him," he said.

"I know."

"That's the thing everybody's going to think and it isn't it." He rubbed his face. "I've been lying there working out what it actually was and I've got it now. He wasn't *there.* That's the whole of it. There was a man kneeling in front of me in the dark. He had my name. He had the rule about six feet. And it took him — I don't know. Two seconds? To be the one who knew it." He looked up. "I don't want to be the reason somebody has to come back from somewhere. I'd like to not be that again."

"That's fair," said Dessa.

"What's it cost him?" said Wyck.

He was in the cart with a blanket over his legs and he had not moved since the dark.

"He's been awake twenty-seven days. Not short. *Awake.*" Wyck's voice was flat. "He does the last hour and he sits the rest, and we've been taking bits of the rota off him for a fortnight and thinking we were clever. And he went over last night at twenty past one against a wheel, sitting up, in the middle of his own watch, because a body does that in the end. That's not him choosing to rest. That's a thing falling over."

"And in four days," said Dessa, "or nine, or a fortnight, it happens somewhere that matters."

Nobody said anything.

---

"I'd like to say the practical part," Dessa said, "because nobody else will, and then I'd like Senna to say the true one, because she's the only grown person here."

She put the slate down flat on her knees.

"He stopped at a stone yesterday for four and a half minutes. I counted, same as Toren did. There was a fresh mark cut into the east face of it. Toren says it's the same mark as the brass in his coat, and none of us are going to pretend otherwise." She went on in exactly the voice she used for reading out the number of crows in an elm. "He took the north road. He didn't tell anybody why. He pulled the cart for two miles, which he has never done in twenty-seven days, and he pulled it at a speed that had nothing to do with us.

"He got Milo's drill wrong last night in a way that means he was somewhere else while he was standing in front of me.

"And he told us at a stone heap, in the open, in front of everybody, that there's an account, and that it's got his whole life in it, and that there isn't anybody left to tell it but him." She stopped. "Those are the five things. I'm not putting anything on top of them."

"Then I'll put something on top of them," said Senna Voss.

---

She was sitting up against the rail with a blanket round her shoulders and her hands in her lap, and Toren saw with a small cold lurch that she had got herself up there on her own in the dark and had been sitting like that through the whole of it.

"There's a thing that happens to people who carry one thing on their own for a very long time," she said. "I'm going to describe it and none of you are going to like it, and then we'll be finished and you can all do as you please.

"It doesn't get heavier. That's the mistake everybody makes. It gets *quieter.* You get so used to the weight of it that you stop being able to feel where your own edges are. Then one day you put your hand out to steady yourself on a doorframe and there isn't one. That's the day it goes wrong." She folded her hands. "I have watched two people do it. One of them was my husband. Neither of them was a bad man and neither of them ever once did a thing on purpose."

"Senna," said Toren.

"I'm nearly finished, love." She looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, and came to rest on Dessa. "That man has kept us alive for twenty-seven days and he has not asked one of us for a single thing. So you have all decided that the polite thing is to let him have his privacy. And I want you to understand that there is a point at which letting a person keep a thing to themselves stops being a courtesy and starts being the five of us agreeing not to look."

"He said he wasn't ready," said Milo.

"He is not going to be ready." Senna's voice was perfectly gentle. "He's had eleven years to get ready. You do not ask a man like that when he's ready. You go and stand in front of him, all of you, and you make it harder to carry on than to stop."

The fire was cold. The light was coming up gray over the valley behind them, and down at the bottom of the field a man was walking back along the wall with his coat wet through to the shoulders.

Dessa stood up off her stone.

"Tonight," she said. "At the fire. All five of us, and nobody leaves, and nobody lets him make it about the road."

Nobody argued.

Wyck put his hand out flat over the rail of the cart, palm down, which was not a gesture any of them had, and did not explain it. After a second Milo put his on top of it. Dessa put hers on Milo's. Senna laid her thin one on Dessa's. Toren stood there a second too long being thirteen about it, and then put his hand on the top of the pile.

They took them back before the man got to the wall, and none of them ever mentioned it again.
