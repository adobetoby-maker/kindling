# Chapter 17 — Jab: Hold It High

The lamp had gone out at the first hour, and Jab went on sitting in the dark after the others had gone in.

He heard Toren's door, and then Callie's. He heard the stove in eleven take a new piece of wood and settle. After that there was nothing to hear but the frost working in the concrete of the row, a small dry ticking, as if the whole of Yard C were cooling down from something.

His hands were in his lap. He could not see them, but he could feel them going. It was a fine fast tremor that started somewhere up in the wrists and ran out to the ends of the fingers, and it had not stopped for three days. He had watched Rook's hands do exactly this every morning on the north lip. He had not understood, watching, that it was not something a person did. It was something that happened to a person while he sat still and waited for it to finish.

He went down to look.

He knew he should not. Rook had said *don't check*, lying in the chair at the foot of the ward bed with his own hands shaking, and Jab had understood him perfectly and gone down to look that same night, and every night since. It was like touching a tooth that has come out. You knew there was nothing there. The knowing was not the point.

Under his breastbone, a finger's width behind it, where the lean had always lived, there was nothing. There was no bucket to find, and no bottom and no line scratched on the inside of it. There was not even the dark, dry stone he had found on the bench on the first night in Meridian, when he had been empty and known he was empty. Empty had been a thing he could stand in. This was a place where a step should have been.

He came back up.

The door of twelve opened behind him, and warm air came out onto the step, and Otto's voice said, "Come in. It's cold."

---

Otto had kept the stove up. He must have been doing it for hours, a piece at a time, because the hut was warmer than it had been since the frost came, and there was a pan on the stove-box with water in it that had been hot and was going warm.

"I thought you'd want tea," said Otto. "Then I thought you wouldn't. Then I thought I'd have it ready anyway."

Jab sat down on his bed. Otto made the tea. He made it the way he did everything, carefully, with his tongue between his teeth, and when it was made he held the cup out, and Jab put out his hand for it, and they both watched the hand.

Otto did not give him the cup. He put it down on the floor between Jab's boots, where it could not be spilled, and sat down on his own bed across the stove.

"I can't sleep either," he said.

Jab looked at him. Otto was a big young man of about twenty, with a slow face and a stitched forearm that he scratched when he thought nobody was watching. He had a way of going red at things that other people would have said out loud, and he was not red now. He was looking at the stove.

"I had one of the yard lifters by the coat," he said. "On the east track. When it went. The big one who never says anything. He went down on the frost and I got him by the collar and I pulled him up the track to the lip, and I didn't look back once." He turned his hands over on his knees. "Callie was ten paces under me. And the weighbridge girl. And Toren. I didn't look back."

"Katori said up."

"Katori said up," Otto agreed. "I've been telling myself that for three days. It's true. I'd do it again. I'd pull him again." He rubbed the stitched place on his arm. "I just keep hearing the ground. The sound it made. And then I think about who was under me when I heard it."

Jab did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing. Otto did not seem to need anything. After a while he picked up the cup from between Jab's boots and held it where Jab could get both hands round it, and Jab got both hands round it, and drank.

"I'm going to ask Wyck," said Otto, "to show me how he stands. In a gap. Not the falling. I've done the falling. The other."

"He'll say you stand like a man waiting for a cart."

"He's said that already." Otto took the cup back and set it on the floor again. "He can say it again. I'd like to be the one who doesn't move, next time. Instead of the one who pulls."

Jab looked at him across the stove for a while.

"You got a man up a track in the fog," he said. "Otto. That's not nothing."

Otto went red at last.

"Is it back?" he said. "Yours. The thing."

"Don't ask me that."

"Sorry." Otto looked at the floor. Then he looked up again, the way he did when he had worked something out and wanted to be sure he had it the right way round. "I'll not ask that again. I'll ask how your hands are. I can see those."

"They're shaking."

"I know," said Otto. "I can see."

---

In the morning the long cord was still tied to the empty bed.

Jab saw it from the doors of the ward. The sixth bed on the right had been made up tight, with a clean blanket squared over it and nobody in it, and the long cord ran from its rail across the gap to the fifth bed, where it went under a blanket and came out again at a small wrist. The knot on the rail was the one he had woken up to on the twenty-fifth day, clumsy and huge, tied by a nurse who had been told how to do it twice by a four-year-old.

Nobody had untied it. Tonk had said nobody was to, and nobody had.

Tonk was on the bar.

He had both hands on the short smooth rail that hung over his bed on its chain, and he was pulling. His face was red and his mouth was shut in a line, and his shoulders came up off the pillow, and up, until he was sitting with his back curved and his arms straight above him, holding himself there.

"*Look*," he said, through his teeth.

"I'm looking."

"You're not. You're at the *door*."

Jab came down the ward and sat in the chair between the beds. His brother held himself up on the bar for the time it took Jab to sit, and then for three breaths more, and then let go and fell back onto the pillow with an enormous sigh.

"That's further," he said.

"That's much further."

"I did it eleven times yesterday. The nurse counted. She's bad at counting." Tonk turned his head on the pillow and looked at Jab's hands, which were in Jab's lap. He looked at them for a long time, with the frank attention he gave to everything that was new. "Your hands are doing Rook."

"They'll stop."

"When?"

"When they're ready."

Tonk considered this. He did not seem to find it a satisfying answer, but he had heard a great many unsatisfying answers in this building and had learned to put them somewhere.

"You can hold the bar," he offered. "It stops mine."

"It's your bar."

"You can *borrow* it," said Tonk, with the generosity of a man who knows exactly how valuable a thing is.

In the next bed, Senna lay against her pillows with her hands folded on the blanket where she could see them. She had been watching the whole of this with her pale eyes, and she did not say anything, but when Jab looked across at her she moved one of her folded hands a little, a quarter of an inch, which was as close as Senna came to a wave.

"He did eleven," she said. "I counted too. I'm better at it than the nurse."

---

Mrs Lask stopped him at the desk on his way out.

She did not usually stop anybody. She sat behind her list and said *visiting is in the afternoons* and let the building go past her. This morning she had a second list, on a separate sheet, and she put her hand flat on it when she saw him coming, as if it might blow away.

"I haven't sent anybody to you," she said. "I want you to know that. I've sent every one of them to Doctor Sowerby, or to the desk on the other side, or home."

"Every one of who?"

She looked at him over the top of her spectacles. Then she turned the sheet round on the desk so that he could read it.

There were five lines on it, in her small upright hand. A name he did not know and *girl, 6, fever three days, would not break.* A name and *husband in the east ward, wants to be told whether he's worse.* A name and *burn, left hand, the smithy.* A name and *baby, not feeding.* A name and nothing else, and then in brackets, *would not say what for. Asked for "the boy."*

The first line had a mark beside it in a different ink. *Broke Tuesday night. Sowerby's powders.*

"The mother came back to tell me," said Mrs Lask. "She said to tell the girl at the weighbridge that she was right. The girl with the hook." She took the sheet back. "She wanted to tell you as well. I said I'd pass it on."

Jab stood at the desk.

"I can't do anything for any of them," he said.

"I know."

"I can't even—" He stopped. He looked at his hands, and then did not look at them. "I can't find it. The thing they're asking for. It's not there."

"I know that too," said Mrs Lask. "I've written it down." She tapped the bottom of the sheet, where there was a line he had not read, drawn under all the others. *Not fit. Not asked. Not to be sent for.* "That's the answer I give them. It's the true one, and it's the one they don't want. I've been giving that kind of answer for twenty years. It doesn't get any easier to give, but it gets easier to stand behind."

She put the sheet away in a drawer, under the other list.

"Visiting," she said, "is in the afternoons."

---

Rook was lying on the cot in the east shed when Jab came in, with his coat over him and his boots off and his eyes open, looking at the tin roof.

He had been there most of three days. He had held the whole bottom of the Sag alone for thirty-one minutes, and then he had sat all night in a chair at the foot of Jab's bed, and after that Sowerby had come across the yard and said things to him in front of everybody that Jab had heard about from four different people, all of whom had enjoyed it. Since then he had been on the cot. He got up to eat. He got up to go outside. Each time, Jab had heard, he got up in more movements than he had the day before, and nobody was allowed to count them out loud.

Dee was at the table with the brass scale.

"Sit," she said, without looking round. "Show me your hands."

Jab sat on the other side of the table and put his hands on it. They lay on the wood and shook. Dee looked at them for a while without touching them. Then she put two fingers on the inside of his left wrist and looked away at the stove while she counted.

"Still nothing?" she said.

"Nothing to look at."

"You've been looking."

"Every night," said Jab.

"I know. He told me not to tell you to stop, because he says it'll make you look more." She took her fingers away. "He's right about that, which is annoying. I'm not going to tell you to stop. I'm going to find out what you can do with nothing to look at. Sit still."

She weighed out ash. She did it the way she had done it on the twenty-sixth day, with the horn spoon, tipping grey powder from a jar into the little pan until the beam came level, and then tipping the pan out onto a square of paper. It was a tenth of a Handful. It was a heap Jab could have hidden under the end of his thumb.

"Palm on it," she said. "Flat. Like lifting."

"I haven't got anywhere to put it."

"I didn't ask where you'd put it. I asked you to put your hand on it." She put her own two fingers on the inside of his other wrist, the way she had in the shed on the twentieth day, and waited. "Go on."

He put his palm flat on the paper.

It came up into his hand. It came the way it always came, cold and heavy and slow, like water finding its way into a boot, and there was nobody in it. He felt it arrive in the heel of his palm and lie there. And then, before he had done anything at all, before he had even thought about where it might go, it went. It went up through his wrist and out through his other wrist, fast, into the two fingers lying on it.

Dee's eyebrows went up a hair.

"Same as the twentieth," she said. "About the time it takes to swallow." She did not take her fingers away. "You don't hold. You pass. I told you that. I didn't know until now that you'd pass with nothing in the house."

"Is that bad?"

"It's interesting." She let go and wrote in her book. "It means the door's fine. Your door's a door. It opens and things go through it. It's your house that's empty." She looked up. "That's worth knowing, Jab. Some people go through their floor and the door shuts behind them, and it takes a season to open again. Yours didn't shut. You've just got nothing behind it."

Jab looked at his palm. There was a faint grey dust in the creases of it still, not much.

"So it'll come back," he said.

"I didn't say that. I said the door's open." She put the pencil down. "Whatever's supposed to be behind it will come back when it comes. I can't hurry it, and I'm not going to pour ash into an empty house to try. Ash wants a floor to settle on. You haven't got one this morning. It'd run through you like water through a sieve, and you'd feel full for about a minute, and then you'd feel worse than you do now."

---

"Hold it high," said Rook, from the cot.

They both looked at him. He had not moved. He was still lying with his coat over him and his eyes on the roof.

"Like a lift," he said. "On the track. You put your palm on the drift and it comes up into your hand, and you don't let it go down into you. You hold it in the hand, high and open, till you're at the jar. Every lifter does it. Callie does it better than I do." He turned his head on the cot and looked at Jab. "You've done it. On the east track, the week before the lift. I watched you. You held a Handful in your hand all the way from the drift to the jar."

"That was different."

"Was it?"

Jab thought about it. He thought about kneeling on the dry stone of the east track with his palm flat on a drift, and the ash coming up, and holding his hand up and open with the fingers apart, the way Rook had shown Callie on the first morning, and walking with it to the jar. It had not gone anywhere. He had not let it.

"It stayed in the hand," he said slowly.

"It stayed in the hand," said Rook. "Because you held it there. You didn't ask it to settle. You didn't ask it to go anywhere. You carried it." He looked back at the roof. "Try that."

Dee looked at Rook for a long moment. Then she weighed out another tenth.

This time Jab put his palm on it and felt it come up into his hand, and before it could go, he lifted the hand. He lifted it high, and turned it palm up, and spread the fingers, the way a lifter walks a Handful to the jar. He did not let it down into his wrist.

It stayed.

He could feel it in his palm. It was a small cold weight, like a coin that had been left out in the frost, and it lay in the hollow of his hand and did not want anything, and it did not go through him. It did not go anywhere. He held it there, and counted his breaths, and at eleven the bucket at the far end of the shed went *tick* into its water, and he jumped, and the ash went out of his palm and through his wrist and was gone. He felt it go down into the place where there was nothing, and not land.

"Eleven breaths," said Dee. She had not stopped watching his face. "And then the drip frightened you."

"It went."

"It went," she agreed. "That's what carried ash does when you flinch. Every lifter on the Sag has lost a Handful to a flinch. Elias lost one to a woman shifting her feet on a lip." She wrote. "That's not settling. I want that clear before anybody gets excited. Settling's when it becomes part of your reserve, and it stays when you're frightened, and you can spend it later. This is carrying. You've got it in your hand the way you'd have water in your hand. It stays while you keep your hand still."

"But he can carry it," said Rook.

"He can carry it for eleven breaths in a quiet shed."

"He can carry it," said Rook again, "with nothing in the house."

Dee put her pencil down and turned round on her stool and looked at him properly.

---

"I know what you're thinking, Elias," she said. "I'd like you to stop thinking it where he can hear."

"I'm thinking he could work off a jar."

"I know you are."

"One hand on the jar and one on whoever he's working on. The jar goes into his hand and through him and out the other hand, and he never touches what's his." Rook lifted himself onto one elbow on the cot, slowly, and stopped there. "He passes. You said so. So let him pass."

"He passes in a shed with my fingers on his wrist and nothing on the other end but me," said Dee. "You want to put him on a person."

"I want to put him on a person because that's the only place he'll ever learn it." Rook's voice did not rise, but it went harder. "You can't learn a body off a dish, Dee. You can learn which is yours and which isn't. You can learn how much there is. You can't learn what's wrong in a person unless there's a person there with something wrong in them. That's the whole of it. It isn't a thing you can be told. It's a thing you have to find with your hand."

"And when he finds it," said Dee, "and he's got his hand on it, and the jar's empty, and he doesn't notice because he never notices, because he's busy wanting the person to live? What then?"

Rook did not answer.

"I'll tell you what then," said Dee. "Then he passes himself. He does what he did on the slab, only faster, because there's no bucket to empty first. There's no warning. The jar goes dry and the next thing through the door is him." She turned back to the table. "Not until his bucket's back. Not until he's got a floor to stand on and I've measured it. I'm not having him on a person with nothing behind the door."

"He was on a person with nothing behind the door on the twenty-fifth."

"Yes," said Dee, without turning round. "And I lifted his hand off her. I'd like never to do that again."

The shed was very quiet. Jab sat at the table with the grey dust in the creases of his palm and looked from one of them to the other. It was the same argument as on the second floor, the day of the reviews. He had thought then that it was an old one. He could see now that it had never been finished, and that both of them knew it, and that neither of them knew how to finish it.

---

Rook lay back down.

He did it slowly, one hand on the frame of the cot, and when he was down he lay with his eyes on the roof for a long time.

"Vell had a table," he said.

Dee's pencil stopped.

"At Homura," said Rook. "In the long room, under the east windows. A long table with benches down both sides, and jars on it, and those little glasses, like hers. The medical children sat at it every morning for two hours before breakfast. Every morning. Nine of them, the year I came." He did not look at either of them. "They took ash in, and let it settle, and spent it into a dish of water or a sick dog or each other, and then they did a thing Vell called venting, and then they sat still for a quarter of an hour, and then they did it again. All morning. Every morning. Writing it down. The same thing, over and over, and Vell going up and down behind the benches with his stick."

"I know," said Dee. "I sat at it."

"I know you did." Rook turned his head and looked at her. "I didn't. I was in the doors line. With Vera, and Ander, and the others. We were the ones he was trying to prove things about. The medical line was the dull end of the station. I walked past that table every morning for three years on my way to the yard, and I thought it was the most tedious thing I'd ever seen, and one morning when I was fifteen I said so, out loud, standing behind Vell's chair."

"What did he do?"

"He hit me across the back of the legs with his stick," said Rook, "and told me I'd want it one day, and I'd have to learn it off a child." He looked back at the roof. "I've been using a thing for eleven years that I never once sat down at that table for. I found out where my floor was lying on a riverbank four nights running. I found it again on the Level. I found it on the north road and in the Sag. I've never once known where it was until I was under it, because the one place anybody ever taught it, I walked past with my nose in the air."

Nobody said anything. The bucket went *tick*.

"She's got his method," said Rook. "I've got the doing. The doing isn't worth much if you can't come back from it, and I can't teach him to come back from it, because I've never learned. That's the truth, and I've been lying on this cot for three days working up to saying it."

Dee sat very still on her stool with her back to him.

"Then sit at mine," she said.

"I'm too old for your table."

"You're thirty, Elias."

"I'm thirty with a hole in my back."

"Then you'll sit at it with a hole in your back," said Dee, "and I'll go up and down behind you. I haven't got a stick." She picked her pencil up. "I'll find one."

---

Rook tried to get up before Jab left.

He did it without being asked, and without saying he was going to. He swung his legs off the cot and put his stockinged feet on the concrete and sat there with his hands on his knees. Then he took his hands off his knees and folded his arms across his chest, and leaned forward, and tried to stand up without them.

He got an inch off the cot. Then he sat back down.

"Tomorrow," said Dee, without looking round.

"Tomorrow," said Rook.

Jab stood in the doorway with his hands in his coat pockets, where they could shake without anybody watching. He looked at Dee's back, bent over the book. He looked at the little brass glass on the table and the jars in their rack.

"Teach me that," he said. "The table. His."

Dee turned round.

"It's twelve weeks," she said. "That's what it was at Homura. Twelve weeks at the table before Vell would let any of us near a person. Some of the children took twenty."

"We haven't got twelve weeks."

She looked at him for a long time. He saw her understand what he meant, and what he had not said, and decide not to say it for him.

"No," she said. "We've got whatever the man in the shed says we've got." She put the pencil down. "Listen to me, Jab. I'll teach you the table. I'll start tomorrow, whether you can find your bucket or not, because there's a lot of it you can do with your hands before you need anything behind the door. But I'm not teaching it to you so you can go somewhere." She held his eyes. "I'm teaching it to you so you come back from wherever you go. Those are different. I'd like you to hear the difference."

"I heard it."

"Good," said Dee. "Go and see your brother. It's nearly the afternoon."

He went out to the Sag at first light on the thirtieth day because his feet took him there.

He had gone every morning for two weeks to stand in the one door on the west shoulder. His body had learned the way. It got him up in the dark and got his boots on and took him out through the north yard to the gate before he had properly thought about it, and the gate crew let him through the small door and said *morning* the way they said it to everybody. It was only when he came round the lip path in the half-dark, with the fog lying in the bottom of the dip like milk, that he remembered there was nothing to stand in the door for.

There was no holder.

The hollow at the south lip was empty. He saw it from the path, a saucer-sized dip in the gravel at the edge of the made road, with frost lying in the bottom of it, and nobody standing in it. There had been somebody standing in it every morning for two years. Now there was only the frost.

The Sag was not held. And it was not empty either.

There were lamps on poles at both lips, four at the south and three at the north, and more down the east track, and they were set wrong. Jab saw that before he saw anything else. They were not set to light the track for lifters. They were set in a line along the upper edge of the east track, bright and close, with a gap between the third and the fourth where there was no lamp at all. The gap made a dark lane running down from the lip toward the ditch.

Katori was standing at the top of the dark lane with her grey blade on her fist, lit, lying flat along the edge like a stove across a room.

Dessa was at the south lip, sitting on a crate with her bad leg out straight and her eyes shut. Ines was on the west rim above the pipe with her bow. The weighbridge girl was halfway down the lip path with her left arm in plaster to the elbow and her straight grey blade on her right fist, dark. There were two yard lifters with long iron bars. There was nobody lifting, and nobody down in the dip at all.

"Two," said Dessa, with her eyes shut. Her voice was flat and carried. "Coming up the ditch by six. Husks. The second one's slower."

Katori did not move. The lamps along the track stood in their line with their yellow blurs in the fog. Jab watched the fog below the track, and after a while he saw it move, and two low shapes came up out of it, the colour of an old wasp nest, and went straight up the slope at the nearest lamp.

They did not get there. Ines loosed from the rim and the first one stopped moving. The second reached the lamp-line and one of the yard lifters met it with his bar, clumsily, and knocked it over, and the weighbridge girl came down the lip path at a run with her blade lit and put it through the husk's back one-handed. It came apart into grey on the frost.

"Two," said Dessa. "Both down. Nothing else inside thirty feet."

The weighbridge girl stood over the ash with her blade still lit, breathing hard, and then let it go dark and looked at her plaster.

"I can do it *one-handed*," she said, to nobody in particular.

---

Katori saw him on the path. She came up along the lip toward him with her blade gone dark and her cap pulled down, and stopped a pace away.

"You're not fit," she said.

"I know."

"Then go home." She said it without any unkindness at all. "There's nothing for you to stand in. There's nobody to stand behind."

"What is this?"

Katori looked back at the dip. The fog was going thin at the top now, and Jab could see the grey lying on the frost below the lamp-line where the husks had come apart, in two small smears. Nobody was going down to it.

"It's a watch," she said. "Not a hold. There's no holder. There's him on a cot and her in a bed with her spleen in a dish, and nobody else on the register who can lay a line. So we don't lift. We watch. We light the track so the husks come up at the lamps, and we leave the dark so the hounds come up where we want them. Your trick." She did not smile. "Dessa sits at the lip with her eyes shut and tells us what's coming. We kill it at the lips before it gets to the road. And the ash lies where it falls."

"Why don't you lift it?"

"Because there's no line." Katori nodded at the dip. "If you kneel on that track with your hands on a drift and something comes up the ditch behind you, there's nothing between you and it but your own back. That's what a holder's for. We haven't got one, so nobody kneels." She was quiet for a moment. "Every morning we don't lift, a Flask or so goes into the ditch and the silt and the wind. It's the station's money. It's somebody's winter. We stand on the lip and watch it go."

Jab looked at the empty hollow at the south lip.

"How long?"

"Till he can stand up," said Katori. "Or till she can. Sowerby says she can't. So it's him." She looked at Jab. "Go home. You'll be more use to us with your hands still."

He went. At the top of the lip path he looked back once. The fog had gone off the bottom of the Sag, and he could see the slabs, and the white post with the six on it leaning out of the black water, and the dropped corner beside it where he had knelt with his hand on a woman's chest. The day holder's hollow at the south lip had the sun in it now, and the frost in the bottom was going to water.

Behind him, across the north yard, at the long window on the second floor of the yard office, there was a light on. It was the only lit window in the building. He could not see anybody in it.

---

The board at the weighbridge had a row of blanks down it.

He saw it at the middle of the morning, on the way to the ward. The painted names ran down one side in white, and the chalk weights down the other, and along the top of the board the days were painted in a row: the twenty-fourth, the twenty-fifth, the twenty-sixth, up to today. Under every day since the twenty-fifth, against every name, there was nothing at all.

Against the line for hut twelve there was still a Flask of hound ash from the north road night. And beside it, in the space for the weight, in big uneven capitals, the word the night crew had chalked on the twenty-seventh day: *SUSSTAIN.*

Somebody had drawn a box round it in chalk, carefully, with a ruler.

"He did that," said Callie's mother, from the hut window. She was on her stool with the tally board on her knee and her stick against the wall, and she nodded at the old man in the leather apron, who was inside at the beam. "Yesterday. With the ruler off the test-weight shelf. He said if it was going to stay up, it was going to stay up straight."

Jab looked at the box.

"People keep asking," he said.

"They ask at the pump," said Callie's mother. "They ask me, because I'm on a stool in a window and everybody in the station walks past it twice a day." She moved the board on her knee with her left hand. "I tell them you're a boy of seventeen with a fish knife and a brother in a bed. I tell them you did one thing, once, in a ditch, that nobody else did, and it nearly killed you, and you're not doing it again this week." She looked at him steadily. "That's what I tell them. If you'd like me to tell them something else, you'll have to tell me what."

"That's right," said Jab. "What you tell them."

"I know it's right," said Callie's mother. "I've been telling true things at windows for a month." She wrote something on the board, slowly, with her left hand. "My daughter says your hands are shaking."

"They are."

"Mine shake when I'm tired," she said. "The left one. It's got to do the work of two, and it tells me about it in the evening." She did not look up. "Put them in your pockets and go and see your brother. He'll not care what they're doing."

---

Toren came to the step of twelve at dusk with a paper in his hand.

Jab was already sitting there. He had come back from the ward at the fourth hour, because the ward was having a bad afternoon for somebody three beds down and the nurses had wanted the visitors out, and he had sat down on his step with his hands in his pockets and not got up again. The lamp at the end of the row had not come on yet. There was still a little light in the sky over the berm, grey going to nothing.

Toren sat down on the step of eleven, one door along. He did not say anything. He held the paper on his knee with his good hand, and the seam across his palm, which Keel had stitched again on the twenty-fifth day, showed pink along the edge of it.

"You've been writing," said Jab.

"Since last night." Toren looked at the paper. "My father used to do it. On the road. He'd write the number of days, and then under it the ifs. Rook said to do it his way. With the ifs written down."

"Can I see?"

Toren held it out.

It was a sheet torn from the back of Dessa's book, and it was written on both sides in Toren's careful slow hand. At the top, underlined, there were two words. *Caul Hill.* Under that there was a number, *six days*, and a line through it, and *six days there, six back, and however long inside*, and under that, in a column, the ifs.

*If the boxes are still on the floor where Gran says.*
*If they're the same kind (the mark, the wheel with four spokes).*
*If a warm one is a live one (ask the man in the shed).*
*If the sky over the plant is a rip, and what comes out of it.*
*If there's ash on the ground up there like the Director said, and whether we can lift it.*
*If Rook can stand.*
*If we can carry enough jars.*
*If somebody can hold people up the whole time they're inside the fence.*
*If the Director says yes.*

There were more on the back. Jab read all of them. Some of them had costs written after them, in brackets, in a different pencil, small and neat, and he knew the hand. *(a day)*, *(a person)*, *(Pell says this one's two)*. Against *If somebody can hold people up the whole time they're inside the fence*, there was nothing in brackets at all.

He read that one twice.

"You haven't priced that one," he said.

"I don't know how." Toren looked down the row at the unlit lamp. "Pell doesn't either. He said to ask the people who'd be doing it."

Jab sat with the paper on his knee.

He thought about a quarter of a Handful weighed out onto paper and going into a man's shaking hands, and a woman standing in her own Ward afterward for two turns and a half of a brass glass, exactly where she had stood at first light. He thought about a Handful, settled, that had carried the day holder up the road on a stretcher to the lip. He thought about hundreds of grey boxes lying on a floor where a crew had dropped them because they were heavy and warm, and eleven days, and an old woman's hands.

"Have you got a pencil?" he said.

Toren gave him one.

Jab wrote at the bottom of the back of the sheet, under all the others. His hand shook, and the letters came out big and bad, like Rook's on the twenty-second day, and he did not try to make them neat.

*If J. can't find it.*

He gave the paper back.

Toren read it. He read it for a long time. Then he folded the sheet once, carefully, along the middle, with the new line on the inside.

"Then I'll cross it out when you can," he said.

---

The lamp came on at the end of the row, flat and white.

Down the row, on the step of fourteen, Callie had come out and sat down with her knees up. She did not ask what they were looking at. She looked at the lamp. After a while, without looking round, she said, "Katori says the watch had four this morning. Before the fog went. Three husks and a hound. All at the lips." She was quiet. "Nobody lifted any of it."

"I saw," said Jab.

"Mum says the store under the weighbridge is going down," said Callie. "She's got the book. She reads it out to me at night when she can't sleep. It's the only thing she reads out now." She looked at the lamp. "She says the Director comes down and looks at it herself. Every evening. At the book. She stands at the window and reads the number and doesn't say anything and goes back up the stair."

Nobody answered her.

Jab sat on his step with his hands in his pockets.

He wanted to go down and look. It was the hour when he always went. The light had gone, and the lamp was on, and the others were quiet on their steps, and some part of him that had nothing to do with deciding was already turning to go down under his breastbone to the place where the bucket had been, to look at the nothing there and come back up.

He did not go.

He sat on the step and made himself not go, the way he had once made himself not ask a thing that was right four times in five whether his brother was worse. It was harder than it should have been. It was like holding a tenth of a Handful in the palm of his hand in a quiet shed with a bucket going *tick* at the far end, waiting for the drip, and not flinching.

He counted his breaths instead. At eleven, he nearly went. At twenty, he did not. At forty he stopped counting, and sat on his step in the cold under the flat white lamp between the other two, and found that he had not looked, and that it had cost him something, and that he was still there.

# Chapter 18 — Toren: Five Questions

Rook got up off the cot without his hands on the thirty-first morning.

Toren saw it because he had come to the east shed at first light with the paper folded in his coat, meaning to ask Rook one more time whether it was ready. He came in out of the frost and stopped just inside the door. Rook was sitting on the edge of the cot with his stockinged feet on the concrete and his arms folded across his chest, the way he had sat for two mornings running, and he was leaning forward.

He came up off the cot in one movement. It was slow, and it was not graceful, and at the top of it he stood swaying with his arms still folded and his face gone the colour of the blanket. But his hands had not touched anything. He had got up off a bed on his own two legs, which was a thing Toren had watched him fail to do for three days.

Dee was at the table with her back to him. She did not turn round.

"One," she said, and wrote it down.

Rook stood there until the swaying stopped. Then he unfolded his arms and looked at Toren in the doorway.

"Well," he said. "Read it to me again."

Toren read it again. He had written it on the night of the twenty-ninth, at the stove in eleven, after everybody else was asleep but Dessa, who had lain on her bed with her eyes open and her bad leg out straight and said nothing, which was how Dessa watched people. He had written it the way his father had written a road: the number at the top, and under the number the ifs, one to a line, as many as he could think of. He had shown it to Pell on the thirtieth, and Pell had sat on the floor of fourteen with it for an hour and written costs after some of the ifs, in brackets, in his small neat hand. He had shown it to Jab on the step that evening, and Jab had written one more at the bottom.

Rook listened to the whole of it standing up. When Toren got to the last line, *If J. can't find it*, he did not say anything for a while.

"Take it upstairs," he said.

"You come."

"I'll come," said Rook. "I'll stand in the room. I won't read it." He looked at Toren. "It's your paper. You'll say it."

---

The outside stair of the yard office was steel, and it rang, and Rook climbed it at the pace of a man carrying something up a ladder. Toren went a step below him, not holding, and counted the rings of Rook's boots on the treads without meaning to. Dee came behind Toren. Behind Dee came Pell with the atlas under his arm, and behind Pell came Jab with his hands in his pockets, and Callie last, with the hook left at the bottom of the stair because the Director's office was not a place you took a hook.

Toren had asked them all. He had asked them the night before, on the steps, one after another. He had not known how to ask it, so he had asked it plainly. *I'm taking it to her in the morning. You're on it. Will you come.* Nobody had said no. Nobody had said much of anything.

The man in the long coat opened the door before they knocked.

The long room was the same as it had been on the thirteenth day. The window ran the whole length of the north wall, and through it Toren could see the yard and the berm and the road beyond the berm, and at the end of the road, small and sharp, the south lip of the Sag. There was nobody standing on it. There were lamps on poles along the east track, set in a line, and a small figure sitting on a crate at the lip that would be Dessa, and another figure that would be Katori. The slate on its easel had a new number chalked at the bottom of the column. *26.*

The Director sat at the head of the long table with four sheets squared in front of her and her spectacles on their cord.

"Sit," she said. "Mr Marsh, the chair with arms. I'm told you stood up this morning. Congratulations. Don't do it again in my office."

Rook took the chair with arms.

The Director looked at them one at a time. She put her spectacles on to look at her sheets and took them off to look at the people, and she did it twice.

"Mr Voss," she said. "You asked to see me. You've brought six people and a paper. Say what's on it."

Toren stood up. He had not meant to stand. He found that he could not say it sitting down.

He unfolded the paper and read it. He read it the way Senna had told him to write it, as he had written it, not tidied. *Caul Hill. Six days north and west on the old line.* The fence. The instrument rooms under the main building, down the stairs. The racks, and the boxes on the floor where a crew had thrown them in 2130, hundreds, with the same mark as the plate he had copied in the shed. The sky gone wrong over the old plant since the summer, and the ash lying in drifts that nobody lifted. The fourth box, and the fifth waking slow beside it. Then the ifs, every one, with Pell's prices after them where Pell had put them. *If Rook can stand. If we can carry enough jars. If somebody can hold people up the whole time they're inside the fence.* The one Jab had written at the bottom. And the last, which he had written first, and moved to the end because he did not know where else to put it: *If the Director says yes.*

When he had finished he folded the paper and stood with it in his good hand.

The Director had not moved.

"No," she said.

---

She did not say it hard. She said it the way Pell said a distance.

"I'm going to tell you why," she said, "because you've earned the why. You've written a better paper than half the ones that come up that stair from my engineers, and you've written the ifs down, which nobody does. So here's the why, and it's not one thing. It's five, and then one more."

She held up her hand and put down a finger for each of them.

"One. How much ash? Not *enough jars*. How many Flasks, graded how, clean or thin, and where from? There are thirty-one Flasks and a Handful in the store under my weighbridge. There's nothing coming in, because my Sag's been on a watch since the twenty-fifth with nobody lifting. Your mother's the tally, miss," she said to Callie, without looking at her. "She'll have told you the number. If you want some of it, tell me how much, and why that much, and what happens when it's gone.

"Two. How long inside the fence? Hours? Days? What does the fence cost a person per hour, and how do you know? Mrs Voss lost her hands in eleven days. You'll not be there eleven days. So what will you lose in one?

"Three. Who holds whom up, and in what order? There are three people on my register who can put a hand on a body and keep it going. One of them's in that chair and stood up for the first time this morning. One's against the wall by the door with a crooked wrist. One's on the floor by the stove with his hands in his pockets, because he can't find whatever it is he's supposed to have, and his own card says it doesn't come when he calls it." She did not look at Jab. "You want all three on one hill, which means that while they're on it, this station has none. I'd like to know how they take turns, and who watches the one whose turn it is.

"Four. When do you turn round? Don't tell me *when it's bad*. Everybody turns round when it's bad. By then it's too late to turn. Write me the line. The actual line, in numbers, and who draws it, and who's allowed to cross it.

"Five." She put down the last finger and looked at her closed hand. "Who holds my Sag?"

Nobody answered.

"There's one holder on my register," said the Director. "He's in that chair. My Sag's been unheld for six days. It's being watched by a woman in a cap and a girl who can't walk down a slope, with lamps on poles, and it's working, and I don't know how long it'll go on working. Twenty-six last week. You're asking me for the only man in this station who can lay a line across that dip, and you're asking to take him six days away with a hole in his back and put him in a fence that took a woman's hands."

She put her hand flat on the table.

"And the one more," she said. "Mr Marsh's hands. You've written *if Rook can stand.* You haven't written what happens when he can't. When he's on that hill and he's done his share, or half of it, and he can't do the rest. Who does the rest? What does it cost the ones who do it? You've not priced him, Mr Voss. You've written him down like a road."

---

Toren stood with the paper in his hand.

He had known it would be no. He had known on the stair, and on the step last night, and at the stove in eleven with the pencil in his hand, and he had thought that knowing would make it easier to stand in front of. It did not. He stood at the long table with six people behind him who had come because he asked, and he felt the no go into him and lie there like a stone in a pocket.

"The machine," he said. "The man in the shed says—"

"He came up my stair this morning," said the Director. "Before you. He does it every week now. He stands where you're standing and tells me how long, and I write it down." She put her spectacles on and looked at her top sheet. "The fifty-fourth day. By the fifty-fourth day he's guessing. He says guessing isn't stopping. He says Sowerby can dose Mrs Voss off her blood, the way they did before there were machines, for a few cycles, badly." She took the spectacles off. "I know about the machine, Mr Voss. It's the only one there is. Without it, this station is a berm with a hospital in it that can't see inside anybody. I've known that for thirty years. I know it better than anybody in this room, and I'm still saying no."

"Then when?"

The Director looked at him.

"When you bring me the answers," she said. "All five, and the one more. Written down by people who were there. Not by a man at a window, and not by me." She glanced at Rook, and something moved at the corner of her mouth. "I'm told that's a condition in this yard."

"And then you'll say yes."

"Then I'll say no to a plan," said the Director, "instead of saying no to a hope. It's a different thing to say no to. I might say it differently. I might not." She folded her hands. "That's all I've got for you today."

At the end of the room the man in the long coat was writing.

Toren folded the paper again and put it in his coat, on the side with the copied plate. He sat down. He had not known that his legs would want to, and they did. Beside him Callie was looking at the Director with her whole face very still, reading her. On the floor by the stove Jab had not taken his hands out of his pockets.

"Mr Voss," said the Director, as they got up to go.

He turned at the door.

"You said your father did it this way. The ifs."

"Yes."

"Did his paper have prices on it?"

Toren thought about the paper he had carried down the road for sixty-four days. Nineteen days, and three ifs, and nothing after any of them.

"No," he said.

"Then it wasn't finished," said the Director. "Yours isn't either. That's not a criticism. It's a description." She looked down at her sheets. "Go and finish it."

In the afternoon Jab taught him how to light less.

It happened in the first lane by accident, because Toren had gone there instead of back to eleven and Jab had followed him, and neither of them had wanted to talk about the long room. Wyck was on top of the sand heap with his bar across his knees. Wyck's pendulum sack hung from the timber frame at the end of the lane on its rope, turning a little in the wind.

Toren let the spike come and asked it for the light.

It came. It came the way it had come in the door on the twenty-fifth day, thin and hard and white-grey along the whole inner curve of the crooked blade, from the root to the point, and it shook. It always shook when he first asked. He held it and it steadied, and he stood in the lane with the whole of the ugly thing lit on his fist and felt it drawing on him from somewhere behind his breastbone, like water going out of a barrel through a pinhole.

"Why all of it?" said Jab.

Toren looked at him.

"You light the whole of it," said Jab. "Every time. Root to point. Why?"

"Because that's what it does."

"It's what it does when you ask it for the light. It isn't what it does when you ask it for the light *there*." Jab had his hands in his pockets still. He nodded at the spike. "Which bit do you use?"

Toren thought about it. He thought about the ditch at the jam, hooking branches out of the water with the heel. He thought about the husk on the slab in the fog, and the weighbridge girl going down, and putting the heel into the husk's shoulder and dragging it round before he put in the point. He thought about the door, and the husks coming up the slope at his light, and what he had put through them.

"The heel," he said slowly. "Mostly. And the point, at the end."

"Then why are you lighting the middle?"

Toren did not have an answer.

"You're lighting all of it because you don't know which bit you'll need," said Jab. "So you pay for all of it, all the time, in case. I did the same at thirteen. My aunt used to stand behind me at the racks and tell me I was lighting a whole knife to cut a string." He took one hand out of his pocket and made a small shape in the air, the length of his first finger. "Choose. Light what you'll use. Leave the rest dark. It's cheaper, and it holds longer, because there's less of it to hold."

"How?"

"Look at where you want it," said Jab. "You told me that. On the step. The light stops where you're looking."

Toren looked at the heel.

He let the light go out along the spike, and then he asked for it again, and this time he did not look at the whole blade. He looked at the heel, the bad thick knob at the root of the crooked thing that stuck out at an angle like the back of a boathook. He kept his eyes on it.

The light came along the edge from the root. It came as far as the heel, and lit it, a hand's width of thin white-grey lying along the hooked curve, and then it went on past the heel toward the middle of the blade, because it always had, and he felt it go and felt the cost go with it.

He let it go out.

"Again," said Jab.

It took him nine tries. On the ninth the light came along the edge to the heel and stopped there. It lay along a hand's width of the hooked curve, and the rest of the spike stayed dark, blunt and grey and heavy. Toren stood with it on his fist and felt the draw behind his breastbone, and it was less. It was not half. It was much less than half, a thin trickle instead of a pinhole.

"There," said Jab.

"It's so small."

"It's the bit you use," said Jab. "Now hit something with it."

Jab took his other hand out of his pocket.

The last inch lit.

He stood in the first lane with the knife held up in front of him and the last inch of it burning, and he looked at it. His face did not move at all. His hands had been shaking for four days, in his lap and his pockets and on the table in the shed, and his hand under the knife was shaking now, and the light on the hook did not shake with it.

Toren saw him start to go down to look. He saw it in his face, the small inward turn of somebody going under their own breastbone to see what was there.

"Don't," said Toren.

Jab looked at him.

"Don't check," said Toren. "You told me. Rook told you. Don't check." He heard his own voice come out too loud for the lane. "It's lit. That's all you need to know. It's lit."

Jab looked back at the hook for a long moment. Then he let the light go out, and then the knife, and it went back into the metal on its neck cord. He put his hands back in his pockets. He did not say anything. He turned round and walked back along the first lane to the sand heap and sat down on the bottom of it, below Wyck, and sat there with his head down and his hands in his pockets.

Wyck looked down at the top of his head.

"Write it," said Wyck.

Dessa wrote it. When Toren went and looked, later, it said: *J., light, the hook only, unasked. First since the 25th. T. said don't check. He didn't.*

On the thirty-second morning Wyck moved the mark.

He had come down off the heap to do it, carefully, the way Keel made him do everything, and he painted a new short line on the cinders with Dee's brush one pace further out than the old one. Seven paces. The sack would swing further and arrive harder.

"You've held at six five times out of seven," he said. "Dessa's got it written. Five out of seven's not a trick any more. It's a thing you do." He stood the brush back in the pot. "So now it's seven."

"I'll go under at seven."

"Then you'll go under at seven," said Wyck, "and we'll know where seven is."

Toren stood under the crossbar with his feet set and his left arm strapped against his chest. He did not build the struts from the knee. He had not built them from the knee for eleven days. He thought about the left one, the late one, and where it had come from on the whitewash, and put the foot of it first, down in the cinders a foot outside his left boot at a slant, and let it come up from there toward him. It came. It met his knee coming the other way and stood.

Then he did the other thing, the thing that was harder. He left himself soft behind it.

He had to think about it every time. His body wanted to lock. Everything in him that had ever braced for a blow wanted to go hard, knees and hips and spine, all of it, into one post. Wyck had told him on the sand heap what that did. So he stood behind the strut with his knees soft and his hips loose and his weight low, like a man leaning in a doorway rather than a man who was the door.

"Now," said Wyck, and Otto let the sack go.

It came down the lane with a noise like wind and hit the strut at the knee.

The weight came into the strut. Toren felt it arrive, the whole swinging mass of the straw sack, and he felt it go down the strut at its slant into the ground, where the foot of it was. It did not come up into him. His knees gave a little, because they were soft, and took the last of it, and the sack stopped against the strut and hung there, pressing, and swung back.

He was still standing.

"Six," said Dessa, from her table.

He did it again. And again. On the fourth he locked, because the sack came harder than he was ready for and his body did what it had always done, and the weight came up through the strut into his knee and his hip and jarred the arm. He went down on the cinders and rolled and came up on one knee, and sat there for a while with the arm held against his chest, breathing through his teeth.

"Where?" said Dee, from the shed door.

"Same place. Not as bad."

"Then that's your answer about seven," said Wyck. "Seven holds when you're soft. It doesn't when you're not. That's all seven is." He sat back down on the heap. "Once more. Then stop."

Toren held the last one.

He stood under the crossbar afterward with the strut gone and his breath going out of him in clouds, and he looked at the whitewash mark at seven paces. He thought about the fog on the twenty-fifth day, and the husk that had come in under his point and taken him down at the knee in the door. The strut had not been there then. There had been nothing between the husk and his leg but his leg.

Five out of seven at six. Four out of five at seven, when he was soft.

"Is it Guard?" he said. "Now?"

"It's a sack on a rope," said Wyck. "You'll know it's Guard when something that isn't a sack comes at you and it holds." He turned the bar over in his left hand. "I'd take it at eight, if I could. You'd sit on the heap and watch. Don't think I haven't thought about it."

"When you're off the strapping."

"When I'm off the strapping," said Wyck, "I'll take it at eight. And you'll learn what a man looks like when he doesn't move, instead of a boy who's found out how to lean." He did not say it unkindly. He said it the way he said everything that was true. "Go and do your ash. She's waiting."

Dee had him take a tenth of a Handful in over a hundred breaths.

She sat him at the table in the shed with the paper in front of him and the little grey heap on it, and she put her fingers on the inside of his wrist, and she told him that if he hauled it she would take the paper away and he could go and be sick behind the shed again, and Wyck could say what he liked from the heap.

"A hundred breaths," she said. "I'll count. You'll put your palm on it and you'll take it the way the drift comes, not the way you want it. When I say ten you should have about a tenth of a tenth. When I say fifty, half. If you've got it all by twenty, you've hauled, and it'll come back out of you in about a minute, and you'll feel full, and you won't be."

"How do I not haul?"

"You don't ask for it," said Dee. "You put your hand there and you wait."

It was the hardest thing he had done in the yard. The ash came up into his palm, cold and slow, and every part of him wanted to lean on it, the way he leaned on everything, the way he leaned on a door. He did not. He sat with his palm flat on the paper and waited for it to come at the rate it came, and Dee counted, and at ten there was a little cold in the heel of his hand, and at fifty there was more, and at ninety the paper was nearly clean.

At a hundred it was clean.

He did not feel full. He felt as if somebody had put a small warm stone at the bottom of something inside him, a long way down, and it was lying there.

"That's settled," said Dee. She took her fingers off his wrist. "It took you a hundred and four. Callie takes about thirty. The weighbridge girl takes forty." She wrote it. "You're the slowest in the yard. By a long way."

"Is that bad?"

"It's a fact. It means you'll never take ash in a hurry. Not in a fight. Not at a fence. If you need it, you'll have to take it the night before, slowly, sitting down, and carry it settled in the morning." She looked at him. "You'll always have to be the first one full. Can you live with that?"

"Yes."

"Good. Now spend it."

"On what?"

"On the thing you just did at seven paces," said Dee. "Go and stand under Wyck's sack and ask the strut to come up out of the ground, and don't ask your own well for it. Ask the stone."

He did not know what she meant until he tried it. He stood under the crossbar and put the foot of the strut down in the cinders outside his boot, and when he asked it to come up he did not reach for the draw behind his breastbone that he always reached for. He reached lower, for the small warm stone lying at the bottom.

The strut came up out of the ground and met his knee and stood. It felt the same as always. Otto let the sack go from six paces, and it hit, and the strut held, and he was soft behind it, and the weight went into the ground.

He stood under the crossbar afterward and went down to look, once, properly, the way Dee had taught him on the seventeenth day: not checking, measuring. The stone at the bottom was smaller. It was not gone. Above it, where his own reserve lay, he could feel nothing different at all.

"It came out of the stone," he said.

"It came out of the stone," said Dee, from the shed door. "Any door. Same fuel. It doesn't care whose door it goes through." She wrote something. "A tenth of a Handful. That's about four holds at six paces, if you're soft. More if you're softer. You'll find out."

---

At the ninth hour on the thirty-second day, his grandmother could not stand up for the chair.

It had been two steps. The nurse got her to the edge of the bed, and Toren brought the chair round, and Senna put her hands on the arms of it where she could see them, and stood, and turned, and sat. Two steps. She had been doing it that way since the tenth day, with somebody telling her where the floor was. This morning the nurse got her to the edge of the bed, and Toren brought the chair round, and Senna put her hands on the arms of it and looked down at her feet, and said, "Tell me where it is."

"It's there, Gran. Right under you."

"I know it's under me." She did not move. She was looking at her feet. "I can see it's under me. I've told my feet it's under me. They're not listening." She was quiet for a moment. "They were listening the day before yesterday."

Two orderlies lifted her. They did it well, the way people do a thing they have done a thousand times, one under each arm and a word between them to go together, and she went from the bed to the chair in the air with her face shut, and they set her down.

"That's the step gone," said Senna. "I've been expecting it. Sowerby drew it on a page." She put her hands in her lap and watched them. "Push, Tor. I'll not be late for it. It's the only appointment I've got."

He pushed. Down the long slope under the north end of the long building, into the cool grey air where the walls went thick, to the yellow line and the wide door. Callie was already on the bench with the hook across her knees. She did not say anything when they came. She looked at Senna's face, and then at Senna's feet on the footrest of the chair, and then at the wide door.

The grey-haired man warmed the machine seven times.

Callie counted. She counted low and flat, the way she did now for the technician, because he had asked her to and because it was the one kind of counting that was for somebody else. Toren stood at the handle of the chair and listened to her.

"Ten," she said. "Ten. Eleven. Ten. Ten. Ten." A gap. "Ten. Eleven. Ten."

When it was finished she said, "It's worse than the twenty-first. It's worse than the twenty-eighth."

"Yes," said the grey-haired man from the doorway. He had ink all up the side of his right hand and his eyes were red to the rims. "It is."

"How long?" said Toren.

"I told her upstairs," said the man. "The fifty-fourth." He looked at Senna in the chair. "Mrs Voss. It'll be a while. I'll need the doctor."

"I've a great deal of time," said Senna. "Take some of it."

---

She was in there two hours. When she came out on the rolling bed she was grey and small, and she opened her eyes at the yellow line and found Toren, and said the thing she always said now.

"It sang flat."

"I know."

"Three times. He didn't like his screen. I could tell by his back." She shut her eyes. Then she opened them again, which she did not usually do, and looked up at Toren from the bed with her pale eyes. "What were they?"

"What were what?"

"Her questions. Upstairs. Callie says there were five, and one more. Callie says she put her fingers down for each of them." Senna's voice was very tired and very steady. "Tell me."

So he told her. He stood beside the rolling bed in the cool grey corridor with Callie on the bench and the grey-haired man in the doorway, and he told her the five questions and the one more, in order, the way the Director had said them, with the fingers. How much ash. How long inside the fence. Who holds whom, in what order. When you turn round. Who holds the Sag. And Rook's hands.

Senna listened with her eyes open.

"I can answer the second one," she said.

Toren looked at her.

"How long inside. What the fence costs a person, by the day." She moved one of her hands on the blanket, a little, where she could see it. "I can't tell her by the hour. We didn't count by the hour. We counted by the day, because that's how you count a crew. Who was sick first, and when. Who couldn't hold a cup, and when. Who went, and when." She looked at the ceiling. "Nobody's asked me that in twenty years. I remember every one of them."

"Gran—"

"Fetch the book," said Senna. "And fetch her."

"The Director?"

"She came down to the shed. On the twenty-seventh. I heard about it from four people." Senna shut her eyes. "She can come down to a ward."

---

The Director came down to the ward at the seventh hour of the evening.

She came alone. She had left the man in the long coat on the second floor, and she came in through the doors past Mrs Lask, who stood up, which Toren had never seen Mrs Lask do for anybody. She walked down the ward between the beds, small and square in her grey coat with her spectacles on their cord, and people in the beds turned their heads to watch her go past. In the fifth bed on the right Tonk sat up on his bar to see better, and said, "Who's *that*?" loudly, and Jab put his hand over his face.

The Director stopped at the foot of the fourth bed.

"Mrs Voss," she said.

"Director," said Senna. "Sit down. There's a chair. The boy's got the book."

The Director sat. She took a pencil out of her coat and a small folded sheet, and put her spectacles on, and waited. She did not say anything about being asked to come down to a ward by an old woman who could not stand up. Toren watched her not say it.

"I'm going to tell you what the fence costs," said Senna. "The way we counted it. By the day, not the hour. He'll write it in Dessa's book as I say it, not tidied. You'll write whatever you like on your own paper. If you've a question, ask it when I stop for breath. I stop for breath a good deal these days."

"Go on," said the Director.

"We went in on the first day at the east gate," said Senna. "Not the main gate. The main gate's where the drain from the plant ran out under the fence, and the man who ran the crew wouldn't go near it. He'd been in twice. The east gate's where the fence crosses the river on a footbridge. There's a pump house on the far bank. We camped outside the fence by the pump house every night, eleven nights. He wouldn't let anybody sleep inside."

Toren wrote. *East gate. River. Footbridge. Pump house. Camp outside, every night.*

"His rules," said Senna. "Write them in a list. He made us say them at the gate every morning, like a grace. Nothing to eat inside the fence. Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, every night, coat off, shake it, wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat. And never carry one of the boxes. Never. Not on your back. Not in your arms." She was quiet a moment. "He'd seen a man's back."

"Did people keep the rules?" said the Director.

"The ones who kept them are the ones who were still alive ten years ago," said Senna. "Write that."

Toren wrote it.

"The second day, nothing. The third day, the two men who'd slept inside on the second night because they were drunk were sick in the morning. Both of them. Sick like a dog, all morning, and grey, and then better by the afternoon, and they thought they'd got away with it. Write *they thought they'd got away with it*." She stopped for breath. "The man who carried the water was sick on the fifth. He'd been drinking from the ditch inside the fence when he thought nobody saw. The sixth day, my hands. The ends of my fingers, like I'd had them in snow. The same as the tenth day in this ward, twenty years later. I didn't know what it was. I thought it was the cold."

The Director's pencil moved.

"How long were you inside each day?" she said. "Not the camp. Inside."

"Too long," said Senna. "Five hours. Six. The copper was deep. It took an hour to get down to the instrument rooms and an hour to get back out with the wire, and in between we worked." She looked at her hands. "The ones who went deepest lost the most. That's the only thing I know for certain. I went as deep as anybody. I was fifty-three and I thought I was stronger than I was."

"And the boxes," said the Director. "Where were they, exactly?"

"Down the stair with the rail painted yellow," said Senna. "Inside the main building, off the long corridor, on the left. The same yellow as the line in the shed. I've been looking at that line for three weeks, eleven days at a time, and every time I see it I'm fifty-three again at the top of those stairs." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at the Director. "The rooms with the racks were on the left of the corridor at the bottom. Eleven of them. We did them in order, one a day. The boxes with the wheel on the plate were in the racks on the left walls. The ones on the right walls were bigger, with a different mark, and they were cold, and we didn't care about them because they had no wire behind them worth having."

"The warm ones had the wheel," said Toren.

"The warm ones had the wheel," said Senna. "Write it."

---

It took her an hour and a quarter. She stopped for breath a good deal. Twice she stopped for longer than breath and lay with her eyes shut, and the Director sat in the chair with her pencil still and waited, and did not look at the nurse who came to the end of the bed and went away again.

When Senna had finished, the Director took her spectacles off and sat for a while with them in her hand.

"You'll sign it," she said.

"I can't hold a pencil."

"Then you'll tell him where to put your name," said the Director, "and he'll put it there, and I'll witness it." She stood up. "It's the only account of that fence on any paper in this station. I've looked. There's a line in the depot records from forty years ago that says *Caul Hill: restricted*, and nothing else. This is the first thing anybody's written about it that I'd trust."

Senna told him where. He wrote her name at the bottom of the page in the big book, *S. Voss*, where her hands could not, and under it *dictated*, and the Director took the pencil and wrote a mark beside it that was not her name, a small cross in a circle, and gave the pencil back.

"That's one of my five," she said.

"It's the second," said Senna.

"It's part of the second." The Director put her spectacles on their cord. "You've told me what the fence cost eleven people who didn't know what they were doing, by the day. I need to know what it'll cost six who do, by the hour. That's not yours to tell me. That's theirs." She looked at Toren. "But it's a start. It's a better start than I've had from anybody."

She turned to go. At the foot of the fifth bed she stopped, because Tonk was still sitting up on his bar, staring at her with enormous eyes.

"Who *are* you?" said Tonk.

The Director looked at him for a moment over her spectacles.

"I run the station," she said.

Tonk considered this.

"Do you run the *machine*?"

"No," said the Director. "A man with ink on his hand runs the machine. I run the people who run the machine."

"It's broken," said Tonk. "A bit. At the back."

"I know," said the Director. "I'm told."

"Then you should *get* one," said Tonk, and let go of his bar and lay back down, satisfied, because he had said it once before and been right.

The Director stood at the foot of his bed. Toren watched her. He watched her face do something, very briefly, that he would not have known how to describe, and then stop doing it.

"Yes," she said. "I should." And she went away down the ward between the beds, and past Mrs Lask, who stood up again, and out.

---

Senna lay with her eyes shut after she had gone.

"She's frightened," she said.

Toren looked up from the book.

"Of the hill," said Senna. "Did you see? When I said *the ones who went deepest lost the most*, her pencil stopped. She's frightened of it. Not for herself. For whatever she'd have to write down if she said yes and it went wrong." She let out a long breath. "That's the best thing I've seen all month. A woman who's frightened of what she'd have to write down is a woman who writes things down properly."

"She said no."

"She said no to a hope. You heard her." Senna opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling. "You've got one answer now, Tor. Part of one. Go and get the other four. Get them from the people who'll have to pay for them. Not from me. I've paid for mine."

He sat with the book on his knee. In the next bed Tonk had gone to sleep under his frame, with the cord running from his wrist to the rail of the empty sixth bed, in a knot that nobody was allowed to untie. Jab sat in the chair between the beds with his hands in his pockets, and he had heard all of it, and he did not say anything.

At the tenth hour, when the nurses turned the visitors out, Senna said the name to the ceiling.

"Milo Faust."

"Milo Faust," said Tonk, in his sleep, or nearly.

Toren closed the book on the page with his grandmother's name at the bottom, and a cross in a circle beside it, and carried it back across the dark yard to Dessa under his good arm.

# Chapter 19 — Callie: The Long Table

Rook laid a line across the bottom of the Sag on the thirty-third morning, for ten minutes, and it was the first time anybody had lifted since the twenty-fifth day.

Callie read the ground before he did it. She went down the east track at first light with Katori beside her and Miss Wren behind, and the lamps of the watch still burning on their poles along the top of the track, and she looked at the bottom of the dip for a long time before she said anything. The fog had not come this morning. The slabs lay in the grey light with the black water between them. The slab by the sixth post had gone down on the twenty-fifth day and stayed down, a foot lower than it had been, tilted into the water at the corner like a door off its hinges, and she looked at the broken edge of it, and at the joints round it, and at the silt.

"It's settled," she said. "Where it went. It's lying on something now. The joints round it are closed up, and there's no bright dirt anywhere new." She looked along the track. "The drift's between four and five. The night crew's lamps say it's two husks' worth. It's on dry stone. Nobody needs to go below five."

"Nobody's going below four," said Katori.

At the north lip Rook stood with his hands at his sides. Callie had seen him stand there every morning for two weeks before the lift, and he had looked tired then. He did not look tired now. He looked like a man standing on the edge of a roof, working out how far down it was.

"Ten minutes," said Miss Wren. She was at the south lip beside Dessa's crate, with the brass glass in her hand. "Not eleven. I'll call it at ten and you'll let it go at ten, and the watch will have it back before you've got your hands down."

"You said that on the stair," said Rook.

"I'm saying it again in front of witnesses."

Rook put his hands out from his sides.

Callie felt the ground go. She was standing on the east track above post four, and the bottom of the Sag in front of her stopped being ground, the way it always did when a line went down on it. It was like a clean sheet of paper laid over a page she had been reading, so that she could see where the words had been and not the words. It lay from ditch to ditch. It was thinner at the east edge than the day holder's had been, and she could feel that it was thinner, and she did not say so.

"Down," said Katori. "Lift."

---

They lifted.

Callie knelt at the drift with her palm flat on it, the way she had knelt every morning for two weeks, and beside her the weighbridge girl knelt too, with her left arm in its plaster held against her chest and her right palm flat on the frost. Otto was above them with the two yard lifters. The lamps of the watch stayed lit along the upper edge of the track. Ines stayed on the rim. Katori stood over all of them with her blade dark on her fist, and at the south lip Dessa sat on her crate with her eyes shut.

The ash came up. It came up into Callie's hand the way it had on the plain and on the track and at the long table, cold and slow, with nobody in it, and she held it high and open and walked it to the jar and tipped her palm against the glass, and went back. Beside her the weighbridge girl did the same, one-handed, with her tongue between her teeth like Otto, and did not spill any.

"Five," said Miss Wren, at the south lip.

Callie did not look up. She could feel the line in the bottom of the dip, the clean sheet lying across it, and she could feel it thin at the east edge. It was holding. At seven minutes she thought it had thinned a little further, the way a rope thins when you feel the strands begin to go under your hands. She did not say so. It was not hers to say. It was Miss Wren's glass and Rook's line.

"Nine," said Miss Wren. "Up. Everybody up the track."

They came up with the jars. Callie was the last off the drift, because she was always the last, and she came up past the lamp-line to Katori.

"Ten," said Miss Wren. "Elias. Now."

"Watch has it," said Katori, loud enough to carry.

At the north lip Rook let his hands down.

The sheet went off the bottom of the Sag. Callie felt it go, all at once, the way a held breath goes, and the ground came back under her eyes. There were the slabs and the joints and the silt again, readable. Nothing had come up. Nothing was coming.

Rook stood on the north lip with his hands at his sides for a long moment. Then they began to shake, the long fine tremor from the wrists to the fingertips, and he did not hide them, and he did not sit down. He stood there shaking in front of everybody for as long as it took the sand to run out of the glass in Miss Wren's hand, and then he walked round the lip path to the south lip at his own slow pace.

"You called it at ten," he said to her.

"I said I would."

"I'd have gone to fifteen."

"I know you would," said Miss Wren. "That's why I called it at ten." She turned the glass over. "That's two, Elias. Standing up, and a short hold. The next one's harder."

---

Callie's mother weighed the lift at the weighbridge at the middle of the morning.

She was on her stool in the hut window with the tally board on her knee, and when Otto set the first jar on the plate she did not look at the jar. She looked at the beam. The old man in the leather apron moved the little weight along it with one finger, and the beam came up, and her mother said, "A Handful," before the old man did.

"A Handful," said the old man.

The second jar was half a Handful, and clean, and her mother said so. She wrote it on the board with her left hand in big careful figures and read it back. Then she leaned out of the window and looked up at the black board on the wall of the hut, at the row of blanks running under every day since the twenty-fifth.

The old man took a piece of chalk off the ledge and wrote a number in the column for the thirty-third day, against Katori's name. *1½, clean.* It was the first chalk on the board in eight days.

"There," said her mother. "That's something."

"It's a Handful and a half," said Callie.

"It's a Handful and a half more than yesterday." Her mother looked down at her from the stool. "Don't do sums at me, love. I can see you doing them. I know what's in the store better than you do. I read it out to you every night." She put the board on her knee. "Go to your table. The woman with the boots has been across twice to see if you're coming."

---

Miss Wren had built the table out of four crates and three planks.

It ran down the middle of the east shed from the stove to the bucket, long and low, with a bench along each side made of more planks on more crates. There were jars on it, stoppered, in a row down the middle. There were squares of paper in front of every place, and a horn spoon, and in the middle of the table, where everybody could see it, the brass glass. At the end of the table nearest the stove there was a shallow tin tray full of pale sand.

"Sit," said Miss Wren.

They sat. Callie on one bench, with the weighbridge girl beside her and Toren across. Jab at the end of the other bench, with his hands on the table. Dessa at her own small table by the door with the big book open. And at the head of the long table, on the end of the bench nearest the stove, with his back very straight and his face like somebody who had been made to sit in a church, Rook.

"I'm here," said Rook, to the room. "I'd like that written somewhere."

"It's written," said Dessa, without looking up.

Miss Wren stood at the other end of the table with her hands behind her back.

"This is Vell's table," she said. "Not the planks. The planks are the station's, and one of them's got a nail in it that Otto's going to deal with. The method's Vell's. I sat at it every morning at Homura, for the months I had there, before breakfast, and I hated it, and it's the reason I'm alive." She looked along the benches. "You've done one and two. Which is yours, and how much of yours there is. You've done three and four. Take in a little you've measured, and let it settle. This is what comes after. It's six things, and they go round and round, and that's all it is."

She held up her hand and put the fingers down one at a time, the way the Director had on the second floor.

"Take it in. Let it settle. Spend it through your door. Put out what's left over. Rest. Do it again." She put her hand down. "In, settle, spend, vent, rest, again. That's the table. That's the whole of it. You'll do it until you can do it without thinking, and then you'll do it some more."

"What's it for?" said the weighbridge girl.

"It's for depth," said Miss Wren. "Not size. I want that clear before anybody gets any ideas. Nobody at this table is going to get bigger. Your soul's the size it is. You were Kindled at thirteen and you got a door and a house behind it, and the house is the house." She looked at them. "What you can learn is how much of the house you can use. Most people use the front room. They take ash in with a rush and it slops about in the hall and half of it goes out the back door before they've spent any. They spend it and they don't put out what's left over, and it sits in them and aches. They don't rest. And then they do it again the next day and wonder why they're worse at it." She put her hands flat on the end of the table. "The table teaches you to take it in slowly, settle it all the way down, spend it clean, and put out the leftovers before they lodge. Do that for long enough and you can take in more, and spend more, and come back from it. That's working depth. It's the only thing Vell ever measured that I'd bet my life on."

"I've bet mine on it," said Rook, from the head of the table, "without ever sitting here."

"Yes," said Miss Wren. "And look where you're sitting now."

---

They did it for two hours.

A quarter of a Handful each, weighed out onto the paper. In, over seventy-odd breaths for Callie, a hundred for the weighbridge girl, and more than two hundred and fifty for Toren, who sat with his palm flat on the paper and his face shut and did not haul. Settle, for sixty breaths, while Miss Wren walked up and down behind the benches and watched their faces. Then spend.

"Through your door," said Miss Wren. "Into something. At Homura it was a dish of water, or a sick dog, or the child next to you. Here it's whatever your door does. Light your blade and hold it. Stand in your Ward. Put up your strut. And don't use your own well for it. Use the stone at the bottom. The one you settled."

The weighbridge girl lit her blade.

She did it one-handed, with her plaster held against her chest, the plain grey straight blade coming out of her right fist all at once with its frost-pale line along the edge. She held it level over the table. Callie watched her face, because her face was always the best thing about her. It went from concentrating to surprised to something else, something bright and startled, like a child who has put her hand in a pocket and found a coin.

"It's not mine," said the weighbridge girl. "The light. It's not coming out of me. It's coming out of the—" She did not have the word. "The heap."

"Yes," said Miss Wren. "That's the point."

"It's *nobody's*," said the girl, and laughed out loud, and nearly let the blade go, and did not. She held it for a turn of the glass and most of another, on a quarter of a Handful, and when it guttered she let it go and sat with her right hand flat on the table and her face shining. "I've been on the weighbridge a year. I've watched it go past in jars every day. I thought it was money."

"It is money," said Miss Wren. "It's also that. Vent."

The girl put her palm flat in the tin tray of pale sand at the end of the table. She did it the way she had been shown, and Callie saw her shoulders come down a little, as if she had put down something she had not known she was carrying.

"What was that?" said the girl.

"What's left over," said Miss Wren. "When you spend, it doesn't all go clean. Some of it stays in your hands, like the heat stays in a pan after you've taken it off the stove. If you leave it, it goes down into you, and you have the ache tomorrow. So you put it out, into something nobody's in." She nodded at the tray. "It's only sand. It doesn't mind."

---

Callie could not spend.

She settled the quarter-Handful in seventy-four breaths. She felt it go down through her wrist and her arm and into the well, and lie at the bottom under the water that was already there, a small stone of cold. She felt the cold go out of it, slowly, the way cold goes out of a stone you have brought in from the yard, until it was part of the well. It was the best settling at the table. Miss Wren said so, and wrote it down.

Then she stood the hook on its end against the table with the curve up, and put her hand on the conduit, and asked for the Ward in the steel, and asked it to come out of the stone at the bottom.

The Ward came up through her heels and into the hook the way it always came. The hook went pale. And she felt where it had come from, and it had not come out of the stone. It had come out of her. Out of the water above the stone, her own, the way it had come every time since she was thirteen. The stone lay at the bottom of the well and did not move.

She let the Ward go.

"Again," said Miss Wren.

She did it again. She felt for the stone this time before she asked, and found it, lying at the bottom, settled and cold and hers now, and she asked the Ward to come out of that. The Ward came. It came out of her own water again, as if the stone were not there at all.

"Again."

It was the same. Five times, and six, and seven. She could feel the stone. She could feel exactly where it was. She could not get it to move. It was like having a coin at the bottom of a jar and being told to pay with it, and her hand would not go into the jar.

"You've locked it," said Miss Wren.

She had come round behind Callie's bench and was standing there with her hands behind her back.

"You settled it too well," she said. "That happens. Not often. It happens to people who hold things. You took it in and you put it at the bottom and you shut your hand over it, and now it's yours and you won't let it go anywhere." She did not say it unkindly. "It's in there. It isn't doing anything. It's the same as a sack you've been told to set down that you're still holding."

"I'm not holding it."

"You are," said Miss Wren. "I can see you holding it from here. Your shoulders are up round your ears."

Callie put her shoulders down. She asked for the Ward again. It came out of her own water.

At the head of the table Rook said, "She held a hook on a hill with a wagon on it."

Callie looked at him.

"Callie did," said Rook, to Miss Wren. "On the scarp, on the road in. Katori told me. The whole wagon on the hook, and her mother on the wagon, and her hands shut on the conduit so hard she couldn't open them afterward." He was not looking at Callie. "She holds. It's the best thing about her. It kept her mother alive for four hundred miles. It isn't going to let go of a quarter of a Handful because you've asked it nicely."

"I know that," said Miss Wren.

"Then don't ask her to let go of it at a table," said Rook. "She'll never let go of anything at a table. Give her something to hold."

Miss Wren looked at him for a long moment.

"Vent," she said to Callie. "What you've spent of your own. Then rest. We'll do it again at the fourth hour."

Callie put her palm flat in the sand. She felt what was left in her hands go out into it, a little grey tiredness she had not known was there. The stone at the bottom of the well stayed where it was, settled and cold and shut up in her hand, and she sat at the long table with her shoulders round her ears and could not put them down.

---

Across the table, Jab had put his palm flat on his paper and passed the whole quarter-Handful into Rook.

He had not meant to. Callie saw it happen. Miss Wren had put him at the end of the bench, next to Rook, with a jar and a paper, and told him to hold it high the way he had in the shed, and carry it, and not let it go down. He had held it high for twenty breaths, which was better than yesterday. And then Rook, beside him, had put his shaking hands flat on the table to steady them. And Jab's other hand, the empty one, had gone down onto the back of Rook's hand without asking anybody, and the ash had gone out of the high palm and through Jab and into Rook, in about the time it takes to swallow.

Rook's hands stopped shaking.

They stopped slowly, the way a bell slows. Rook looked down at them on the table, and at Jab's hand lying on the back of one of them, and did not say anything.

"Jab," said Miss Wren.

"I didn't mean to."

"I know you didn't. That's the trouble." She came down the table and stood over them. "That's not a method. That's a leak with a person on the end of it."

"It's a method," said Rook, without looking up. "It's a lifter's method. A jar in one hand and a person on the other, and it goes through him. He never touches what's his. I told you in the shed."

"Everything that comes out of that jar goes into whoever his other hand's on. Whether he means it or not."

"Everything at Vell's table was a leak with a person on the end of it," said Rook. "That's what the dogs were for."

Miss Wren stood over them for a long time. Callie watched her face. It did not move much, but it moved, the way a tally board moves when somebody rubs out a number and does not write the new one yet.

"Write it," she said at last, to Dessa. "*J., jar to R., through, a quarter, twenty breaths held first. R.'s hands still for—*" She looked at Rook's hands. "Count it, Dessa."

"I'm counting," said Dessa.

---

Callie's mother woke on the thirty-fourth morning and said her name.

It was the first thing she said. It was not *something left* or *what time is it* or a word she had to go looking for in the dark. She opened her eyes on the bed by the stove with the blanket over the window and the grey light coming round the edges of it, and turned her head, and found Callie on the floor, and said, "Callie," as if she had never once lost a word in her life.

Callie sat up.

"I've slept," said her mother. "I've slept right through. When did that last happen?" She looked at the ceiling. "Don't answer that. You'll know, and you'll say, and I'd rather not."

She got up by herself. She put her left hand on the frame of the bed and her right hand on the stick, and the right hand closed on the stick. It closed properly. Callie watched it close, and saw her mother watch it close, and neither of them said anything. Her mother stood by the bed and looked down at her own right hand on the stick for a while, the way Senna looked at her hands, as if it belonged to somebody sitting next to her.

"Well," she said. "Look at that."

At breakfast she held the spoon in her right hand. Not for long. For three mouthfuls, and then it went, and she changed it to her left without comment. But for three mouthfuls it was her right hand taking instruction, and Pell looked up from the atlas and saw it and looked down again very quickly, and Ines said nothing and put more porridge in the bowl.

She walked to the weighbridge. The leg did not go. It did not go on the step of fourteen, or on the frozen concrete of the row, or on the cinders of the north yard, or on the step up into the hut. She walked the whole way with the stick in her left hand and Callie beside her not touching, and when she got to her stool she climbed onto it and sat and laughed, out loud, a short surprised laugh, like somebody who has come down a stair in the dark and found one fewer step than they expected.

"Your grandfather," she said. "Did I ever tell you about the man with the short sacks?"

"No."

"He came to the scale house every autumn for six years with forty sacks of barley, and every autumn your grandfather weighed them and every autumn they were short. Not much. A pound a sack. And the man said the same thing every year, *it's the drying, it's the drying*." Her mother put the tally board on her knee. "The sixth year your grandfather didn't weigh them. He picked one up. Just picked it up off the cart and stood there holding it, with his head on one side, the way he held a thing when he was listening to it. And he said, *There's a brick in this one.* And there was. At the bottom. Wrapped in sacking." She laughed again. "He'd been putting a brick in one sack in forty, so the whole load would weigh right, and all the others were short. Your grandfather found it with his arms."

Callie sat on the step of the hut and laughed. She could not help it.

She did not do a sum. She wanted to. She felt the wanting come up in her, the way it always came, under everything: *a word found, a hand for three mouthfuls, a leg that held the whole way.* The column of good things, next to the column of bad. She felt herself start to write it down in her head. She stopped at the first line and did not write the second, and sat on the step and let the morning be a morning.

"There," said her mother, watching her. "That's better. That's the face I like."

---

At the middle of the morning, with nobody on the plate, her mother opened the store book.

It was a long narrow book with a canvas cover that lived on the shelf beside the test weights, and the old man let nobody touch it but himself and, now, her. She turned the pages with her left hand to the last written one and read it out, the way she had read it out to Callie every night on the floor by the stove when she could not sleep.

"Thirty-one Flasks and a Handful," she said. "Four of them thin. And a Handful and a half since yesterday, clean, which makes it thirty-one Flasks and two Handfuls and a half." She put her finger on the line. "The Director came down at the fifth hour yesterday and read that with her own eyes, standing where you're sitting. She read it twice. She didn't say anything."

"How many does a Flask make? In Handfuls?"

"Eight," said her mother, without looking up. "Clean. A thin one's nearer seven. It's on the inside of the cover. Your grandfather would have had a fit. At Thistle Ford we'd have weighed every one." She closed the book. "Why?"

Callie did not answer.

Her mother looked at her for a while. Then she put the store book back on its shelf beside the test weights and put her hand, her left hand, flat on the ledge of the hut window.

"Senna told me about the hill," she said. "Everybody tells me everything. I'm on a stool in a window." She did not look away. "Are you going?"

"They haven't said yes."

"I didn't ask about them."

Callie sat on the step with her hands in her lap.

"I don't know," she said.

"Then find out," said her mother. "And when you know, you'll tell me first. Before the Director and before that little man with the folder and before Katori. Me." She took her hand off the ledge. "That's all. That's the only rule I've got about it. You'll tell me first."

"I'll tell you first."

"Good," said her mother, and looked out at the plate, where Otto was coming up the yard with a jar under his arm from the watch's morning, and said, before the old man could move the weight along the beam, "That's a dirty one. There's ditch in it. Tip it and see."

---

Wyck and Rook had built the rack in the afternoon.

It stood at the end of the first lane under the timber frame. It was a real rack, or the frame of one, two uprights of old fence post with three crossbars nailed between them. The crossbars were hung with sand sacks tied on with rope, so that it was as heavy as a door and a half, and it stood on its end with its foot against a stake driven into the cinders, like a book stood on a shelf. A rope ran from the top of it up over the crossbar of the timber frame and down to a weighted sack that Otto stood holding. When Otto let the rope out, the rack leaned. When he pulled it, the rack stood up again.

Under the rack, where it would come down if it came down, lay a straw sack in Otto's old coat. One of its arms, a stuffed sleeve, was tied to the stake with a strap.

"That's the body," said Wyck, from the top of the sand heap. "It's got a name. Otto named it. I'm not saying the name."

"It's Norris," said Otto, going red.

"It's a sack," said Wyck. "Here's the job. Otto lets the rope go. The rack comes down on the body. You three get the body out, whole, and nobody under the rack when it lands. That's all." He turned the bar over in his left hand. "And Otto's allowed to move the rope whenever he likes, as much as he likes, and he won't tell you when."

"Why?" said Toren.

"Because things that fall on people don't hold still," said Wyck, "while you work out what to do about them."

Rook sat on his crate at the side of the lane. Miss Wren stood at the shed door with the glass. Dessa had the book open.

"One problem," said Rook. "Three of you. You know what you've got. Do it."

---

The first time, they killed Norris.

It went like this. Otto let the rope go. The rack came down, and Toren was already moving. He put the foot of the strut into the cinders outside his left boot and let it come up from the ground at its slant to meet the falling rack at the middle crossbar, and he was soft behind it, and it held. The rack stopped a foot above the sack in Otto's coat, leaning on a line of light that ran from the cinders to Toren's knee.

"Holding," said Toren, through his teeth.

Callie put the curve of the hook over the top crossbar and put the Ward in the steel.

She did it the way she had done it at the jam and at the pipe, not gripping, getting under it. The hook went pale, and the rack stopped being a thing that leaned on Toren's strut and became a thing that was simply there, fixed in the air at a slant, going nowhere. She felt the weight of it come off Toren and onto the hook and down through her heels into the ground.

Toren felt it go too. He stumbled. His strut had been braced against a load, and the load had gone off it all at once, without warning, and he went forward a step into the empty space under the rack and caught himself with his good hand on a sandbag.

"Out," said Callie. "I've got it. Out."

He came out from under. Jab was already in. He had the knife on his fist, and he lit the last inch of it, the hook, the way he had lit it at the jam, and put it against the strap that held Norris's sleeve to the stake and drew it through. The strap parted. He got his arms under the body and dragged it along the cinders and out from under the rack in one long pull.

"Clear," said Jab.

Callie took the Ward out of the hook.

She did it the way you take your hand off a balanced thing, all at once, and in the same instant she leaned for the edge, because the next thing was to let the rack down clean and not have it swing. The Ward went out of the steel. The edge did not come. One. The rack lurched. Two. It went. It went down off the hook in the gap, the whole heavy door-and-a-half of it, and hit the cinders where Norris had been with a sound like a cart tipping over, and bounced, and came down again.

On the second bounce the end of it came down across Norris's legs, which Jab had pulled clear of the middle but not of the end.

Nobody said anything.

"That's his legs," said Wyck, from the heap. "Both of them. He'll not walk. That's a man carried home on a board."

---

They stood round the rack.

Callie looked at the hook in her hands. The edge had come on three. It had come off the curve in its long grey sweep and hung there over nothing, and she let it go. Three. She had been down to two on the stakes. She had counted two, over and over, on the morning of the jam. Under a load that was moving, it was three.

"I let go," she said. "Before Toren was under it."

"I wasn't under it," said Toren. "I'd come out. You said *out*."

"I know I said *out*."

"You took it off me without saying," said Toren. He was holding his left arm against his chest. "I was braced. It went off the strut all at once. I didn't know you'd taken it till it was gone. I nearly went in under it."

Callie looked at him.

"You said *holding*," she said.

"I said *holding*. You didn't say anything. You just took it."

Nobody answered that. Callie stood with the hook in her hands and felt her face go hot. It was true. He had said *holding*, and she had put the hook on the rack and put the Ward in it and taken the load, and she had not said a word. She had not said *taking*. She had not said *it's mine*. She had taken it off him because she could, the way she took everything, because it was easier to hold a thing yourself than to wait for somebody else to hand it to you.

And then she had let it go without anybody under it, because she had thought she could do it in the gap.

"Nobody set a minute," said Dessa, from her table.

They looked at her.

"On the twenty-fifth," said Dessa. "At the changeover. Pell called it and the holder said no, not with six moving, and Rook kept his hands down, and nobody said when they'd try again. It's in the book. *No new minute set.*" She did not look up. "You did it again. Toren had it, and Callie took it, and nobody said. Callie had it, and let it go, and nobody said. It's the same thing."

Rook, on his crate, said nothing. He did not need to.

"Again," said Wyck. "And this time say it. Every time it moves from one of you to another, out loud, so the other one hears. Otto, pull it up."

---

The second time, they said it.

Otto let the rope go. The rack came down. Toren put the strut up from the ground and met it at the middle crossbar and held it, soft behind.

"Holding," said Toren.

Callie put the hook over the top crossbar.

"Taking," she said.

"Yours," said Toren.

She put the Ward in the steel. The rack went fixed. She felt the weight come off the strut and onto the hook, and this time Toren had heard it coming and was ready for it. He let the strut go and stood where he was, and did not stumble.

"Mine," said Callie. "Out."

He came out. Jab went in, and lit the hook of his knife, and cut the strap.

And Otto moved the rope.

He did it without warning, the way Wyck had said he would. He hauled on it hard and then let it out, and the whole rack twisted where it hung, the top of it swinging a foot to the left. If it had been on Toren's strut it would have gone off the end of it sideways, because the strut was built for a weight coming one way and not another. It was on the hook. Callie felt the twist come into the hook and go down through the steel and into the ground and stop. The Ward did not care which way the rack went. Nothing moved the hook. So the rack did not move either, no matter what Otto did to the rope.

"Clear," said Jab, from the far side, with Norris in his arms.

Now the handoff. Callie had the rack on the hook and nothing on herself, and she had to let the Ward go and put the edge on the curve, and in the gap the rack would fall.

"Toren," she said. "Under it."

Toren went back in.

He did not wait to be told twice. He put the strut up from the ground at its slant, under the middle crossbar, a hand's width below it, and stood behind it soft, and said, "I'm under it."

"Letting go," said Callie.

She let the Ward go out of the hook. She did not do it all at once, this time, the way you take your hand off a balanced thing. She did it the way Katori had told her at the jam, on the nineteenth day, and she had not understood: like changing hands on a rope. She leaned for the edge while the Ward was still going out, and let one go as the other came, and she was not holding the rack at all in the gap. Toren was. The rack dropped its hand's width onto his strut and stopped, and she felt it stop, and she did not have to be the one who stopped it.

Two.

The edge came off the curve, long and grey and cold. She put it through the top rope where it tied to the crossbar, and the rope parted, and the counterweight went down with a thump into the cinders beside Otto's boots.

"Yours," said Callie. "Let it down."

Toren let the strut go, slowly, a little at a time, soft behind, and the rack came down off it onto the cinders where nobody was, and lay there.

---

Callie stood in the lane with the hook in her hands.

Something had happened. She did not know at first what it was. She had let the Ward go into the gap with Toren under the rack, and she had leaned for the edge, and the edge had come. And when it came, something had come with it. Something had gone out of her into the steel, cold and small and not hers any more, and it had gone out along the grey curve of the edge and been spent. She had felt it go.

She went down to look.

The stone was gone. The quarter-Handful she had settled at the long table in the morning, and shut her hand over, and not been able to spend all day, was not lying at the bottom of the well any more. The water above it, her own, was where it had been. It had not gone down at all.

"It went," she said.

Miss Wren was standing at the shed door with the glass in her hand. She had been watching Callie's face the whole time.

"Into the edge," said Callie. "The stone. The one I couldn't—it went into the edge. When I let go of the Ward." She looked at the hook. "I didn't ask it to. I was letting go of the rack. Toren had the rack. I wasn't holding it. And it went."

"Yes," said Miss Wren.

"Why then?"

"You tell me," said Miss Wren.

Callie looked at Toren, standing beside the rack with his arm held against his chest and his face white round the mouth. She looked at Jab, on the cinders with Norris across his knees. She thought about her shoulders at the long table, up round her ears all morning. She thought about her grandfather at the hopper gate, changing hands on a rope with a sack on the end of it, and never letting go of one hand until the other had it.

He had never held the sack with both hands at once. He had not needed to, because the rope was there.

"Because I wasn't holding everything," she said. "Somebody else had it."

Miss Wren did not say anything. She wrote in her book.

"Once more," said Callie. "I want to try it on purpose. Put the Ward in the hook off the stone. Not off me. I think I know where it is now."

"You haven't got a stone," said Miss Wren. "You've just spent it."

"Then give me another one."

---

She settled a second quarter-Handful at the long table in seventy breaths, with her shoulders down.

Then she went back out to the lane. Otto hauled the rack up on its rope and let it go, and Toren braced it, and said *holding*, and she said *taking*, and he said *yours*. She put the hook on the crossbar, and when she asked for the Ward she did not reach for her own water at all. She reached for the stone at the bottom, and she did not shut her hand over it. She let it go. The Ward came up out of it and into the steel.

The hook went pale. The rack went fixed.

"Glass," said Callie.

Miss Wren turned the glass over.

Callie stood behind the hook with the rack on it and nothing on herself, and the sand ran through the waist of the glass in its thin grey thread. She did not count it. She let Miss Wren count it. She felt the stone at the bottom of the well going out into the steel, a little at a time, steady, the way water goes out of a jar with a crack in it, and her own water above it did not move.

One turn. Miss Wren turned the glass.

Half of another. She felt the stone come near the end of itself.

"There," said Callie. "That's the stone. I'm stopping before I start on mine."

She let the Ward go. Toren was not under it this time, because there was nobody to catch; the rack came down onto the cinders with a crash, on nothing.

"A turn and a half," said Miss Wren. "On a quarter of a Handful. In the steel." She looked at Callie. "Go down and look."

Callie went down. Her own water lay at the bottom of the well above the floor, a finger and a bit of it, cold and clear. It was where it had been at the long table in the morning.

"It's all there," she said. "Mine. It hasn't moved."

"Thirty-eight seconds, standing, on your own," said Miss Wren, writing. "Fifty-eight in the hook at the pipe, to your floor, on your own. And now a turn and a half in the hook, on a quarter of a Handful, and your floor untouched." She closed the book. "That's what the jar's for, Callie. It's what I told you in the shed on the twenty-sixth. *Not yet* is a kind of yes."

Callie stood in the lane with the hook in her hands.

She did not feel clever, and she did not feel strong. She felt the way she had felt on the floor by the stove on the ninth day, when she had let her mother's breathing go past her uncounted for the first time. It was not the feeling of having done something. It was the feeling of having stopped doing something, and finding that the thing went on without her.

"Only in the steel," said Miss Wren. "I want that written as well. The Ward in the hook's cheap because the hook doesn't move. It doesn't breathe, or shift its feet, or lift the back one. On you it's three times the price, and on somebody else it's more than that, because they move." She looked at Callie. "You can't have it on you and in the hook at once. You'll have to choose, every time, which load you're holding. And when you choose the hook, you'll have nothing on you. Same as the pipe."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Miss Wren. "I'm saying it so Dessa can write it."

Dessa wrote it.

---

Rook and Miss Wren argued at the shed door in the evening, and Callie heard it because she was sitting on the step of the shed with the hook across her knees, and neither of them asked her to move.

It had started with the rack. Rook had said, from his crate, that the rack was the best thing the yard had done, better than the jam, because it had moved. Miss Wren had said it was a good drill. Rook had said a drill was a drill, and the fence was the fence, and there was one thing on that paper of Toren's that no drill in the yard could teach, and she knew what it was.

"He's never felt it," said Rook.

He was standing in the doorway of the shed, holding on to the frame. Miss Wren was inside at the table. Callie could see both of them.

"The boy found a woman bleeding inside in the fog at forty yards," said Rook. "He can find anything he's ever felt. He found Wyck's collarbone through a shirt with his eyes shut. He found a pin going bad in his brother at the second hour before the fever came up. He can find anything he's felt once." He was quiet a moment. "He's never felt the thing we're asking him to find. Not once. He doesn't know what it is. He'll feel it for the first time on that hill, inside the fence, in you or me or her, with the jars going down, and he'll have to learn what it is in the same breath as he learns how to get it out."

"I won't put it in a body to teach a boy," said Miss Wren.

"Then you'll put it in a body on a hill."

Miss Wren did not answer.

Callie sat very still on the step. She could see Miss Wren's back at the table, bent over the book, not writing. She could see Rook's hand on the door frame.

"I learned it on a road," said Rook, more quietly. "On Senna. For three hundred miles, in a cart. I didn't know what I was doing. I knew there was something in her that wasn't her, and wasn't old, and was busy. And every night I could take a little of it out, and it made my hands ache for an hour after. I didn't know what it was until Sowerby said the word *course*. I'd been doing his course by hand, badly, for a month, on a flask of road-ash." He looked at his hand on the frame. "I can show him how to find it. I can put my hand over his. I can't show him on nothing."

For a long time Miss Wren said nothing at all.

"No," she said at last. "You can't."

Rook waited.

"I can teach him how much," said Miss Wren. She did not turn round. "How long. How to rotate. How to vent, and when to stop, and when to make somebody stop. I can teach him all of it. I've got the table. I've got Vell's whole method in my head and most of it in this book. I can't teach him what to find." She put the pencil down. "Nobody can. Not from outside. It isn't a thing you can be told. You were right about that on the twenty-ninth and I didn't want you to be."

"Then how did you learn it?"

Miss Wren turned round on her stool.

"On Vell," she said. "On his arm."

Rook's hand tightened on the door frame.

"He had a source," said Miss Wren. "A little one, in lead, in a box in his room. Every spring, when the new medical children came to the table, he'd sit down at the head of it and roll his sleeve up, and open the box, and hold his forearm to the window of it for a count of sixty. And then he'd put his arm on the table and make every one of us find it. What was in him that wasn't him. And draw it." She looked at her own left wrist, the crooked one. "He was seventy. He did it every spring for eleven years. He said a teacher who wouldn't be the patient wasn't a teacher, and a healer who'd never felt it from the inside was guessing." She was quiet a moment. "He said it was the only honest way."

"I never knew that," said Rook.

"You were in the doors line," said Miss Wren. "With your nose in the air."

---

Callie heard herself speak before she had decided to.

"There's a box in the shed," she said.

They both looked at her.

"The machine shed," said Callie. "The grey-haired man's got a dead one. Row one, nine along. It died before he came. He keeps it for the screws." She had the hook across her knees and her hands flat on the conduit. "Toren carried it. On the eleventh day. He copied the plate off it. He said it was heavy, far heavier than anything that size should be, and the man said it was lead, all round, to keep what's in the middle from talking to what's next door." She stopped. "The man said the box is dead. He said the thing in the middle isn't. He said it's as good as the day they sealed it."

Miss Wren looked at her for a long time.

"Toren carried it," she said.

"On his hip. Like a barrow."

Miss Wren stood up. She stood at the table with her hand flat on the book, and Callie could see her doing a sum. Not the kind Callie did, with numbers going past one after another. The other kind, the kind Pell did, with a price on every line.

"Fetch him," she said. "The man from the shed. Now, before he goes to his bed. And Callie." She held Callie's eyes. "Don't tell anybody else yet. Not Toren, and not Jab. I want to hear what the man says before anybody hopes."

---

The grey-haired man came across the dark yard with his sleeves rolled and the ink up the side of his right hand, and stood in the doorway of the east shed and listened to all of it without saying a word.

When Miss Wren had finished he rubbed the ink with his thumb.

"It's dead," he said. "The box. The part that reads it's dead. The middle's not." He looked at the floor. "I don't know how much is left in it. It was sealed before I was born. It was a reference for pictures nobody takes any more, and the kind of thing they put in those runs down slow. Slow like a river runs down. Seventy years. It'll have lost more than half." He looked up. "More than half of a thing that could make a man sick in an afternoon if he sat on it."

"And less than half?"

"Less than half," said the man, "is a count of sixty with your arm at the window, like your old man did. Maybe less. I'd want to measure it." He looked at Miss Wren. "I've got a counter. I've got two. One's older than me."

"Would you do it?" said Rook.

The man was quiet for a long time.

"I'd want the doctor," he said. "Sowerby. In the room, with his kit. I'd want it somewhere with a drain and hot water and people who know how to hose a man down without asking why. I'd want it after dark, when nobody's walking past. And I'd want the only hands that go near that box to be mine." He rubbed his thumb along the ink. "And I'd want it written. Every bit of it. Before, and during, and after."

"Hallet's pen," said Rook. "At the north gate. It's got a drain. It's got hoses. It's got the grey coats."

"It's got Hallet," said the grey-haired man, and something at the corner of his mouth moved. "He'll want it on his sheet."

"He can have it on his sheet," said Miss Wren.

---

The man in the long coat carried it up the outside stair at the ninth hour of the evening, with the grey-haired man's conditions written on a card in the grey-haired man's hand, and Sowerby's name under them. Callie heard later that Sowerby had read the card at his desk in the long building, and put his head in his hands for about a minute, and then taken the pencil and written *I'd rather this was on my sheet in a pen than on a hill in a fence* underneath, and signed it.

The answer came down the stair at the tenth hour.

*Yes. In Mr Hallet's pen, after the north gate closes. On Mr Hallet's sheet. Under Doctor Sowerby. The technician's hands only on the box. Miss Wren's arm only at the window. Everything written.*

It was not signed. It did not need to be.

---

On the thirty-fifth morning the grey-haired man taught Callie the counter.

He did it at the bottom of the long slope under the north end of the long building, on the bench by the yellow line, because it was the only place in the station where he could be sure of the air. The counter was a grey box the size of a boot brush, with a needle behind a little window, and a grille on the side, and a handle on the top. When he turned the switch on the side it began to tick.

It was not like the machine's knocking. The machine knocked in runs, eleven and a gap and eleven. This ticked the way rain starts, one and then nothing and then two close together, uneven, never the same twice.

"That's the room," said the man. "That's everything. The stone of the walls, and the ground, and the air, and you, and me. Everything talks a little. That's what it sounds like when nothing's wrong." He held the counter out to her. "Count it. For ten breaths."

Callie counted. Three ticks, and a gap, and one, and two together. Seven in ten breaths.

"Seven," she said.

"About that. Sometimes five, sometimes ten. That's the floor of it." He took a small grey stone out of his pocket, no bigger than a thumbnail, and held it an inch from the grille.

The ticking went up. Not a great deal. It went from rain starting to rain falling, steady, a tick in every breath and then two.

"That's a bit of old granite from the depot," said the man. "It talks a little more than most. It's nothing." He put the stone away. "Tonight, when the window's open, it'll do more than that. I'll tell you the numbers I want. You'll count them out loud, every ten breaths, flat, the way you count for me in the shed. I'll want to hear it without looking at you. And if the needle goes past the red line on the window—" he tapped it, a small red mark near the right-hand end "—you'll say *red*, as loud as you can, and everybody goes out through the gate."

"Everybody."

"Everybody," said the man. "Me as well."

Callie held the counter on her knees. It ticked. It was like holding something small and alive that could not stop telling the truth.

"Why me?" she said.

The grey-haired man looked at her for a moment with his red-rimmed eyes.

"Because you counted ten four times in twenty-two runs on the sixteenth day," he said, "and you were right, and I'd told nobody." He stood up. "And because you're the only one of them who'll say the number when it's a bad number. I've been listening to you do it for a month."

---

She told her mother at the middle of the day, because she had said she would tell her first, and this was not the hill, but it was the nearest thing to the hill there had been.

Her mother was on her stool with the tally board. She listened to all of it without saying anything: the pen, and the box, and Miss Wren's arm at the window for a count of sixty, and the counter, and *red*, and *everybody*. When Callie had finished, her mother sat for a while looking at the plate.

"And you'll be where?" she said.

"On the bench. By the hatch. Thirty feet off. With the counter."

"Counting."

"Counting."

Her mother nodded slowly.

"That's a good place for you," she said. "It's the one kind of counting that's for somebody else." She put her left hand out, palm up, on the ledge of the window, and Callie put her own in it. "Something left?"

"Something left," said Callie. "A finger and a bit. And I've a stone settled for tonight, and I know where it is, and I know how to let go of it."

Her mother's fingers closed on hers.

"Then you've learned something I never did," she said, "and I'm very proud of you, and I'd like my hand back now, because Otto's coming up the yard with a jar, and I can hear from here there's ditch in it."

# Chapter 20 — Jab: Clean In, Dirty Out

The north gate shut at the eighth hour of the evening, and at the ninth the grey coats opened the pen for them from the inside and stood back.

Jab had been in the pen once before, on the night of the north road, with his boots in a bin and his collar open and his brother gone in through the gate ahead of him. It had not changed. It was the same poured concrete, forty feet by twenty, with the drain in the middle under its iron grating, and the bench along one long wall with the hot pipes behind it, and the hatch at the far end with the window in it. The light in the ceiling behind its cage of wire was flat and white and did not move. The air smelled of wet stone and the soap the grey coats used.

Ivor Hallet was behind the window.

He had his cardigan on, buttoned right this time, and his pen on its string, and the big ledger open in front of him. He looked along the pen at all of them coming in, the way he had looked along the bench on the first night.

"I was in my bed last time," he said through the glass. "This time I'm at my window. I'd like that noted. I'm going to write this down in the order it happens. I'd ask everybody to let it happen in an order."

"That's the idea, Mr Hallet," said Dee.

"I've heard that before," said Hallet, "about eleven hundred times." He picked up the pen. "Go on."

---

The grey-haired man set the box down in the middle of the pen.

He had brought it across the north yard on a low flat trolley, with a canvas over it, and he lifted it off the trolley himself, in heavy leather gloves that came up to his elbows. Jab watched his arms take the weight. They took it the way Toren's had taken it on the eleventh day, by Toren's account: dropping three inches before they understood. It was grey steel, about the size of a loaf, with a little dark window in the front and a plate riveted on beside it with the wheel stamped in the corner. Across the window there was a steel slide in a groove, shut.

He set it on an iron stool from the depot three feet from the drain, with its window facing the gate end of the pen, away from the hatch and away from the bench. Then he took a piece of chalk out of his apron and drew a ring on the concrete round the stool, six feet out, all the way round.

"Nobody inside the chalk," he said. "Nobody but me. And her arm, when I say, and only her arm, and only at the window." He stood up. "The slide's stiff. It'll not have been opened since before I was born. I'll do it with the tongs, and I'll close it with the tongs, and nobody else touches anything."

"Is it safe?" said Toren, from the gate.

The grey-haired man looked at him.

"No," he said. "It's a box of something that's been talking in the dark for seventy years with nobody listening. It's safe the way a stove's safe. You don't sit on it." He turned the box a quarter on the stool, carefully, with both gloved hands, so that the plate faced him. There were screws down the back of it, round a plate the size of his palm. Jab counted them from where he stood without meaning to. Four. There were holes for more.

The man saw him counting.

"Eleven," he said. "There were eleven. I've had seven off it over the years for the rack. It's never needed them. Nobody's moved it in twenty years but me and him." He nodded at Toren in the gate. "It's a dead box. You take the screws off a dead box."

---

Then they went to their places, and Dee said the rules out loud, and Hallet wrote them down.

The grey-haired man stood at the stool with his tongs, inside the chalk. Dee knelt on the concrete just outside the chalk on the window side, with her right sleeve rolled to the elbow and her forearm bare. Jab knelt beside her on her left, outside the chalk, with his hands on his knees. Rook sat on a crate behind Jab, because he could not kneel for long and would not stand for it, close enough to put his hand on Jab's shoulder.

On the bench by the hot pipes, at Jab's back, there was a wooden rack of six jars, stoppered, grey to the shoulder. Six clean Flasks. On the floor against the far wall by the gate, well away from the rack, there were three more jars, open, with a finger of pale dust in the bottom of each: spent ash, used and colourless, that Dee had kept from the long table. Nobody was in it any more. It did not mind.

"Clean on the bench," said Dee. "Dirty on the floor. Never the same shelf. Never the same hand."

Callie sat on the bench at the hatch end, thirty feet off, with the grey-haired man's counter on her knees and Dee's brass glass on the bench beside her. The counter ticked. From where he knelt Jab could hear it, the way you hear rain starting: one, and nothing, and two close together. Doctor Sowerby stood at the hatch end with a basin of warm water from the tap behind the pipes, and soap, and a pair of scissors, and blankets, and a face like a shut door. Toren stood at the pen gate with the jar bag on his good shoulder, as runner. Outside the gate the two grey coats stood with the canvas hose.

"Say it," said Hallet, through the glass.

"Only his hands on the box," said Dee. "Only my arm at the window. The slide open for one turn of the glass. Callie calls the ticks every ten breaths, out loud. If the needle goes past the red, she says *red*, and everybody goes out through that gate, him too. If he says *shut*, it's shut and my arm comes away." She did not look round. "Jab touches me. Nobody else. Elias's hand touches Jab. Nobody else's. Clean in the left hand, out of the jar. Dirty out the right, into the dust on the floor. I say *vent*, you vent. I say *off*, you're off. If you get to your floor, you say *floor* and you're off, and I'll not argue."

"Written," said Hallet.

"That's the smallest thing I know how to do," said Dee. "I'd like that written too."

Hallet wrote it.

---

Jab looked at his hands on his knees.

They were steady. They had been steady since the thirty-first day, since the afternoon in the first lane when he had lit the last inch of his knife for Toren without asking it, and it had lit, and Toren had said *don't check*. He had not checked. He had gone down that night, not to look for anything, only to see, the way Dee had taught him to measure on the seventeenth, and the bucket had been there. It had not been much. A finger of dark at the bottom, and the line scratched on the inside at the same place, a finger up. By the thirty-third it was two fingers. By this morning, a hand, near enough. His floor had not moved.

He had spent two days at the long table learning a thing Dee said she did not like and would teach him anyway. A jar in his left hand, held to his palm. His right hand on a dish of warm water, or on the back of Rook's hand, or on a straw sack. The ash coming in on the left and going through him and out of the right without touching what was his. Clean in, dirty out. He had done it two hundred times on things that had nothing wrong with them.

He had not done it on anything that had something wrong with it.

"Ready," said the grey-haired man.

He put the ends of the tongs into the notch of the slide and pulled.

It did not move. He pulled again, and it did not move, and he set his feet and pulled harder, and the slide went back in its groove with a shriek of old steel like a gate that has not been opened in a season. It went back all the way, and the little dark window in the front of the box was open.

On the bench, the counter went from rain starting to rain falling. Then to rain on a tin roof.

"Forty," said Callie, flat and clear. "Ten breaths. Forty."

"Arm," said the grey-haired man.

Dee put her right forearm out into the chalk, level, at the height of the window, with the inside of it facing the dark glass, and held it there, a foot from the box. Jab watched her hold it. Her arm did not move at all. Her face did not move either. She looked at the wall past the box and breathed.

"Turn," said Callie, and turned the glass.

The sand ran. Jab knelt beside Dee with his hands on his knees and did not touch her, because he had not been told to. He watched her arm. There was nothing to see. There was a woman's forearm held out level in the flat white light, with the fine dark hairs on it, and the lump on the outside of the wrist where a bone had knit wrong on a riverbank, and nothing else.

"Forty-four," said Callie. "Ten breaths. Forty-four."

He could feel it.

He had not reached for it. He had his hands on his knees and he was not touching anybody, and he could feel it anyway, the way he had felt the day holder at forty yards through fog. It was not a lean. It was something smaller and stranger. It was as if the arm held out beside him had a warmth in it that was not the arm's warmth. As if somebody had set a small stone that had been lying in the sun down on the inside of her forearm, and it was lying there getting warmer, and it did not belong to her.

"Half," said Callie.

"You can feel it," said Rook, behind him. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Don't touch it yet. Just know where it is."

"It's on the inside of her arm. It's—" He did not have words for it. "It's warm. Not her warm."

"That's it," said Rook quietly. "That's all it is. Remember what that's like."

"Forty-one," said Callie. "Ten breaths. Forty-one." And then: "Turn. That's the turn."

"Shut," said the grey-haired man.

Dee took her arm out of the chalk.

It happened very fast, and afterward Jab could put every part of it in order, because he had watched every part of it with his whole attention on the table, the way he had watched a jam from the bottom up.

The grey-haired man put the tongs in the notch of the slide and pushed.

The slide went a third of the way across the window and stuck. It stuck with a small hard sound, like a key that will not turn, and the man pushed again with the tongs, and it did not go. He put the tongs down on the concrete. He put his left gloved hand on the top of the box to hold it still on the stool, and the heel of his right hand against the end of the slide, and set his feet, and shoved.

The box moved on the stool.

It turned. Not much. A hand's width, on the iron seat, the way a heavy thing turns when you push on one corner of it and hold another. And the back of it, the plate the size of his palm, held by four screws where there had been eleven, took the whole twisting weight of seventy years of lead at once.

It went.

It did not creak first. There was no warning in it anywhere. There was a crack like a stick breaking, and the back plate came off the box and fell and rang on the concrete. Something came out of the open back after it. It was a plug of dull grey metal, heavy, and after that a small thing the size of a man's thumb that caught the light, steel, rolling.

The box tipped off the stool.

The grey-haired man caught it. He did it without thinking, the way Rook had turned his head on the ninth day toward a woman shifting her feet, the way everybody's hands go out to a falling thing. His right hand went under the front of the box as it came off the stool, and the weight of it came down on his hand and took his hand down with it to the concrete, and the box landed on its face with his hand under it, and he made a sound.

The small steel thing rolled across the concrete and hit the iron edge of the drain grating and stopped.

Jab saw it split.

It did not burst. It opened along a line down its length like a seed-pod, a seam that had been sealed seventy years ago by people who knew what the Fall would do and built it anyway, and a little grey-white powder came out of it onto the grating and the concrete, like fine salt. Some of it went down through the grating. Some of it lay on the iron. A little of it went up into the air in the flat white light, very fine, and hung there.

On the bench the counter stopped ticking.

It did not stop. It went faster than ticking. The ticks ran together into one sound, a thin high buzz like a wasp shut in a jar.

"*Red*," said Callie.

She said it loud, the way the grey-haired man had asked her to. It went off the concrete walls of the pen and came back.

Nobody went out through the gate.

The grey-haired man was on his knees at the stool with the box on his hand. He had his left hand flat on the back of the box and he was trying to push it off, and it was too heavy to push, and every time he pushed, it rocked on its front edge and came down on his hand again. The small split thing lay by the drain eighteen inches from his knee with its salt spilled round it, and the powder was on the concrete under him. It was on his right sleeve, where the cuff of the long glove met the coat. It was on his knees.

"Out," said the grey-haired man. His voice was very steady and it was not his voice. "All of you. Out. *Red.* She said red."

"You're under it," said Dee.

"I know I'm under it. Get out."

Dee did not get out. She was on her feet. Jab did not see her get up. She was standing at the chalk line with her sleeve still rolled and her face white and absolutely calm, and she said, in the voice she used for the four things, not loud, very clear:

"Nobody breathe it. Sleeves over your mouths. Doctor, cloths."

Sowerby had already thrown them. He had put his whole armful of cloths into the basin of warm water and pulled them out wet and thrown them, one after another, down the pen, and one hit Jab in the chest and he caught it and put it over his nose and mouth. It was warm and smelled of soap. He saw Rook do the same, behind him, and Callie on the bench with the counter buzzing in her lap and a wet cloth over her face above it.

"Toren," said Dee.

Toren came in through the gate.

He came faster than a person could run. There was the flat crack of struts loading and a scatter of grit off the concrete, and he was across the pen from the gate to the stool before Jab had turned his head. He did not go inside the chalk. He stopped at the edge of it, badly. His right boot slid on the wet floor where Sowerby's cloths had landed, and he nearly went over, and then he stopped from his heels, and his weight went down.

"Where?" he said.

"The box," said Dee. "It's on his hand. It's rocking. Every time he moves it comes back down on him."

Toren looked at the box for as long as it takes to breathe in.

Then he did the thing Jab had watched him do at seven paces under Wyck's sack, over and over, for two days. He put the foot of the strut down on the concrete outside his left boot, at a slant, and let it come up from there toward him, thin and bright, and it met his knee coming the other way. Then he put his knee, and the strut with it, against the back of the box where it rocked. He was inside the chalk now. He did not seem to notice.

The box stopped rocking.

It was leaning back against the strut, on its front edge, with the man's hand still under that edge. It was not coming down any further.

"Holding," said Toren, through the wet cloth.

Callie was already coming down the pen.

She had put the counter on the bench, still buzzing, and picked up the hook from where it leaned against the pipes. It was bolted onto its pipe, the long reach, four feet of conduit and a foot of curve. She did not come down the middle of the pen. Jab saw that. She came down along the bench, on the far side of it from the drain, fast, with the hook in both hands, and she was not looking at the box. She was looking at the floor.

"The floor goes to the drain," she said. She said it through the cloth, flat and loud, the way she said the ticks. "All of it. It's a bowl. The powder's at the drain and it's going down the grating and it's on his knees, and there's some on the floor on the gate side where his boots went back when he shoved." She pointed with the hook's handle without stopping. "Don't walk the middle. Don't walk the gate side. Bench side's clean. Along the bench and round."

She got to the chalk on the bench side and stopped outside it. She put the curve of the hook down on the concrete and slid it in under the front edge of the box beside the man's trapped hand, where the edge was on the floor, and she set the pipe of the hook across the rim of the iron stool.

It was a lever. Four feet of pipe over the stool, and a foot of curve under the box.

"Taking," she said.

"Yours," said Toren. He did not let his strut go.

Callie leaned on the end of the pipe.

The front of the box came up off the concrete. It came up an inch, and another half, on the curve of the hook, with Toren's strut holding it from going back and the stool under the pipe for a fulcrum. The grey-haired man's hand was in the gap. The hook went pale, frost on iron from the curve back along the pipe to Callie's hands, and the box stopped. It hung there with its front edge an inch and a half off the concrete and nothing moving it, and nothing going to.

"Mine," said Callie. "Get his hand out."

Jab got his hand out.

He did it because he was nearest, and because he had dragged a straw sack called Norris out from under a rack on the thirty-fourth day, and his body knew the pull before his head had told it anything. He came round the chalk on the bench side, the way Callie had said, and knelt at the edge of it, and reached in and got the grey-haired man by the left arm above the elbow.

"Pull your hand," he said.

"It won't—"

"It will. It's off the floor. Pull it."

The man pulled. His right hand came out from under the front edge of the box in its long leather glove, and the glove caught on something and stayed, and the hand came out of the glove. It was white and it was the wrong shape across the back, and the powder was on it, grey-white, like salt. The ink was on it too, all up the side, the ink that had been there for twenty years.

"Out of the chalk," said Dee. "Jab. Bench side."

Jab got the man by the collar of his coat, the way you get a straw sack, and pulled. The man came, on his knees and then on his back, sliding along the wet concrete, and Jab went backward on his heels along the bench side of the pen with Rook's voice behind him saying *bench, bench, stay on the bench* and Callie's saying nothing at all because she was holding a box in the air.

He got him to the hatch end. Thirty feet. Sowerby was there with the basin.

"Down," said Sowerby. "Flat. Don't let him touch his face. Don't let him put that sleeve anywhere near his face."

The grey-haired man was trying to sit up. He had his right hand, the white one with the powder on it, held away from himself at the end of his arm, and his right sleeve was grey-white from the cuff to the elbow where the powder had gone into the cloth, and he was trying to get the coat off, and every time he moved it the sleeve swung.

"Callie," said Dee, from the chalk.

Callie was still at the stool with the box on her hook. She was looking at the man at the hatch end, thirty feet away, with the sleeve swinging. Then she looked at Toren beside her with his strut against the back of the box.

The box came down its inch and a half onto Toren's strut and stopped there, and Toren said nothing at all. He took it the way he took the sack at seven paces, soft behind.

"Two," said Callie, to nobody.

The edge came off the curve. It came short, a foot of it, the long grey curve she had made short on the morning of the jam, cold and colourless, following the line of the hook. She pulled the hook out from under the box. She came down the bench side of the pen at a walk with the hook held out in front of her, the whole four feet of it, and the foot of grey edge on the end.

She stopped five feet from the grey-haired man, where she could reach him with the hook and he could not reach her.

He held still. She put the edge against the cuff of the grey-white sleeve, the underside, where it met the glove-mark on his wrist, and drew it up the length of the sleeve to the shoulder in one pull. It went through the cloth like a knife through a sack, and it did not touch his arm. Jab saw it not touch his arm. It went along an inch from the skin all the way up, following the line of the arm the way it followed the line of the hook.

Sowerby had gloves on. He took the coat by the collar and peeled it back off the man's shoulders and down his left arm and off, and the right sleeve came off open, like the skin off a fish, without touching anything, and Sowerby dropped the whole coat in the basin.

Callie let the edge go.

"Toren," she said. "The box. On the salt."

"It's lead," said the grey-haired man, from the floor. His voice had gone high and thin. "The case. The jacket inside it. It's open at the back. Put the back of it over the—the thing. The seed. Put the open end over it."

Then he put his good hand on the top of it and his boot against the side, and turned it. He did not lift it. He could not have lifted it. He turned it on its edge on the wet floor, a quarter, and another quarter, the way you walk a barrel. The open back of it came round to face the drain, and he pushed it, and it slid on the wet concrete, and the open back went down over the grating and over the split thing lying on it.

On the bench the buzz of the counter dropped.

"Ninety," said Callie. She had gone back to the bench and picked it up. "Ten breaths. Ninety. Under the red."

"Out of the chalk, Toren," said Dee. "Bench side. Don't touch your face."

Toren came out along the bench side with his left arm held against his chest and his right hand held away from himself, the way the grey-haired man had held his. There was powder on the knee of his trousers where the strut had been. He stopped where Dee pointed, by the bench, and stood still.

"Now," said Dee.

"As long as it takes to get the salt off his skin," said Dee. "Not longer. If I start drawing before you've got it off him, I'll be drawing what's on his skin for the rest of the night and never get to what's inside." She knelt beside the grey-haired man on the side away from the basin. "What's inside is the bit I care about."

Sowerby washed him. He did it fast and carefully with a wet cloth and soap from the basin, the right hand first, holding it by the wrist in his gloved fingers and not by the back where it was the wrong shape. Then the face and the neck and the hair, and the eyes, with his thumb holding the lids. The grey-haired man lay on his back on the concrete and let him. His teeth were chattering.

"Did it go in your mouth?" said Sowerby.

"I don't know."

"Did you breathe any?"

"I don't know," said the man. "It went up. I saw it go up." He shut his eyes. "I'd have breathed. I was right over it."

"Then you breathed some," said Sowerby. "That's all right. That's what she's for." He sat back on his heels and stripped off his gloves and dropped them in the basin, and looked at Dee. "He's yours."

"Elias," said Dee. "Here. On the chest. Jab, under him. I'm the watch and the reserve. Callie, the glass and the ticks. Toren, the jars from the bench, when I say, stopper out, into my hand. Never onto the floor. Never near the floor." She looked up at the window. "Mr Hallet, the time."

"Nine hours and twenty-three minutes of the evening," said Hallet, through the glass. "Written."

Rook came down off his crate onto his knees beside the grey-haired man, slowly, with one hand on the bench.

He cut the man's shirt open down the front with the scissors from the basin and laid it back. The man's chest was pale and thin and went up and down fast. Rook put Jab's right hand on it, flat, over the breastbone, and put his own hand over the back of Jab's.

His hand was warm and heavy and it did not shake. It had not shaken since the table on the thirty-third day.

"Don't look for what's wrong," said Rook. "Everything's wrong. He's frightened and he's cold and his hand's broken and he's lying on a floor. That's all wrong, and it's all him. Don't look for any of that."

"What do I look for?"

"What's busy," said Rook, "and isn't him."

Jab shut his eyes.

He did not reach. He had learned that much. He put his attention down under his palm, into the chest, the way he put it down on a table and took his hands off it. He did not ask anything. He let himself feel what was there.

There was a great deal there. The heart, going very fast and very hard, a bird in a box. The breath, quick and high. The cold, coming in from the concrete. The hand, far off at the end of the arm, which was a thing he could feel the way you feel the edge of a fire you are not looking at: a broken wrongness, fixed, like the old leaning wall he had felt in Dessa's leg on the fourteenth day. It was not moving and not busy. It was done.

And under all of it, through all of it, there was something else.

It was the thing he had felt in Dee's arm at the window, but it was not a small stone lying in the sun. It was everywhere. It was in the right arm all the way up, thick, like warm water standing in a pipe. It was in the chest under his palm, a fine warm grit, as if somebody had breathed in a handful of sand that had been lying in the sun. It was in the belly, less. It was in the face and the neck. And it was busy. That was the only word he had for it. Everything else in the man was doing what a body does when it is frightened and hurt, but it was doing it as him. This was not him. This was something in him that was doing something of its own, quietly, everywhere at once, the way a room is different when there is somebody pacing in it who does not live there.

"There," he said. "It's everywhere. It's in the arm most. It's warm, not his warm. It's—" He heard himself. "It's busy."

"That's it," said Rook. His hand did not move on the back of Jab's. "That's the whole of it. That's what she held her arm to the window for. That's what you're looking for, every time, for the rest of your life. The busy thing that isn't them." He let his breath out. "Now lift it."

"How?"

"Like a drift," said Rook. "The same as you've lifted every morning on the east track. Flat palm. Don't haul. It comes at the rate it comes."

---

"Clean in," said Dee.

She had a jar in her hand, stopper out. She put the mouth of it against the palm of Jab's left hand, the empty one, and held it there.

The ash came into his hand. Cold and heavy and slow, like water finding its way into a boot, with nobody in it. And it went through him. It went the way it had gone through him two hundred times at the long table, in at the left and across and out of the right, without touching the bucket. It went out of his right palm, under Rook's hand, into the grey-haired man's chest.

He felt it pay.

He did not know how else to say it, afterward. The clean cold went into the chest, and the busy warm thing under his palm came loose. Not all of it. A little. The part nearest his hand. It came away from the man the way a burr comes away from wool when you have worked it long enough, and it came up toward Jab's palm, and it was in his palm.

It was not like ash. Ash lay in the hand, cold, wanting nothing. This lay in his palm like a handful of hot sand, prickling, and it wanted something. It wanted to go down. He felt it start to go, up through his wrist, the way the ash had always gone down through him before he learned to hold it high.

"Hold it high," said Rook, very low, in his ear. "Don't let it down."

He held it. He did not know how. He held it the way he had held a tenth of a Handful in his palm in the shed on the twenty-ninth, for eleven breaths, waiting for the drip. He held it in the palm, and did not let it into the wrist.

"Vent," said Dee.

He lifted his right hand off the chest, out from under Rook's. Dee had a vent jar on the floor by his right knee, one of the three from the far wall, the pale spent dust in the bottom. He put his palm flat on the dust.

It went. It went out of his palm into the dust the way the ash had gone out of the grey coat's glove into the bin on the first night. He felt it go into the dust and lie there, and he felt that nobody was in the dust, and that it did not mind.

"Back on," said Dee. "Clean in."

He put his right hand back on the chest. Rook's hand came down over it. The jar came back against his left palm.

"Ticks," said Dee.

"A hundred and forty," said Callie. She had come down the bench with the counter and was holding it a foot from the man's chest, and she was looking at the needle and not at anybody's face. "Ten breaths. At his chest. A hundred and forty."

"Again," said Dee.

---

They did it again.

Draw, ten breaths, and hold it high. Vent, three breaths, into the dust. Back on. Clean in. Draw. Hold it high. Vent. Dee counted. Callie turned the glass every minute and said *turn*, and every other turn held the counter to the man's chest and said the number, flat. Toren stood at the bench with the rack of jars and put a new one in Dee's hand, stopper out, when she held her hand out behind her without looking, and took the empty one away and set it at the other end of the bench, and never went near the floor.

"A hundred and twenty," said Callie.

Rook worked beside him. He had his left hand over Jab's right, on the chest. He had put his right hand on the man's belly, low, flat, and Jab could feel him drawing there. It was not the same as Jab's drawing. It was slower, and steadier, and it came up out of the belly into Rook's palm as if Rook had been doing it all his life, which he had. But Rook did not have a jar. Nobody had put a jar in Rook's other hand.

"Where are you venting?" said Dee.

Rook did not answer.

Jab felt it before Dee said anything. He felt it through the back of his own hand, where Rook's lay on it. Rook drew the busy warm thing up out of the man's belly into his palm, and held it, and then it went down out of him. It went down through him, not into him but through, the way a Ward goes down, into the ground through his knees.

"Elias," said Dee. Her voice did not rise. "Not into my floor."

"It's what I do."

"On a road. In a ditch. On a hill where the ground's already dirty. This is a pen with a drain and a boy kneeling in it." She had not stopped counting for Jab. "Jar. There's a jar by your knee. Vent into it or get off him."

"*Ticks at his knees*," said Callie, from above them. She had moved the counter. "Rook. The floor under his knees. Fifty. Ten breaths. It was eight."

Rook looked down at the concrete under his own knees.

Then he lifted his right hand off the man's belly and put it flat in the second vent jar, and Jab felt through the back of his hand what went out of Rook into the dust. It was more than had gone out of Jab. It was more than it should have been.

"Change," said Dee. "Jab, you draw alone. Elias, reserve. Keep your hand over his and don't draw. Just be there."

---

"A hundred," said Callie.

He drew alone. It was harder without Rook drawing beside him and easier than he had thought it would be, because Rook's hand was still over his, and he could still feel through it what Rook was feeling, the way you can feel through a rope what the man at the other end is doing. He found the busy thing under his palm and lifted it, and held it high, and vented, and went back on.

"Eighty-five."

Dee held the jar to his palm. He felt it going down. He felt it the way he felt a bucket going down, a little every breath, and he knew it was not his bucket. It was the jar's. He knew the difference. He had spent two days at the long table learning the difference, and it was the one thing Dee had said at the start, on the fifteenth day, in the yard, with her covered dishes. *Know which is yours and which isn't.*

"Turn," said Callie. "That's fifteen turns."

"Change," said Dee. "Elias draws. Jab under him. Elias, into the jar."

Rook drew. Jab felt it through the back of his hand, which was now under Rook's again. It was slower than before. He could feel it slower. Rook drew, and held, and vented into the jar by his knee, and drew again, and each time it came up into his palm more slowly, as if he were lifting it through water.

"Ticks," said Dee. "At his hands. Elias's hands, Callie. Now."

Callie held the counter to Rook's palm when he lifted it out of the vent jar.

The counter went up. It went from rain falling to rain on a tin roof.

"Sixty," said Callie. "On his palm. After he's vented. Sixty. It should be ten."

"It's lodging," said Dee.

"It's not."

"It is. You're drawing it and you're not getting it all out. It's staying in your hands." Dee did not look at him. She was looking at Jab's face, and counting. "You're warm. Your palms. I can see it from here. They've gone red like you've held a pan."

"I can do another turn."

"You can do another turn," said Dee, "and then you'll be the one lying on this floor with Jab's hand on your chest, and I'll have two patients and one healer. Off, Elias."

Rook did not move.

"Off," said Dee. "Now. You're the next patient. You've been the next patient for three minutes."

Rook took his hand off the man's chest. He took it off Jab's hand. He sat back on his heels on the wet concrete with his two hands held out in front of him, palms up, and Jab saw them. They were red. Not the red of cold. The red of something that had been too near a stove, the whole of both palms and the insides of the fingers. They were not shaking. They were perfectly still and bright red and held out at the ends of his arms as if they belonged to somebody else.

"Twenty-seven minutes," said Hallet, through the glass. "Written."

---

"Can you find it without him?" said Dee.

Jab had his hand on the chest alone now. There was no hand over it. He had not known how much the hand over it had been doing until it was gone.

"Yes," he said.

"Then draw. I'm your reserve. I'm your watch. I'll say *vent* and I'll say *off* and I'll hold the jar. Callie's got the ticks. Toren's got the rack." Dee held the jar to his left palm. "You've done this two hundred times at a table. This is the same."

"It's not the same."

"No," said Dee. "It's not. Draw."

He drew.

It came up. The busy warm grit under his palm, thinner now, not in the chest so much any more but still thick in the arm, and he could feel the arm without touching it, a long warm pipe of it running down to the hand that was already done. He drew what he could reach from the chest, and held it high, and vented, and went back on, and drew.

"Seventy," said Callie.

"Jar," said Dee, and held her hand out behind her.

Toren put a jar in it.

And Jab's jar was empty.

He felt it go. It went in the middle of a draw, with his right palm on the chest and the busy warm thing coming loose under it. The clean cold coming in at his left hand simply stopped, the way a pour stops when the jug is empty, and the draw did not stop. The draw went on. Something had to pay for it, and there was nothing in his left hand to pay with. So it paid out of him.

He felt the bucket go.

Not a little. It went all at once, the way it had gone on the slab, a pour out of the bottom of him and across and out of his right palm into the chest, fast, faster than he could have spent it on purpose. The cold came into the ends of his fingers. It came in the way it had come in the snow, and on the slab in the fog, from the fingertips inward, and he knew it. He knew it the way you know the edge of a step in the dark with your foot.

The line was there. A finger up from the bottom. He could feel it coming up toward him the way the ground comes up at a man falling.

*That's your floor. You never go below it.*

*Not for anybody.*

*Especially not for anybody.*

"Floor," said Jab.

He said it out loud. He did not know he was going to until he heard it. And he took his hand off the chest.

He did it himself. Nobody lifted it off him. Nobody took his wrist. He took his own hand off the grey-haired man's chest and held it up in the air in front of him with the busy warm grit still prickling in the palm, and the cold in the ends of his fingers, and the line a hair under him and not crossed.

"Vent," said Dee.

He put his palm in the dust.

"Good," said Dee.

That was all she said. She did not look at him. She was pulling the stopper out of the new jar with her teeth. But he heard it. He heard it the way you hear your own name called in a crowd.

"New jar," she said. "Clean in. When you're ready. Not before."

He sat on his heels for three breaths with his palm in the vent jar and the cold going slowly back out of his fingers.

"Ready," he said.

She put the jar to his left palm. The clean cold came in. He put his right hand back on the chest.

---

It took fifty-two minutes in all.

At forty the grey-haired man turned his head on the concrete and was sick, into the basin Sowerby held for him, suddenly and a great deal. Sowerby said, "Good. Get it out of you. That's the belly letting go of what it swallowed," and wiped his mouth. At forty-four the man began to shake. It came up through him from his shoulders to his heels, hard, and again, and his teeth knocked together, and Jab kept his hand on his chest through it and thought of the Elder in her kitchen with the lamp lit, four hundred miles north, telling him what to watch for on a road. *Shaking is the body fighting. Be glad when it shakes.*

He was glad.

At forty-eight Jab reached down the arm for the last of it and came to the hand, and stopped.

"I can't get the hand," he said.

"Try," said Dee.

"I am trying. There's nothing to get. It's not busy. It's—" He did not know how to say it. "It's done. It's like the old wall in Dessa's leg. It's not getting worse and it's not going anywhere. It's just what happened. It happened when the box was on it, before anybody got to him."

"Leave it," said Rook.

He was sitting against the hot pipes under the bench with his red hands palm up on his knees, and his face was the colour of the concrete. He had not moved for twenty minutes.

"You can't get what's done," said Rook. "Nobody can. Not me, not her, not Vell. Get what's still busy. Leave what's done to the doctor." He shut his eyes. "That's the whole of the difference, and it's the one nobody ever wants to hear."

Jab left it.

At fifty-two Callie held the counter to the grey-haired man's chest and watched the needle for ten breaths.

"Twelve," she said. "Ten breaths. At his chest. Twelve."

"And the room?"

"Seven," said Callie. "Eight. The room at the hatch end, away from the box. Seven or eight."

Dee knelt back on her heels. She looked at the grey-haired man's face for a long time without touching him. Then she put two fingers on the inside of his left wrist and looked away over the pen at the wall while she counted.

"Off," she said. "Jab. Off. That's what's left, and it's not busy enough to be worth another jar. The rest is his body's to deal with and the doctor's to watch." She took her fingers away. "Vent, and then off, and then give me your hands."

Jab vented. Then he held his hands out to her.

She took them in hers. She did it the way she had taken Rook's on the north lip on the twelfth day, flat, palm to palm. He felt her draw. It was not like his. It was very quiet and very sure, and it went into his palms and found something there. It was a little warm grit, left over in the creases of him where he had held it high and not quite got it all out, and she drew it up out of him into her own palm and turned and put her hand in the third vent jar, and it was gone.

"You'd have carried that home," she said. "And it would have ached tomorrow, and you'd have thought it was the work. It isn't the work. It's what you didn't get out." She let go. "Everybody who draws keeps a little. Everybody. The watch takes it off the one who worked, before they rest. That's the last rule."

"Who takes it off you?"

Dee looked at him.

"Tonight?" she said. "Nobody. I haven't drawn." She looked across at Rook under the bench, with his red hands on his knees. "Him I'll do when I've got my breath. It'll take longer than yours."

"Nine hours and fifteen minutes of the evening," said Hallet, through the glass. "No. Ten. Ten hours and fifteen. Fifty-two minutes. Written." He put his pen down, and then he picked it up again, and wrote something else, and did not read it out.

---

Callie read the jars out before anybody asked.

She stood at the bench with the counter in one hand and looked at the rack, and at the empties Toren had set at the far end, and she counted them the way she counted everything, flat, out loud, for somebody else.

"Two full jars gone," she said. "Two Flasks. And three Handfuls out of the third, because Miss Wren weighed it when she took it off the rack and it's light by three." She looked at Dee. "And the Handful you'd settled in you before we came. You said so on the stair. And whatever Rook put in the floor."

"And Rook," said Dee.

"And Rook," said Callie. "To his floor. I don't know how much that is."

"Neither does he," said Dee.

The pen was very quiet. The counter in Callie's hand ticked, one and nothing and two close together. At the gate end, the box lay on its face over the drain with its open back over the split thing on the grating, and the grey coats had come to the bars of the gate with the hose and were waiting.

"Two Flasks and three Handfuls," said Jab. "For one man."

"For one man," said Dee. "For fifty-two minutes. With a spilled seed on the floor and his hand under a box." She sat back against the hot pipes beside Rook, and let her head go back against them, and shut her eyes. "Nineteen Handfuls. Out of the jars. Say it, Jab. Say the rest of it."

Jab looked at the empty jars at the end of the bench.

He thought about a hill with the top taken off, and a fence eleven miles round it, and rooms under a building going down and down, and hundreds of grey boxes lying on floors where a crew had dropped them because they were heavy and warm. He thought about six people inside that fence, not for fifty-two minutes but for hours. Not one of them with a seed spilled in his lap. All of them.

"We'd run out," he said.

"We'd run out," said Dee. "If we did it like this. If we went up that road with a rack of jars and good intentions, and did it like tonight, we'd run out on the first day, inside the fence, and then we'd do what you did on the slab. All of us. One after another." She opened her eyes and looked at him. "That's what tonight was for. Not the arm at the window. This. So we'd know."

"It worked, though," said Toren, from the bench.

Everybody looked at him.

"It worked," said Toren. He was standing where Dee had told him to stand, with his right hand held away from himself and the powder still on the knee of his trousers. "The ticks were a hundred and forty and they're twelve. He's alive. His hand's broken and it's done, and the rest of him's—" He stopped. "It worked. That's the other thing tonight was for."

Dee looked at him for a long time.

"Yes," she said. "It worked. Write both, Mr Hallet."

"I've written both," said Hallet.

---

The grey coats hosed them at the gate.

They did it the way they had done it on the north road night, and on the morning Callie came in through the south gate, and on every night anybody came in off a road with something on them. They took the boots. They took the coats. They took the jar rack and the vent jars, the vent jars with long tongs into a lead-lined bin that two of them carried between them with their faces turned away. They took Callie's hook.

"Third time, miss," said the grey coat who took it. He took it by the conduit, not the curve. "Some people make a habit of it."

"I know," said Callie. She stood in the road in her socks with the water running off her. "I'll have it back at the hatch."

"Washed," said the grey coat. "Tagged. It's been in something tonight."

The water was warm. Jab stood in it with his face up and his eyes shut and felt it go down him. He heard Toren beside him, and Rook sitting down in the road to be hosed in three movements, and Sowerby's voice saying things to the stretcher crew about the grey-haired man's hand. He heard Hallet come out of his door at the end of the pen for the first time anybody could remember, and stand in the road in his cardigan and watch.

"I'd like it noted," said Hallet, to nobody, "that I came out."

---

Otto came to the shed at the first hour with a message from the long building.

Jab was on the cot. Dee had put him there and told him to lie on it and not get up, and had gone to the table with Rook and taken his red hands in hers, flat, palm to palm, and had been drawing for most of an hour while Rook sat with his eyes shut and his face grey. Now she was writing. Rook was asleep, sitting up, on the end of the long bench. The bucket at the far end of the shed went *tick*.

Jab went down to look.

He did not do it to check. He did it the way Dee had taught him, to measure, once, properly. The bucket was there. There was dark in the bottom of it, less than there had been in the morning, a good deal less, and the line scratched on the inside a finger up from the bottom. The dark was above the line. Not by much. By a hair, by the width of a knife-edge. But above it.

He had stopped.

He came back up and lay on the cot and looked at the tin roof.

"He says," said Otto, in the doorway, going red, because he had been told to say it exactly and was trying to, "the grey-haired man. Mrs Lask says he said it to Sowerby, and Sowerby said to tell you, and Mrs Lask told me. He says to tell the boy—" Otto stopped, and got it right. "He says to tell the boy the ink'll come off now."

Nobody said anything.

Dee put her pencil down. Jab lay on the cot. After a while he found that he was laughing, very quietly, with his hand over his eyes, and that it was not quite laughing, and that he could not stop.

"Is that good?" said Otto, anxiously.

"Yes," said Dee. "That's good. That's a man who's decided he's going to live long enough to be annoyed about his hand." She picked up the pencil again. "Go to bed, Otto. Thank you."

Otto went.

Dee sat at the long table under the lamp with the book open and did sums. Jab could hear the pencil. He could hear her stop, and start, and stop. Two Flasks and three Handfuls, and a settled Handful, and a man to his floor. One man. Fifty-two minutes.

"Dee," he said.

"Go to sleep."

"I stopped."

The pencil stopped.

"I know you did," said Dee. She did not turn round. "I was there. I was holding the jar." She was quiet for a moment. "I'll write it down in the morning, in Dessa's book, where everybody can read it. Tonight I'm writing the other thing." She started the pencil again. "Go to sleep, Jab. You'll need a floor tomorrow. So will I."

# Chapter 21 — Toren: What It Costs

In the morning Jab drew the little that was left out of Toren's knee.

It was not much. Toren had knelt with his strut against the back of the box for the time it took Callie to lever it up and Jab to get the man's hand out, and then for the time it took Callie to walk the length of the pen and cut a sleeve, and then for the time it took him to walk a lead box on its edge like a barrel across wet concrete to the drain. Dee had written the times down in the shed at the second hour of the morning, from Callie's glass and Hallet's sheet. Eleven breaths, and forty, and nineteen. Seventy breaths inside the chalk, a foot from an open box, with salt on the floor.

"About what a morning at the fence would give you," said Dee. "Near the gate. Not deep." She held the second counter, the one older than the grey-haired man, to the knee of his trousers. They were clean trousers from the hatch; his own were in a bin. The counter ticked faster than rain starting and slower than rain falling. "It's in the knee and the shin, where the strut was, and a little up the leg. Nothing you'd feel. Nothing you'd ever know about until you were sixty." She took the counter away. "Jab. Draw it."

"On my own?"

"I'm the watch," said Dee. "Elias is asleep, and if he wakes up and tries to be your reserve I'll tie him to the bench. Callie's got the jar." She held out her hand, and Callie, on the long bench with a single clean jar in her lap, took the stopper out and put it in Dee's palm. "Clean in the left. Dirty out the right. You know."

Jab knelt in front of Toren on the shed floor and put his right hand flat on Toren's shin, below the knee, over the trouser leg.

Toren felt it.

He had not known he would. He had thought it would be like a hand on his leg and nothing else, because Dee said it was nothing he would feel. But when Jab's palm settled on his shin and Callie held the jar to Jab's other hand, something in his leg that he had not known was there came loose. It was like having a splinter drawn that you had not known was in you: a small tight place in the flesh that you had been carrying without noticing, and then it was not there, and you noticed it by its going. It went up out of his shin toward Jab's hand. He felt it go.

Jab lifted his palm and put it in the vent jar on the floor and held it there for three breaths, with his face very quiet.

"Again," said Dee.

Three times. Then Dee held the counter to his knee and it ticked like rain starting, one and nothing and two close together, and she said, "That's the room," and wrote it down.

"That was all?" said Toren.

"That was all." Dee looked at Jab, who was still kneeling with his hand in the vent jar. "How much did it cost?"

"A Handful," said Callie, looking at the jar in her lap. "Near enough. It's lighter by one."

"A Handful," said Dee. "For seventy breaths inside the chalk, near an open box, on one boy's leg." She wrote. "Now do me."

She rolled up her right sleeve. The forearm she had held to the window last night was the same forearm it had always been, with the fine dark hairs on it and the lump at the wrist where a bone had knit wrong. Jab put his palm on it, and Callie held the jar, and Toren watched Jab's face go quiet the way it went when he put his attention down on a table and took his hands off it.

"It's still there," said Jab. "The stone. The one from the window. It's smaller than last night."

"It's had all night to do its work," said Dee. "Draw what's left. Leave what's done."

---

After that they went to see what it had cost everybody else.

The pen was shut. There was a rope across the gate of it with a card tied to the rope in Hallet's hand, *CLOSED, BY ORDER, ASK AT THE WINDOW*, and the grey coats had chalked a second ring on the concrete round the drain, wider than the first, and written *NOBODY PAST* inside it in capitals. The lead box lay on its face over the grating where Toren had walked it. At the second hour the grey-haired man's two assistants from the shed had come with a lead-lined bin and long tongs and a trolley. They were a young man and an old woman Toren had never seen before, who did not give names. By the fourth hour the box and the split seed under it and a square of the concrete floor round the drain had gone in the bin to the depot.

"Things go into that depot," said Pell, at the rope, watching them go. "Nothing comes out."

"That one can stay in," said Callie.

The grey-haired man was in the long building, in a room at the far end of the east corridor with a window that looked at a wall. Sowerby let Toren stand in the doorway for as long as it took to say one thing and no longer.

The man was lying on his back with his right hand on a pillow beside him, wrapped from the fingertips to the elbow in white. His face was grey and it was his own. He turned his head when Toren came to the door.

"The lad from the yellow line," he said. "Who carried it."

"I'm sorry," said Toren.

"What for? You carried it on the eleventh day and nothing happened. I carried it last night and I'd had seven screws off it." He looked at the white wrapping on the pillow. "Sowerby says three weeks. He says in three weeks he'll know what it did to the hand. The skin, and the ends of the fingers. It'll blister, he says, and then we'll see." He did not sound frightened. He sounded like a man reading a number off a screen that he did not like. "It's the right one. The one I write with. The one I work the screen with."

"Can you still—"

"I can work the screen with the left," said the man. "Slower. I've been doing it all morning in my head." He shut his eyes. "The forty-third's still hers. Tell your gran. I'll be at the desk. I'll be slower. I'll warm it more times. But I'll be at the desk."

"I'll tell her."

"And tell the boy," said the man, with his eyes shut, "what I said last night was a joke. Mostly."

---

Sowerby caught him in the corridor on the way out.

"I'm going to say this to you," said Sowerby, "because you're the one with the paper, and because nobody else will stand still long enough to hear it." He had his folder under his arm and he did not open it. "It worked. What she did last night. What the boy did, and the man with the back. I watched it. I've spent thirty years in this building watching things that are supposed to work and don't." He looked at the floor. "He'd had enough of that salt on him and in him to put him in a bed for the rest of the winter and take his marrow in the spring. I've seen it once before, in a man off the river, and I sat with him till he went. This one's going to lose some of his hand, and he's going to be sick for a week, and he's going to be at his desk on the forty-third day, slower. That's what it bought."

"Then it works."

"It works," said Sowerby, "and it costs more than anything I've ever seen work. Two Flasks and three Handfuls, and a Handful of hers, and the man with the back down to nothing again. For one man. For less than an hour." He put his hand flat on the folder. "Mr Voss. I want you to hear the bill as well as the good thing. You'll go and tell her upstairs that it works, and she'll hear *it works*, and she'll have to be reminded of the rest. You remind her."

"I will."

"And the other thing." Sowerby looked at him. "It does nothing for what's done. The hand was done before anybody got to it. Your gran's hands were done twenty years ago. Nobody's going to put their palm on her and give her back a finger. I know you know that. I'm saying it out loud in a corridor so that in a month nobody can tell you they thought otherwise."

"She knows," said Toren.

"She's always known," said Sowerby. "It's the rest of you I worry about." And he went away down the corridor as fast as he had come.

---

On the thirty-seventh day they built the ledger in the east shed, and it took from the first hour of the morning to the tenth of the evening.

Toren had not known, going in, that it would take that long. He had thought they would sit at the long table and say the five answers, and Dessa would write them down, and that would be the paper. It was not like that. It was like the adults' argument on the twenty-second day, only everybody was in it, and it did not end with a piece of paper that said *signed, not agreed*. It ended with a piece of paper that every one of them could stand behind, and the difference between those two things took eleven hours.

He found out, early, what his part in it was. It was not to have the answers. He did not have any of them. It was to ask for them, one at a time, from the people who would have to pay for them, and not let anybody answer a question that belonged to somebody else. He had not known that was a thing a person could do. He found that he could do it.

"How much ash," he said, at the first hour. "That's the first one. Who's got it?"

Nobody answered.

"Callie," said Toren. "You've got the store book in your head. And Dee's got last night. And Pell's got the prices."

Callie looked at him. Then she put the hook down on the bench beside her and put her hands flat on the table.

"Thirty-one Flasks and two Handfuls and a half," she said. "In the store. Four of them thin. That's the number Mum read out last night. Eight Handfuls to a clean Flask. Seven to a thin one." She did not look at anybody. "Last night was nineteen Handfuls out of the jars for one man. For fifty-two minutes. With a seed in his lap."

"At the fence there's no seed in anybody's lap," said Dee. "It's slower. It's in the air and the dust and the water, and it goes into you slowly. The counter will tell us how slowly when we get there, and I'll change the numbers then. Until then I'm going to guess, and write the guess down as a guess." She had her own book open. "Senna's crew lost their hands in eleven days, working five and six hours a day, deep. From what she said and what the counter did last night, I'd say near the fence gate, one person, one hour, needs about a Handful drawn out of them to stay clean. Deep, in the rooms under the building, three."

"Six people," said Pell.

"Five inside," said Dee. "You're not going in."

Pell looked at her.

"You're keeping the cart," said Dee. "At the pump house. Outside the fence. With the ash for the road home, and the horse, and the atlas, and a lamp. You're the one who's still clean if the rest of us aren't. You're the one who writes down what time we went in and what time we came out, and who, and what we were carrying." She did not look away. "Somebody has to be outside the fence who's never been inside it. Senna's crew didn't have one. I'd like us to have one."

Pell was quiet for a long time.

"I'd have said that myself," he said at last, "if you'd given me another minute."

"I know you would," said Dee. "I haven't got a lot of minutes."

---

It went on like that.

Callie did the sums. She did them out loud, flat, on the long table, with Pell writing the prices beside them and Dee saying *guess* or *not a guess* after every line, and Dessa writing all of it in the big book with a ruler.

Five inside. Near the gate, a Handful each, each hour: five Handfuls an hour. Deep, three each: fifteen. An hour from the gate to the stair with the yellow rail, by Senna's account, and an hour back. Two hours in the rooms. Five, and thirty, and five. Forty Handfuls for the people inside, if nothing went wrong.

"Things go wrong," said Pell.

"Venting," said Dee. "You lose some on every vent. You saw last night. The jars came out lighter than what went in the man. Call it one in five. Eight Handfuls."

"And whatever we meet," said Rook.

He was sitting at the head of the table, on the end of the bench nearest the stove, with his hands wrapped in white to the wrists where Dee had put salve on them at the second hour. He had not said anything all morning. Toren looked at him.

"The Director said the sky's wrong," said Rook. "Something's opened there. If it's opened, things are coming out of it. Husks. Hounds. Whatever else. They'll come at worked power, or they'll come at the dark. We'll spend on them. Lights, struts, the Ward in the hook." He looked at his wrapped hands. "I'd not count on less than a Flask for that. I'd not count on a Flask being enough."

"Eight Handfuls for whatever we meet," said Callie. "That's fifty-six." She stopped. "Seven Flasks. Near enough."

"Call it eight," said Pell. "Inside."

"Eight inside," said Callie. She wrote it on the table with her finger, the way her mother wrote on a tally board. "And the road there. Six days. Things on the road, like the north road. Hounds at a culvert."

"One Flask for the road," said Rook. "It was a Flask on the north road for five hounds, lights and struts and the Ward and all. One."

"And the road home," said Toren.

Everybody looked at him.

"Six days back," said Toren. "With whatever we've got on us, and whatever we're carrying. If we've spent everything inside the fence, we've nothing for the road home. We'd be the north road with nothing." He looked at Pell. "That's the one my father never priced. The way back. He always priced the way there."

Pell looked at him for a long moment. Then he wrote in the margin of his own paper, small, and turned it round so Toren could see. *The way home. T. Concur. P.*

"Three Flasks for the road home," said Dee. "At the cart. With Pell. Never goes inside the fence, for anything. Not if we're dying in there. If we're dying in there, we come out and we use it to get home, and we don't go back in."

"Eight and one and three," said Callie. "Twelve Flasks." She looked at the table. "Out of thirty-one."

Nobody said anything.

"That's nearly half the store," said Dessa, from her book, very quietly.

"It's nearly half the store," said Callie. "And none of it counts anything from the hill. Not one grain. The Director said *don't sell me ash you haven't lifted.* So it doesn't."

---

"Then why the rip?" said Toren.

He had not meant to ask it out loud. It was the question that had been sitting at the bottom of the whole ledger all morning like a stone at the bottom of a well, and he had not known it was there until he heard himself say it.

"If twelve Flasks gets us in and out without counting the hill," he said, "then what's the rip for? Why not go for the boxes and come home, and leave the rip alone? Why's it one thing and not two?"

"Because of the stair," said Callie.

They looked at her.

"Senna said," said Callie. "On the thirty-second. You wrote it. The boxes with the wheel are in the rooms at the bottom of the stair with the yellow rail, inside the main building, off the long corridor, on the left. Eleven rooms. They did one a day." She put her finger on the table where she had written *eight* with it. "Eight Flasks inside is an hour to the stair, two hours in the rooms, an hour out. Two hours in the rooms is one room, maybe two, if they're where she left them and nothing's changed in twenty years. If the first room's got the boxes, we take them and we come home, and that's the paper." She looked up. "If it hasn't, there's no more ash. Not for a second room. Not for a second day. Not for anything that goes wrong."

"And the only ash up there," said Dee, "is lying on the ground under that sky. Where nobody lifts it. Because whatever opened in the summer is sitting on top of it."

"The main building's under the sky," said Callie. "The Director said *over the old plant.* The plant's the main building. The stair's in the main building." She sat back. "You can't get to the stair without walking under it. And you can't get past the first room without lifting there. So it's one thing. It's always been one thing. It's only two things on paper."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Rook said, from the head of the table, "Write that. The way she said it."

Dessa wrote it. When Toren went and read it, later, it said: *Why one mission (C.): the boxes are down the yellow stair in the main building, under the wrong sky. 8 Flasks inside is one room, maybe two, if nothing's changed. Anything more, a second room, a second day, anything that goes wrong, has to be paid for with ash lifted up there, under the rip. You can't get to the stair without walking under it. It's only two things on paper.*

---

In the afternoon they did the rotation, and that was Dee's, and nobody else's, and she did it standing up at the end of the long table with the brass glass in her hand.

"Three healers," she said. "One works. One's the reserve. One watches. Every twenty minutes, by this glass, turned twenty times, it changes. The one who worked becomes the watch. The watch becomes the reserve. The reserve works. Round and round. That's Homura. That's Vell." She put the glass down. "The one who works draws. Clean in the left, dirty out the right, into the vent jar, every ten breaths. The reserve's got settled ash in them and a hand free, and if the one who's working goes at the knees, the reserve's hand is on the patient before they hit the floor. The watch holds the jars and the counter, and counts, and watches the worker's face and hands, and says *vent* and says *off*. And at the end of every turn the watch takes the leftovers off the one who worked, before they rest. Like I did for Jab last night."

"Who's first?" said Toren.

"I am," said Dee. "I've done it most. Then Jab. Then Elias." She looked at Rook. "And Elias works ten minutes, not twenty. Half a turn. Every time."

Rook looked at her across the length of the table.

"I did twenty-seven last night," he said.

"You did twenty-seven last night and your palms went red at twenty and you lodged it and I spent an hour of my own drawing it back out of you at the second hour of the morning." Dee did not raise her voice. "You've got less than you had. You had less than you had before the Sag, and the Sag took more, and last night took more again. I've measured it. It's in the book. Your floor's where it always was. What's above it isn't." She put her hand flat on the table. "Ten minutes. Half a turn. That's your share. It's written down, and the Director's going to read it, and you're going to sign it."

Rook did not answer for a long time.

"And when I can't do ten," he said.

"Then you're off," said Dee, "and it's two healers, not three. Jab and me. Twenty minutes each and no rest between but the watch, and Callie's the watch." She looked at Callie. "You can't draw. You can count, and you can read a face, and you can hold a counter to a palm and say the number when it's a bad one. That's the watch. You did it last night."

Callie nodded once.

"And with two healers," said Dee, "we can't stay in the deep rooms for two hours. We'll burn out in one. So the deep window halves. If Elias drops out before we're down the stair, we go down for one hour, not two. If the boxes aren't in the first room in one hour, we come out without them." She turned to Toren. "That's the answer to her *one more*. That's what happens when he can't. Write it."

Toren wrote it. His hand did not want to close on the pencil, and he made it.

*If R. cannot do his share: two healers (D. W., J.), C. as watch. Deep window halves to one hour. If the boxes are not in the first room in one hour, out without them.*

"*Out without them*," said Jab, from the end of the bench. He had not said anything all afternoon. "Say that again."

"Out without them," said Dee. "That's the line. It's not *when it's bad.* It's in numbers." She looked round the table. "And there are four more. I'll say them now and I'll say them at the gate every morning, the way Senna's crew said *nothing to eat, nothing to drink, nobody sits down*. When there's two Flasks left inside, everybody comes out, wherever we are, whatever we've got. Any healer at their floor twice, we come out. Any healer whose palms are still warm after the watch has taken the leftovers, they're off, and if that's two healers, we come out." She looked at Jab. "And if Jab goes below his floor once, even once, I turn us round."

Jab did not look away from her.

"Once," he said.

"Once," said Dee. "The Director's going to ask for that one by name. I'd rather it came from me first."

---

The rest of it came in the evening, and most of it came from people who would not be going.

"Who holds the Sag," said Toren. "That's the fifth one. That's not ours."

"It's ours," said Katori.

She had been standing in the shed doorway since the fourth hour with her cap pulled down, and Dessa had been sitting at her small table by the door with the big book since the first. Wyck was on the step outside with his bar. Ines had come in at the sixth hour with her bow and sat on the floor by the stove. The weighbridge girl had come at the seventh with her plaster, and sat on the end of the bench beside Callie, and not said anything at all, which Toren had never seen her do.

"It's been ours since the twenty-sixth," said Katori. "There's no holder. There's him," she nodded at Rook, "who can do ten minutes when she says. So we've been watching it. The lamps and the dark lane and the lips, and Dessa on her crate. Nobody's lifting. Nobody's in the dip. We kill what comes up at the lips before it gets to the road." She looked at Dessa. "How many?"

"Thirty-eight," said Dessa, without looking up. "Since the twenty-sixth. Eleven days. Thirty-one husks and seven hounds. All at the lips. One hound got as far as the berm on the thirtieth, at night, and one of the yard lifters got bitten on the arm, and Sowerby stitched it. That's all." She turned a page. "It's in the book. Every one. Where it came up and what killed it and how long it took."

"Eleven days," said Katori. "Without a holder. We can do it for as long as they're gone."

"You can't lift," said Pell.

"No," said Katori. "The station loses its lift. A Flask a day, more or less, into the ditch. The Director knows that. She reads the store book every evening." She looked at Toren. "That's the price of the home line. It's not ours to pay. It's the station's. Put it on the paper so she can see it."

Toren wrote it. *Home line: the watch (K., D. C., I., O., the weighbridge girl, two yard lifters). Holding since D+26, 11 days, 38 arrivals, one bite. No lift while R. is away: about a Flask a day into the ditch. The station's price.*

"And me," said Wyck, from the step.

Everybody looked at the door.

Wyck did not come in. He sat on the step with his back to them and his bar across his knees and his strapped arm against his chest, and he said it to the yard.

"I'd like it on the paper that I asked to go," he said. "I asked Keel this morning. She said a word I've not heard her say before and then she said the fifty-sixth day, and not a day before, and she'd know if I tried." He turned the bar over in his left hand. "So I'm not going. I'd like that on the paper as well. That I asked, and she said no, and I'm not."

"It's on," said Dessa, writing.

"And I'll be on the watch," said Wyck. "Not with a blade. I haven't got a blade and I can't hold a bar in the one hand long enough to hit anything worth hitting." He looked out at the dark yard. "The yard lifters stand like men waiting for a cart. The two of them on the lamp-line. One of them's the one who got bitten. He got bitten because a hound came up the slope at him and he went back a step, and it went round the lamp he'd stepped off." Wyck did not raise his voice. "I'll teach them to stand. In the gap, between the lamps. So that when a thing comes at them, they don't go back. It's the only thing I've got that's any use to anybody, and there's twelve days to do it in."

Nobody said anything.

"Write it," said Dessa, to herself, and wrote it.

---

"And Dessa," said Toren.

Dessa looked up from the book.

"You're the home line," said Toren. "You're not the ledger for it. You're it. You've been sitting on that crate with your eyes shut for eleven days telling Katori what's coming, and you've written every one of them down." He looked at the page in front of her. "Somebody's got to decide things while we're gone. When to call the watch in. When to send for Sowerby. When to tell the Director it isn't working. That's not Katori's, because Katori's the blade, and the blade can't be the one deciding where it goes." He heard himself say it and did not know where he had got it from. "It's yours. It's the thirty feet. You'll always know where everybody is."

Dessa sat with the pencil in her hand for a long time.

"That's not a book," she said.

"No."

"That's a—" She stopped. "I'd be telling the Director things."

"You tell her things now," said Katori, from the door. "Every evening. She comes down and reads your page at the south lip before she reads the store book. You didn't think anybody saw her do it."

Dessa looked at Katori. Then at Toren. Then down at the book, at the page with the watch on it, thirty-eight arrivals in her own hand, each one with where and what and how long.

"All right," she said. "Write it. Not me. Somebody else has to write it. I'm not writing myself down as that."

Toren took the pencil out of her hand and wrote it. *Home line, command: D. Cole.* He wrote it in the book below the watch, in his own careful slow hand, the way he had written Senna's name at the bottom of her page. Dessa watched him do it. She did not take the pencil back for a while.

---

Late in the evening, when most of it was written, he went to the long building to ask the day holder about the Sag.

It was Dessa's question, and Dessa had asked him to take it because she could not do the stairs in the east corridor on her leg at the end of a day. *Ask her where it breaks. She held it two years. She'll know where it goes first when nobody's holding.*

The day holder was in a room at the end of the east corridor, two doors along from the grey-haired man's. She was sitting up in a chair beside the bed with a blanket over her knees, and she was wearing her grey yard coat. It had been given back to her on the twenty-seventh day, on the Director's order, and Toren had heard from four people that she had not taken it off since, even in bed, even when the nurses asked.

She looked at him when he came to the door. She did not say *morning*.

"You're the runner," she said. Her voice was low and rough.

"Yes."

"Come in, then. Don't stand in the door. I've had enough of people standing in doors looking at me."

He came in and stood at the foot of the bed and asked Dessa's question. Where does it break, when nobody's holding it.

She answered it straight. She told him the Sag went first at six, where the slab had been, and then at the pipe, both mouths, because the pipe ran under the road where the line did not cover it and never had. She told him that in fog it came up the east ditch, and in clear weather it came up the joints in the bottom and ran for the lips, and that hounds in clear weather came up the west side because the west side was darker in the mornings, the sun being where it was. She told him that nineteen a week was bad and twenty-three was worse and twenty-six meant something had changed, and that she had felt it change in the ground under her hollow in the summer and told nobody, because there was nobody to tell. She told him all of it in a flat voice, like Pell reading a distance, and he wrote it down on a sheet from the back of Dessa's book.

When she had finished she sat with her hands on the blanket and looked at him.

"They wrote you in that book," she said. "You saw me go. At the fold. You saw me leave my hollow."

Toren stood at the foot of the bed with the pencil in his hand.

"Yes," he said.

"And you didn't call it."

"No."

She looked at him for a long time. He did not look away. He had said it in the shed on the twenty-sixth day, to everybody, and it was in the book in Dessa's hand. He had thought saying it would make it lighter. It had not. It had only made it said.

"I'd have gone anyway," said the day holder. "If you'd called it. I'd have gone down into that fog if the whole north yard had been shouting at me. That's what you do." She moved her hands on the blanket. "But somebody would have known where I was. Somebody would have been coming. For nine minutes I was down there with nobody coming, and I knew it, and that's the part I think about."

"I know."

"So next time," said the day holder, "shout."

"I will."

"I know you will." She looked away, at the window, which looked at a wall. "You're going up to that hill. Everybody in this building knows. You're the runner again, I suppose."

"I don't know what I am," said Toren. "I'm the one with the paper."

"Then you're the runner," said the day holder. "The one with the paper's the one who knows where everybody is." She shut her eyes. "Shout, boy. When somebody leaves their place. Up there. Shout, and keep shouting till somebody's coming."

---

They took the ledger up the stair on the thirty-eighth morning.

It was the same room and the same long table, and the same window running the whole north wall, and through it the same yard and the same road. At the end of the road the south lip of the Sag had lamps on poles along it and a figure on a crate. The slate on its easel had a new number at the bottom of the column. *27.*

This time there were more of them. Toren, with the ledger. Rook, in the chair with arms, with his hands wrapped to the wrists. Dee, against the wall by the door. Pell, with the atlas. Callie and Jab. Katori. Dessa, who had come up the outside stair one step at a time with her bad leg and Wyck behind her not holding. And Senna, who had not come, but was there anyway, on a page in the big book, with her name at the bottom in Toren's hand and a cross in a circle beside it.

"Sit," said the Director. "Mr Marsh, the chair with arms. What have you done to your hands?"

"Held them in the wrong place," said Rook.

"I read Mr Hallet's sheet," said the Director. "I know exactly where you held them." She put her spectacles on. "Mr Voss. You went away with five questions and one more. Say the answers."

Toren stood up. He had not meant to stand, again. He found again that he could not say it sitting down.

He said it in order, the way it was written, and when he came to a line that was somebody else's, he stopped and let them say it.

Callie said the ash. Thirty-one Flasks and two Handfuls and a half in the store, four of them thin. Twelve clean Flasks asked for. One for the road there. Three for the road home, left with Pell at the cart outside the fence, never to go inside. Eight inside. Nine committed before a grain of ash could be lifted at the hill, and three that could not be touched. Nothing counted from the hill. The rates from the pen, written as guesses, and the counter to change them at the fence.

Dee said the rotation. Work, reserve, watch. Twenty minutes by the glass. Herself, then Jab, then Rook. Rook ten minutes, not twenty. Clean in the left and dirty out the right. The vent jars left on the hill, sealed, never carried home. The watch taking the leftovers off the one who worked before they rested. What the counter said at the palm, and what the palms looked like, and what the worker's face did.

Dee said the lines. Two Flasks left inside and everybody out. Any healer at their floor twice. Any healer warm after the watch had drawn them. Two healers off. Jab below his floor, once.

Rook said the one more. He said it himself, from the chair with arms, with his wrapped hands on his knees. If he could not do his share, two healers and Callie as watch; the deep window halved to one hour; if the boxes were not in the first room in the hour, out without them.

Callie said why it was one mission and not two. She said it the way it was written in the book, word for word, *it's only two things on paper*, and the Director's pencil stopped while she said it and did not start again until she had finished.

Katori said the watch. Dessa said the thirty-eight, and the one bite, and the Flask a day into the ditch. Wyck, from the door, said the two yard lifters and the gap between the lamps, and that he had asked to go and Keel had said no and he was not going.

Pell said the road. Six days north and west on the old line, which was red in his atlas. The cart, and Duchess, the crew's grey mare, whose leg was sound now because the weighbridge girl had made the stable man change the poultice twice a day. The pump house on the far bank at the east gate, where Senna's crew had camped for eleven nights and never once slept inside. Himself at the cart with the lamp and the three Flasks and the atlas, writing down who went in and when and who came out.

And then Toren said the last of it, which was his own.

"Callie and I aren't healers," he said. "We're the rest. She reads the floor inside the fence, the dust and the water and where it's gone, the way she read the pen. She picks the clean way. She holds the hook across a door or a stair with the Ward in it, off settled ash, so that nothing comes through while the healers have their hands on somebody. She's the watch if Rook's off." He looked at the paper. "I'm the runner. I carry the jars from Jab's frame to the worker, and I'm the one who knows where everybody is. I put a strut up under whatever falls. I light the heel of the spike where the healers aren't, so the hounds go round. And the boxes." He stopped. "Senna's man said never carry one. The man in the shed says the same. So we don't. They go in lead, in a box his people in the shed are making out of depot lead, on the cart. Never on anybody's back."

He folded the paper.

"That's all of it," he said. "The Director asked for it written by people who were there. Everybody who wrote it is in this room. Except my grandmother, and she's on the page."

---

The Director did not say anything for a long time.

She sat at the head of the long table with the big book open in front of her at Senna's page, and Toren's paper beside it, and Hallet's sheet from the pen, and a fourth sheet that Toren could see was Sowerby's, because it was in writing too small for anybody else's. She put her spectacles on and read them, one after another. She took her spectacles off. She put them on again and read Sowerby's sheet a second time.

At the end of the room the man in the long coat stood with his pencil and did not write anything.

"Mr Marsh's hands," said the Director at last. "I'll start there, because I said I would. Ten minutes. Half a turn. It's written. Do you sign it?"

"I'll sign it," said Rook.

"Miss Wren. The boy below his floor, once, and you turn them round. Do you sign it?"

"I wrote it," said Dee.

"I didn't ask if you wrote it."

"I sign it," said Dee.

The Director looked down the long room at Jab.

"Do you?" she said.

"Yes," said Jab.

The Director looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked at the slate on its easel with *27* at the bottom of the column. Then she turned in her chair and looked out of the long window at the south lip of the Sag, a long way off, with its lamps on poles and a figure on a crate.

"I'm going to tell you what I'll take," she said.

She turned back.

"I'll take the twelve Flasks. Not give. Lend. Every jar's written against the name of the one carrying it, and every Handful's accounted for when you come back, spent or not, on the weighbridge board, in chalk, where everybody in the north yard can read it. What you lift up there comes back to the store and goes on the same board.

"I'll take the watch. For as long as you're gone. If the watch fails before you go, if anything comes over my berm that the watch should have stopped, nobody goes. Miss Cole will tell me. She tells me things now." The Director did not look at Dessa. "I'm told she knows.

"I'll take the reviews. Every household with somebody on that road is held over. Not extended. Held. The clock stops the morning you go through the gate and it doesn't start until you come back through it. Nobody loses a bed in this station because somebody from it is on the station's road." She put her spectacles on and made a mark. "That's not generosity. It's arithmetic. I can't send you and then turn your mothers out.

"I'll take Miss Wren's word as mine, on that road. If she turns you round, you're turned round by me. If anybody argues with her up there, they're arguing with this table.

"And I'll take a narrow thing, and I want it heard as narrow." She put both hands flat on the big book. "You're going for the boxes. The live ones, with the wheel on the plate, in lead, on the cart. Two, three if you can. And you're going to look at that sky and count what comes out of it and write it down, and bring me the count. You're going to lift at the edge of it if the ledger says you have to lift. You are not going into it. You are not going looking for whatever's under it. If it comes to you, you do what you have to do and you come home. If it doesn't, you leave it where it is and you bring me the count, and I'll decide what to do about it with a Sag held and a machine that can see."

Nobody said anything.

"That's my yes," said the Director. "It's not a big one. It's the only kind I've got."

She signed it at the bottom of Toren's paper, under all of theirs, under Senna's cross in a circle. Rook signed with his wrapped hand, big and bad, *E. M.* Dee signed *D. W.* Pell signed *P.* and then, after a moment, wrote *concur* after it for the first time anybody had seen him write it on something he was going to have to walk. Callie signed *C.*, and Jab signed, and Katori wrote a *K.*, and Dessa wrote *D. C.* very small, and Wyck came in from the door and wrote a *W.* with his left hand that went off the edge of the line.

Toren signed last. *T. Voss.* He wrote it the way he had written his grandmother's name, careful and slow, with the seam across his palm pulling.

"Your father's paper," she said. "Did it have the way home priced?"

"No."

"This one does." She took her spectacles off and let them hang on their cord. "I've one more condition. It isn't yours to meet, Mr Voss, and it isn't on your paper, and it won't be." At the bottom Toren stood in the yard with the big book under his good arm and the signed paper in his hand and looked up at the long window. The Director was standing in it, small and square. She was not looking down at them. She was looking out along the road at the south lip of the Sag, with its lamps and its figure on a crate.

He thought about his father's paper, which had said nineteen days with three ifs under the number and nothing after them. He thought about the way back, which his father had never once priced, because on his father's road there had only ever been a way there.

He opened the big book, standing in the yard, to the page for the twenty-second day, and found the line Dessa had written there from Pell's mouth, before anybody had gone near the Sag. *Everybody out. That's the one.*

Underneath it, in a different pencil, in Pell's small neat hand, from last night, Pell had written two more words.

*Everybody back.*

# Chapter 22 — Jab: Terms

The Director came down the outside stair a second time on the thirty-eighth afternoon, and walked across the north yard and into Yard C, and stopped at the step of twelve.

Jab was sitting on it. He had come back from the long room at the middle of the day with everybody else and not gone in. Otto was inside, doing something to the stove that involved a great deal of careful noise. The row was empty. It was a cold clear day with the sun low over the berm, and the concrete of the row had not thawed since the morning, and the Director came along it in her grey coat with her spectacles on their cord. People in the yard behind her had stopped what they were doing to watch her go.

She did not have the man in the long coat with her. She had nobody. She stopped in front of the step and looked at him, and then she looked at the step of eleven next to it, which was empty, and sat down on it.

She did it carefully, the way a woman of sixty sits on a cold step, one hand on the door frame. When she was down she sat with her hands on her knees and looked along the row at the lamp on its pole at the end, unlit.

"I said I'd ask again," she said.

"You did."

"You were in a bed the first time. You're not in a bed now." She did not look at him. "I read Mr Hallet's sheet from the pen. And Sowerby's, which he wrote in his own hand again, and sent up the stair again, which he's now done twice in a fortnight and never before in thirty years. And Miss Wren's, which says you said *floor* at the forty-first minute and took your own hand off, and she didn't lift it." She took her spectacles off and held them. "My technician's alive. The station owes you for that. And for the holder. That's two."

Jab said nothing.

"So I'll ask," said the Director. "The same as I asked in the shed. The roll of the long building. A hut by it, four years settled, not twenty-eight days. Training under Miss Wren on her terms. And on call, when something comes through my gate that's worse and there's nobody else." She turned her head and looked at him. "That's what I want. It hasn't changed. I don't change what I want because a thing went well. I'd be no use if I did."

"And what'll you take?"

The corner of her mouth moved.

"You've been talking to the girl with the hook," she said. "Tell me what you'll give, and I'll tell you."

---

Jab looked at his hands.

They were steady. They had been steady for a week. There was a faint redness still in the creases of his right palm, from the pen, where Dee had drawn the last of the warm grit out of him and said *you'd have carried that home*. He looked at it for a while.

He had thought about this. He had thought about it on the cot in the shed at the first hour of the thirty-sixth morning, after Otto had come with the message about the ink, and on the step every night since. He had thought he would have it in order when she came. He did not. He had it the way you have a road you have walked once in the dark: every turn, and none of it in words.

"My brother," he said.

The Director waited.

"His bed," said Jab. "His care. That's clinical, you've said. Everybody's said. I believe it." He did not look up. "His bed's not the thing. It's where he sleeps after. When he comes out of that frame in the spring and he's done with the ward. Where he sleeps then. And every year till he's done growing, when he comes back to be measured." He made himself say the rest. "Whatever happens to me. On the hill. If I don't come back down that road. He's not reviewed. He's not conditional. He's not a household of one child that's got nothing on its sheet. He's settled. Till he's done growing."

"That's thirteen years," said the Director.

"Yes."

"You're asking me to settle a household of one child, who's four, for thirteen years, on the strength of a boy who's walking up a hill on the forty-first day that took an old woman's hands in eleven."

"I'm telling you that's the first thing," said Jab. "Before any of the rest."

The Director sat on the step of eleven with her spectacles in her hand and looked along the row for a long time.

"A child in a bed," she said at last, "is the cheapest thing this station does. It costs me less to keep your brother inside this berm for thirteen years than it costs me in paper to review him every twenty-eight days. I've done that sum. I did it the night you came in." She put her spectacles on and took a small book out of her coat, and a pencil, and wrote in it. "I'll not write it as a thing you've bought. I'll write it as a thing the station owes. There's a man in the east corridor with a white hand who's alive, and a woman in a coat in the next room who's alive. The station owes for both of them. I'll pay it to the boy in the bed." She finished writing. "That way it doesn't matter what you sign or don't. It's paid."

Jab looked at her.

"That's not what I asked," he said.

"It's better than what you asked," said the Director. "A debt I owe him can't be taken back if you go and do something I don't like. A bargain with you could." She closed the little book. "Go on. That was one."

---

"Dee," said Jab. "Miss Wren. I learn from her. I'll sit at her table every morning for as long as she'll have me. That's not signing. That's not your roll."

"Agreed."

"You write what I can do. On your line. The long one." He found the words coming more easily now, the way the first few steps of a road in the dark are the hardest and after that your feet remember. "You can write it all. The lean, and the warmth, and the pen. Write it the way Dessa writes things. What happened and how long." He looked at her. "You don't write that I'm yours."

"The register doesn't say whose," said the Director. "It never has. It's got eleven hundred names on it and not one of them says whose."

"Then write that it doesn't," said Jab. "On mine. Write that it isn't anybody's."

The Director looked at him for a moment. Then she opened the little book again and wrote a line, and turned it round so he could read it.

*His own.*

"Is that it?" she said.

"That's it."

"Go on."

"Nobody sends for me," said Jab. "Not in visiting hours. Not on the day of his picture. Not when he's had a bad night. You can ask. Mrs Lask can ask. Anybody can ask. I can say no, and it goes in the margin that I said it, and nobody's allowed to think it means anything but no." He stopped. "And I go to who I choose. Not who's on a list. I'll go to a lot of them. I'd go to most of them. I'll choose."

"You'll choose," said the Director. "And when you choose wrong? When you go to one and not the other, and the other one dies?"

Jab looked at the unlit lamp at the end of the row.

"Then I'll have chosen wrong," he said. "And it'll be mine. Not yours. Not the roll's. I'd rather carry that than be sent."

The Director was quiet for a long time.

"That's the one I'd have fought you on," she said at last. "In the shed, the first time. I'd have fought you hard. I'd have said a station can't run on a boy choosing." She wrote. "I'm not going to fight you on it. I've read what you did with nobody sending you, and I've read what the station did on the twenty-fifth with everybody where they'd been sent. I'd rather have you choosing."

"And the hill," said Jab.

"What about the hill?"

"I'm going because of the shed," he said. "Because of the machine, and Senna, and whoever comes through your gate next and needs a picture. Because a box is going yellow. Not because you've sent me."

"I haven't sent anybody," said the Director. "I've let. There's a difference, and I'd like you to know I know it." She closed the little book and put it away in her coat. "Is that all of it?"

"That's all of it."

"Then here's what I'll take," said the Director.

She did not say it at once. She sat on the step of eleven with her hands on her knees and looked along the row, and Jab saw her decide something, the way he had seen Dee decide things at the long table.

"I told them upstairs I had one more condition," she said. "That it wasn't Mr Voss's to meet. It's yours." She turned her head and looked at him. "When you come back, you'll sit at that table on the other side."

Jab did not understand.

"Miss Wren's table," said the Director. "The long one. In the shed that leaks. At Homura, she tells me, there was an old man who went up and down behind the benches with a stick. He's dead. She's the only one left who sat at his table, and she's twenty-five, and she can't sit on both sides of it for ever." She did not look away. "There'll be others. Not this winter. Next winter, and the one after. Somebody's going to come through my gate with a lean in them that tells them when their mother's worse, and a card that says it doesn't come when they call it. They'll need somebody at that table who's been where they are." She put her hand flat on her knee. "Once a week. Not on call. Not on my roll. At the table. You teach whoever comes, with whatever you've got."

"That's not yours to ask," said Jab.

"No," said the Director. "It's not. I'm asking it anyway. It's the only thing I want from you that I'm not allowed to take."

Jab sat on his step for a long time.

He thought about a woman on a gravel bar four hundred miles north with a baby on her back, looking at the ugliest blade anybody in the valley had ever seen, and saying *well, it looks like you.* He thought about his aunt at the smoking racks, who had not told him he was doing it wrong for the first hundred fish, because he had to find it. He thought about a room with a long table in it and a bucket going *tick* at the end, and somebody he did not know yet sitting on the bench with their hand on a paper, looking at a little grey heap and not knowing which part of the cold was theirs.

"Yes," he said.

The Director stood up. She did it carefully, one hand on the door frame of eleven.

"I'll ask again," she said. "About the roll. When you come back."

"Ask me again," said Jab. "When I'm back."

She nodded once and went away along the row toward the north yard. People in the yard stopped what they were doing to watch her go. At the corner of the row she stopped and turned round, small and square in the low sun.

"Mr—" she said, and stopped. "Jab. Come back."

Then she was gone.

The frame had been under his bed in twelve since the second day.

It had come back from the hatch in two pieces, washed and tagged, with the rest of the things they had taken off him at the north gate: the boots, the pack, the hide cover, the three twists of bark in their paper. He had put the boots on and the pack on the nail and the bark in the stove, a twist at a time, the way you use a thing you carried a long way for somebody else. He had put the frame under the bed. He had not taken it out. Every night for thirty-six nights he had lain on the bed with it under him and not taken it out.

He took it out on the thirty-eighth night, after the Director had gone.

The two halves lay on the floor of twelve by the stove, and Otto sat on his own bed and looked at them. They were ash, the whole lattice, split and shaved and lashed in two evenings in a house with nobody left in it. The hide bands were furred and pale where the wet had got at them on the road. The left shoulder lashing had three turns of boot-lace over a cord gone to fur. The curved ash rail that had carried Tonk's leg stood up off the lower half, and the hound had gone through the middle of the lattice on the north road with a crack like a shot and split it from the hip belt to the yoke.

"Can you mend it?" said Otto.

"I made it," said Jab.

It took him until the second hour of the morning.

He did not mend it to carry a person. He knew that before he began. He took the split lattice apart where the hound had broken it and did not try to put it back the way it had been. He cut new cross-pieces from a length of ash Otto went and got from the wood pile behind the long building, the same pile Pell had cut Callie's mother's stick from. He lashed them in, tight, and cut the ends short. *Tight and short.* He could hear his mother say it at the smoking racks, *he lashes tight and he doesn't cut his cords long enough*, and he cut them shorter than he had ever cut them, and she would have said nothing about it at all.

He made a rack of it. Eight sockets, two rows of four, from offcuts of yard timber Otto found in the scrap bins. Each one was lined with folded canvas, so that a jar would stand in it upright and not knock against its neighbour. He set the rack into the lattice where Tonk's seat had been, between the yoke and the hip belt, so that the weight of eight jars would ride on his hips and not his shoulders.

The curved ash rail he did not take off.

He looked at it for a long time. It stood up off the bottom of the frame at the angle it had stood at for eighteen days on the road, the rail that had carried his brother's bad leg so it did not hang. He did not need it. It was only in the way. He left it on and lashed the top row of the rack to it, so that the rail was the thing the jars leaned against.

At the first hour Wyck came in without knocking.

"I saw the light," he said, and stood in the doorway with his bar and his strapped arm and looked at the frame on the floor. He looked at it the way he had looked at Otto's barrow strap on the third day, all of it, and where the weight would go.

"You've got the belt too low," he said.

Jab looked up.

"For jars," said Wyck. "For a boy, that's where you want it. He's on your back. His weight's up high and it wants to go backward, so you want the belt low on the hips to hold it down." He came in and crouched beside the frame, carefully, with his arm against his chest. "Jars don't want to go backward. They want to go down. You want the belt up. On the bone. Here." He put his left hand flat on his own side, on the point of the hip. "And put the chest band under the yoke, not over. Same as the barrow. Your chest doesn't mind."

Jab moved the belt. He moved the chest band. Wyck watched him do it and did not say *better*.

"It was his," said Wyck, after a while. It was not a question.

"It was his."

Wyck nodded. He sat back on his heels and looked at the rail, lashed now with the top row of jars leaning against it.

"You kept that bit," he said.

"I kept that bit."

Wyck did not say anything else. He got up, carefully, and went to the door, and at the door he stopped.

"Six sand jars," he said. "Fill them tomorrow. Carry them round the yard all morning before you carry anything that matters. Your hips'll tell you what's wrong by the fourth hour." He went out. Then his voice came back through the door, flat. "I'd carry it for you if I could. I'd like that known."

Tonk did the chair on the thirty-ninth morning.

Jab had not known it was going to be that morning. Keel had not told anybody. She came down the ward at the ninth hour with her sleeves rolled and a nurse behind her pushing a chair he had never seen: low, with small wheels and a wooden board sticking out level from the front of the seat like a shelf, padded, at the height of a small boy's knee.

Tonk looked at it from his pillow.

"What's *that*?"

"That's a chair," said Keel. "It's yours. You're going to get into it."

"*Myself*?"

"Most of the way," said Keel. "The nurse is going to hold the board level, and hold your frame steady on it when it gets there, and that's all she's going to do. The rest is you. Your arms, and the bar, and your good leg." She looked at Jab in the chair between the beds. "And you're going to sit there."

Jab looked at her.

"You're going to sit in that chair with your hands behind your back," said Keel, "and you're not going to touch him. Not his arm, not his leg, not his frame. Not if he wobbles. Not if he shouts. If he falls, the nurse catches him, and she's caught more children than you've carried. If you put your hands on him, I'll send you out of the ward." She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way she said everything, fast and flat. "He's done this in his head eleven times this week. He told me. He's not done it with you there. So you're there, and you're not helping. That's your job this morning."

Jab put his hands behind his back.

---

It took eleven minutes.

Tonk reached up and got both hands on the bar and pulled, and came up off the pillow, the way he had every day since the twenty-fifth, shoulders and then back, until he was sitting. Then he did a thing Jab had never seen him do. He held on to the bar with one hand and put the other hand flat on the mattress beside him, and he shifted. He lifted his bottom off the bed on his arms and moved it three inches toward the edge, and set it down, and breathed, with his face red and his mouth shut in a line.

The frame came with him. The four pins and the bar on the outside of the leg moved three inches along the mattress. His face went white round the mouth when it moved, and then went red again.

"Good," said Keel.

He did it again. And again. Eleven inches, to the edge of the bed. Then he let go of the hanging bar and put both hands on the mattress behind him, and swung the good leg over the edge, and it hung down. The bad leg in its frame lay along the mattress still, pointing the wrong way.

"Now the leg," said Keel. "Both hands under the thigh. Not the frame. Never the frame. Under the thigh, above the knee, and lift it."

The nurse had wheeled the chair up close to the bed with its board level at the edge of the mattress. She stood with one hand on the board and the other hand ready, not touching.

Tonk put both hands under his thigh.

Jab sat in the chair with his hands behind his back. He could feel them there, both of them, knotted together at the wrists. They wanted to go forward. They wanted it more than he had wanted anything since the slab. His body knew this leg. It had carried this leg for eighteen days and every night for a month. It knew exactly where to put a hand under the knee and a hand under the heel, and lift, and turn. It knew how much it weighed with the frame on. It knew the sound his brother made when the pins moved.

He kept them behind his back.

Tonk lifted the leg. He lifted it with both hands under the thigh, an inch off the mattress, shaking, with the frame swaying, and he turned on his bottom on the edge of the bed, a little at a time, bringing the leg round with him. It took a long time. Twice he had to put the leg down and breathe. The second time he said a word he had learned from Wyck, and Keel pretended not to hear it.

The leg came round. It came off the edge of the mattress and onto the padded board, and the nurse put her hand on the frame to hold it steady on the board, and that was all she did.

"Now you," said Keel. "Down. Into the seat. On your arms."

Tonk put his hands on the arms of the chair. He lowered himself off the edge of the bed toward the seat, on his arms, with his good leg taking some of it and his bad leg on the board. His arms shook. He came down, slowly, and down, until his bottom was a hand's width off the seat and his arms would not do any more.

The nurse put her hand under him and took the last hand's width.

He was in the chair.

He sat in it.

He sat in it with his bad leg out straight in front of him on the board, in its frame, and his good leg hanging down, and his hands on the arms, and his face the colour of a tomato, and he looked round the ward. He had not seen the ward from this height in five weeks. He had seen it from a pillow. He looked at the window over his bed and the door at the end and Senna in the next bed and the ceiling, which was further off than it had been.

"I did the *chair*," he said.

"Most of it," said Keel.

"*All* of it."

"The nurse did the last hand's width," said Keel. "I watched."

Tonk thought about this.

"*Most* way," he said.

"Most way," Keel agreed. "That'll do." She looked at Jab. "You can take your hands out."

Jab took his hands out from behind his back. He found that his wrists hurt where he had been holding them. He sat in the chair between the beds with his hands on his knees and looked at his brother sitting up in a chair on his own, with a leg out on a board. He found he could not say anything.

It was the same thing as on the twenty-fifth day, when he had woken up in the sixth bed and seen Tonk sitting up on the bar. It was two things at once. There was the big thing, so big it filled his chest like water filling a boot. And there was the small sharp thing under it, like a splinter under a nail. *He didn't need me.* The first time, he had been asleep for it. This time he had been sitting a yard away with his hands tied behind his own back by nobody but himself, and his brother had not needed him. Not for the bar, or the edge of the bed, or the leg. Only the nurse, for the last hand's width.

"Jab," said Tonk.

"Mm."

"Did you *see*?"

"I saw," said Jab. "I saw all of it. I was right here."

"You didn't *help*."

"I didn't help."

Tonk looked at him from the chair with enormous satisfaction.

"Good," he said.

In the fourth bed, Senna lay against her pillows with her hands folded where she could see them.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "I counted. The nurse says eleven and a half. The nurse is wrong." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at Jab. "Your hands were behind your back the whole time. I watched them. I've never seen anybody hold their own hands so hard."

"I wanted to."

"I know you did," said Senna. "That's why it counted." She looked at Tonk in his chair. "I got into a chair once, the first week. By myself. Two steps. I can't do that now." "That's how it goes, brother. Some of us are going up the hill and some of us are coming down it, and everybody gets a chair."

He should not have taken the frame to the ward. He knew that afterward.

He had not meant to take it to the ward. He had meant to take it across the north yard to the east shed, because Dee wanted to see it, and to load it with six jars of sand and carry it round the yard all afternoon the way Wyck had said. He had it on his back with the belt high on the bone of his hips and the chest band under the yoke, and six sand jars in the rack, and he came out of Yard C and along the side of the long building on his way to the yard.

The nurse had wheeled Tonk to the doors.

It was the first time he had been outside the ward since the night of the north road. Keel had said he could go as far as the doors in the chair, if it was not raining, and it was not raining. So the nurse had wheeled him down the ward past Mrs Lask, who had stood up, and out through the doors, and stopped the chair on the step at the front of the long building in a strip of low cold sun. And Tonk was sitting in it with his leg out on the board and a blanket over his knees and his face turned up to the sky, which he had not seen in five weeks.

He saw Jab.

He saw Jab coming along the side of the building with the frame on his back. And his face did the thing it had done at the gate on the first night, when he saw the lamp over the gate. It lit.

"You mended the *frame*!"

Jab stopped.

"You *mended* it," said Tonk. He was sitting forward in the chair, as far as the leg would let him. "It was *broken*. The hound broke it. It went in a *bin*. And you mended it." His voice went up. "It's got my *rail*."

"Tonk—"

"So I'm going," said Tonk.

He said it the way he said all the facts he had put together himself, with complete satisfaction, as if it were the end of a sum. Jab had mended the frame. The frame was for carrying Tonk. Jab was going somewhere. So Tonk was going.

"I've got *two* frames," said Tonk. "One on my leg and one on your back. So I can *definitely* go."

Jab took the frame off.

He took it off because he could not stand in front of his brother with it on his back and say what he was going to say. He undid the chest band and the hip belt, and slid out of the straps, and set the frame down on the cinders at the foot of the step with its six sand jars in the rack and the curved ash rail standing up at the top. Then he went up the step and crouched beside the chair, so that his face was lower than his brother's.

"No," he said.

Tonk looked at him.

"You're not going," said Jab. "I'm going. You're staying here."

"But you *mended* it—"

"I mended it to carry jars," said Jab. "Ash. In jars. Eight of them, in a rack, where you used to sit. Look." He pointed down at the frame on the cinders. "That's not your seat. It's a rack. It's for jars."

Tonk looked at the frame. He looked at the rack of six sand jars where his seat had been. He looked at the curved ash rail at the top, with the top row of jars leaning on it.

"That's my *rail*," he said.

"It's your rail. I kept it."

"Then why can't I *go*?"

Jab did the thing he had made a rule about on the road, and had kept every single time since, even when it cost him a night.

"Because of the pins," he said. "The nails in your leg. They go into the bone, and the bone's got to knit round them, and if the frame on your leg gets knocked about for six days on a road, the pins move, and the skin round them gets angry, like it did at the top one. And then you get hot, and it goes into the bone, and Keel has to go in again." He did not look away. "Six days there. Six back. On a cart. That's twelve days of the pins moving. Keel would say no. Sowerby would say no. I'm saying no."

"*You* carried me. On the road. With the nails."

"There weren't nails on the road," said Jab. "There was a broken leg on the road. And it went bad on the road, and it nearly killed you, and it took a woman with her sleeves rolled up two hours to take the bad bit out." He put his hand on the arm of the chair, near his brother's hand, not on it. "And where I'm going, there's a thing in the ground that makes people sick. It took Senna's hands. It'd take yours. I'm not taking you there. I'm not taking you anywhere you'd get sick."

"Then don't *go*," said Tonk.

---

His voice had gone high and cracked. His face was doing the thing it did now instead of crying, going red and very still, with his mouth shut hard.

"You'll be *asleep* again," he said.

Jab said nothing.

"You went *before*," said Tonk. "In the fog. And you came back on a bed with *wheels*. And your mouth was open. And I said *Jab* and you didn't say *anything*." He was not looking at Jab. He was looking at the frame on the cinders. "And I had to sit up by *myself*. And you weren't *there*."

"I know."

"And now you're going *again*. Further. For *twelve days*." He knew the number. He had heard it. He had been lying in a bed four feet from Senna for five weeks, and he heard everything. "And you'll come back on a bed with wheels. Or you *won't*."

Jab crouched on the step of the long building with his hand on the arm of the chair.

He did not lie. He had a rule about it. He did not say *I'll come back* the way you say it to a child, as if saying it made it so. He had said *they'd have to carry me out* on the first night at the gate, and it had been a lie, and his brother had said *you lied* with a four-year-old's terrible aim, and he had not lied to him since.

"I don't know," he said. "I'm going to try very hard. There's a paper with everything written on it, and a woman who'll turn us all round if I go below my floor once. Once. I said yes to that. I signed it." He looked at his brother's face. "That's the truest thing I've got. I'm going to try very hard, and there's somebody whose whole job is to make me come back."

Tonk did not answer.

"You did the chair," said Jab. "This morning. Most way. With me sitting there with my hands behind my back." He heard his voice go and let it. "You don't need me for the chair. You'll need me for other things. I'll be back for those."

Tonk turned his face away.

He turned it to the wall of the long building, as far as he could in the chair with his leg out on the board, and looked at the concrete, and did not say anything at all. Jab crouched beside him for a long time. The nurse stood behind the chair with her hands on the handles and looked at the sky.

"I'll take him in," said the nurse at last, quietly.

"Yes."

She turned the chair. Tonk kept his face to the side, away from Jab, the whole way back through the doors and down the ward. He did not look round once. Jab stood on the step and watched the doors swing shut behind the chair.

Then he went down the step and put the frame on his back again, and did up the belt high on the bone of his hips, and the chest band under the yoke. He carried six jars of sand round the north yard until the fourth hour, and his hips told him nothing was wrong at all.

---

Dee measured him at the long table that evening, three times.

It was the last measuring before the gate. She had said so in the morning. A quarter of a Handful each time: in, the way he did it now, holding it high in the palm with his other hand flat on the table, forty breaths before it tried to pass. Then settle. He could settle now, a little. Not the way Callie did it, all the way down. Enough that it lay in the bottom of the bucket and stayed when the drip went *tick* at the far end of the shed. Then spend, into Rook's hands, through the jar, the way he would at the fence. Then vent, into the sand. Then rest, for a quarter of an hour, with his hands on his knees.

Three times. Dee watched his face the whole time.

"Clean," she said, after the third. "All three. Nothing left in your palms. Nothing gone below the line." She wrote. "Go down and tell me."

He went down.

The bucket was there. A hand of dark in the bottom of it, and a bit more. The line scratched on the inside a finger up from the bottom, where it had always been, where somebody who had been there before him had scratched it and known.

"A hand and a bit," he said. "Floor's a finger. Same as the fourteenth."

"Same floor," said Dee. "More above it. Not because you're bigger. You're not. Because you've stopped spilling it on the way in, and you've stopped letting it lodge on the way out, and you rest." She closed the book. "That's working depth. Two weeks of it. At Homura it would have been twelve. You've done two, and you're going."

"Is it enough?"

"It's what there is," said Dee. "It's more than you had on the slab. It's more than Elias had at your age, on a riverbank, with me." She looked at him across the long table. "You'll work second. After me. Twenty minutes by the glass. Then watch. Then reserve. You'll call your own floor."

"I did."

"I know you did," said Dee. "I'm telling you I expect it."

---

Rook was on the step of eleven when he got back to the row.

He was sitting with his back against the door frame and his wrapped hands on his knees, and the lamp at the end of the row had come on, flat and white. Jab sat down on the step of twelve.

"Callie's mother told me," said Rook. "About the doors of the long building. Everybody in the station walks past her window twice a day." He looked at the lamp. "He's four."

"He's right," said Jab. "I will be asleep again. On a hill. Or I won't."

Rook did not say anything for a while.

"I went to Homura at fourteen," he said. "You know that. To buy my mother a doctor twice a year." He turned his wrapped hands over on his knees. "I told her I'd be back at harvest. She was at the table with her hands in flour, and she said *go*, and didn't get up. She didn't look round. I stood in the door for about a minute waiting for her to say *don't*. She didn't." He was quiet. "I was angry with her for that for about four years. I thought she didn't mind."

"Did she?"

"I found out later she'd sat at that table with her hands in the flour for an hour after I'd gone," said Rook. "My brother told me. She didn't move. She'd made up her mind to say *go* and not get up, because if she got up she'd have said *don't*." He looked at Jab. "I've been glad for sixteen years she didn't get up."

Jab sat on his step.

"He's not going to say go," he said. "He's four."

"No," said Rook. "He's not. He's going to be angry with you for twelve days and then some. You'll have to carry that as well as the jars." He shut his eyes. "It's lighter than the other thing. The thing where he says *don't* and you stay. I promise you that. I've carried both."

---

At the tenth hour, when the ward turned the visitors out, Jab went up anyway.

Mrs Lask did not stop him. She looked at him over her spectacles and went back to her list. He went down the ward between the beds in the half-dark with the small lamp turned low at the far end.

Tonk was lying on his back in the fifth bed with his face to the wall. His chair stood at the foot of the bed with the board sticking out. The long cord ran from his wrist across the gap to the rail of the empty sixth bed, where the knot was, the one the nurse had tied twice, that nobody was allowed to untie.

Jab stood between the beds.

"Tonk," he said.

His brother did not turn round. His breathing was not a sleeping breathing. Jab knew every kind of breathing his brother did. He had counted them on the road for eighteen days.

"I'm going on the forty-first," said Jab. "In the morning. I'll come and see you before. At the eighth hour."

Nothing.

He stood there a while longer. Then he put his hand, very lightly, on the cord where it crossed the gap between the beds, halfway, not near either end. He did not pull it. He only put his hand on it, the way you put your hand on a rope to feel what the man at the other end is doing.

It did not move.

In the fourth bed Senna lay with her hands folded and her eyes on the ceiling.

"Milo Faust," she said, to the ceiling, the way she said it every night.

Nobody said it with her.

She waited. Jab heard her wait. Then she said, quietly, to nobody, "That's all right. He's angry. You can't say a name when you're angry. It comes out wrong." She shut her eyes. "He'll say it tomorrow."

Jab took his hand off the cord and went out past Mrs Lask, who did not look up, and down the frozen street in the lit bits under three lamps that would go out at the first hour. At the loose grating between the second pole and the third he stepped wide without thinking about it.

On the step of twelve he went down, once, to measure.

A hand and a bit. A finger from the bottom, the line. Above the line, the dark, still and his and not going anywhere.

*Mine*, he thought. He did not say it out loud. He had said it on the north road into the dark with his brother behind him, and on the slab in the fog with his hand on a woman's chest. It had meant *they come at me*, both times. It meant something else tonight, and he did not know yet what, and he sat on the step in the cold and did not try to find out.

# Chapter 23 — Callie: Stop Holding

She had told her mother first.

She had come down the outside stair of the yard office on the thirty-eighth morning behind everybody else, with the ringing of their boots on the steel going on under her, and at the bottom she had not gone with them. She had turned the other way across the north yard, toward the weighbridge, and walked fast, and then run, which hurt her ribs and which she did anyway. Her mother had been on her stool in the hut window with the tally board on her knee and nobody on the plate. She had looked up and seen Callie's face and put the pencil down.

"They said yes," said Callie. "Not a big one. The only kind she's got. I'm going."

Her mother had looked at her for a long time.

"First?" she said.

"First. Before anybody. They're still on the stair."

"Good," said her mother, and put out her left hand, palm up, on the ledge of the window, and Callie put her own hand in it and held on until Otto came up the yard with a jar.

That had been yesterday.

---

On the thirty-ninth morning her mother weighed the ash for the hill.

The old man in the leather apron brought it up from the store himself. The store was under the weighbridge, down a short steel ladder through a hatch in the concrete beside the hut, a low room with racks along the walls and jars on the racks, grey to the shoulder, each with a paper label tied round its neck. Callie had never been down it. Nobody went down it but the old man. He went down it at the eighth hour with the Director's order in his apron pocket, written in the Director's own hand, and came up it twelve times with a jar under his arm, and set each one on the plate.

Her mother sat on her stool in the window and read the beam.

"Flask," she said, at the first. "Clean." She wrote it.

"Flask," at the second. "Clean."

At the fourth she stopped with the pencil above the board.

"Give it here," she said to Callie. "No. Give it to her," to the old man, "and let her put her fingers in it. She's better than me with her fingers. I'm better than her with the beam."

The old man lifted the jar off the plate and unstoppered it and held it out, and Callie put two fingers in and brought them out grey and rubbed them together, slowly, finger and thumb, the way Rook had on the east track on the ninth day.

It went through her fingers like flour.

"Thin," she said.

"Thin," said her mother. "The beam came up a hair slow. I thought so." She wrote it in the margin. "Put it back. Bring another."

"It's a Flask by weight," said the old man. It was not an argument. He said it the way he said a weight.

"It's a Flask by weight," said her mother. "It's seven by what's in it. I'm not sending seven up a hill with my daughter and calling it eight." She did not raise her voice. "You've got four thin ones down there. I've read the book. You've got twenty-seven clean. She's got a paper that says clean. Bring clean."

The old man looked at her for a moment. Then he stoppered the jar and took it back down the ladder, and came up with another, and set it on the plate.

"Flask," said her mother. "Clean."

At the ninth she sent another one back.

---

When there were twelve on the plate, in a row, the old man went into the hut and took the chalk off the test-weight shelf and came out and stood in front of the black board on the wall.

He did not write a number. He wrote a line across the whole width of the board, under the painted names, with the ruler off the shelf, and under the line he wrote, in his big careful capitals:

*LENT. CAUL HILL. 12 FLASKS, CLEAN.*

And under that, two lines. *J., 8, IN THE FRAME. P., 4, ON THE CART. ROAD 1. HOME 3.*

He stood back and looked at it.

"She said every jar against a name," he said. "Where everybody could read it." He put the chalk back on the shelf. "I'll have them back on this plate."

"You'll have them back on this board," said her mother. "Spent or not. In chalk. With where they went." She looked at the row of twelve jars on the plate, grey to the shoulder in the low sun. "That's what she said, and she's right. I'd want to know. If it were mine."

"It is yours," said the old man.

Her mother looked at him.

"You weighed it," said the old man. "You sent two back. I'd have sent one." He went back into the hut to the beam, and from inside, not looking at her, he said, "Hm."

Callie had never heard him say it. It was the sound Jab said his father used to make, the one the old man on the review board had made on the fourteenth day: a short low note in the nose that meant *I've heard you and I'm not finished*.

Her mother sat on her stool with the board on her knee and did not say anything. But Callie saw her left hand, on the board, close a little on the pencil, and stay closed.

---

The second counter came to the east shed at the middle of the day, in the arms of the old woman from the grey-haired man's shed.

She was very old and very small and she did not give a name, and she carried the counter the way the grey-haired man had carried the dead box, close against her chest like a sleeping animal. She set it on the long table in front of Callie and stood back.

"He says it's yours," she said. "Till you bring it back. He says it's older than him and it's never been out of the building and he doesn't trust it, which is why he wants you carrying it. He says you'll know when it's lying." She looked at Callie. "He says to tell you the needle sticks at the top of the red. If it sticks, tap the glass with your nail. Once. Not twice."

"Once," said Callie.

"He's in the east corridor with his hand on a pillow," said the old woman, "telling us all how to do his work with the wrong hand. He'll be at the desk on the forty-third. Tell the old lady." She went away.

Callie sat at the long table with the counter in front of her and turned the switch on the side. It ticked. One, and nothing, and two close together. The room. She held it to the back of her own hand and it ticked the same. She held it to the hook, which had come back from the hatch on the thirty-sixth washed and tagged with a new paper tag on the handle that said *pen, 35th*. It ticked the same. Everything was the room.

She turned it off and put it in the canvas bag Dessa had given her for it, and put the bag over her shoulder, and felt the weight of it settle against her hip beside the hook.

---

In the afternoon she did the stair with Miss Wren.

It was not a real stair. It was the door frame of the east shed, which was the nearest thing in the north yard to a narrow place with walls on both sides. Rook had said the stair with the yellow rail would be narrow, because Senna had said a man could not pass another on it with a coil of wire on his back, and a narrow place was the one place the hook was worth more than anything else they were carrying.

"Across it," said Miss Wren. "The way you did the pipe. The way you did the rail at the jam. Corner to corner. Off the stone."

Callie had settled a quarter of a Handful at the long table at the second hour, in seventy breaths, with her shoulders down. She stood in the door of the shed with the hook bolted onto its pipe, the long reach, and put the curve of it into the frame on one side and the end of the pipe against the frame on the other, across the doorway at the height of a man's chest. She did not grip.

She reached for the stone.

It was there, at the bottom of the well, under her own water, settled and cold and hers. She did not shut her hand over it. She had learned that on the thirty-fourth day with a rack coming down, and she had done it every day since, three times a day, at the long table and in the lane, until she could find the stone and let it go the way she could find a husk line in the dark. She let it go into the steel.

The hook went pale across the doorway.

"Glass," said Callie.

Miss Wren turned it.

"Otto," said Miss Wren.

Otto came at the doorway from the yard side and put both his big hands flat on the pale steel and pushed. He pushed the way he had pushed Wyck on the twentieth day, going red, with his boots sliding on the cinders. The hook did not move. The doorway was shut. Nothing was going to come through it that was not the size of a mouse.

The sand ran through the glass. Miss Wren turned it. It ran again.

"That's the stone," said Callie, at a turn and three quarters. "I'm stopping."

She let it go. The hook went dark. Otto, still pushing, fell into the shed and lay on the floor.

"A turn and three quarters," said Miss Wren. "On a quarter. Better than the thirty-fourth." She wrote it. "And your own?"

"Where it was." Callie went down and looked. A finger and a bit above the floor, cold and clear. Hers. "It hasn't moved."

"On the hill you'll carry a settled Handful from the gate," said Miss Wren. "A whole one, not a quarter. That's four of these, near enough. Seven minutes of a stair held shut while somebody's got their hands on somebody." She looked at Callie. "And every one of those minutes, you've nothing on you. You know that."

"I know."

"I'm saying it every day till we're through that gate," said Miss Wren. "I'll say it every morning at the fence, with the rules."

On the floor of the shed, Otto sat up and rubbed his elbow.

"I'm going to be the one who doesn't move," he said, to nobody. "Wyck says. On the watch. In the gap between the lamps. He's teaching me." He looked at the hook in Callie's hands. "Not like that. Nothing's like that. But like him."

"You'll be good at it," said Callie.

"I pulled a man up a track," said Otto, "and didn't look back." He got up. "This time I'd like to be the one they're pulled behind."

---

She did not sleep much the night of the thirty-ninth, and it was not because of the hill.

It was her mother's breathing. She lay on the floor by the stove with the blanket over the window and the red line of the grate the only light, and she heard it, the way she always heard it, slow and deep. And she did not count it. She had not counted it since the ninth day. She lay and let it go past her, in and out, a real breathing in a real bed under a real ceiling, the way she had learned to.

But she heard it. That was different from counting, and she had never told anybody, because she did not know how to say it. You could stop counting a thing and still hear it. You could hear every breath and not put a number on any of them, and it was harder, because a number was a thing you could hold, and a breath without a number was only a breath going past.

She heard it all night. It was slow and deep and it did not change.

At the fifth hour it changed.

---

It was the sound first. A catch in the breath, as if her mother had started to say something in her sleep and stopped. Then another. And then the sound Callie had heard on the plain nine times, and on the third night in the hut, and never in her life got used to. A long low sound that was not a word, from the bed by the stove, and the bed frame knocking.

She was up before she knew it. She was across the floor in the dark with her hands out, and she found her mother by the sound and got her head, the way she always got her head, one hand under the back of it and the other on the shoulder, turning her on her side toward the stove so that nothing would go down the wrong way.

"*Katori*," she said, not loud, and Katori was already there.

Katori had the lamp. She lit it with one hand and held it low, and the light came up over the bed, and Callie saw her mother's face.

It was not her mother's face. It never was, in a fit. It was a face somebody had borrowed, with the eyes gone up and away and the jaw set, and the whole of her going, arms and legs and back, in long hard jerks that shook the bed. Her right hand, the one that did not take instruction, was taking instruction now from something that was not her, and it beat on the blanket, and Callie put her own hand over it to keep it off the bed frame.

And she counted.

She counted out loud, because Sowerby had asked her to on the first day and because it was medicine. It was the one kind of counting that was for somebody else, and she did not stop herself.

"Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two."

Ines was on the other side of the bed with a folded blanket, putting it between her mother's head and the frame. Pell was at the door with his back to them, because there was nothing for him to do and he knew it, holding the door shut against the cold.

"Forty-one," said Callie.

It did not stop at forty-one.

"Forty-two. Forty-three."

She heard her voice go on. She heard it go past the number that had been the number for five weeks, since the third night in the hut, the one she had written on the card in her head that said *short*. She heard it keep going, flat, the way she said the ticks for the grey-haired man, the way she said a bad number when it was a bad number.

"Sixty. Sixty-one."

Katori's hand came down on her shoulder and stayed there.

"Ninety."

She did not stop. She did not decide not to stop. She counted because her mother's body was doing a thing her mother could not see, and somebody had to see it, and in the morning a small bald man in a hurry would need the number.

"A hundred and twenty. A hundred and twenty-one."

At a hundred and thirty-one it stopped.

---

It stopped all at once, the way it always did, as if somebody had cut a string. Her mother went slack on the bed on her side toward the stove, and breathed, a great ragged pull of air, and another. Her eyes came down from wherever they had gone. They did not find anything. They looked at the stove and did not see it.

"A hundred and thirty-one," said Callie. "Two minutes. Eleven."

Nobody said anything.

She sat on the edge of the bed with her mother's head in her hands and waited. She knew what came after. It came slowly, a long way back, the way somebody comes back up a ladder out of a grain bin. First the breathing. Then the eyes. Then the hands. Then the words, if the words came.

The eyes came, after a long time. They turned on the pillow and found the lamp, and then found Katori holding it, and then found Callie.

Her mother looked at her.

Callie waited for her name.

Her mother's mouth moved. It moved on nothing, the way it did now sometimes, going down into the dark for a word the way you reach into a bag for a tool. One second. Two. Callie did not count them out loud. She counted them anyway.

"You," said her mother. Her voice was thick and slurred and very small. "You. The—" Her mouth moved. "You."

"It's me," said Callie. "It's all right. It's me."

"*You*," said her mother, and her face did something Callie had never seen it do, in all the fits, in all the mornings. It crumpled. It crumpled the way a child's face does, all at once, and she began to cry. Not quietly. She cried with her mouth open and her left hand pulling at the blanket, and she said *you* and *you* and *no*, and the word she wanted did not come, and Callie knew what the word was, because it was her own name, and it was not there.

"It's Callie," she said. "Mum. It's Callie. It's all right. You don't have to."

"*No*," said her mother, and cried, and her right hand lay on the blanket like a glove on a table and did not move at all.

---

Her mother's right side did not come back that morning.

At the eighth hour Otto brought the barrow to the step of fourteen, and Callie and Katori got her into it on the folded blanket, with her back against the front of the barrow and her legs out. The right leg lay a little further out than the left, the way it lay now, as if somebody else had put it down there. Her hat was on the chair beside the bed. Callie put it on her head and pulled the brim down against the light, and her mother let her, and did not say anything, because the words were still somewhere else.

She had found *tea*, on the floor by the stove at the sixth hour, and said it three times, very pleased with it. She had found *no*, and *cold*. She had not found *Callie*. She had looked at Callie a great many times with her eyes going over Callie's face as if it were a page with the words rubbed out, and each time she had opened her mouth and each time she had shut it again, and at the seventh hour she had stopped trying and turned her face to the wall.

Otto pulled the barrow along the row and up the main street and along the side of the long building to the doors, carefully, with the strap under the handle the way Wyck had taught him, so that the weight was on his chest and not his hip. Callie walked beside it with one hand on the rim. Katori walked on the other side. At the step of the long building a nurse came out with a chair and Katori and Otto lifted her mother into it, and the nurse wheeled her in past Mrs Lask, who stood up.

Sowerby was at the fourth bed on the left, the one her mother came to every morning at the eighth hour for her pills, with his folder under his arm.

"Tell me," he said.

Callie told him. She told him in order, standing at the foot of the bed with her hands at her sides, flat, the way she told the ticks.

"A fit at about the fifth hour. In her sleep. A hundred and thirty-one seconds. Two minutes and eleven. It started with her breathing catching, twice, and then the sound, and then all of her. The right hand was going the same as the rest, not less. She came back slowly. Her eyes took about three minutes. The right side hasn't come back. The hand's like it was at the gate on the first day and the leg won't hold. She's lost words. She's got *tea* and *no* and *cold*." She stopped. "She hasn't got my name."

Sowerby wrote all of it down.

"Anything else?"

Callie looked at her mother sitting in the chair by the bed with her hat on and her right hand in her lap and her eyes on the window.

"No," she said. "That's all of it."

---

Sowerby sat on the edge of the bed, which he never did, and looked at her mother.

"I'm going to put the second medicine up," he said to her mother, not to Callie. "The one for the fits. More of it, morning and night. It'll make you sleepier for a few days. You'll not like it." He waited, the way he waited for her to say *stop*, and she did not say anything. "It's a bad one. They come. It may be the swelling round the thing. It may be the thing doing what it does. I won't know which for a while, and I'm not going to guess." He looked at her mother's right hand in her lap. "The hand and the leg and the words, most often, come back after a bad one. Not always all the way. Most often most of the way. I'll know more tomorrow, and more the day after."

Her mother looked at him. Her mouth moved.

"*No*," she said.

Sowerby waited.

"*No*," said her mother again, and lifted her left hand off her lap, and put it flat on the closed folder under his arm, and held it there. She looked at him, and her eyes were perfectly clear, even with the words gone.

"I know," said Sowerby. "I'm not opening it. It stays shut." He did not move the folder. "Whatever you say, whenever you say. Including nothing."

Her mother took her hand away.

---

Callie went to his door.

She had not meant to. She went out of the ward after the pills were done and her mother was lying on the fourth bed on the left with her eyes shut, because the nurse had said she could lie there till the afternoon if she wanted and she had said *no* and then lain down anyway. Callie went out through the doors and did not go back to fourteen. She went along the corridor inside the long building, past Mrs Lask, to the door at the end where Sowerby had his desk, and stood in front of it.

The door was open. He was at the desk with his back to her, writing.

She stood in the doorway.

She had it worked out. She had worked it out on the floor by the stove between the sixth hour and the seventh, lying with her eyes open. She was going tomorrow. Six days there and six days back and however long inside, twelve days at the least. Her mother had had a fit that ran three times longer than the longest one in five weeks and she had lost her right side and her words and Callie's name. If there was a number in that folder, and it was a small number, Callie could not go. Nobody would ask her to. Nobody would let her. And if it was not a small number, she could go and come back, and it would be there when she came back.

She only needed to know which. She did not need the number. She only needed to know whether it was big or small.

She knew, standing in the doorway, that that was a lie. She knew it the way she knew a husk line. It was not a track so much as a direction. If he said *small*, she would want to know how small, and if he said *big*, she would want to know how big, and by the time she had walked back to fourteen she would have a number. She would do a sum every morning before she had her boots on. She would take one off every day before she said good morning.

Sowerby turned round in his chair.

He looked at her in the doorway for a long time. He did not ask her what she wanted. He knew what she wanted. She could see him know it. He had his hand on the folder, flat, on the desk beside him.

"Is there—" said Callie.

He waited.

"Is there anything I should know," said Callie. "Before tomorrow."

"There's a number in a folder," said Sowerby. "It's hers. I told her I'd keep it till she asked." He did not take his hand off it. "She put her hand on it this morning. You saw."

"I saw."

"Then you know what I'm going to say," said Sowerby, "and I'd rather not have to say it to your face, because I like your face. It's the one face in this building that tells me exactly the right amount."

Callie stood in the doorway.

"I'm not asking," she said.

"I know you're not," said Sowerby. "I watched you not ask. It took about a minute. It was the longest minute I've had this week, and I had the pen." He turned back to his desk. "Go and sit with her. She'll want you there when the words come back. They mostly do."

---

She told them at the shed at the middle of the day.

They were all there. They were always all there now, at the long table, from the first hour of the morning to the tenth of the evening, the way people are always at the stove in a house where somebody is going away. Miss Wren at the end with the book. Rook at the head with his hands unwrapped for the first time, red in the palms still but healing, lying on the table. Toren with the ledger. Jab at the end of the bench with his hands in his pockets, which he had not done for a week. Pell on the cot with the atlas. Dessa at her small table by the door.

"I'm not going," said Callie.

Nobody said anything.

"Mum had a fit at the fifth hour," she said. "Two minutes and eleven. Her right side's gone and it hasn't come back. She's lost her words. She's lost my name." She did not sit down. "Sowerby says they mostly come back. He doesn't know. He won't know for a few days." She put the counter in its bag down on the long table. "I'm not going up a road for twelve days with her like that. I'm not."

She waited for somebody to say something.

"Then we don't go tomorrow," said Toren.

Callie looked at him.

"We don't go tomorrow," said Toren. He had not stood up. He said it sitting at the long table with the ledger open in front of him and his good hand flat on it. "The paper's got you on it. You read the floor. You hold the stair. You're the watch if Rook's off. If you're not on it, it's a different paper, and nobody's signed a different paper. So we don't go tomorrow." He looked at her. "We wait."

"We can go without her," said Miss Wren.

Everybody looked at Miss Wren.

"We can," she said. She did not look away from Callie. "It's a worse plan. It's a thinner one. With no Ward on the stair, the healers have nothing between them and whatever comes while their hands are on somebody, except Toren's light and Elias, and Elias can't hold a road and work at the same time. With no watch if Elias drops, it's Jab and me and nobody counting. The deep window halves again." She put her pencil down. "It's worse. It's not impossible. I'm saying it out loud because somebody should, and I'd rather it was me than have Callie think we couldn't."

"It's a different plan," said Pell, from the cot. "I'll price it tonight."

"It's her mother," said Rook.

He said it to the table, not to Callie. His red hands lay on the wood in front of him.

"That's the whole of it," he said. "It's her mother. There isn't a price for it. Pell can price it all night and it'll come out the same." He looked up at Callie. "Whatever you decide, it's yours. Nobody at this table's going to tell you otherwise. I'd not have gone either, at thirteen. I went at fourteen, and I've told the boy why, and I don't know that I was right."

---

Callie stood at the long table with her hands at her sides.

She heard all of it. She heard Toren say *we wait*. She heard Miss Wren say *it's worse*. She heard Pell say *I'll price it*, and Rook say *it's yours*, and she heard what every one of them had said underneath what they had said.

*We wait.* Wait how long? For the words to come back, which Sowerby did not know? For the fifty-fourth day, which would come whether anybody waited or not? The grey-haired man would be at his desk on the forty-third with his wrong hand, warming the machine and guessing. Senna would be on the narrow bed. Every day they waited was a day off the far end, the end where the man stopped or guessed.

*It's worse.* Worse meant the healers with their hands on somebody inside a fence and nothing across the stair. Worse meant Jab with his hand on Toren's chest in a room at the bottom of a yellow stair and a hound coming down it.

*I'll price it.* Pell priced an if at a day on a road, and at a person on the Sag.

She stood at the table and heard herself become the price.

She had not known that was a thing you could hear. It was like hearing a floor give. It was not a sound so much as a direction. Her mother was lying in the fourth bed on the left in the long building with her words gone. And because of that, and because Callie loved her, and because Callie had said *I'm not going*, a hill was not going to be climbed tomorrow. Or it was going to be climbed thinner. Or a box was going to go on lying on a floor in the dark while a machine in a shed woke slower and slower, and an old woman with no feet lay on a narrow bed every eleven days while a man with a white hand guessed at her marrow.

All of that was going to happen because of her mother.

Her mother, who had said at the bottom of the scarp *you don't get to spend me*. Who had said on a stair on the thirteenth day *strike that*. Who had put her left hand on Sowerby's folder this morning with her words gone and her eyes perfectly clear, and held it there.

"I'll tell you tonight," said Callie.

She picked up the counter in its bag and went out.

---

The weighbridge was quiet in the afternoon. The old man was on the stool.

He had never sat on it before, that Callie knew of. It was her mother's stool. He sat on it now in his leather apron with the tally board on his knee, and when Callie came round the side of the hut he looked at her and did not say anything.

"Bad morning," said Callie.

"Bad morning," the old man agreed.

He did not ask anything. She stood by the hut with her back against the wall and looked at the plate. Otto came up the yard from the watch with a jar, a Handful from the lips, dirty, and set it on the plate. The old man moved the weight along the beam and read it and wrote it on the board with his own hand, which was big and square and not as careful as her mother's.

"She told me," he said, when Otto had gone. "Yesterday. When she'd sent the second thin one back."

Callie looked at him.

"She said her daughter was going up a hill," said the old man. "She said it with the board on her knee. She said she'd weighed every jar herself and she'd send them all back if they were thin, and she'd sit on this stool till they came home, and she'd read them back onto the board." He looked at the beam. "That's all she said. I thought you'd want to know she'd said it. Before this morning."

Callie did not say anything.

"I'll keep the stool warm," said the old man. "She'll be in at the eighth hour tomorrow. Or the day after. She'll want it."

"I'll be here," said Callie. It came out before she could stop it. It was what her mother said to him every afternoon when her leg went on the step. *I'll be in at the eighth hour.* And he said *I'll be here*.

The old man looked at her for a long moment.

"No," he said. "I'll be here. That's my line." He turned back to the beam. "Go and sit with her."

---

Her mother's words came back at the seventh hour of the evening, some of them, the way Sowerby had said they mostly did.

Callie was on the floor by the stove in fourteen with her back against the bed frame. Her mother was on the bed. They had brought her home in the barrow at the fourth hour, because she had said *no* to the ward four times and then *home*, which was a new word, and Sowerby had heard it and said *all right*. She had slept. The second medicine made her sleep. Katori was at the stove. Ines was on her bed with her bow across her knees. Pell had gone to the shed to price a plan. Otto had gone to the watch.

"Callie," said her mother.

Callie turned round.

Her mother was lying on her side facing the stove with her eyes open, and she was looking at Callie, and her face was her own face.

"Callie," she said again, slowly, as if she were holding the word up to the light to be sure of it. "There. I had it at the fourth hour, in the barrow, and it went again. I've been lying here holding on to it." Her voice was thick still, and slow, and some of the words came later than others. None of them came wrong. "Callie. Callie. I'm going to say it about ninety times tonight. Like the little lad in the ward. Don't stop me."

Callie could not say anything at all.

Her mother looked at her for a while. Then she turned her head a little on the pillow and looked at Katori at the stove.

"Katori," she said. "Go and sit on the step. Take Ines. This one's ours."

Katori put down the kettle and went out. Ines went after her with her bow. The door shut. The stove ticked.

"You told them you're staying," said her mother.

"Who told you?"

"Everybody tells me everything," said her mother. "I'm on a stool in a window." She moved her left hand on the blanket. "Katori told me. At the fourth hour, in the barrow, when I got your name back. I asked her what you'd done today, and she told me. She doesn't lie to me. It's the only thing I like about her." She looked at Callie. "Is it true?"

"Yes."

"Then come here," said her mother. "Not the floor. Up here. Where I can see you without my neck going."

---

Callie got up on the edge of the bed. She sat where Sowerby had sat in the morning, with her hip against her mother's knees, and her mother looked up at her from the pillow.

"You went to the little man's door," said her mother. "This morning. After the pills."

Callie did not ask how she knew.

"Yes."

"Did you ask him?"

"No."

Her mother looked at her for a long time.

"Good," she said. "Then you've done the hardest thing already today. This one's easier." She let out a breath. "I'm going to say it once. Properly. And then nobody says it for me. You know the rule."

"I know the rule."

"I'm not the reason you don't go," said her mother.

Callie opened her mouth.

"I'm not," said her mother. "I refuse. I've been the reason for things since the winter. I was the reason for a wagon and four hundred miles, and a scarp, and a Handful drunk on a hill, and two lost wage days, and a card with *I count* on the bottom of it. I didn't choose any of those. I was in the back of a wagon being decided about." She moved her left hand on the blanket until it found Callie's knee. "I'm choosing this one. I'm not the reason a hill doesn't get climbed. I'm not the reason an old woman across the ward from my bed gets guessed at on the fifty-fourth day. I'm not the reason my daughter's friends go up a stair with nothing across it." Her voice went slow on the last of it, and she waited, and it came. "I won't be that. Not for you. Not for anybody. It's the only thing I've got left in this bed that's mine to decide, and I've decided it."

"You lost my name," said Callie. "This morning. You lost my name."

"I did," said her mother. "And I got it back. In a barrow. And I'll lose it again one day, and maybe I'll not get it back, and you'll be here or you'll be up a hill, and it'll go either way." She held Callie's eyes. "You being here doesn't keep it. You sitting on the floor by this stove every night for the rest of whatever there is, counting my breathing, doesn't keep one word in my head. You know that. You've known it since the ninth day, when you stopped counting."

"I didn't stop," said Callie. "I stopped counting. I didn't stop hearing it."

Her mother looked at her.

"No," she said, more quietly. "No. I know you didn't. I could tell."

---

Her mother's left hand came up off the blanket.

It found Callie's right hand, which was lying on the edge of the bed, and Callie realised that her right hand had been holding on to her mother's left wrist. It had been holding it all afternoon, since the barrow, since she sat down on the floor by the stove, without her knowing. Her fingers were shut round the wrist, hard, the way they had been shut round the conduit on the hill with the wagon on it, when she could not open them afterward.

Her mother did not pull her wrist away. She took hold of Callie's fingers with her own, one at a time, and opened them.

The first finger. The second. She did it slowly, with her left hand, the only one that took instruction, and it took a long time. Callie let her. She watched her mother's fingers open her own, one after another, off her mother's wrist, until her hand lay open on the blanket and her mother's wrist lay beside it, and neither was holding the other.

"Your grandfather," said her mother, "never gripped a sack."

"I know."

"You get under it. You don't grip it. I told you that at the bottom of the scarp." She left her hand lying next to Callie's on the blanket, not holding. "You've been gripping me since the Reach. I let you, because I needed gripping, on the road. I don't need it here. Look." She did not lift her head. She moved her eyes toward the door. "There's a woman in a cap on that step who's staying behind for the first time in her life, and she'd have stayed for me anyway. There's a girl with a bow. There's Otto, who'll pull my barrow with the strap under the handle for as long as I want pulling. There's an old man in an apron who's sat on my stool all afternoon so it'll be warm. There's a little bald man with a folder he won't open, and an old woman across the ward who says Milo's name every night and will say mine if she has to. There's a store book that I've read to the last line. And there's a Director with spectacles on a cord who's stopped the clock on my bed till you come back through that gate." She looked back at Callie. "I'm held, love. I'm held on every side. By a whole station. You built half of it. You read the ground and counted the ticks and weighed the ash and you built it." Her voice slowed, and waited, and came. "Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold."

Callie sat on the edge of the bed with her hand open on the blanket.

It was not a speech. Her mother did not make speeches. She had said a thing she had said before, on a hill and on a stair and on the floor by a stove, and she had said it again with half her words gone, lying on her side with her right hand dead on the blanket, because it was still true and somebody had to say it out loud.

"If you lose it again," said Callie. "My name. While I'm up there."

"Then I'll lose it," said her mother. "And Katori will tell me it, because she doesn't lie. And when you come back through that gate you'll tell me it, and I'll say it ninety times. That's all." She shut her eyes. "You're going. I'm not asking you. I'm telling you what I've decided, and you're going to let me decide it, because it's mine."

"Yes," said Callie.

Her mother opened her eyes.

"Say it properly."

"I'm going," said Callie.

"Good," said her mother. "And tomorrow, at the eighth hour, when it's time, I'll let go first."

Callie looked at her.

"Of your hand," said her mother. "You'll not have to. I'll do it. Tomorrow I'll let go first." She shut her eyes again. "Now go and tell the boy with the paper. He'll be sitting on his step waiting to be told. They always are."

---

Toren was on the step of eleven.

He was sitting with his back against the door frame and the ledger on his knees, and Pell was beside him on the step with the atlas, and between them on the frozen concrete there was a sheet of paper with a great many lines on it in Pell's small neat hand. Callie could see from the step of fourteen that half the lines were crossed out.

She came along the row and stopped in front of them.

"I'm going," she said.

Toren looked up at her.

Pell looked at her for a long moment, over the top of the sheet. Then he picked up the sheet, and folded it once, and tore it along the fold, and tore it again, and put the pieces in his coat pocket.

"That's a relief," he said. "It was a very bad plan. I'd priced it at two people."

"She said I'm not the reason," said Callie. "She said she won't be."

"No," said Toren. "I didn't think she would." He did not get up. He looked at her the way he looked at people now, reading where their weight was. "Is she—"

"She's got my name back," said Callie. "At the fourth hour. In the barrow. She's going to say it ninety times tonight."

Toren nodded. He looked down at the ledger on his knees.

"I said *we wait*," he said. "At the table. I'd have waited."

"I know," said Callie. "I heard you." She stood on the frozen concrete in front of the step. "Everybody else said a price. You didn't."

"I didn't know one."

"No," said Callie. "That's why."

---

She settled her Handful at the stove in fourteen at the tenth hour.

Miss Wren had weighed it out on the thirty-seventh and put it in a paper twist, and Callie had carried it in her coat for three days. She sat on the floor by the stove with her back against the bed frame and opened the twist on her knee, and put her palm flat on it.

It came up into her hand, cold and slow, with nobody in it. She did not hold it high. She let it go down, through her wrist and her arm and into the well, and she felt it arrive and lie at the bottom under her own water. It was bigger than a quarter. It was the biggest stone she had ever settled. She did not shut her hand over it. She let it lie there and settle, and the cold went out of it slowly, the way cold goes out of a stone brought in from the yard, until it was part of the well.

Three hundred breaths, near enough. She did not count them. She knew.

Behind her, on the bed, her mother was asleep. Callie could hear her breathing, slow and deep, the second medicine taking her down a long way.

Callie did not count it.

She heard it. She heard every breath, in and out, going past her in the dark, and she did not put a number on any of them, and she did not hold on to any of them. She let them go by. She sat on the floor with her back against the bed and her hand open on her knee, and she let her mother breathe without her.

At the eleventh hour her mother said, in her sleep, very clearly, "Callie."

And then, a while later, "Callie."

Callie did not count those either. She sat by the stove and let them come, one after another, and she found, somewhere past the twentieth, that she was smiling in the dark, and that her face was wet, and that neither of those things needed a number.

The hook hung on its nail by the door with its new paper tag on the handle. The counter was in its bag beside it. In the morning there would be the gate, and the ward at the eighth hour first, because her mother would want her pills and would want Callie to walk beside the barrow, not holding, only there.

She would let go first. Her mother had said so, at the bottom of it all, in the voice she used for things that were decided. *Tomorrow I'll let go first.*

Callie sat by the stove and let her.

# Chapter 24 — Toren: Everybody Back

He had taken his Handful in the night before, the way Dee said he always would have to.

He had done it at the stove in eleven at the ninth hour of the evening, sitting on the floor with the paper twist open on his knee and Dessa on her bed watching him with her bad leg out straight. It had taken him most of an hour, a thousand breaths and more. He had not hauled. He had put his palm flat on the grey heap and waited for it to come at the rate it came, and every part of him had wanted to lean on it, and he had not. At the end there had been a warm stone lying at the bottom of something inside him, a long way down, bigger than any he had settled before.

He woke at the fourth hour with it still there.

The hut was dark. The stove was a red line. He lay on his bed with his coat on and went down once, not to check, only to know, the way Dee had taught him. The stone was there. Above it his own reserve lay where it lay, two turns and a bit of the brass glass, dark. The copied plate was in his coat pocket on one side and his own stone on the other, warm and faint and breathing.

He got his boots on one-handed. The splint on his left arm had come off on the thirty-ninth day. Keel had put the arm in a stiff leather cuff from the wrist to the elbow instead, laced, that he could get on and off himself, and she had told him it was knit and it was not strong, and that if he hauled anything with it on the road she would know when he came back, and he would not enjoy the knowing. He laced the cuff in the dark with his teeth and his right hand.

Rook was already up.

He was sitting on the edge of his bed with his boots on, and his hands lay on his knees. They were not wrapped any more. The red had gone out of the palms to a pink, like skin after a burn has healed over, and they did not shake. He looked at Toren in the red light of the stove.

"You're up," he said.

"So are you."

"I'm always up," said Rook. "Nobody in this station's ever up at the fourth hour. Except everybody."

He stood up. He did it in one movement, without his hands, and stood there with his arms at his sides, and did not sway.

On her bed, Dessa said, "One," without opening her eyes. And then, "I'm writing that down. I'm writing that you got up in one on the forty-first."

"You're not writing anything. You're lying down."

"I'll write it when I'm up," said Dessa. "I've got a very good memory. It's the only thing I've got that doesn't hurt."

---

Wyck was on the step when Toren went out. He was sitting in the dark with the bar across his knees and his strapped arm against his chest and the frost on the concrete round his boots.

"You haven't slept," said Toren.

"I've slept. I slept from the tenth hour to the second. Then I came out here." Wyck turned the bar over in his left hand. "It's the last night I get to say I'm not going because Keel said so. Tomorrow it's just that I'm not going." He looked at the row. "I'd like to have been going."

Toren sat down beside him.

"I know," he said.

"Don't say you know. You don't know. You're going." Wyck did not say it angrily. He said it the way he said everything that was true. "Listen. I'm going to say one thing and then I'm going to go and stand on a lamp-line with two men who stand like they're waiting for a cart, and teach them not to, for twelve days." He looked at Toren in the dark. "Fall like a sack. Up there. On the road, and inside the fence, and on that stair. Whatever you're carrying. You fall like a sack and you roll and you get up, and you don't put your hand out. Not that hand." He nodded at the leather cuff. "Not for anything. Not if your gran's at the bottom of the stair."

"My gran's in a bed."

"Then not if Jab's at the bottom," said Wyck. "Not if Callie is. You'll want to. You'll want to more than anything. Don't." He turned the bar over. "That's all. That's the only thing I know that's any use to anybody going up a hill."

"It's a lot of use."

"It's one thing," said Wyck. He stood up, carefully, the way Keel had made him promise to do everything, and stood on the step with the bar in his left hand. "The fifty-sixth. That's when she takes it off. You'll be back by then, or near it. And when you're back I'm taking your sack at eight paces. You'll sit on the heap."

"I'll sit on the heap," said Toren.

Wyck went in. Toren sat on the step alone with the stone warm in one pocket and the plate in the other, and waited for the light.

At first light the yard was full.

He had not expected that. He had thought it would be the six of them, and the cart, and the horse, and perhaps Dessa with the book. But when he came out of Yard C into the north yard with Rook at his left side, half a step behind, where the hole in his back had been, the yard was full of people who were not going anywhere.

Katori's crew wagon stood by the yard gate. Toren had seen it only once before, on the day it came in, under a canvas net at the south stable with the crates. It had been under the net for six weeks. Now the net was off and the crates were out and Otto was putting things into it where Pell told him to: a rolled tent, water in skins and in a small barrel, food in a sack, a lamp, Pell's atlas in its oilcloth, a coil of rope. Jab had the frame on.

She looked at the long building. "Her leg's come back a bit. Not all of it. Katori's with her. And Ines. They'll be an hour. She says we're to go ahead and she'll be there."

"Go," he said. "All three. The eighth hour. Go and do it properly." He put his hand on the side of the wagon. "Dee and I've done ours. Pell's done his. We'll be at the gate."

Mrs Lask was at her desk inside the doors.

She looked up when the three of them came in out of the frost. She looked at Jab with the frame on his back and the jars in it, and at Callie with the hook, and at Toren with his arm in its leather cuff. She put down her pen.

"Visiting," she said, "is in the afternoons."

The ward was grey with the early light coming in at the windows, and the small lamp at the far end was still burning, turned low. Most of the beds were asleep. In the fourth bed on the right, his grandmother was sitting up against her pillows with her hands folded on the blanket where she could see them.

In the fifth bed, Tonk was sitting up on his bar.

He had both hands on the short smooth rail over his bed and he had pulled himself up on it, all the way, until he was sitting with his back off the pillow. He was not looking at the door. He was looking at the window over his bed, where there was nothing to see but grey. He did not turn his head when they came in. The long cord ran from his wrist across the gap between the beds to the rail of the empty sixth bed, and the knot was on the rail, the one the nurse had tied twice, that nobody was allowed to untie.

The fourth bed on the left, across the ward, was empty and made up tight. It was the one Callie's mother came to at the eighth hour for her pills.

Jab stopped in the middle of the ward with the frame on his back.

Toren went to his grandmother.

---

"You've got your cuff on," said Senna.

"Keel put it on."

"I know she did. She came and told me. She said she'd told you it was knit and not strong and you'd said yes Doctor Keel in a voice she didn't believe." Senna looked at him with her pale eyes. "Sit down. Not on my feet. I can't feel them, but the nurse can, and she'll be rude."

He sat on the chair by the bed.

"Say them," said Senna.

He knew what she meant. He had written them on the thirty-second day in the big book as she said them, not tidied, and he had said them every night since, on the step of eleven, to nobody, until he knew them the way he knew the plate.

"Nothing to eat inside the fence," he said. "Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, every time, coat off, shake it, wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat." He stopped. "And never carry one of the boxes. Not on your back. Not in your arms."

"That's them," said Senna. "Say them at the gate every morning. Out loud. All of you. Like a grace." She moved one of her folded hands, a quarter of an inch. "The man who ran the crew made us. We thought he was an old woman about it. The ones who kept them are the ones who were still alive ten years ago."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so you've heard it from me on the morning, and not only on a page." She looked at the ceiling. "There's one more thing. I didn't put it on the page. I didn't think of it till the night before last, lying here, and then I couldn't think of anything else."

He waited.

"At the bottom of the yellow stair," said Senna, "there's a door. Before the long corridor. A steel door, with the wheel on it. It sticks." She was quiet a moment. "It opens toward you. Not away. Every man on that crew put his shoulder to it on the first morning and it didn't move, and we stood at the bottom of the stair shoving at it for a quarter of an hour, eleven of us, in the dark, with the stuff in the air. The man who ran the crew stood at the top and laughed at us. Then he came down and pulled it." Her mouth moved at the corner. "He laughed at us about it for a week. He said a quarter of an hour at the bottom of that stair was a day off the end of somebody's life, and we'd spent it pushing a door that pulled."

Toren sat very still.

"Pull it," said Senna. "Don't push. Don't stand at the bottom of that stair for a quarter of an hour. You haven't got a quarter of an hour. I've done the sums on it every night since the thirty-second. I know what a quarter of an hour at the bottom of that stair costs a person now. It's on your paper, by the hour." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him. "That's the only thing I've got that isn't on the page. Nobody else alive knows it. I've been saving it for this morning."

"Pull it," said Toren.

"Pull it," said Senna.

---

She was quiet for a while. Toren sat by the bed with his hand on his knee.

Across the ward, the doors opened.

Callie's mother came in on her stick with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. She was walking slowly, the right leg coming a little behind the left, the way it did now, as if somebody else had to tell it when. She had her battered hat on, pulled down. She came down the ward between the beds past the sleepers, and people who were awake turned their heads to watch her come, because she was walking.

She got to the fourth bed on the left and turned round and sat on the edge of it. It took her a while. Nobody hurried her.

Callie was standing at the foot of the bed with the hook on her shoulder.

"There," said her mother. Her voice was slow still, and thick, and the words came one at a time. None of them came wrong. "I walked. Everybody saw. Callie. Callie. That's eleven since the stove. I'm keeping count, for once." She looked at Katori. "Go and find the little man and tell him I'm here and I'd like my pills and I'd like him to not look at my face for once, because my face is busy."

Katori went. Ines stood by the window with her bow.

The nurse came with the pills in a paper cup and a cup of water. Callie's mother took the paper cup in her left hand and the water in her right. The right hand closed on the water cup. It closed slowly, and not all the way, and it shook, and it held. She drank. She put the cup down. She did not say anything about the hand, and nobody else did.

Then she put her left hand out, palm up, on the blanket beside her.

Callie came round the end of the bed and put her own hand in it.

Toren watched them from across the ward. He could not hear what they said. He saw Callie's mother's lips move, and saw Callie's move in answer, and he knew what it was, because he had heard it on the steps a dozen times without meaning to. *Something left?* And Callie's answer, which he could not hear, but which he could see she said all the way through, not short. And her mother's fingers closing on Callie's, and holding.

They held for a long time. Longer than he had ever seen them hold.

Then Callie's mother opened her hand.

She did it first. She did it on purpose, with her eyes on Callie's face, one finger and then the next, the way you let go of a rope when you are sure the man on the other end has it. Her hand lay open on the blanket, palm up, empty. Callie's hand lay in it for a moment longer, not held, and then Callie took it back.

Callie did not grip anything. She stood by the bed with her hand at her side, open, and looked at her mother, and her mother looked back.

"Go on," said her mother. Toren heard that. She said it loud enough for the ward. "Go on, love. I've let go. It's your turn now."

---

In the fifth bed, Tonk had not looked round once.

Jab was standing between the fifth bed and the sixth, with the frame on his back and the eight jars in it and the curved ash rail standing up over his shoulder. He had been standing there since they came in. He had not sat down in the chair, because he could not sit in it with the frame on. He had not taken the frame off, and Toren did not know why, and then he did.

Tonk was sitting up on the bar with his face to the window. His hands were on the bar and his arms were straight and shaking. He had been holding himself up like that since they came in, and he would not let go of the bar and lie down, because lying down would mean turning his head.

"Tonk," said Jab.

Nothing.

"It's the eighth hour," said Jab. "I said I'd come. I've come."

Tonk's arms shook on the bar.

Toren watched Jab stand there. He saw him not reach out. He saw his hands, which had been in his pockets for three days and were not in them now, hanging at his sides, and he saw them want to go forward. He had seen Jab hold them behind his back on the thirty-ninth morning, Senna had told him, for eleven minutes, while his brother did the chair. He was holding them at his sides now the same way, without anything to hold them with.

In the fourth bed, Senna turned her head on the pillow toward the fifth.

"Tonk," she said.

Tonk did not turn round. But his shoulders moved. He was listening. He was always listening.

"I'm never going up that road," said Senna. "The one they're going up. I went up it once, when I was fifty-three, and it took my hands and my feet and twenty years of my life. And I'll never go up it again. I've planned it for them. Every step. I've told them where the gate is and where to camp and what to eat and which way the door opens at the bottom of the stair. And I'll lie in this bed and not walk one yard of it." She said it without any weight at all, the way she said *Milo Faust* to the ceiling every night. "It's a road I can plan and never walk. That's what it is. That's all it is."

Tonk's arms shook on the bar.

"Some of us plan it," said Senna, "and some of us walk it. And some of us are four, and have nails in our legs, and have to sit in a bed next to an old woman with no feet and let the ones who can walk go and walk it." She turned her face back to the ceiling. "It's the worst job there is. I'd know. I've got it too."

---

Tonk let go of the bar.

He let go and fell back on the pillow, not gently, and lay there with his eyes on the ceiling and his face red. Then he turned his head. He turned it all the way round on the pillow, away from the window, and looked at the sixth bed, and at the rail of it, and at the knot.

"Chair," he said.

The nurse was at the end of the ward. She came. She had been waiting, Toren thought, the whole time, the way nurses wait, a long way off, not looking.

"Chair," said Tonk. "I'm doing it. Most way. Don't *help*," he said to Jab, without looking at him. "Don't *you* help."

Jab put his hands behind his back.

It took Tonk nine minutes. Two fewer than the thirty-ninth; Senna said so afterward, from the pillow. He pulled himself up on the bar, and shifted himself to the edge of the bed on his arms a few inches at a time, with the frame on his leg coming with him and his face going white round the mouth each time it moved. He swung his good leg over. He got both hands under his thigh and lifted the bad leg and brought it round, shaking, onto the board the nurse held level. He let himself down into the seat on his arms. The nurse took the last hand's width.

He sat in the chair.

"*Push* me," he said to the nurse. "Not him. You."

The nurse pushed him. Not far. Four feet, round the end of the fifth bed, to the side of the sixth, so that the chair stood beside the rail of the empty bed and the knot was at the height of Tonk's hands.

He looked at the knot.

It was the one he had made a nurse tie twice on the twenty-fifth day, clumsy and huge, pulled tight by somebody who had been told to make it hold and had made it hold. He had said nobody was to untie it, and nobody had. It had been on that rail for sixteen days.

He put his hands on it.

Toren watched him try. His fingers were small and the knot was big and pulled very tight, and he could not get a nail into it. He tried from the top and from the side. He made a noise through his teeth. Jab, behind him, with his hands behind his back, made a small movement forward and stopped it.

"Don't," said Tonk, without turning round.

"I'm not."

"You *moved*."

"I moved," said Jab. "I stopped."

Tonk went back to the knot. He found a loop in it, the loose part where the nurse's second try had gone over the first, and he got his finger into it and pulled, and the loop came. Then another. It took a long time. Nobody in the ward said anything. Across the ward, Callie's mother sat on the edge of her bed with her pills done and her hat on and watched. Callie stood at the foot of her mother's bed and watched. Katori had come back with Sowerby, and they stood in the aisle and watched. Senna lay on her pillow with her eyes turned toward the chair and did not say anything at all.

The knot came undone.

The end of the long cord came off the rail of the sixth bed and lay loose in Tonk's hand. The other end was round his own wrist, where it had been every day since the second, knotted, the twelve-foot cord from the road.

---

Tonk sat in his chair with the loose end in his hand and looked at it.

Then he began to wind it.

He wound it round his own hand, the loose end first, loop after loop, slowly, the whole twelve feet of it from the road, until it was a thick coil round his fist and wrist, and there was no loose end any more. It was all on him. None of it went anywhere.

"I'm keeping my end," he said.

He said it to the coil. He did not look at Jab.

"You don't need yours," he said. "You know where I *am*." And then, because he was four, and it was important, and he had to be sure it was understood: "I'm in the fifth bed. On the *right*."

"I know where you are," said Jab. His voice did not work very well.

Tonk looked at the coil on his fist for a while. Then he put his other hand under the blanket over his knees, where Toren had not seen anything, and brought it out closed.

"Bend down," he said.

Jab bent down. He did it carefully, with eight jars on his back, bending at the knees and not the waist, the way Wyck had taught him to carry a load, until his face was level with his brother's.

Tonk opened his hand.

It was the fish. The small black wooden fish, as long as Jab's thumb, smoked black by a lifetime of hanging near a fire, with one fin broken off before Tonk was born. It had been in his hand through two operations and three pictures and every night of the road. It lay in the middle of his palm.

"Little's minding you," said Tonk.

Jab did not take it.

"*Take* it," said Tonk. "He's minding you. On the hill. That's his job now." He held it out further, at the end of his arm, as far as he could reach from the chair with his leg on the board. "You've to bring him *back*. He's *mine*. He's not yours. You're only *borrowing* him."

Jab took it.

He took it the way you take a thing you have been handed that is worth more than anything else you are carrying. He held it in his palm and looked at it. Then he put it inside his coat, on the left side, over the place under his breastbone where the lean lived, and buttoned the coat over it.

"And don't eat *inside*," said Tonk.

Jab looked at him.

"The fence," said Tonk. "She said." He jerked his head at the fourth bed. "Nothing to eat inside. Nothing to drink. No sitting down. I *heard*." He looked at Jab with enormous seriousness. "Don't eat inside, and bring Little back, and don't be asleep."

"I'll try very hard," said Jab. "Not to be asleep."

"Try *harder*," said Tonk.

Then he held up his fist, with the cord wound round it, and Jab put his own hand round it, round the coil and the small hand inside it, and held it. Only for a moment. Then Tonk took his fist back, himself, and put it in his lap, and looked at the window.

"You can go now," he said.

---

Toren sat by his grandmother's bed with his hand on his knee.

He had watched all of it. He had watched Callie's mother open her hand first and Callie not grip. He had watched a four-year-old untie a knot he had made somebody tie twice, and wind twelve feet of cord round his own fist, and hand his brother a fish. He had watched Jab stand there with his hands behind his back and not help.

He looked down the ward.

Four beds. His grandmother in the fourth on the right, who would be lifted into a chair by two orderlies on the forty-third day and pushed down the long slope to the yellow line by Wyck, complaining about the camber, and would lie on a narrow bed while a man with a white hand warmed the machine more times than he wanted to and guessed. Tonk in the fifth, in his chair, with a cord wound round his fist and a frame on his leg until the spring. The sixth, empty, with the rail bare. And across the ward, the fourth on the left, where a woman in a battered hat sat on the edge with her right hand shaking round a water cup, because she had walked in.

He had thought, writing the paper on the night of the twenty-ninth at the stove in eleven, that the paper was about a hill. Six days north and west, and a fence, and a stair, and a box. The ifs had all been about the hill. *If the boxes are still on the floor. If a warm one's a live one. If Rook can stand.* Then Pell had written prices after them, and Dee had written lines, and Callie had done the sums, and the Director had signed it. It had become a paper about ash, and hours, and who drew and who watched and who carried.

It was not about any of that. He could see it now, from the chair by his grandmother's bed, the way you see the shape of a road from the top of a hill after you have walked it.

It was about these beds.

It had always been about these beds. His grandmother had planned a road she could never walk. Tonk had given up his end of a cord. Callie's mother had opened her hand first. They had sent the three of them out of this ward and up a hill on purpose, knowing what it cost, because it was the only way to keep the shed working, and the shed was what kept these beds beds. Every if on his paper was really the same if, and he had not written it down, because he had not known it was there.

*If we come back.*

His father had never priced the way home. On his father's road there had only been a way there, because there had been nothing behind them to go back to, only a burned place and a paper with nineteen days on it. On this road there was a ward. There were three people in it who had let them go. The way home was not a line at the bottom of a sheet with three Flasks against it. It was this room. It was bringing three people back through those doors and down this aisle to these beds, and saying *we're back*, and meaning all of them.

That was the job. Not the ifs. Not the runner, or the one with the paper, or the one who knew where everybody was. Those were all the same job, and this was it: taking people out of the rooms that made them go, and bringing them back to those rooms, all of them, whole enough to sit in a chair by a bed.

He had not known that was what leading was. He had thought it was the other thing, the thing his father did, going first.

"Tor," said his grandmother.

He looked at her.

"You've gone quiet," she said. "You're doing a sum. I can see it on you. You look exactly like your father when you do that." She moved one of her hands on the blanket, a quarter of an inch. "What's the answer?"

"Everybody back," said Toren.

Senna looked at him for a long time with her pale eyes.

"Yes," she said. "That's the one." She shut her eyes. "Go on. You'll be late for the gate, and Pell will say a number at you."

He stood up. He bent down and put his forehead against hers, the way she had let him on the cart, once, on the worst night of the road. She could not put her arms round him. She did not need to.

"Pull the door," she said, with her eyes shut.

"Pull the door."

"And count them in at the pump house," said Senna. "Every night. Out and in. The man who ran the crew wrote it on his wrist with a burnt stick. That's why I know how many came out." She did not open her eyes. "Write it somewhere better than your wrist."

---

The home line was at the yard gate.

All of it. Toren had not known it was all going to be there, and it was. Dessa was on a crate by the gate with the big book open on her knees and a pencil in her hand, and her bad leg out straight. Wyck stood beside her with his bar. Katori was there, with her cap pulled down and her hands at her sides, and Ines with her bow, and Otto with his big hands in his pockets, going red about nothing. The weighbridge girl had Duchess's lead rope. The two yard lifters were there, the big quiet men from the lamp-line, and one of them had his arm bandaged where the hound had bitten it on the thirtieth. They were standing with their weight in their heels, and Toren saw Wyck see them doing it and say nothing.

And in a chair by the gate, in a grey yard coat, with a blanket over her knees and Sowerby standing behind the chair with a face like a thundercloud, the day holder.

"She asked," said Sowerby, to nobody. "She asked me eleven times since yesterday. I said no eleven times. Then she said she'd walk, and I believed her." He looked at the chair. "Twenty minutes. Then she's back in the east corridor."

The day holder did not look at Sowerby. She was looking at the road beyond the gate, the two hundred paces of made road to the south lip of the Sag, where the lamps of the watch stood on their poles along the east track. Her hollow was at the edge of the lip, a saucer-sized dip in the gravel with frost in the bottom of it.

"Morning," she said to Rook.

"Morning," said Rook.

That was all either of them said. Rook stood by the chair a moment longer, the way you stand by a gate you have leaned on for a long time. Then he went on.

---

Dee measured their floors at the gate. She did it the way she had done it on the twenty-fifth, fast, by the light of the gate lamp, two fingers on the inside of each wrist.

"How much?"

"The Handful I settled last night," said Callie. "And a finger and a bit of mine. Above the floor."

"A Handful settled," said Toren. "Most of an hour, last night. And two turns and a bit. I measured it once. I'm not measuring it again."

"That'll do."

"A hand and a bit," said Jab. "Floor's a finger."

Dee held his wrist a moment longer than the others, the way she had before the lift.

"Remember it," she said.

"I know where it is."

"I know you know where it is," said Dee. "I watched you find it with nothing in the jar." She let go. "I'm asking you to remember it anyway. Every morning. At the gate. With the rules."

Then she measured Rook. She put two fingers on the inside of his wrist and looked away over the yard, and counted. Toren watched her face. It did not change. She took her fingers away and wrote something in her own book, and did not say what, and Rook did not ask.

"Ten minutes," was all she said. "Half a turn."

"Half a turn," said Rook.

---

Katori was standing by the gate post.

Rook stopped in front of her. Toren was a pace behind him on his left, where he always was, and he did not move away, because there was nowhere to move to, and because Katori looked at him once and did not tell him to.

"Four seasons," said Katori. "You signed."

"I'll be back for the rest of them."

"I haven't told you," said Katori. "Why the same word. The stone and the thing. I said I would. Not on a step." She did not look at the stone in Rook's pocket. She looked at his face. "I haven't learned how to put it down yet. I'm still holding it."

"Then keep holding it," said Rook. "Till I'm back."

"I'm staying," said Katori. "I've never stayed behind in my life. I've always been the one on the road." She looked at the watch, the lamps on their poles, the figure on the crate. "I don't know how."

"You sit on a step," said Rook.

"That's what Callie said." Katori almost smiled. "I'll learn it off her mother. She's very good at it." She put her cap straight. "Come back, Elias. I've not said the rest."

"Then I'll have to," said Rook.

He went through the gate.

---

Dessa wrote them out.

She wrote them in the big book, on a new page, in her own neat hand, with a ruler, while they stood at the gate. Toren came and looked over her shoulder. She did not cover it with her hand.

*41st day. First light. Out through the north yard gate, for Caul Hill, on the Director's narrow yes:*

*E. Marsh. D. Wren. P. (and the cart, and Duchess). J. C. T. Voss.*

*12 Flasks lent, clean, weighed by T. at the weighbridge, 2 sent back thin. J., 8, in the frame. P., 4, on the cart. Road 1. Home 3.*

*Home line: D. Cole. The watch: K., I., O., the weighbridge girl, the two yard lifters (W. teaching them to stand). Holding since the 26th.*

*S. V.: pull the door.*

She stopped with the pencil above the page.

"I'll write *back*," she said, "when you're back. Under this. Same page." She looked up at Toren. "I've left room."

"How much room?"

"Six lines," said Dessa. "One for each." She looked back down at the page. "I'll not need more than six. I'd like that written somewhere too, and I'm not writing it here, so I'm saying it out loud." She shut her mouth hard. "That's all. Go on. You're letting the cold in."

He looked at the first page of the book, which he could see under her hand, because she had turned the book to the back to write, and the front was showing. The one line in the middle of the page with a great deal of white round it.

*Say it out loud. Even when it's awful.*

"Dessa," he said.

"What."

"We'll be back."

Dessa did not look up.

"That's not a thing you can say," she said. "That's a thing you can do." Then her pencil moved, very small, at the bottom of the page. He did not see what it wrote.

---

They went through the gate.

The gate crew opened the small door in the big one, and said *morning, holder* to Rook the way they had said it every morning for six weeks. They went through it one at a time: Pell at Duchess's head with the lead rope the weighbridge girl had given him, and the cart creaking behind; then Dee; then Rook; then Jab with the frame on his back and a fish buttoned inside his coat; then Callie with the hook on her shoulder and the counter on her hip; then Toren, last, the runner.

The road went two hundred paces to the south lip of the Sag.

The watch was at the lip. Toren saw it as they came along the made road: the lamps on their poles, lit though it was light, in their line along the upper edge of the east track, with the gap between the third and the fourth where the dark lane ran down toward the ditch. A figure on a crate at the south lip, with a book, eyes shut. That was Dessa, who had got there ahead of them on her bad leg somehow. Katori at the top of the dark lane with her blade on her fist, dark. Ines on the west rim. Otto on the lamp-line between the two yard lifters, standing with his weight in his heels.

"Nothing inside thirty feet," said Dessa, from the crate, with her eyes shut. Her voice was flat and carried. "The bottom's quiet. It's been quiet since the fifth hour. Go now." And then, louder, the voice nobody argued with: "*Go now.*"

Pell led Duchess down into the dip.

The road went down across the bottom of the Sag, over the tilted slabs with the black water between them, past the white post with the six on it leaning out of the water beside the dropped corner. There was no line on it. There was no holder. The day holder's hollow at the south lip was empty behind them, with the frost in the bottom of it. The only thing between the six of them and the joints in the bottom of the dip was a girl with a bad leg on a crate with her eyes shut, telling them where everything was.

Duchess did not stop. She did not put her ears back. She walked down across the bottom of the Sag with the cart creaking behind her, and up the other side toward the north lip, and nothing came up out of the joints at all.

---

At the north lip Toren stopped and turned round.

He could see all of it from there. The whole bowl of the Sag below him, three hundred paces from lip to lip, with the lamps of the watch along the east side and the figures at their places. The south lip, with the empty hollow in the gravel and the crate beside it. The two hundred paces of made road going back to the yard gate. The berm, and the north yard beyond it with the whitewash lanes and the shed that leaked. And the long building beyond that, low and white with frost, with the windows of the ward along its side.

And the yard office, with the long window on the second floor running the length of the north wall. There was somebody standing in it. He could not see her face from here. He could see that she was small and square, and that she was not looking at the yard. She was looking out along the road at the north lip of the Sag, where six people and a horse and a cart had stopped.

He counted them. He could not help it. He had been counting other people since the second of the month.

Pell, at Duchess's head. Dee, beside the cart, with her glass in her coat and her book under her arm. Rook, on his left, half a step behind, where the hole in his back had been, standing up without having to be told. Jab, with eight jars on his back and a fish over his heart, looking not at the road ahead but back across the Sag at the long building. Callie, with the hook on her shoulder and the counter on her hip, not holding anything, her hand open at her side.

Six. And a horse.

"Persons in party," he said, out loud, to nobody.

Jab turned his head and looked at him.

"Six," said Toren. And then, because it was not the whole of it, and because Jab had once stood at a window in his socks and said *two* when there was one of him: "And everybody in the beds."

Jab looked back across the Sag at the long building for a moment longer. Then he nodded.

"And everybody in the beds," he said.

---

The road went north from the Sag, past the culvert where a boy had once knelt in the angle of a wall with a child behind him, and on into the grey. It was the old north road for two days. Then, Pell said, there was a place where a line of iron posts came in from the west across the fields, and a road beside them, and that was the old line. It was red in the atlas.

Toren went last.

He walked at the back with the paper in his coat, the signed one, folded on the side with the plate. The stone was in the other pocket, warm and faint and breathing, with a Handful settled at the bottom of him that he had taken in slowly the night before. His left arm was in its leather cuff against his chest. In front of him the cart creaked, and Duchess's breath went out in clouds. Pell said something to Dee about the camber, and Dee said something back that made Rook laugh, once, the place where a laugh would have been.

He did the sum, because he could not help it.

Six days north and west on the old line. However long inside. Six days back. Twelve Flasks, lent. Nine committed. Three untouchable, on the cart, for the way home. A fence eleven miles round. A yellow stair. A door at the bottom that opened toward you.

And at the end of it, under all of it, the thing his father had never once written down.

*Everybody back.*

It had a price. He did not know yet what it was. He had not been up the road. Nobody had, who was still alive, except an old woman in a bed who could not walk one yard of it. But it was written. It was at the bottom of the paper, in Pell's small neat hand, under *everybody out*, in a different pencil. It was on six lines Dessa had left blank at the back of a book. It was in a small boy's fist, wound round twelve feet of cord, and on a woman's open hand on a blanket, and in an old woman's *pull the door*.

It was the first sum he had ever done that had the way home in it.

He walked at the back of the party, up the old north road in the first grey light of the forty-first day, and did not look back again.
