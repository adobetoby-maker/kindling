## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six to eight connected
chapters per movement (six by default), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,
  consistent with an action/interiority-forward book rather than a dialogue-driven one.
- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —
  present throughout, not just in set-piece fight chapters.
- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult
  crossover register, short-to-medium sentence construction, low jargon-density outside the
  progression-vocabulary spikes.
- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a
  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

# Chapter 40 — Callie: Graded

Jab drew them at the river, all of them, alone.

There was nobody else to do it. Miss Wren was at her floor. Rook was off, and hot, and lying on the canvas with his eyes open. So Jab stood on the gravel below the pump house in the last of the sun with a jar of the lifter's ash against his left palm and his right hand flat on one chest after another, and drew.

Callie held the counter for him. She held it with her right hand, because her left arm would not go out from her side without the ribs saying so. She held it at arm's length to each chest in turn and said the number, and Jab drew until she said a smaller one.

"Ninety," she said, at Toren. And later, "Twenty." And later, "Twelve. Off."

He did Toren. He did Callie, with his palm flat on the inside of her wrist, because the ribs were where the rest of it would have been. He did Miss Wren.

He did Miss Wren longest.

She stood in front of him with her hands at her sides and let him. Callie watched her face while he did it. It was white, and it was not steady now. It had been steady the whole time under the hill, over the jar and the glass and Jab's back, nineteen times. It had stopped being steady on the bridge.

"A hundred and ten," said Callie, at Miss Wren's chest. "Ten breaths."

Jab did not say anything. He drew. He held it high and vented it into the gravel downstream of the pump house, away from the water, the road's and not the river's. He went back on.

"Sixty," said Callie, after a long time. "Thirty. Fourteen."

"Off," said Miss Wren. Her voice was thin. "That's enough. That's the room."

Jab came off.

Then he stood with his hands held out a little from his sides, the way he had stood in the cut at the wash. Nobody took them. There was nobody to take them.

"I'll vent," he said. "Long."

He went down the gravel to the far end, past the stone with the chalk cross on it and the two hot jars from yesterday standing beside it, and knelt, and put both palms flat on the stones there. He stayed there while the sun went down behind the hill.

---

She graded the jars at the fire.

Nobody asked her to. She took the counter and went to the frame where it stood against the pump house wall on its two flat stones, and she held the counter to each jar in the rack at a hand's width, one at a time, and waited ten breaths for each. And said it out loud, because that was what you did with a number.

"The fence's last whole one," she said. "Eight. The room. It's clean. Eight in it." She moved along. "The fence's fourth. The one under the line. Eight. Clean. Five in it."

She weighed it on her palm. Five, near enough.

Then the lifter's.

"Jab's first," she said. "Nine. Clean. Full. Eight." She moved along. "Jab's second. Eight. Clean. Six in it. He's drawn two out of it at the river."

"Mine," said Toren, from the fire.

"Yours is empty," said Callie. "It went on the four of us first. It was only a half." She moved along. "Mine. The first." She held the counter to it for ten breaths and then for ten more, because she wanted to be sure. "Ten. It's clean. Full. Eight."

She came to the last one.

"My second," she said. "Nineteen."

She held it up to the fire and looked through the glass at the grey in the bottom. It was grey. But there were darker grains in it, fine, like pepper in flour, all through.

"It's dirty," she said. "There's grit in it. The hill. It came up with the rest. I felt it come and I didn't know it was coming." She put it in the rack apart from the others, in the socket at the end where jar B had been, with the broken glass taken out of it now. "Four in it. Dirty. It'll burn wrong."

"It'll burn wrong," said Miss Wren, from the canvas by Rook. "It'll not go on anybody's back unless there's nothing else. Write it dirty."

Toren wrote it.

Callie stood by the frame and said the whole of it. She said it the way her mother said the store book, flat, for somebody else. The way she had said it every halt on the old line and every night since.

"Inside," she said. "The fence's, thirteen. Five in the fourth, eight in the last. The lifter's, twenty-two, clean. Four, dirty. Thirty-five clean. Four dirty." She stopped. "Home, twenty-four. On the cart. Not touched. The road jar's empty. The vent jars are under the hill, and two by the stone."

"Say what we went in with," said Toren.

"Forty," said Callie. "Clean. On the forty-seventh."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Thirty-five and four," said Pell, from the cart. "Against forty." He was quiet. "And two boxes."

"And three under the line, inside two turns," said Miss Wren. "That goes in front of her with our names on it. Not as a thing we'll do again. As a thing we did." She did not stop. "And my floor. And his." She nodded at Toren. "And her ribs. And his hand." She did not look toward the far end of the gravel, where Jab was still kneeling. "And the rest of it on the floor of that room."

"How much is on the floor of that room?" said Pell.

Callie thought about it. She thought about the long slope of grey from the carrier to the threshold, knee-deep in the middle. The bank of it along the corridor wall, the shape of a rope. The tray full of it, the whole length of the corridor into the dark. The stair.

"More than we lifted," she said. "Four times. Five." She shook her head. "I don't know. It's not a number. It's more than we could lift in a day, with nobody hurt."

"Then write that," said Toren. "*More than we could lift.*"

---

"What was it?" said Pell.

He said it to Rook. He said it the way he asked the weighbridge about a jar, not unkindly. Rook was lying on his side on the canvas with his face to the fire and his eyes half shut.

"The Director'll want a word for it," said Pell. "On the paper. They grade them. A Flask, or a Barrel. She'll want to know which." He looked across the river at the hill in the dusk. "It came along a rope and had its hand in Jab. It mended everything anybody did to it every thirteen breaths. It had the whole hill." He looked at Rook. "That's a Barrel, surely."

Rook opened his eyes.

"A Barrel's a thing on its own," he said.

He said it slowly, because he was hot and his mouth was dry. Miss Wren held the cup for him and he drank and went on.

"A Barrel comes out of a tear all by itself and it's big on its own account. What's in it is its own." He looked at the fire. "That wasn't big. That was a Flask. Maybe. A Flask with its hand in everybody's pocket." He shut his eyes again. "All that grey on the floor wasn't it. It was everybody else's. The husks'. The hounds' at the wash. The jar that broke on the rail. Twenty years of whatever came out of that sky and fell on the hill and lay there. It went and got it all, and put it on, like a coat that's too big." He was quiet. "Write it a Flask. Write the rest as what it had."

Toren wrote it. Callie watched him do it. *Its own: a Flask (R.). What it had: more than we could lift.*

"You'd know," said Pell.

"I'd know," said Rook. "It had my job."

---

Miss Wren was sick in the night.

Callie heard it before she saw it. She was on the first watch with Pell, because Pell was awake and Callie could not lie down without the ribs, and she was standing by the cart wheel in the dark with the hook for a stick. She heard somebody get up off the gravel by the wall, fast, and go down toward the river, and be sick. It went on for a long time.

Jab was already there. He had not been asleep either. He went down the gravel to her in the dark, and Callie saw the two of them against the grey of the water, Miss Wren on her knees with her hand on a stone and Jab crouched beside her.

"It's the room," said Miss Wren, when she could. Her voice carried along the water. "It's twenty turns in that room after, at a hundred and thirty, with nobody drawing me. It's the room coming out." She was sick again. "It'll be the room for a day."

"Hold still," said Jab.

"I'm holding still."

"You're not. You're shaking."

"I'm allowed to shake," said Miss Wren. "I'm not allowed to be drawn at my floor. There's nobody to draw you after."

"There's nobody to draw me now," said Jab. "You're busy. I can feel it from here. Hold still."

Callie did not hear what Miss Wren said to that. She saw her sit back on her heels on the stones. She saw Jab put his palm flat on her back, between the shoulders, and his other hand on the jar he had brought, and bend his head.

She watched them for a long time. Then she looked away, because it was not hers to watch.

---

Pell was counting the sky.

He sat on the cart with his back to the rack and his leg out, the way he had sat all three days, with the sheet on his knee that he could not see to mark in the dark. He was looking across the river at the hill. Over its flat top there were no stars. Round the edge of the place with no stars there was the light the colour of the bottom of a pond.

"It's not ten and three," said Pell.

"I know. We saw it at the top of the road."

"I've been counting it since dusk." He did not take his eyes off it. "It's not anything. Eight, and five. Then twelve and two. Then fourteen and nothing, and it goes again. It's thickening and thinning, the same as it was. But there's no count to it. It's like a man breathing in his sleep who's having a bad dream." He was quiet. "It had a count for three days. Every one. I'd have put my name to it."

"It had the lifter," said Callie.

"Or the lifter had it," said Pell. "Jab doesn't know which. I don't. I'd like to know which." He moved his leg on the boards and made a small sound. "Write it down. Tomorrow. That woman'll want it more than the boxes, I think. Or as much."

"She'll want the boxes."

"She'll want the boxes," Pell agreed. "And she'll want to know why a thing that counted to thirteen for three days stopped counting the afternoon somebody put a point in a heap of grey under it." He looked at Callie in the dark. "I wouldn't like to be the one who has to decide what to do about that. With a Sag held and a machine that can see."

Callie looked at the sky over the hill. It thickened for a long time, and thinned, and thickened at once.

She did not count it. It had nothing to count.

---

She thought about the door.

She had not let herself think about it all afternoon. There had been the stair, and the road, and the bridge, and the river, and the jars. Now there was only the dark and Pell and the cart wheel, and she thought about it.

She had held it. She had stood in it with her back to the room for twenty turns and more, with the stone going out of her into a door and a bit until it was gone, and then her own, and a husk pushed against the pale a foot from her face. She had never once seen what was happening behind her. She had heard it. She had heard the sack hit the wall. She had heard Toren say *holding* with his teeth together, and Jab say *busy*, and Miss Wren say *off*, over and over, and the glass going over. She had heard the line said. She had heard *two turns*, and said *I've got two turns, I've not got three*, and she had not known, saying it, whether two would be enough. She had not been able to do anything about it if it was not.

She had looked round once. Rook had said she would.

She had turned her head and seen Jab on the floor with his hand out, and Miss Wren behind him with the jar, and the room behind that, grey. And Jab had looked at her and said *busy*. Just that. The word he said to tell you he was working and how long you had to hold.

And she had turned her head back and held.

She had not carried them. That was what she understood, standing by the cart wheel in the dark with her arm across her side. She had carried people all her life. She had carried her mother across four hundred miles in her head, every breath, every step, counting her. She had held a wagon on a hook with her mother in it. On the lip at the wash she had held a lee off her own stone over two bodies and taken a third in and out of it with her eyes. She had thought that was what it was for. Holding people.

Under the hill she had held a door. She had held the rest of the world off three people so they could do the thing only they could do, where she could not see them do it. She had not been holding them. She had been holding the edge of them. And they had held the rest.

*Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.*

She had thought, when her mother said it, that it meant letting go. It did not mean letting go. It meant choosing what to hold, and holding that, all of it, and trusting the people on the other side of it to hold their own.

She had not known that it would feel like this. It did not feel like forgetting. It felt like the ache in the palm after lifting, when you have put a thing down that was worth carrying.

---

In the morning Miss Wren measured them.

She did it sitting on a stone by the cold fire, because she was not inside a fence and she was allowed, and because her legs would not have done it standing. Her face was grey and there were marks under her eyes like bruises. But her hands were steady again. Callie watched them go to each wrist in turn.

"Your own's back," she said to Toren. "A turn. Not two. It'll be two by tonight if you do nothing."

"I'll do nothing."

"You'll carry a wheel," said Miss Wren. "But not today." She did Callie. "Your own's back. Half. You slept?"

"No."

"You slept an hour," said Miss Wren. "Jab said. Your own's half." She put her palm flat against Callie's side, not pressing, over the coat. "And that's cracked. Two, I think. The same two." She took her hand away. "I'll strap it. You'll not lift anything heavier than a counter for a week. You'll not lean on that hook."

"I'll lean on it to walk."

"You'll lean on it to walk," Miss Wren agreed. "That's what it's for this week."

She did Jab. She held his palms in both of hers, turned up. The white place on the heel of the right one was red round its edges now, and shiny, and there was a small blister at one side of it.

"They're warm," she said.

"I vented half the night."

"I know you did. I watched you. I was being sick, and I watched you." She put her palms flat on his, palm to palm, and Callie saw her find it. She drew. It was the watch taking it off the one who had worked, the way she had done it in the cut at the wash. Her face went quiet while she did it. "There," she said. "Cool."

"You're at your floor," said Jab.

"I'm a finger over it," said Miss Wren. "I slept too. After you'd finished with me." She let go of his hands. "That's enough for a watch. It's not enough for anything else. Not today."

She did her own, last, with her two fingers on the inside of her crooked wrist.

"A finger," she said. "Over." She wrote it.

---

Rook was holding.

That was Miss Wren's word for it, and she said it the way she had said it about the post on the arch. The red round the stitches had not gone further. It had not come in. He was hot, but not as hot as the first morning, and he drank the third of Sowerby's papers in boiled water and made the face.

"One left," said Miss Wren.

"Keep it," said Rook. "For whoever's worse."

"You're the worst."

"Then keep it for when I'm worse than this," said Rook. He looked at her across the cup. "You were sick."

"I was the watch," said Miss Wren. "Nobody watched me." She took the cup. "I'll not do it that way again."

"Yes you will," said Rook. "If it's the same thing again, you'll do it the same way." He shut his eyes. "I'd like it written down that I said so."

"I'll write that you said so," said Miss Wren. "I'll not write that you were right."

He laughed. It was the low careful laugh, the way a man laughs with a hole in his back. Callie saw Miss Wren's mouth move at the corner, once, and stop.

---

They decided at the cart.

Pell had the atlas open on his knee. Toren had the paper. Everybody else stood, or sat on what they were allowed to sit on, round the tail of the cart where the carrier sat on its board behind the home jars with two live boxes in it and the lid in its groove.

"Say the clock," said Toren.

"The forty-ninth," said Pell. "Today. The cycle's the fifty-fourth. Five days." He put his finger on the atlas. "It was six days from the gate to here, with the siding. With the road whole."

"It isn't whole," said Callie.

"It isn't whole," said Pell. "The crown's gone into the wash, and the post, and the arch. There's a gap in the embankment four paces wide with the wash running through the bottom of it and the drifts heaped on both sides." He looked at the three of them. "North round the head of the drifts, toward the hill. Or south through the marsh to the river and down this bank. Two days more, either way, and there's no bed for the cart on either. That's what I said on the forty-sixth. It's still true."

"That's the fifty-seventh," said Toren. "Home."

"Or later," said Pell. "With me on the cart and not walking."

"And the other thing?" said Toren. "What you said at the fire. That it'll take people across the bottom on foot."

"On foot," said Pell. "Through the drifts. Quick, and wash after. It won't take the cart." He looked at the cart under him, and the carrier on it, and the three home jars. "Nothing takes a cart across that."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Nothing takes a cart across that," he said again, slowly. He put his hand flat on the boards of the cart bed. "A cart's not a thing. It's a lot of things. Two wheels. An axle. A bed. Two shafts. Pins." He looked up. "A cart in pieces goes across in pieces."

Nobody said anything.

"The wheels roll," said Pell. "You roll them. Down one side of the gap and up the other. They're as high as her shoulder." He nodded at Callie. "The axle's two men. The bed's four, or two and a horse with a rope on it. The shafts are one each. The carrier's on its board, and a board slides on a drift. It's flour." He looked at Toren. "And there's a bank on each side of the gap, loose. And you set steps on a loose bank at the wash. On stones."

"I set them on my own," said Toren. "The last of it."

"You'll have a day's worth by tomorrow," said Pell. "Miss Wren says two turns by tonight. And there's a jar of that thing's ash to settle, if it's clean."

"And you?" said Toren.

"I'm a bed and two poles and a bit of canvas," said Pell. "Two of you. I'll not say anything about the camber."

---

"Price it," said Toren.

He said it to Callie.

She stood by the tail of the cart with her arm across her side and the counter in her other hand, and she priced it. She did it out loud, because that was the rule, and because saying it was how she found out whether it was true.

"The drifts were eighty, ninety at the foot of the bank," she said. "In the wind. With no wind, lying still, they'll be less. Sixty, seventy. Where the crown went in, where it's turned over, more. A hundred." She looked east, toward the ridge and the cut and the wash behind it, a mile off. "An hour. For all of it. Down, across, up, the pieces, and Pell, and the horse, twice over for the wheels. An hour at eighty. All of us, and the horse."

"How much?" said Miss Wren.

"Ten," said Callie. "Maybe twelve. For the drawing after, in the cut on the far side. And the settling for Toren's steps." She looked at the frame against the wall. "Out of the lifter's."

"Out of the lifter's," said Toren.

"Out of the lifter's," said Callie. "It's got twenty and a half clean. A Handful and a half went on Miss Wren in the night." She did not look at Miss Wren. "It'd have ten and a half left, and the fence's thirteen, and the dirty four. And home, twenty-four, not touched." She stopped. "And we'd be on the old line east of the gap by the fiftieth. With the cart back together. And it's five days from there, on the road we know. With the siding." She did the last sum. "The fifty-fifth. Home."

"Not the fifty-fourth," said Toren.

"Not the fifty-fourth," said Callie. "Nothing's the fifty-fourth. The fifty-fourth was gone before we crossed the bridge."

Toren looked at the paper for a long time.

"It's the fifty-fifth, or the fifty-seventh," he said. "Or later." He looked up. "That's Sowerby dosing her off her blood, badly, a day past the cycle. Or three. And whatever the fifty-fourth does, with a man guessing." He did not say *Gran*. "And ten Handfuls of that thing's ash, and an hour in the drifts, and Callie's ribs, and Dee a finger over her floor."

"Say which," said Miss Wren.

"The wash," said Toren. "In pieces. Tomorrow, first light, if the wind's down. Not today. Nobody carries a wheel through a drift at their floor."

"Not today," said Miss Wren, and wrote it. "And if the wind's up?"

"Then we sit at the cut and wait for it to drop," said Toren. "And I'll write what it costs."

Pell shut the atlas.

"Then today we go to the cut," he said. "It's a mile. The cart'll do that whole. It's this side of the gap." He looked at Duchess, tied to the wheel with her rope halter, eating. "She'll pull it to the cut. And tomorrow she'll walk through a drift, because somebody she knows is walking in front of her. And then she'll hate every one of us for a week."

"She'll be right," said Rook, from the canvas.

"She's always right," said Pell. "She's a horse."

---

They loaded the cart.

The frame went on the cart this time, in the rack behind the driver's bench beside the home jars, because Jab's palm was blistered and Miss Wren said he would not carry eight jars a mile on a blister. Rook went on the cart on the canvas, on his side, with his back off the boards. Pell stayed where he was. The carrier sat behind them on its board with the lid in its groove.

Toren went round the cart once, the whole of it, and counted what was on it. Then he counted them.

"Pell," he said. "Dee. Rook." He stopped on Rook the way he always did, longest. "Jab. Callie." He looked at the mare. "Six. And a horse."

He wrote it on the back of the signed paper, standing at the pump house wall.

*49th. The pump house. Out: 6. To the cut. The wash tomorrow, in pieces (Pell's). Home the 55th, if the wind's down.*

He looked at it.

"Dessa left six lines," he said. "At the foot of the page. For *back*."

"I know," said Callie.

"I'll not write in them yet," said Toren. He folded the paper and put it in his coat, beside the plate.

---

Callie went out in front.

She did it without being asked, the way she had done it on the first morning on the old north road. She went out twenty paces in front of Duchess with the hook for a stick and her left arm strapped across her side under her coat. She began to read the road for the wheels.

It was the road they had come on, three days ago, at dusk. The old line going up from the river on its long bare slope to the ridge, and the cut through the ridge, and the wash behind it. She knew it. She read it anyway. She read it for the wheels. The loose stones on the bed. The place where the frost had heaved it. The mark where the cart had come down it on the forty-sixth with Pell on it and his foot on a coil of rope.

At the top of the slope, at the mouth of the cut, she stopped and turned round.

She looked back once. Everybody does.

The river lay low and dark in its stony bed, with the pump house on its near bank, roofless, and the two broken pipes going into the water. The footbridge went across it on its posts, with two grey poles of wrack wood still lying along it over the crack. At the far end, the gate, with a twist of green wire through its chain, left so it would come.

And beyond the gate the yard, and the road going up the hill in its long Z, and the long building standing along the rim with its windows black. And the drum behind it with its broken top. And over the whole of the top of the hill, the sky.

The edge of it thickened. She did not count it. It thinned, and thickened at once, and thinned for a long time. It had no count. It was a thing breathing in its sleep, having a bad dream.

Under it, in a room under a hill, there was more ash lying on a floor than she could have lifted in a week. There were hundreds of boxes, and a third live one talking to the room, and three stoppered jars with a chalk cross beside them. She would put the number of every one of them on the Director's table.

In the cart behind Duchess there were two.

It was the forty-ninth day. At home it would be the ninth hour, near enough. Her mother would have walked to the ward for her pills at the eighth hour, with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. Senna would be on the fourth bed on the right, with five days to the narrow bed and the machine that sang flat. Tonk would be in the fifth, with a cord round his fist. They were all breathing without her. They had been breathing without her for eight days, and nothing had happened to any of them because she was not counting.

She did not know what would happen on the fifty-fourth. She did not know if the wind would be down tomorrow.

She knew what was in the cart, and what was in the frame, graded. She knew who was on the cart and who was walking. She knew the road to the cut, and she knew the wash.

She turned round and went on into the cut, twenty paces in front of the horse, and read the road for the wheels.

Behind her, at the back, she heard Toren counting them.

## 1. Verdict: the strongest Book Two engine

**The team survived the road. Meridian is where they find out that surviving
it wasn't the same as being safe — and that the only place any of them
actually gets better is back outside the wall, doing the work Meridian cannot
spare enough qualified adults to do.** Concretely: Meridian's Sag line is
worsening faster than its registered defenders can cover; a new and more
dangerous rip has opened near Caul Hill; and the one machine that keeps Senna's
marrow-support course calibrated—and gives Meridian its only advanced imaging
for cases such as Tilda's and Tonk's—needs a part from the irradiated plant
beside it. Reaching the part and securing the rip are one mission. The team must carry large stores of
graded ash because Sustain can recall active radiation from a living body only
while someone keeps paying for the work. That necessity turns three separated
arrivals into one company: Callie, Jab, and Toren go with Rook and Dee Wren,
while their coaches and cohort make the expedition possible.
Training, combat, ash economics, and found-family bonding all happen because
the mission must be prepared for, survived, and paid for. Institutional
politics supplies pressure and consequence. It does not replace the adventure.

## 2. Book promise, emotional promise, progression promise, irreversible ending change

- **Book promise:** the three roads that became one group at Meridian's gate
  become one working company by this book's end — not by declaration, by
  having solved a problem together that none of their three separate roads
  could have solved alone.
- **Emotional promise:** each protagonist discovers that the cost of the road
  didn't end at the gate. Callie's counting, Jab's caregiving arithmetic, and

  won't let her count, a brother who will need him for thirteen years, a

  them here.
- **Progression promise:** all three protagonists cross a real threshold
  under real stakes, never merely in a drill: Toren gets Guard to hold on
  purpose; Callie's twin-kindled Ward (Edge and Ward together,
  `UNIVERSE_BIBLE.md`) is tested at group scale; and Jab learns that his

## 3. The central outward mission

### One mission: the part and the rip (`OWNER`)

The former Options A and B are now one operation. Senna's treatment shed runs
on a single, pre-Fall machine at least seventy years old (`CANON.md`, Ch. 27–28), and a part in its
calibration train is failing. The only known compatible salvage is at Caul Hill
— the irradiated pre-Fall plant where Senna received her original exposure.
A new and more dangerous rip has opened at or beneath the same complex. It has
made the site harder to enter, but it has also left the one resource the team
cannot make the attempt without: deep stores of ownerless reserve-ash.

The objectives cannot be separated:

1. Carry enough clean, graded ash from Meridian to fund the approach and the
   first radiation-recall cycles. The team cannot count on ash it has not yet
   won.
2. Cross the contaminated approach while Rook, Jab, and Dee Wren
   rotate Sustain duty, recalling active radiological burden before it fixes
   into lasting tissue damage.
3. Reach the calibration assembly and recover the compatible part.
4. Contain or collapse the new rip, kill its new Riftspawn expression through
   a full-team action, and recover the resulting ash so the healers can bring
   everyone home.

The third former option — making the disk mystery the journey's reason — stays
retired. The disk receives one costly clue late in the book, not the book's
engine.

### Why it must leave Meridian

The shed's machine cannot be repaired from spare parts on hand; Meridian's own
hardened depot inventory has nothing compatible (a callback to Annice Fell's
ledger, Ch. 28: things go into that depot, they do not come out). The active
rip also cannot be ignored: if it grows, it changes the Sag from a strained
defense line into an open road for stronger Riftspawn. Caul Hill is roughly a
week's travel past the Sag's worst stretch — close enough to reach inside the
machine's failure clock, far enough to be a true expedition.

### Why adults cannot simply solve it

Two separate, compounding reasons, both institutional rather than a plot
contrivance:

1. **Every ordinary defender who could lead it is already spoken for.** Meridian's
   registered defenders — the people who can actually hold a road shut —
   are stretched thin covering the worsening Sag line itself (this is the
   book's own opening pressure). Pulling one of them off the wall for a
   week to escort a salvage run means a week of the Sag going unheld, which
   the Director's office will not authorize under any argument.
2. **The site selects for a very rare team, not for youth.** Caul Hill's
   radiation cannot be endured by general toughness or fast healing. Sustain
   can recall the *active burden* from tissue, but it cannot undo old damage
   after that damage has fixed. Rook and Jab can do the work; the Homura
   cross-medic understands how to direct it, rotate healers, and increase the
   amount of ash a Kindled soul can safely hold and spend. Callie and Toren
   supply the Ward, Guard, and extraction capability that keep those healers
   alive. The party is selected by complementary doors and training, not by
   the premise that children tolerate radiation better than adults.

### What failure costs

If the part is not recovered, Senna's and (per the same shed, same cycle)
every other patient's course begins to fail within roughly eleven days — the
same interval already established as the treatment's own clock (Ch. 27–28).
Failure is not abstract: it is a countable number of cycles before Senna's
marrow support lapses, stated on the page the way Pell states a distance.
Secondary cost: if the mission is caught leaving without authorization, the
whole clan's residency review is jeopardized, which threatens every household
riding on it (Callie's, Jab's, the Hobb's Wall clan's).

### How it joins the Sag / residency / ash / defender threads

The mission is staffed, provisioned, and made survivable using everything the
book's other threads have taught. The team carries large stores of lifted,
graded ash as fuel, not as trade cargo and not as a lucky find. Toren's Guard
and Callie's widened Ward protect the Sustain rotation. Jab senses changes in
the bodies under his care and learns to recall radiation deliberately. Rook
teaches him the lived technique; Dee teaches the repeatable
capacity discipline: take ash in, settle it without confusing it for personal
reserve, spend it through Sustain, vent the harmful load, and refill before
the soul bottoms out. The defender cohort covers the gap at Meridian while
the expedition is away. The ash secured at the rip then becomes the material
proof that the team can protect Meridian and alter the exploitative residency
economy, rather than simply argue against it.

## 6. Youth-defender cohort design

- **Purpose:** the Sag is worsening faster than Meridian's adult defenders
  can cover, and the station's own liability doctrine (registered Kindled
  residents are sorted into labor by door) already pulls Kindled teenagers
  toward dangerous work informally. The cohort formalizes that pull into
  something with adult oversight, rather than pretending it isn't happening.
- **Membership logic:** not "every Kindled kid at Meridian" — a small,
  specific group built from need and consent: Callie, Jab (if his household's
  situation allows; his instinct to keep working through the Elder's ethic
  makes this an easy yes for him and a harder one for the book to justify
  given Tonk), Toren, plus willing members of the Hobb's Wall clan (Dessa,
  Wyck) and at least one Meridian-native trainee to keep the cohort from
  reading as "the Book One cast plus scenery." Recommend one new named
  Meridian local, invented at drafting, who has no Book One history and
  therefore reacts to everything (the ash economy, the found-family's
  closeness, Rook's method) the way a reader coming in cold might.
- **Adult safeguards:** Dee Wren oversees formal reserve and Sustain drills; Rook
  oversees live Sag-line rotations; no cohort member holds a line alone
  without an adult in immediate support distance; injuries and overdraws are
  logged and reviewed, mirroring the station's own paperwork culture (a
  believable, in-world safeguard rather than an authorial promise the plot
  ignores).
- **Training progression:** shadowing → supervised jar-and-sack work behind
  an adult's held line → controlled short holds under direct supervision →
  full rotations with an adult in support range → the Caul Hill mission as
  the first expedition beyond Meridian's support perimeter. It is not an
  unsupervised youth test: Rook and Dee travel, while Katori and
  Pell divide field/navigation coaching and the defense of the people left
  behind.
- **Moral pressure:** the book should not resolve whether this is right. The
  Director's office is correct that the Sag needs more hands than it has;
  Pell and Senna's mother-thread (Callie's own mother's "you don't get to
  spend yourself on me") are correct that this trades children's bodies for
  a shortfall adults created. Both positions stay true at once. The named
  residency-injustice case (§3, §11) should be a cohort member's family —
  someone who took the ash-lifting-equivalent contract without a found
  family able to absorb the cost, exactly as both prior drafts already
  recommend — so the moral weight lands on a specific person, not a policy
  debate.
- **The first school without calling it one:** no uniforms, no ranked class
  structure, no shared dormitory-and-bell-schedule texture. Training happens
  in the same huts, the same stove-side conversations, and the same north
  yard the rest of the book already lives in. What *does* begin here is
  durable: three marked practice lanes, a shared equipment rack, and Dessa's
  append-only record of drills, injuries, reserve, recovery, and repeatable
  results. Every cohort member keeps a private want unrelated to combat
  (Callie's counting, Jab's caregiving, Toren's leadership anxiety, and
  whatever the new local recruit wants that has nothing to do with the Sag).

## 7. Ash-learning ladder

Grounded directly in the drafted Rook ash-collection scene
(`book-01-tallow-gate/book-02-drafts/movement-01-ash-collection.md`) and the
Ch. 27–28 / Ch. 18 "the jar" material — this ladder should be taught through
exactly that kind of scene, not through explanation.

1. **Recognition and collection.** Reading a drift's Yield by eye and feel
   before touching it (Flask vs. Handful, "a fair one" vs. "a dirty one," per
   the ash-collection draft); the flat-palm technique, working a span at a
   time, holding it low and open rather than closed. Failure mode: hauling —
   trying to pull ash faster than it comes, which yields less, not more.
   Teaching scene: Rook's drafted scene itself, adapted to include a cohort
   member learning alongside Wyck and Dessa rather than only overheard.
2. **Grading.** Handful / Flask / Barrel / Well (`CANON.md`, Yield) as a
   trade vocabulary, learned first as an insult-proof way to size up danger
   ("Handful or Flask?"), then as the literal unit the weighbridge pays in.
   Teaching scene: a weighbridge dispute (Ch. 18's "that's thinner than it
   ought to come" beat is the model) where a cohort member's read is
   challenged and has to be defended with evidence, not confidence.
3. **Cleaning and contamination.** Silt, ditch-water, and "a dirty Handful"
   as concrete hazards — contaminated ash burns unreliably and is priced
   down, or in the worst case damages whoever spends it (a training-safe
   version of an overdraw, introduced here in miniature). Teaching scene: a
   cohort member's first real weighbridge failure, graded down publicly, and
   having to learn why rather than being told the rule.
4. **Storage and carriage.** Jars, sacks, flasks, folded canvas between them
   (all attested in Ch. 18, 27, and the ash-collection draft); why quantity
   and grade both degrade under bad handling, and why a station's economy
   treats a jar of clean Flask as worth defending on the road.
5. **Taking it in — ash held by the soul.** Ash is ownerless, unlike a
   person's expended reserve. Skin-contact absorption is deliberate and
   unhurried: the same flat-palm stillness used to collect it, turned inward.
   Ash does not become a second organ or named storage tank; the soul holds
   it as usable reserve. Rushing intake causes spill, nausea, false fullness,
   or a lock that prevents clean spending.
6. **Increasing usable working depth.** Dee teaches that a
   Kindled soul does not become metaphysically larger in a week; the person
   learns to use more of the depth already present. Intake, settle, spend,
   vent, rest, repeat. Capacity is measured by clean cycles and recovery, not
   by how much ash someone can force in once. This is the training that makes
   the Caul Hill reserve practical instead of merely heavy cargo.
7. **Radiation recall through Sustain.** Rook teaches Jab to feel the active
   harmful burden in a body without confusing it with old injury. Sustain
   draws or recalls that burden while ash pays for the work. The technique
   prevents further damage during exposure; it does not erase damage already
   fixed in tissue and therefore does not retroactively cure Senna. The
   healer must vent the recalled burden and rotate out before it begins to
   lodge in the healer instead.
8. **Spending through a door under pressure.** Absorbed ash can power Edge,
   Stride, Ward, Sustain, or Resilience without first emptying personal
   reserve. Teaching scene: a controlled Sag-line failure where ash-fuel,
   not personal endurance, is the difference between holding and breaking.
9. **Advanced team use and overdraw.** The expedition uses a measured
   rotation rather than a vague shared pool: one person works, one holds the
   reserve, one watches for spill and overdraw. Spending past safe working
   depth causes visible damage even when ash remains available. This is why
   the party needs stores, trained bodies, and discipline rather than one
   miraculous healer.

## The lifter (the Sustain-echo Riftspawn)

Named by Jab's description and Rook's word ("It's got my job"), written by Toren as
**the lifter** (ch 36). A creature term, not a continuing named character.

- **Body:** a low grey heap, at first waist-high and two of Callie long, with a hump
  along its back; no face. At every three a line of light the colour of the pond-light
  opens along the top of the hump — the vent. It moves only by drawing itself along its
  own rope ("the way a man pulls himself along a rope hand over hand, only there were no
  hands"). It lays new rope behind it as it goes (the floor rope, ch 37).
- **Ropes:** grey ash in the cable trays and along floors, from finger-thick strands to
  thigh-thick; in room 1 the strands lie among the boxes where the copper was stripped.
  Three arm-ropes near the body lift and swing like wet rope at worked things within a
  few paces.
- **Mechanic observed on the page:** it **draws loose ash for ten breaths** (threads
  off floors and up walls into the ropes, the whole rope creeping west) and at the
  **three pushes** into any "hole": a cut rope, a heap (which stands up as a husk), a
  wound in itself, or **a hand that is drawing on it**. Killed husks lie for ten
  breaths, are drawn in, and stand up again at the three. Cut strands grow back
  together at the three. It never reached into a stoppered jar or the frame's jars;
  ash lifted and stoppered in a vent jar did not come back (the next husk stood up "a
  hand short").
- **Husks were its hands:** when it died, every husk it had made came apart on its own,
  in the room and in the corridor.
- **Yield (grading, ch 40):** Rook — *its own: a Flask, maybe; the rest was what it had*
  ("a Flask with its hand in everybody's pocket"). Not a Barrel. The Breach remains
  reserved.

## The Caul Hill fight (D+48), as staged

**Terrain:** room 1 (above). The door is in the north wall; the corridor outside runs
east–west. The lifter lies over the carrier and sledge by the east-wall boxes, its hump
toward the corner heap. The floor rope enters over the threshold; the ceiling rope

corridor by the steel door. The counter is on Dee's shoulder (off Callie's hip).

threshold, lee up into the corridor and leaned back up the wall over the hole — **a door
and a bit**, with her back to the room. Dee by the cabinets near the door (glass, open

**Sequence (Dee's count; a three and ten ≈ one glass turn). Repaired staging: Toren holds
Guard or Edge, never both.**

   the threshold** (heel drag, "like a sack of flour opened with a hook"), steps in and

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.
