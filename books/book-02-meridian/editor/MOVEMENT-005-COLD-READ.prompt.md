# Chapter 33 — Toren: Out: Four

Toren woke before the light and counted them.

He did it lying on the gravel with his arm in its cuff against his chest and his eyes still shut. Pell, on the cart, asleep. Dee, awake. Rook. Jab, asleep at last, on his side by the dead fire with one hand flat over the left side of his coat. Callie, asleep, with her hand on the hook.

He went back to Rook, because something about Rook was wrong.

He opened his eyes. The sky over the river was grey, going white at the bottom. The pump house wall stood black above him. Across the river the fence ran north along the far bank, and beyond it the hill went up into the dark. Over its flat top there was still the place where the sky was wrong, and round its edge the light the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened, and thinned.

He did not count it. He knew what it would come to.

Dee was kneeling by Rook with two fingers on his wrist. She had not lain down again after her watch. Toren could see that from her coat, which had no gravel on it.

"He's hot," she said, without looking round.

---

Rook was lying on his side on the canvas, where she had put him the night before so his back was off the ground. His eyes were open. His face was wet, though the morning was cold enough to put frost on the cart.

"I'm warm," said Rook. "It's a warm morning."

"There's ice on the wheel," said Dee.

"Then I'm warm and the wheel's cold," said Rook. "We're both of us wrong."

Dee did not smile. She took the knot out of the canvas strip round his chest and lifted the pad off his back.

Toren came and knelt on the other side of the canvas, where he could see.

The stitches ran in a short dark line between Rook's shoulders, small and neat and tight, the way Dee had put them in at the cut. The skin round them was red. The red went out a hand's width on each side of the line, and it was swollen, and it was shiny. When Dee put the back of her fingers near it, not on it, she took them away again at once.

"It's declared," she said.

"That was quick of it," said Rook.

Jab was awake. Toren had not heard him get up. He was standing at the end of the canvas with his hand still on his coat, looking down at Rook's back.

"He's been hot since the fourth hour," said Jab. "I felt it. I didn't know if I'd dreamed it."

"Put your hand over it," said Dee. "Don't touch."

Jab knelt and held his right palm an inch above the red. He shut his eyes. Toren watched his face go still, the way it went at a table.

"There's nothing busy," said Jab after a while. "Not the grey. That's gone. We had all of that out." He did not open his eyes. "It's him. It's him, doing something. Fighting it. It's hot all through, not in bits." He took his hand away. "It's not the thing I can draw."

"No," said Dee. "It's the dog."

She said it to Toren, because Toren had written the dog down.

"The grey came out yesterday. What's left is what a hound's mouth puts in a man when it bites him. It's the same as a dog bite in a yard, or a nail in a foot." She laid the pad back on. "That's not Sustain. That's Sowerby's. And Sowerby's eighty miles off."

She opened her small bag. Out of it she took four folded papers, white, each the size of a thumb, tied together with thread. She untied one.

"Fever powder," she said. "He gave me four. For the road. For a hound bite, or a wound gone bad, or a fever in the night." She looked at the paper in her fingers. "I'm to put it in boiled water and see he drinks all of it. I'm to do it once a day. I'm not to do it twice to make myself feel better."

"And if it doesn't break?" said Toren.

"Then by tomorrow night he'll be worse," said Dee. "And then we'll not be waiting for anything."

---

The water was the first thing, then.

Dee's skin was empty. She had put the whole of it into Rook's back at the cut, a little at a time. Toren went to the cart and counted the others, because nobody had.

There were two, hanging from the rail behind the driver's bench. One was full. The other was a third full, near enough, when he hefted it.

"One and a third," he said.

"That's for drinking," said Dee. "Not for washing. Not for his back." She looked at the river. "The river's for the rest. If it's clean."

Callie was already up. She took the counter out of its bag and went down the gravel to the river's edge above the pump house, where the water came down out of the north fast and dark over the stones. She held the counter out over it at the length of her arm and waited ten breaths.

"Seven," she said. "The room." She moved down past the pump house and did it again, below the place where they had washed. "Nine. Our wash is in it."

"Above the pump house for drinking," said Dee. "Below it for washing. And nothing out of it goes in his back till it's boiled."

So they needed a fire, and the fire needed wood, and the pump house had none. It was stone, and the roof was gone. There was only the thorn they had brought on the cart and burned last night, and that was ash now.

Toren and Callie went down the bank below the pump house. The river had been high at some time, and it had left a line of wrack along the gravel, a hand's height above where the water ran now. It was sticks and reeds and bleached old branches, dry, jammed among the stones. They picked along it for half a mile and came back with an armful each.

"That's one pot," said Callie. "Maybe two."

"Then it's one now and one tonight," said Dee.

She boiled the pot at the pump house wall. When it had cooled enough, she stirred the powder into a cup of it and held the cup while Rook drank, lying on his side, with his hand round hers on the cup. He drank all of it. He made a face at the end.

"Sowerby's," he said. "It tastes like his office."

"Drink the rest of the pot," said Dee. "Slowly. It's the last boiled water you'll see till dark."

---

Pell had not waited for anybody.

He had got himself sitting up on the cart with his back against the rack behind the driver's bench and his splinted leg out straight along the boards. He had done it alone. Toren could see from his face what it had cost him and that he did not want it mentioned.

Dee knelt on the tailboard beside the foot. She did not undo the splint. She pinched the end of his big toe, where it came out of the canvas strips, and watched it go white and then pink again.

"The blood's going round it," she said. "That's good. It's swelled under the strips. That's not." She loosened one knot, a finger's width. "You'll not put it down."

"I've nowhere to put it down," said Pell. "I'll sit." He had the atlas open on his knee in its oilcloth. "A man on his back can't do much. A man sitting up can count."

"Count what?"

"Whatever wants counting." He looked across the river at the hill. "She said to count what comes out of that. The Director. It's on the paper." He did not look at Toren. "That's a thing a man can do sitting on a cart. I'll do it."

---

Dee measured their floors on the gravel by the cold fire, as if it were the gate.

She did Jab first, the way she always did now. "A hand," she said. "Floor's a finger." She turned his palm up and held her own over it, not touching. "Cool. They've come down."

"They came down about the third hour," said Jab.

"I know. I was awake."

She did Callie. "Your stone's what you settled last night. All of it." She kept her fingers on the wrist. "Your own's come back. Not all. Three parts, near enough."

"I slept," said Callie.

"You slept like a stone in a well," said Dee. "I watched you. That's the only reason it's three parts."

She did Toren.

He had settled last. He had sat by the fire after Jab lay down, with his palm flat on a square of paper and a Handful of jar C's ash on it, and let it come at the rate it came. That was most of an hour, for him. It had been full dark when the paper was clean. The stone had gone down slowly and lain there, warm and faint, a long way down.

"Stone's full," said Dee. "Your own—" She waited. "Two turns. Not the bit. The bit'll come back by noon, if you don't spend it before."

"I'll try not to."

"Don't try," said Dee. "Don't spend it."

She did her own last, two fingers on her own wrist, her eyes on the hill. She did not say anything about it. She wrote in her book.

---

Then there were the coats.

Senna's rules said coat off, shake it, wash at the river, and they had done that at dusk, in the last of the light. But it had been dusk. Now it was day, and Callie went round them with the counter.

Jab's coat had the long pale stain down the back of it where jar B had broken. Callie held the counter to it and waited.

"Eight," she said. "It's clean. It's only the jar." She looked at the stain. "It's the stair's ash. It's clean on you."

Dee's coat was twelve. Callie's own was fourteen. Pell's, on the cart, was twenty, and Dee made him take it off and hand it down, and shook it downwind herself.

Toren's was thirty.

He knew why. He had been outside the lee at the wash, killing two husks against it, with the grey going up over him in a burst each time. He stood in his shirt on the gravel with his arm in its cuff and watched Callie hold the counter to the coat. He was cold.

"Wash it," said Dee. "In the river. Below the pump house. Then beat it on the stones till you're tired, and then some more."

He did it one-handed. He knelt at the edge of the water and pushed the coat under and held it there, and it was so cold his hand stopped feeling after ten breaths. He pulled it out and beat it on a flat stone, and put it under again. Jab came down after a while and did the beating for him without being asked. The grey went off it into the water in a thin cloud and away down the river.

"Sixteen," said Callie, when it had been on the stones in the sun for an hour. "It's still in the seams."

"Sixteen's a coat," said Dee. "Thirty's a coat I'd not let inside."

Then the hook.

Callie held it up and held the counter to it herself. The curve read eighteen, where it had been down the loose bank twice and on the lip in the wind. Then she held the counter to the grip.

The top of the conduit was wrapped in rag where her hands went. The rag was old and grey and soft, and it had been wet with the wash and dried on her hands.

"Thirty-four," said Callie. "It's the rag."

She took out her knife and cut it off. She did it carefully, in one long strip, without letting it touch her other hand. Then she stood with the strip hanging from the point of the knife and looked at Dee.

"Not the river," said Dee. "Not the fire."

So Toren dug a hole at the far end of the gravel, away from the water, with the heel of his boot. Callie dropped the rag in it off the knife. He kicked the gravel back over it and put a flat stone on top, and Callie chalked a cross on the stone with Pell's chalk. *Don't touch.* It was the second one they had left on the road.

She washed the curve of the hook in the river with sand, the way her grandfather had scoured a scale pan. When she held the counter to it again it read eleven. The grip was bare steel now, cold and smooth. There was still a paper tag tied round the conduit below where the rag had been. The grey coats had tied it there in the pen. *pen, 35th.*

"You could cut that off too," said Toren.

"No," said Callie. "They'll want to write under it. When we get back."

---

They went to look at the bridge in daylight at the third hour of the morning. Three of them: Callie with the counter, Dee with the glass, and Toren. Nobody goes to look alone.

It was thirty paces across. It was three planks wide, laid side by side on cross-beams, and the cross-beams sat on posts that went down into the river. The planks were grey and split along the grain and furred with old moss at the edges. There was a rail on the upstream side, the north side, one long pole at the height of a man's hip, lashed to the posts with wire. There was no rail on the downstream side at all.

Below the bridge, downstream, there had been another crossing, beside it. Two iron pipes, each as thick as a man's body, had gone over the river on iron legs, side by side. Toren could see where they came out of the river wall of the pump house on this side, and where they went into the far bank on the other. In the middle the legs had gone. The pipes had broken and dropped into the river, and their ends lay in the water with the current going white round them.

"That was the pump," said Dee. "River water, over to the hill."

"What for?"

"I don't know," said Dee. "For something that needed water. Things like that always need water."

Callie walked out onto the bridge first, with the counter held out in front of her. She did not look at the water. She looked at the planks.

"Seven," she said, at the first post. "Nine. In the middle." She went on. At the far end she stopped, two planks from the bank, and crouched.

"This one's gone," she said.

It was the middle plank. It had cracked across, right through, between two cross-beams, and the two halves sagged down in the middle a finger's width. Toren could see the river through the crack.

"It'll take a person," said Callie. "On the outside planks. Not on this one." She put her hand flat on the plank beside it, and then on the one on the far side. "Those are sound. They're old, but they're sound. This one's rotten at the beam."

"And a load?" said Toren.

"What load?"

"The carrier." He looked back across the river at the cart. "It has to come over. It can't come over on a back. It's got to come over on something."

Callie looked at the crack.

"Not on that," she said.

She stood up and walked the last two planks to the bank, and stood at the gate.

---

The gate was a frame of iron, as tall as a man and a half and as wide as a cart. Wire was stretched across it, the same rusted wire as the fence, in squares as big as a hand. It hung from two hinges on an iron post on the right. On the left, where it met the other post, there was a chain.

The chain went round the frame of the gate and round the post, twice. Its two ends had been brought together and joined. But they had not been joined with a lock. They were joined with a twist of wire. It was copper, gone green, twisted round and round on itself in a tight thick knot the size of a fist, with the ends tucked in.

Toren looked at it for a long time.

Somebody had stood here, twenty years ago, on the last evening, and pulled the gate shut behind eleven people, and twisted a bit of copper wire through the chain so it would stay. Not locked it. Tied it. As if he had meant to come back and open it again.

"His," said Toren. "The man who ran the crew."

"Maybe," said Dee.

"Gran said he wouldn't let anybody sleep inside." Toren looked at the knot. "He'd not have left it open."

He did not touch it. Callie held the counter to it.

"Twelve," she said. "At the gate. It's fourteen through the wire." She held it out through one of the squares, into the fence, at the length of her arm. "Fourteen."

Then all three of them looked through the wire at Caul Hill.

---

The ground inside the fence was flat for a hundred paces, a wide yard of old concrete gone to cracks and weeds, with gravel in the cracks. There was a squat grey hut beside the gate on the inside, with a door and one window, both black. There were long low sheds along the south side of the yard with their roofs fallen in. There was a thing on four iron legs like a water butt on stilts, very tall, with its side rusted through in a long red wound.

The pipes came out of the river bank on the inside and went across the yard on low concrete blocks. They went to the foot of the hill and up it, side by side, beside a road.

The road went up the hill in two long slants, back and forth, a great Z cut into the side of it. At the top of the Z, on the rim of the flat top, there was the building.

It was long. It was the longest building Toren had ever seen that was not the Sag's own berm. It ran along the rim of the hill from north to south, three rows of windows high, grey, flat-faced, with every window black. It stood on the edge of the flat top like a wall somebody had built to keep the hill in. There was a door in the middle of it, at the top of the road. It was a black square.

And behind it, a long way up, over the flat top of the hill, the sky.

It was bigger this close. At dusk from the rise it had been the size of Callie's thumb at the end of her arm. From here it was the size of a cart wheel. It was not over the building. It was over what was behind the building. Toren could see now that there was something behind it. Past the north end of the long building there was the top of a wall that was not straight. It curved. It was a great round wall, higher than the building, broken off jaggedly at the top, like a pot with its rim knocked off.

The sky was over that. The dark patch hung over the round wall, and the light round its edge thickened and thinned over the broken rim.

"That's not the building," said Toren.

"No," said Dee.

"Gran never said anything about a round one."

"She said the main building," said Callie. "She said inside the main building. If you've got a main one, you've got others." She did not take her eyes off the yard. "Look at the ground."

Toren looked.

The yard was not bare. The concrete had grey on it, in the cracks and along the edges of things. It was not heaped in drifts, the way the wash had been. It lay in lines, thin, like hair that has been combed. Every line went the same way. They went across the yard from the south and the east, from the sheds and the fence, and they came together at the foot of the road and went up it. Toren watched the nearest line, beside the gatehouse.

It slid. A hand's width, toward the hill. It stopped.

He counted without meaning to. Ten. Three.

"It's going up the road," said Callie. "All of it. I can see it on the first bend." She pointed with her chin. "On the top bend. And at the top—" She stopped. "It goes in the door. Some of it. Some goes round the end of the building. Over the top. Toward the round thing."

"Some goes in," said Toren.

"Some goes in the door," said Callie. "Where we're going."

---

Dee turned her back on the fence.

She did it deliberately. She stood with her back to the gate and looked at the two of them, and she took out her book and did not open it.

"Say what she said," she said. "The Director. The narrow thing."

Toren knew it. He had written it at the long table, and on the back of the paper, and Dee had quoted it twice at the fire on the forty-sixth. He said it anyway, because she had asked.

"*You're going for the boxes. The live ones, with the wheel on the plate, in lead, on the cart. Two, three if you can. And you're going to look at that sky and count what comes out of it and write it down. You are not going into it. You are not going looking for whatever's under it. If it comes to you, you do what you have to do and you come home.*"

"*Into it,*" said Dee. "What's *it*?"

Toren looked back through the wire. He looked at the round wall with its broken rim and the dark over it.

"That," he said. "The round one. Whatever's in it. Under the sky."

"And the building?"

"The building's in front of it." He made himself say it slowly, in order, the way Pell said a distance. "The door's at the top of the road, on the east side. The stair's inside it. Gran said down the stair, a door, and then a corridor, and the rooms off the corridor on the left. Eleven of them. She didn't say which way the corridor goes." He looked at the building. "If it goes along the building, north or south, it's under the building all the way. If it goes back into the hill, west, it goes under the building for as long as the building's deep, and then it goes on under the top. Toward the round one."

"Which?"

"I don't know."

"Then we'll count doors," said Callie.

Dee looked at her.

"The building's about thirty paces deep," said Callie. "I can see its end from here, past the north corner. If the corridor goes west, and the rooms are eight paces apart, or ten, then three rooms are under the building. Four at the most. The rest are under the yard behind it." She did not look at the sky. "The rest are nearer to that."

Dee opened the book then, and wrote in it standing up, with the book against her forearm.

"Three doors," she said. "If the boxes are in the first three rooms, they're ours. If they're not, they're under that, and they're hers, and she can decide what to do about them with a Sag held and a machine that can see." She wrote. "Nobody goes past the third door. Not to look. Not for a box. Not for anything."

"And the ash that goes in the door?" said Toren.

Dee shut the book.

"The ash that goes in the door is lying where it's lying," she said. "If it's in the first three rooms, we'll be standing in it. We knew that yesterday. It's why we've got two healers and a counter and a line." She looked at him. "It's not a thing. It's ash, going somewhere. If a thing comes to us, then it's come to us. She said what we do then."

---

They said it all out loud at the pump house, because Dee would not have anything decided that had not been said.

Callie said the ash first. She stood by the cart with her hands open at her sides and said it flat.

"Inside, forty. Five whole, in the frame. The line's sixteen. That's two whole." She looked at Jab's frame, standing against the pump house wall on two flat stones. "Twenty-four to work with. Three jars. Home, three. Twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." And then, because Dee was looking at her: "And two empty in the frame. A and C. And the road jar in the rack, empty. And five vent jars on the cart. And one socket with glass in it."

"Settled," said Dee.

"A Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in you." Callie stopped. "Jab carries none. Jab's the jar."

"Four going in," said Dee. "Not five. Say it."

"Four going in," said Callie. "Two healers." She did the sum. Toren watched her do it with her lips not quite moving. "We priced forty-eight for five people. An hour in, two in the rooms, an hour out. The rooms were thirty." She looked at the hill. "It's not five. It's four. But it's four with two healers, not three, and one of them's got to be the jar and the watch in turns, all the time, and nobody's the reserve." She shook her head. "I can't make it come out better than it is. Twenty-four's four people for half the day, with nothing going wrong."

"Then we don't spend twenty-four today," said Toren.

They looked at him.

"Twelve," he said. "Half. We go in, and we find the stair, and the door, and we find which way the corridor goes. We read the first room. If the boxes are in it, we get them in the carrier and we come out. If they're not, we come out anyway." He heard his own voice. "Twelve spent is twenty-eight left. We come out at twenty-eight whatever we've got. Then we've still got a day."

Dee looked at him for a long moment.

"Twenty-eight's today's line," she said. She wrote it. "Sixteen's the line. Both."

"And the rest," said Toren. "Say them."

They said them. Red, and nobody trapped: everybody out, at once. Red, and a body trapped: the fewest who can do it stay, named. Two Flasks left inside: everybody out. A healer at their floor twice. A healer still warm after the watch. Jab below his floor once.

"One more," said Dee.

She said it to Jab, but she said it for all of them.

"There's two of us," she said. "You and me. If one of us goes down—hurt, or under, or anything—there's nobody to draw the one that's left. Not after. Not ever." She did not look away from him. "So if either of us is lost, for any reason, it's everybody out. That moment. Not when we've finished what we're doing."

"Everybody out," said Jab.

Toren wrote it on the back of the paper under the rest. *If a healer's lost, everybody out.*

Then, under that, the way he had written it on the step of eleven the night before they left:

*Everybody back.*

---

Rook had listened to all of it lying on the canvas with his eyes half shut. Nobody had asked him anything. When they had finished, he opened his eyes.

"And the outside," he said.

Dee turned.

"There's two of us out here," said Rook. "One can't walk and one's hot. We can't come in after you. We've both got that. I'd like it said, now, what we're to do if you don't come out."

"If we're not out by the time the sun's on the gate," said Pell, from the cart, "I'll know. I'll have been counting."

"And then?" said Dee.

Pell looked at Rook. Rook looked at Dee.

"Then I don't come in," said Rook.

"Say it again."

"Then I don't come in," said Rook. He said it flatly, to the stones of the pump house wall. "I sit at the near end of that bridge where I can see the gate. I'm the only one of us who can get to the near end of the bridge, and I can get there lying down if I have to. If something comes out of that gate that isn't you, I shout. If it's you, I count you." He was quiet a moment. "If it's dark and it's nothing, I don't come in."

Dee knelt down beside him on the gravel.

"It's the Director's word," she said.

"It's yours," said Rook. "I know whose it is." He shut his eyes. "Go on. You've a day."

---

Then there was the carrier.

It stood in the cart behind the three home jars, on its straw, where it had ridden for seven days. It was the size of a small chest. It was made of the depot's lead in sheets, folded and seamed, grey and dull, with a lid that sat down into a groove round the top and two iron handles, one at each end. It was so heavy empty that it had taken Pell and Toren and Jab together to set it on the cart in the north yard, and Toren had had only one arm to do it with even then.

"It doesn't go on anybody's back," said Toren.

"No," said Pell.

"And the cart can't cross the bridge."

"No," said Pell.

He was sitting on the boards with his leg out. He looked at the carrier, and at the bridge, and at the tailboard of the cart, which was hanging on its chains at the back with the two long pieces of its rail gone. Those two pieces were on his leg.

"Then it drags," he said.

He told them how. He told them the way he had told Toren where to cut the traces: once, slowly, with his finger on each thing.

The tailboard came off its chains. It was a single thick board, as long as a man was tall and as wide as the carrier with a hand to spare on each side. They set it on the gravel. They got the carrier down onto it off the back of the cart, Jab and Callie and Toren, sliding it on two poles of wrack wood with Pell saying *easy* and *easy* and *now*. They lashed it to the board with rope through both handles, crossed under the board and back, and pulled tight.

"Two ropes," said Pell. "One at the front to pull. One at the back to hold." He looked at Toren. "On a slope, down or up, there's a rope round something that doesn't move, and you pay it out. And nobody stands below it. Not ever. Not for a breath."

"Below it," said Toren.

"Downhill of it. If it's on a slope and you're below it, and it runs, you're under a box of lead." Pell put his hand on the edge of the tailboard. "If it runs, let it run. Lead's cheaper than you. There's more lead in the world than there is of any of you."

Callie had opened the lid. She was looking in.

"There's something in it," she said.

She lifted it out. It was a pair of tongs. They were iron, a yard and a half long, with two flat jaws at one end and two ring handles at the other, and they were wrapped in a piece of sacking with a label tied to them. The label was in a hand Toren did not know, square and neat. *For the boxes. Not your hands.*

"The shed," said Dee. "The old woman." She looked at the tongs. "She never said."

"She never said anything," said Callie.

She put them back in the carrier, and put the lid in its groove.

They went in at noon.

The wind had not come all morning. The grey lay still in the yard, in its combed lines. Rook said, from the canvas, "Go while it's down," and nobody argued with that either.

They ate first, at the pump house, bread and the last of the cold meat. They drank as much as they could hold of the boiled water that was left, and then a little from the full skin. Nothing to drink inside the fence. They went to the river below the pump house one at a time and made water, because Dee said so, and because nobody sits down inside the fence.

Dee opened the first jar.

She did it at the pump house, not at the gate. She lifted it out of Jab's frame by the neck, one of the five, and pulled the stopper, and looked in, and stoppered it again loosely so it would come out with a thumb.

"One," said Callie. "Open. Eight in it. Four whole."

"Four whole," said Dee, and wrote it.

They put two vent jars in a canvas bag, and Toren put the strap of the bag over his good shoulder. Callie had the hook and the counter. Dee had the glass and the book and the open jar in the crook of her arm. Jab had the frame, with four full jars in it and the two empties and the broken socket, and his knife not on his fist. The cart's two lanterns hung from Toren's belt and Callie's, unlit, with a twist of tallow wick each and a box of lights in Dee's bag.

Senna had said it went down stairs, where the walls went thick. There would be no windows.

---

Rook had got himself to the near end of the bridge.

Toren did not see him go. He only saw him there, when they came down the gravel with the sledge. He was sitting on the ground at the foot of the first post with his back against it and his coat over his shoulders. His face was wet again. He was where he could see the whole length of the bridge and the gate at the far end.

"You're off," said Dee, as she passed him.

"I'm sitting," said Rook. "Sitting's allowed."

They said the rules at the bridge end, because Senna's crew had said them at the gate every morning, like a grace. Nothing to eat inside the fence. Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, coat off, shake it, wash at the river. Never carry one of the boxes.

"Pull the door," said Toren, at the end.

Pell, on the cart, said it after him. So did Rook, from the post.

The sledge went onto the bridge easily.

Toren had the front rope over his good shoulder and Callie had it behind him, both of them pulling. Jab walked on the outside plank with the frame. Dee walked last, with the back rope loose in her hand. The tailboard slid on the old planks with a long dry sound, and the carrier sat on it without moving.

"That's the thing about a board," Pell called from the bank. "A board doesn't care."

At the far end Callie said, "Stop."

The cracked plank was the middle one. The sledge was as wide as all three planks together. Its front end had come up to the crack and was lying across it, over the sagging middle and onto the two sound planks on either side.

"It'll carry it on the sound ones," said Toren. "The board's wide."

"It'll carry it till the middle of the load comes over the crack," said Callie. "Then it all comes down on the middle. The middle's rotten at the beam."

She crouched at the edge of the bridge and looked down between the planks, at the post and the cross-beam and the water.

"There's stone under," she said. "The post's sitting on a big flat stone in the river. A foot under the water. Where the current's white on it."

Toren looked. He saw it. It was pale and square under the running water, beside the post, like a step.

"Set," he said.

He put the foot of the strut down through the water onto the stone beside the post, and let it come up at its slant under the middle plank, beside the crack. It came up thin and hard through the river and met the underside of the plank. He was soft behind it. He did not lock.

"Pull," he said.

Callie pulled. Jab put down the frame's weight against the rail and took the front rope with one hand. The sledge slid. The middle of the carrier came over the crack, and the weight came down onto the plank, and down the strut into the stone in the river. It did not come up into him. His knees gave a little and took it. He counted breaths.

Three. Four.

The back end of the sledge came off the crack and onto the bank.

He let the strut go. At the end. Not after.

"Sliding," called Pell, across the river, a breath late. Somebody laughed. It was Rook.

---

Toren stood at the gate with the green knot of wire in front of him.

He untwisted it one-handed. It took a long time. The wire was stiff with twenty years, and it did not want to come. He did it a turn at a time, with the ends of his fingers, and Callie held the chain for him so it did not swing. When the last turn came loose, he held the wire in his hand for a moment. It was only a bit of copper wire.

He put it in his pocket. He did not know why. Then he took it out again and hung it on the wire of the fence, beside the gate, where it would be when they came out.

The chain came off the post.

He put his hand on the frame of the gate and pulled. It did not open. He pushed. It went. It swung inward on its hinges, slowly, with a long scrape of iron on the concrete, and stood open.

Behind them, across the river, Pell was writing. Toren could see his head bent over the paper on his knee.

Toren took the signed paper out of his coat and the pencil. He wrote on the back of it, under the night before, standing at the open gate with the paper against the iron.

*47th. The east gate. Noon. Out: 4.*

He wrote *In:* under it.

He looked at the word for a moment. Then he folded the paper with nothing after it, and put it back in his coat, beside the plate.

"Twenty-four," said Callie, beside him. "Twelve today."

"Twelve today," said Toren. He picked up the front rope. "Everybody back."

They pulled the sledge through the gate into Caul Hill.

# Chapter 34 — Callie: The Yellow Stair

Inside the fence the ground was a floor.

Callie knew it the moment she was through the gate. It was not a road any more, and not a field. Everything she looked at, she looked at the way she had looked at the concrete of the pen: where the dust lay, where the water had gone, which way the grey was going, and what was clean. She had read the pen for a quarter of an hour on her knees with a counter. She would read this with the sledge rope on her shoulder, walking.

"Stop," she said, three paces in.

They stopped. Toren had the front rope beside her. Jab stood with the frame on the outside of the sledge, and Miss Wren was behind with the back rope and the glass.

The yard was concrete, cracked into slabs, with weeds in the cracks. The combed lines of grey ran across it from the south and the east toward the foot of the road. They were thin, most of them no wider than a finger. But they were everywhere. There was no straight way from the gate to the road that did not cross forty of them.

"We'll drag the board over them," said Toren.

"We'll drag the board through them," said Callie. "And then it'll have grey on its bottom all the way up the hill, and down the stair, and into the room. And back." She looked along the yard. "And we'll walk in them. And it'll be on our boots."

"Then where?"

She looked for the clean way. She did it the way she had read the pen, one slab at a time.

The pipes came out of the river bank on her right and went across the yard to the foot of the hill, side by side, on their concrete blocks. The blocks were each the size of a crate, one every five paces. The pipes lay in the saddles on top of them, rusted, and between the blocks there was the gap under the pipes where the concrete was in shadow.

The combed lines of grey went under the pipes, through the gaps between the blocks. But along the north side of the blocks, in the lee of them, the concrete was bare. The lines went round the end of each block and under, and on the north side of each one there was a strip a pace wide where the grey had not settled, because the lines had somewhere else to be.

"Along the pipes," said Callie. "On the north side. Close to the blocks. It's clean there. Mostly." She took a step and held out the counter and waited ten breaths. "Eighteen. At the first block. It was twenty-four in the middle."

"Along the pipes," said Miss Wren.

They went along the pipes.

---

It was a hundred paces to the foot of the road. Callie read the counter every ten breaths and said it out loud. Eighteen. Twenty. Nineteen. Twenty-two, where a line of grey came round the end of a block and lay across their way, and she made them lift the front of the board over it.

At the gatehouse she stopped for a breath and looked in at the window.

It was dark inside. There was a floor of concrete with a stove on it, cold and rusted. There were two old bottles on their sides by the stove, and a black ring on the floor where somebody had made a fire that was not in the stove. On the back wall, in white chalk, there were marks. They were strokes, in a row, the kind you make to count days. She counted them without meaning to.

Eleven. And a twelfth, begun, a short stroke that went down half as far as the others and stopped.

"Twenty-two," she said, holding the counter to the window. "At the hut."

She did not say anything about the strokes. She thought about two men who had slept inside on the second night because they were drunk, and been sick in the morning, and thought they had got away with it. She thought about somebody on the last morning picking up the chalk to make a twelfth stroke. They had stopped halfway down, because there was not going to be a twelfth day.

"Callie," said Miss Wren.

"Twenty-two," said Callie again, and went on.

---

The road was the hard part.

It went up the side of the hill in two long slants, the way she had seen it from the gate. It was concrete too, and it had been good concrete once, wide enough for a lorry. The pipes went up beside it on the inside, on their blocks, and the lines of grey went up the middle of it like the ruts on the old north road.

The board would not go up it on its own. Toren and Callie pulled, with the front rope over their shoulders, leaning into it. Jab walked on the inside beside the carrier with his hand not on it. Miss Wren walked behind, not below. Every twenty paces Toren said "Turn," and Callie took the end of the front rope round the nearest pipe block, once, the way Pell had said, and they stood and breathed with the rope holding the weight instead of them.

"Round something that doesn't move," said Toren, at the third one.

"You don't have to say it every time."

"I do," said Toren. "Or I'll forget, at the one that matters."

At the first bend the road had gone.

Not all of it. The outside half had slumped. Something under it had washed out, and the concrete had cracked along the middle of the road and the outer half had tilted down the hill like a lid half off a box. The inside half, against the pipes, was still flat. There was a pile of grey in the corner of the bend, on the inside, heaped against the foot of the pipe blocks where the lines came round and bunched up. It was the first thing like a drift she had seen inside the fence.

"Forty," said Callie, from four paces off. "At the heap." She looked at the slumped half. "The inside's good. The outside's hollow. You can see the crack. There's nothing under it but air."

"Then between," said Miss Wren. "Not the heap. Not the hollow."

There was a pace and a half between the heap and the crack. The board was a pace wide.

They took it through. Toren went first with the rope. Callie went on the heap side, walking with her left boot a hand's width from the grey and her eyes on the crack. The board came round the bend with its outside edge on the crack and its inside edge a finger from the heap, and did not touch either.

"Good," said Miss Wren, behind them, and it was the first time she had said it all day.

---

At the top of the second slant Miss Wren stopped them for the halt.

It was twenty turns by the glass since the gate. They stood on the road below the last bend with the rope round a pipe block, and the wind did not come, and the grey lay still in its lines. Miss Wren put her back to the hill and made them stand in a row in front of her, not sit, and she drew them.

She did it off the open jar, with the mouth of it against her left palm and her right hand flat on each chest in turn. Toren first. Then Callie. Then Jab, who stood with the frame on and his hands out from his sides and watched her do it.

"Busy," said Miss Wren to herself, on Toren. And, after a while, "Clean."

It was a little, on all of them. It was what the yard had given them and the heap at the bend. She drew it and held it high and vented it into the concrete of the road, which was the road's, not hers. Then she held her own hands out to Jab, palms up.

He held his palm over them, an inch off. "Right," he said. "A bit." And took it.

"Say it," said Miss Wren.

Callie weighed the jar on her palm, the way she weighed everything now.

"A Handful gone," she said. "And a bit. For the four of us and the vent." She gave it back. "Six and three quarters in it."

"Six and three quarters," said Miss Wren. She turned the glass. "Twenty more, and we'll be down the stair."

---

The building was bigger than it had looked from the gate.

It stood across the top of the road like a cliff. It went up three rows of windows high, flat and grey, with long streaks of rust running down from the window frames like old tears. Every window was black. Some of them had glass still in them and most did not. The door at the top of the road was a pair of doors that were not there any more. There was only a square of dark as high as two men, with a step in front of it and the hinges hanging off the frame.

The grey went in at the door.

Callie stood on the step with the counter out and watched it. The combed lines came up the road and across the step and over the edge of it, and went in, into the dark, all of them. Ten breaths, and they slid a hand's width over the edge of the step. Three, and they lay still.

"Thirty-eight," she said. "At the door."

She did not go in yet. She stepped back off the step and looked up.

From here she could see past the north end of the building. The round wall stood up behind it, very near now, higher than the building, grey. It was not a wall so much as a great drum, round, with its top broken off in jags. And over the drum, over the whole of the top of the hill, the sky.

It was not the size of a cart wheel now. It was too big to be any size. It was over them. Its edge was a long way up and a long way round, like the edge of a cloud when you are standing under the middle of it. She could see the clouds going into the edge of it on one side and coming out on the other, not quite right. And round the edge, all the way round, the light the colour of the bottom of a pond, thickening, and thinning.

She had promised herself she would not look at it for long. She looked at it now on purpose, because somebody had to, and it was the kind of looking she was good at.

"I'm counting it," she said. "For the paper."

Toren had the paper out already.

The edge thickened. She counted. Ten. It thinned. Three.

Ten. It thickened. And at the end of the ten, as it began to thin, something came out of the bottom of it.

It came out of the underside of the dark, over the drum, and fell. It was not a thing. It was grey. It was a long thin fall of grey, like the rain you see coming down out of the bottom of a cloud a long way off, when it is raining over there and not here. It came down out of the sky in a veil and went down behind the broken rim of the drum, and was gone.

"Something came out," said Callie.

Toren's pencil stopped.

"A fall of grey," said Callie. "Out of the bottom of it. At the three. Into the round one." She did not stop looking. "One."

She watched ten more. Twenty more. The edge thickened and thinned. Nothing fell.

"One," she said. "In twelve counts. Say that. I'll count it again when we come out."

Toren wrote it. She heard the pencil. *Top of the road. The sky: ten and three. 1 fall of grey out of the bottom, into the round wall, at the three. In 12.*

"That's what comes out of it," said Toren quietly. "She asked."

"That's what came out of it while I was looking," said Callie.

Inside it was dark after ten paces.

There was a hall first, with light from the door lying across the floor in a long pale square. The floor was grey, not with lines here but all over, a skin of dust with the lines drawn on it. There was a long counter across one side with nothing on it. There was a sign on the wall above it, the letters gone to nothing, and in the middle of the sign the mark, the wheel with four spokes and a bar under it. It was the mark on Toren's plate.

Toren stopped and looked at it. Then he touched his coat, the side the plate was on, and went on.

On the left of the hall a corridor went away south along the length of the building. It was wide and dark and it had doors down both sides of it.

The grey lines went across the hall and into it, and along it.

"Lanterns," said Miss Wren.

She lit them from the box of lights in her bag. The tallow caught slowly, and the light came up yellow and small, and the corridor came out of the dark round them in a circle ten paces wide. It was concrete, painted a colour that had once been green, with a line of grey dust along the foot of each wall and the combed lines down the middle of the floor.

"Husks come at a light," said Toren.

"Husks come at a light," said Miss Wren. "And we can't see without one. That's two things that are true." She hung one lantern from Toren's belt and gave the other to Jab, who hung it from the frame. "If they come, they come at the lantern. I'd rather they came at the lantern than at somebody's hands."

They went along the corridor.

Ten paces along it, on the left, there was a gap in the wall. It was wide, wider than a door. There was a rail at the edge of it.

The rail was iron. Somebody had painted it yellow a long time ago. Most of the paint had gone, flaked off in patches the size of fingernails, and what was left was pale and dirty. But it was yellow. It was the yellow of the line on the floor of the shed at Meridian, the line Tonk had been rolled across on his bed, and Callie's mother on hers, and Senna on hers every eleven days.

The stair went down beside it, into the dark.

Toren stood at the top of it with the lantern on his belt and his hand on the yellow rail. He did not say anything for a while.

"She's been looking at that line for five weeks," he said.

"I know," said Callie.

It was a long stair.

It went down in a straight flight along the right-hand wall, thirty steps, and turned on a landing, and went down again the other way, twenty more. The walls got thicker as they went down. Callie could see it at the landing, where the corner of the wall was cut back, and the concrete was as deep as her arm was long.

Beside the steps there was a slope.

She saw it at the top and did not understand it at first. It was a smooth ramp of concrete a pace wide, running down beside the steps against the wall, all the way to the landing, and then on down beside the second flight. There was an iron ring set in the wall at the top of it, as thick as her thumb.

"For barrows," said Toren. "For bringing things up. Things you don't carry."

"For wire," said Callie. "Hundreds of rolls of it. For eleven days."

The grey lines went down the stair. They went down the steps and down the ramp, a hand's width at a time, into the dark below the landing.

They took the sledge down the ramp on the back rope.

Toren put the rope through the iron ring at the top and took the end of it round his waist, over his good hip. Callie took it behind him. Miss Wren and Jab stayed at the top, above the sledge, not below it, with the lantern, until it was down. That was the rule. Nobody below a load on a slope.

The board went down the ramp slowly, a pace at a time, with the rope paying out through the ring and round Toren's waist and through Callie's hands. The carrier sat on it. At the landing it stopped against the turn of the wall with a soft knock, and they went down the steps beside it and did it again from a second ring on the landing wall.

"Fifty," said Callie, at the landing. And at the bottom, "Fifty-six."

The door was there.

It stood across the bottom of the stair in a square of concrete wall. The last of the steps came down to a flat place four paces across, and the ramp ended there too, and on the far side of the flat place, facing them, was the door.

It was steel. It had been painted grey once and was now the colour of a knife that has been left out. It was set in a steel frame in the concrete. In the middle of it, at the height of Toren's face, the wheel was stamped into the steel, big, as wide as a hand, with its four spokes and its bar. Below the wheel there was a handle. It was a bar of iron as long as a forearm, bolted across the door at both ends.

Against the foot of the door there was a heap of grey.

It had come down the stair in its lines, and down the ramp, and across the flat place, and met the door, and stopped. It had heaped up against the bottom of the steel the way snow heaps against a shut door in a yard, a hand deep at the door and thinning out toward the stair. As she watched, the lines coming down the stair slid a hand's width into the heap. And at the very bottom of the door, where the steel did not quite meet the concrete, there was a crack no wider than a knife blade, and the grey was going under it. Very thin. In threads. Ten, and three.

"It's been getting under that for twenty years," said Miss Wren quietly.

Nobody went near the door.

Callie understood that they were all doing the same thing. They were all standing at the bottom of the stair in the lantern light, looking at the steel, and every one of them was thinking of an old woman on a pillow. *Every man on that crew put his shoulder to it on the first morning and it didn't move. We stood at the bottom of the stair shoving at it for a quarter of an hour, eleven of us, in the dark.*

"Fifty-eight," said Callie. "At the heap."

"Pull it," said Toren.

He said it quietly, to the door.

He put his good hand on the iron bar.

Callie put the point of the hook through the gap between the bar and the steel, from the side, and took the conduit in both hands. Jab came and put his hand on the bar beside Toren's, and did not take the frame off, and set his feet.

"On three," said Toren. "Not the three. My three."

Jab made a sound that might have been a laugh.

"One," said Toren. "Two. Three."

They pulled.

"Again," said Toren. "It sticks. She said it sticks."

They pulled again. Toren leaned back on the bar with his whole weight, and Jab with his, and Callie with hers on the hook. And the door came. It did not come the way a door comes. It came the way the slide had come on the box in the pen, all at once, with a shriek of steel on steel that went up the stair and round the landing and up and up into the building over their heads. It swung toward them a hand's width, and stuck, and came again. The heap of grey at its foot went over as the bottom of the door swept through it, and slid, and spilled into the dark on the other side.

The door stood open toward them, as wide as a man.

"About thirty breaths," said Miss Wren. "From *pull it*."

"Thirty breaths," he said.

"Write it," said Miss Wren. "Tonight. She'll want it."

Beyond the door there was the long corridor.

Callie knew it was the one, because it was long. The lantern on Toren's belt went into it and did not find the end. It was lower than the corridor upstairs, and narrower, three paces wide, with a concrete floor and concrete walls and a concrete ceiling not much higher than Toren could have reached with his good arm. It went straight away from them into the dark.

West.

She had worked it out before she had finished looking. The stair had come down along the building's east wall and turned, and the door faced back the way they had come in. The corridor went away from it into the hill. Under the building. And then on, under the top, toward the drum.

"West," she said.

"West," said Toren. "Three doors."

On the left of the corridor, a little way along, there was a door. It was open, a black oblong in the concrete. Past it, further along, the lantern found another one. And further, at the very edge of the light, the dark shape of a third.

On the right-hand wall there were no doors. There was a tray.

It ran along the wall high up, a hand below the ceiling, as far as the light went. It was steel, like a long open trough the width of two hands, held to the wall on iron brackets. It was the kind of thing wire had lain in, bundles of it, thick as an arm. Senna's crew had come down this corridor and cut the copper out of it, and rolled it, and taken it up the stair on the ramp.

There was grey in it now.

Callie thought at first it was dust. It lay in the tray all along, a thick soft line of grey, the colour of the dust on the floor. It was the dust of twenty years, fallen into a trough.

"Callie," said Miss Wren. "The floor."

She read the floor. The grey that had spilled through the door when it swept open was spreading out on the concrete in threads, and the threads were going west. They went along the floor at the foot of the right-hand wall, under the tray. And then they went up the wall.

They went up the wall.

She watched them do it. Thin grey threads, finer than hair, going up the bare concrete from the floor to the tray a hand's width at a time, as if the wall were a floor. Ten, and they climbed. Three, and they stopped, stuck to the wall.

They went into the tray.

She lifted the counter and held it out toward the tray at the length of her arm and waited ten breaths.

"Seventy," she said. "At the tray. It's sixty on the floor."

And while she was waiting, while she was counting the ten, she saw the grey in the tray move.

It was not dust. The whole long soft line of it in the trough moved, all together, the way the threads moved. It slid west along the tray a hand's width. Ten breaths. It stopped. Three.

It was not dust lying in a trough. It was a rope. It was a rope of grey as thick as her wrist, lying in the tray the whole length of the corridor, and the threads were going into it off the floor and the wall. And the whole rope was going west.

"Toren," she said.

"I see it."

"It's not lying there," said Callie. "It's going. It's all going. The floor's going into it, and it's going—"

"West," said Jab.

He said it very quietly. He had not said anything since the door. He was standing behind them with the frame on his back and the lantern hanging from it and his hand flat on his coat over the left side. He was not looking at the rope. He had his eyes half shut.

"It's the same," he said. "It's the ten and three. It's the same as the wash." He opened his eyes. "It's pulling harder here."

The first door was twelve paces along on the left. They went to it along the left-hand wall, the side away from the tray, with Callie in front reading the floor, and the sledge on its board scraping behind them over the threads. There was nothing else to do with it.

Over the doorway, on the right-hand wall of the corridor opposite, there was a place where the tray sent a branch across. It went across the ceiling in a smaller trough and through the wall above the door, into the room, through a square hole in the concrete the size of a hand. The rope went with it. A thinner rope, like a branch off a trunk, went across the ceiling in the small trough and in through the hole.

Callie stood in the doorway and held the lantern up.

The room was not large. It was six paces deep and five across, and the air in it was colder than the corridor. On the right-hand wall there were big grey cabinets, as tall as a man and twice as wide, with doors on their fronts, standing in a row. On the left-hand wall there were racks.

They were steel frames, from the floor to the ceiling, the way Senna had said. But their backs had been torn off. She could see the ends of torn metal sticking out at the edges where somebody had levered the backs away from the frames to get at what was behind them. # Chapter 35 — Jab: A Hole in It

It came at the lantern again.

Jab stood in the doorway with the frame on his back and the lantern hanging from it, and the husk came along the foot of the racks toward him the way the first one had. It had no face to come with. It came anyway, low, grey on grey, with the dust going up off the boxes where it passed them.

Toren killed it again.

He did it the same way, because it had worked. The lit heel, low, hooking, the drag round against the racks, and the point. It came apart. The grey fell in a heap on the floor a pace from the corner.

"Don't," said Dee.

She said it a breath too late, and she knew it. Jab could hear that she knew it.

"Don't kill it," she said. "Not again. Every time you kill it, it's on the floor. And then it's in the air." She did not take her eyes off the heap. "Callie."

"A hundred and ten," said Callie. "At the door."

Jab watched the heap.

He did not need to count. He counted anyway. It was the thing he did with his mouth shut, the way Callie read ground and Toren counted people. Ten breaths, and the heap drew out into threads, all of them at once, and went along the floor into the strands at the foot of the racks. It went along the strands into the corner. The floor was bare. Three breaths. The corner swelled.

He felt it swell.

That was the thing he had not expected. He had felt the pull all afternoon, since the stair. It had been the same pull he had felt with his palm on the stone at the mouth of the cut, only nearer, and harder: flat, open, patient, pulling at everything loose. Pulling past him. It had nobody in it. It pulled for ten, and stopped.

But at the three it did something else.

It pushed.

He felt it the way he felt a vent. It was the same thing. It was a palm going flat on a floor and something going out of it, three breaths, into somewhere that was not the palm. Only this went out into the corner, into the heap. And the heap stood up.

"It's venting," said Jab.

They looked at him. The husk was up, and turning.

"At the three," he said. "It draws for ten, and then it vents, for three. Like we do. Only it doesn't vent into the floor to be rid of it. It vents into the heap. On purpose." He heard his own voice. "It vents where it wants a thing. And the thing stands up."

The husk came.

---

Toren did not kill it the third time.

He stood in front of Jab in the doorway with the spike dark on his fist. When the husk came along the racks at them, he did not light the heel. He put the foot of a strut down on the concrete of the floor between him and the husk, at a slant, leaning away from them, the way you lean a plank against a door you want to keep shut.

It came up out of the floor thin and pale and hard, as high as his chest.

The husk walked into it.

It hit the strut the way a husk hits anything, with all of itself, without looking. The strut took it. It did not move. The husk stood against the pale slant of it, pushing, and the strut went down into the floor and did not come up into Toren, because he was soft behind it.

"Holding," said Toren.

"How long?"

"I don't know. It's not a wheel. It's pushing. It keeps pushing." His voice was tight. "Every breath it's on it, I pay."

"Then we've got a strut's worth of time," said Dee, "and I'd like to spend it thinking."

Callie had the counter out at the corner.

"The corner's not as big," she said. "The heap. It's smaller than it was before the first one stood up. It's made two husks out of it, and one's walked back in, and one's on the strut." She moved the counter along the racks. "The strands go into it. All of them. From the boxes. From the rope. That's what feeds it."

"Then cut it," said Toren.

He said it through his teeth, to the husk.

"Cut the strand," he said. "Between the corner and the rest. If it can't get to the heap, it can't send anything to the heap."

Dee looked at the strands at the foot of the racks, running into the corner like roots.

"Callie," she said. "Take the strut."

It was the first time Jab had seen Callie take a hold off Toren.

He had heard about it. They had done it in the yard with the rack, and in the pen, and they had the words for it. But he had not seen it. He saw it now, in the doorway of a room under a hill, with a husk pushing on the end of it.

"Taking," said Callie.

She put the back of the hook's curve down on the concrete at the foot of the strut, still, and he saw it go pale.

"Yours," said Toren.

"Mine."

He put the lit hook down across the strands and dragged it through them, all of them, in one long pull, the way you cut through the cords of a bale.

He felt it find his hand. He felt the rope feel the place where something was drawing on it, lifting, taking, the way a hole takes. It pushed into the place. It pushed into his palm.

It was warm. It was all warm, the whole of it, the hill and the grit and the loose grey all together, and it came up into his palm like hot water up a pump. The clean cold from the jar came into his left hand and met it and held it off his wrist. For a breath. For two.

He understood it then. He understood it all at once, the way you understand you have stepped off the edge of a bank.

It thought he was a hole in it.

It thought he was a wound, and it was mending him. It was doing to him what it did to the cut in the strands and the heap in the corner. It had felt a place where something was being taken away, and it was pouring into the place to fill it. And behind the rope there was the corridor, and the tray, and the stair, and the yard. The whole of the hill. Everything lying where it fell.

It would never stop. There was nobody in it to stop.

---

The warm went into his wrist.

He felt it go past the heel of his hand, where the clean cold had been holding it. It went up into the wrist and into the forearm. It was not hot sand. It was hot water. It went up his arm the way water goes up a wick.

He could not take his hand off.

He could. He knew he could. His hand was only flat on a rope of grey on a wall. There was nothing holding it there but him. But his palm was drawing, and the thing under it was pouring, and every part of him that had ever put a hand on Tonk wanted to keep drawing. It was a body that needed everything, and there was more of it, and more, and he could take it. If he kept his hand there he could take all of it. It was what he was for.

It was like Tonk. It was exactly like Tonk. It was the frame on his back on the road, and the fever in the night, and the bad leg, and a small hot body that needed every part of him, every day, for eighteen days, and more. He had given it every part. He had not minded. He had not known there was an end to him until the day they lifted his brother off his back at the gate, and he had stood in the yard with his hands empty and not known what they were for.

He could not remember his own floor.

He looked for it. He went down to look, the way Dee had taught him, and there was warm water all the way down, and he could not find where he stopped.

*Don't be asleep.*

It was Tonk's voice. It was not Tonk. It was a thing Tonk had said to him, with a wooden fish in his fist and his face furious. *Don't eat inside. Bring Little back. And don't be asleep.*

He was going to sleep. That was what it was. He was going somewhere warm and wide with no floor in it, and he was going to stay there, drawing, while the whole hill poured into his hand.

"Off," said Dee.

He heard it. He did not move.

"*Off*," said Dee.

She took his wrist.

She did not ask him again. She put her own hand round his right wrist, hard, the whole of her hand, and pulled. His palm came off the rope with a small dry sound, like a strip of cloth off a wound.

The warm stopped coming.

It stopped at once, the way water stops when you take the jug away. But what had come was in him. It was in his palm and his wrist and his arm to the elbow, hot, all the way through. It did not hurt. It was worse than hurting. It was full.

"Vent," said Dee. "Into the jar. Now. Toren—"

Toren had the vent jar open. Jab put his right palm on its mouth and let it go.

It went. Some of it went. It went out of his palm into the glass, warm and gritty, and he held his hand on the jar mouth for three breaths, and five, and ten, and it kept going. The counter on Callie's hip was going faster than it had all afternoon. And his arm was still warm, all the way up. The palm was hot.

"Off the jar," said Dee. "Give me the arm."

She did not wait for him to give it. She took his forearm in both her hands and put her palms flat on it, one above the wrist and one below the elbow, and he felt her find it. She drew.

He knelt. He did not mean to. His knees did it, the way Callie's legs had sat her down at the mouth of the cut. He was on his knees on the floor inside the fence, and Dee was crouching over him with her hands flat on his arm, and he felt her take the warm out of him a handful at a time, and turn, and vent, and come back.

"Callie," said Dee. "Jar."

Callie had it. She held the open jar against the back of Dee's hand so Dee could draw off it without letting go of him. Jab felt the clean cold come through Dee into his arm. It was a long way off.

"Busy," said Dee, to herself. "Busy. Busy." And after a long time, "Less."

He looked at his palm.

It was white. Not all of it. There was a place on the heel of it, where he had pressed it flattest against the rope, the size of a coin. It was white, and shiny, like skin that has been scalded. When he put his thumb on it he could not feel his thumb.

"Busy?" said Dee. She had seen him look. She put her palm over the white place, an inch off, and shut her eyes.

He knew before she said it. He could feel it himself, now that the arm was coming down. The heel of his palm was not busy. It was done. It was the old leaning wall. It had happened while his hand was on the rope, and it had fixed.

"It's done," said Dee. She did not open her eyes. "That's in the done part. I can't draw it."

"I know," said Jab.

"The rest's coming out." She moved her hand to his wrist. "The rest'll come. That won't."

He knelt on the floor and let her work. He did not look at the white place again.

"Jar," said Callie.

She said it before it was dry. Jab heard her say it, and heard that it was his word.

"That's one," said Dee. She did not stop drawing. "Open two."

Callie reached over Jab's shoulder to the frame on his back. He felt her hand on the top row, feeling for one of the full ones and not the empties. She lifted it out by the neck. He heard the stopper come out.

"Two," said Callie. "Open. Three whole."

"Say the one."

"The first's done," said Callie. "It went on the four of us, and the yard, and the room. And Jab's lift. And that." She held the new jar against the back of Dee's hand. "Eight out of the first. We're at thirty-two, and two open."

"Twenty-eight's today," said Dee.

"I know."

"Four more, and we're at today's line."

"I *know*, Miss Wren."

Dee took her hands off Jab's arm and sat back on her heels. He felt her go. He felt the cold of her palms leave his skin. His arm was warm still, from the elbow down, but it was the warm of a bruise, not the warm of water. His palm was white at the heel.

"Your floor," said Dee.

He went down to look, and it was there. He could find it now. A finger, where it had always been. And above it, his own, a hand. Less than a hand.

"It's there," he said.

"I know it's there," said Dee. "I was looking for it the whole time. I'd not have stopped till I found it." Her voice was quite steady. "Stand up, Jab. Nobody sits down inside the fence. That goes for knees."

He stood up.

The husk was still on Toren's strut. It had never stopped pushing. Toren was standing behind the strut with his face grey and wet and his teeth together, soft, paying.

"Toren," said Dee. "How long?"

"Since he put his hand on it," said Toren. "The whole of that."

Jab stood in the room with his hand hanging at his side and looked at the rope on the wall.

It had not changed. It came down beside the door frame, as thick as two fingers, and crawled west into the room, ten and three. There was nothing on it to show where his hand had been.

He thought about what it had done.

It had not wanted him. There was nothing in it to want anything. It had felt a hole, and it had poured into the hole, the way it poured into the cut in the strands and the corner. It had done it to him because he was drawing. Because he was a place where something was being taken away. It would have poured into him until there was no more of him to pour into.

It had no floor. That was all it was. It was a thing that drew, and vented where there was a hole, and had no floor, and nobody to find the floor for it. It would do it until everything on the hill was in it.

And he had nearly done the same.

He did not know how to think that. He stood with it. He had put his hand on the rope because it was what he did, and it had been exactly like Tonk, and he had wanted to keep his hand there. He had wanted to take all of it. He could not find where he stopped. Dee had had to find it for him.

He put his left hand on his coat, over the left side, and felt the small hard shape of the fish through the cloth. The body. The tail. The place where the fin had been.

*Bring Little back.*

He would. He had been nearly asleep, and he was not now.

"It's got no floor," he said.

Dee looked at him.

"That's what's wrong with it," said Jab. "It draws and it vents, and it doesn't stop, because there's nobody to say *off*." He looked at the white place on his palm. "I'd not have stopped either."

"No," said Dee. "You'd not." She was quiet a moment. "That's why there's two of us."

"Miss Wren," said Callie.

She was in the doorway with the counter held out into the corridor, and her voice was flat. It was the voice she used for a number she did not like.

"The rope," she said. "In the corridor. It's getting fatter."

Jab went to the door. He went past Toren and the husk and the strut, and stood beside Callie in the doorway, and looked along the corridor to the west, where the lantern light went and stopped.

The tray ran along the right-hand wall into the dark. The rope lay in it, grey, crawling. It had been as thick as a wrist.

It was thicker. It was as thick as a forearm, and it filled the tray, and the tray was bowed down between its iron brackets under the weight of it. And it was getting thicker from the west. He could see it. Further along, at the edge of the lantern light, past the second door, the rope in the tray was as thick as a leg. It bulged out over the edges of the trough. The bracket under it had bent.

And it was not crawling west any more.

It was coming east.

Not the grey in it. The grey in it was still going west, ten and three, as it had all afternoon. But the rope itself was getting thicker from the west end, and the thickness was coming along it toward them. It came the way a swallowed thing goes down the neck of a snake.

Something was coming along the rope.

Jab felt it before he saw it. He felt the pull change. It had been flat all afternoon, and far off, and patient. Now it was near. It was very near, and it was big, and it had nobody in it at all. It was like standing next to a mill wheel you cannot see, in the dark, and feeling the air move.

At the very edge of the light, past the third door, the dark moved.

There was a heap of grey on the floor of the corridor there. It had not been there before. It was higher than the floor, higher than a man's knee, higher than his waist. It lay across the corridor from wall to wall, and the rope in the tray went down into it from above. It was coming toward them. It was not walking. It had nothing to walk with. It was pulling itself along its own rope, the way a man pulls himself along a rope hand over hand, only there were no hands. The grey went forward and the heap came after it.

At the three, in the middle of the heap, something opened.

It was a line of light. It was the colour of the edge of the sky, the colour of the bottom of a pond. It opened in the middle of the grey like a mouth that was not a mouth, for three breaths. And it shut.

Then the ten, and the heap came on.

"Callie," said Dee, very quietly.

"A hundred and thirty," said Callie. "At the door." And then, "A hundred and forty."

The counter on her hip had changed its sound. It was not ticking. It had gone to the thin high sound it had made at the wash, a wasp in a jar.

"A hundred and fifty," said Callie. "Red. The needle's not stuck. It's red."

"Red, and nobody trapped," said Dee. "Everybody out. Now."

"The box," said Toren.

He was still at the strut, with the husk on it. He looked at the carrier on its board by the heap of boxes. One box in it. The lid on the board beside it.

"Lid," said Dee. "Leave it. Out."

Toren let the strut go. The husk fell forward onto the floor where the strut had been, and got up. He did not wait for it. He took the lid off the board with his good hand and dropped it into the groove on the carrier, and it went in with a soft heavy sound, and he turned.

"Out," said Dee. "Stair. Toren last."

Jab went.

# Chapter 36 — Toren: The Door That Opens Toward You

Toren came out of the room last, the way Dee had said, and turned east.

The others were ahead of him along the left-hand wall of the corridor. Jab first, with the frame on his back and the lantern swinging from it, walking fast and holding his right hand away from his side. Dee behind him with the open jar in the crook of her arm and the glass in her fist. Callie behind her with the hook. They were going toward the steel door, twelve paces off, where the stair was.

He did not run. Running was how you went down on a wet floor, and he had one arm.

Behind him, in the room, the short husk had got up off the floor. He heard it come after him, the dry soft sound of it on the concrete. He did not look round. He knew where it was the way he knew where the others were.

He looked west instead, once.

The heap was at the second door now. It filled the corridor from wall to wall, grey, higher than his waist, with the rope in the tray going down into it from above like a rope going into a well. It came on. It was slower than a man walking. It was faster than he had thought anything that size could be.

At the three the line of light opened in the middle of it. It was the colour of the edge of the sky. It stayed open for three breaths, and shut.

Toren turned his back on it and walked east.

"Door," said Callie, ahead of him.

She had stopped. They had all stopped. Jab was standing a pace short of the steel door with his lantern held up, and he was not going through it.

The heap of grey that had been against the door was on the corridor side now. The door had swept it through when they pulled it open. It had lain on the floor of the corridor in a spill, a hand deep, all afternoon, while its threads went west.

It was not lying now. It had swelled.

It had drawn up into a hump against the wall beside the door, between them and the door, the way the corner in the room had drawn up. As Toren came up behind them, the three came, and the hump cracked along the top. Something came up out of it.

He did the sum. It was not a long one. A husk in front and a husk behind, and a thing he did not have a name for behind that. And every one of them, if it was killed, would lie on the floor for ten breaths and stand up again.

"Callie," he said. "Hold the front one."

"Callie saw one going in," said Toren. "In twelve. And two coming out."

"Then she saw what I saw," said Pell. "It's more in the afternoon than the morning. I'd not swear to it." He folded the sheet. "Write it. I'll count it again tomorrow. Somebody will want to know if it's more."

Toren wrote it. *Pell's count, from the cart. Ten and three all day. 7 falls of grey, gate to the top of the road coming out, about 4 hours. More in the afternoon (Pell's not sure).*

He looked at it. It was the Director's count. It was what she had asked for. *Count what comes out of it and write it down.* It was the first thing on the paper that was hers.

They told it at the fire.

Dee lit it with the last of the wrack from the morning, and put the pot on for Rook's back, and they sat round it in the dusk. They did not sit on the ground inside the fence. They sat on the gravel outside it, on the right side of the river, and Toren said it. He said it the way he had said the evidence at the edge of the water on the forty-third, in order, all of it.

The yard. The lines going up the road. The door, and the stair, and the yellow rail. The door at the bottom that opened toward you, and pulled in thirty breaths. The corridor going west. The tray, and the rope in it, crawling. The first room, and the boxes on the floor, hundreds, the way Gran had said, with the rope gone in among them like roots. Three live ones, and one of them open. One in the carrier.

"One in the carrier," said Pell.

"In the room," said Toren. "With the lid on. Where we left it."

He told the rest. The heap in the corner, and the husk that stood up out of it, and that Toren had killed and that had come back. The same one. The cut in the strands, and how it had mended in the three. Callie's lee across the door, and how it had stopped the floor and not the rope. Jab's palm's worth, into a vent jar, and the husk coming back a hand short.

He told them about the rope.

He did not look at Jab while he told it. He told it the way Jab had said it on the floor of the room with his arm in Dee's hands. *It thought I was a hole in it. It thought I was a wound, and it was mending me.* He told them what Dee had done.

Rook, on the canvas by the fire, did not say anything. He had his eyes on Jab's hand.

Then Toren told them about the thing on the rope. The heap as high as a waist, coming along the corridor, pulling itself along its own rope with nothing to pull with. The light that opened in it at the three. The red. The door, and what the door did from the other side.

"It came along the rope," he said. "From the west. From the round one, or from somewhere between. It came to where we were. To where the husk had been killed, and the grey was lying. And to him." He did not look at Jab. "It came to us."

"It came to you," said Rook.

He said it quietly, from the canvas, with his eyes half shut.

"*If it comes to you,*" said Rook, "*you do what you have to do and you come home.*" He opened his eyes. "You came home."

"We came home with one box," said Toren. "In a room, under a hill, behind a door that opens away from it."

"What is it?" said Pell.

Nobody answered for a while.

"It's lifting," said Jab.

He was sitting by the fire with his right hand on his knee, palm up, the white place catching the light. He had not said anything since the river.

"It lifts like we lift," he said. "Flat. At the rate it comes. It draws for ten, and it vents for three. Only it vents where it wants a thing. Into a heap, and a husk stands up. Into a cut, and the cut's not there." He looked at the hand. "Into a hand, if the hand's drawing on it."

"Then it's a lifter," said Rook.

He said it with the corner of his mouth moving, the way it moved when he was going to laugh and was not sure it would be allowed.

"It's got my job," he said. "Four seasons, on the worst of the north yard. And there it is, doing it for nothing, on its own hill, better than me." He looked at the fire. "A lifter."

Nobody laughed. But Toren saw Callie's mouth move, and Pell's.

He wrote it on the paper. *The lifter.* Because Jab had said what it did, and Rook had said what that made it, and nobody had a better word.

---

"You've a box," said Pell.

He said it after Dee had changed Rook's pad, in the dark, with the boiled water cooling in the pot and the fire going down. He had said nothing all through the telling. He had sat on the cart and listened, the way he listened to a hub.

"You've one box," he said, "in a lead box of its own, in a room, under a hill. You've twenty-nine inside. Sixteen's the line. That's thirteen to work with. You've a road home that's cut, and a man with a fever, and a man with one leg." He was not looking at anybody. "And in the room with the box, there's a thing that mends what you do to it, every thirteen breaths, and came along a rope to see you, and had its hand in Jab."

"I know," said Toren.

"I know you know," said Pell. "I'm saying it so it's been said by somebody who's not going in." He was quiet a moment. "A box and a half short, and all of you alive, is a thing I'd take back to that woman's office and put on her table. I'd not be ashamed of it."

"The box is in the room," said Callie.

"Then it's in the room."

"Then we go home with nothing," said Callie. "And that thing's got it. And the ash that went in the door today. And everything lying on the whole of that hill, going up it, ten and three." She said it flat. "And Senna's on the narrow bed on the fifty-fourth with a man guessing."

"She is," said Pell. "And she'd say the same as me."

Toren did not know if that was true. He thought about his grandmother on the pillow with her eyes shut, saying *pull the door.* She had been saving it. She had not said *come back without it*. She had not needed to. She had said it to the whole room with *I know what every man in that line does with a number once he's got it.*

He looked at the four of them round the dying fire. At Pell on the cart with his leg out, who had said it so it had been said. At Rook, hot, on the canvas, not asleep. At Dee with her book on her knee and her hands still. At Callie. At Jab with his hand palm up on his knee.

*Everybody back.*

He had written it. He had meant it. He had not known, when he wrote it, that it might one day mean leaving a thing behind that everybody at home needed, so that everybody could come home.

"Tomorrow," he said.

They looked at him.

"Tomorrow we decide," said Toren. "Not tonight. With the light." He heard himself say it, and knew he had said it before, on the forty-sixth. It had been the right thing then. He did not know if it was now. "Tonight we think about what it did. All of it. Every time it did something, and what we did, and what happened." He looked at Jab. "It mends in the three. You said. It vents where there's a hole. That's the whole of what we know. If there's a way in that, we find it tonight. If there isn't, we go home in the morning with nothing."

"And if there is?" said Dee.

"Then we go back for the box," said Toren. "And I'll say before we go what we'll pay for it. And if it costs more than that, we come out, finished or not."

Dee looked at him across the embers for a long time.

"Write that," she said.

He wrote it. Under the day, under the lifter, under Pell's count.

*47th. The pump house. In: 4. One box in the carrier, left in the first room, lid on. J.'s right palm: a white place on the heel, fixed. It mends in the three.*

And under that, because it was true, and because there was nowhere else to put it:

*Gran's door pulls. From the other side it pushes.*

# Chapter 37 — Callie: The Flap

The pump was still in the pump house.

Callie had not looked at it the night before. She had been too cold, and too tired, and the wall had been something to put a fire against and nothing else. Now she went in through the gap where the door had been, with the lantern, because she could not sit by the fire and think about a rope any more.

It stood in the middle of the stone floor under the open sky. It was iron, and it was big, bigger than the cart. There was a long iron drum lying on its side on a stone bed, and at one end of it a wheel as high as she was, with spokes, and a rod going from the wheel into the drum. A pipe as thick as her body came up out of the floor on the river side and went into the bottom of the drum. Two more came out of the top of it and went through the wall toward the river, toward the broken pipes in the water.

It was all rust. It had not moved in seventy years. She put her hand on the wheel and it did not move now.

"It's a pump," said Pell, from outside.

He was on the cart, drawn up against the outside of the wall. The cart was so close that its rack was a pace from the gap. Callie held the lantern up and he could see in. He was sitting with his back to the rack and his leg out along the boards, the way he had sat all day.

"I know it's a pump," said Callie.

"I know you know," said Pell. "Ask me what you came in to ask me."

She stood by the wheel with the lantern.

"How does it work?" she said.

---

Pell told her.

He told her the way he had told her about the wheel, sitting back with his hands on his knee, not looking at her, looking at the thing.

"It lifts," he said. "And it pushes. That's all a pump does. Two strokes." He nodded at the drum. "There's a plunger in that. The wheel goes round and the rod goes in and out. When it comes out, it lifts. It pulls the water up out of the river through that pipe in the floor, into the drum. When it goes in, it pushes. It pushes the water out of the drum, out through the top, up those pipes, over the river, up the hill. Wherever they wanted it."

"It lifts and it pushes."

"Lift, push. Lift, push." He moved his hand. "Only there's a thing in between. There's a flap."

Callie waited.

"In the bottom of the drum, where the river pipe comes in," said Pell. "A flap. Leather, on a hinge, like a door. When it lifts, the flap opens and the water comes up past it into the drum. When it pushes, the flap shuts. It shuts because the water's pushing on it the wrong way. So what it lifted can't go back down the pipe to the river. It's got nowhere to go but out the top." He looked at the drum. "My father had a little one, on the farm, for the trough. A hand one. The flap went every spring. When the flap goes, you can hear it."

"What does it sound like?"

"Like it's working," said Pell. "That's the thing. It lifts, and it pushes, and it sounds like it's working all day. But it pushes the water straight back down the pipe it came up. It lifts it up and it pushes it down. Up and down, the same water, all day. Nothing comes out of the top." He shook his head. "You'd not know. You'd pump for an hour and go to the trough and it'd be dry."

Callie stood very still with the lantern.

She was thinking about the heap in the corner. The ten, and the heap drawn out into threads and gone into the rope. The three, and the corner swelling. She was thinking about Jab with his hand on the rope by the door, saying *nothing, it's going past*. And the three, and the push, and his face.

"If you wanted to stop it," she said. "The pump. If you were in the drum."

"I'd not be in the drum."

"If you were. If you wanted it to stop pushing water up the hill."

Pell thought about it. She saw him think about it, the way he thought about a distance.

"You'd not do it on the lift," he said slowly. "On the lift it's got the whole river behind it. You'd stand in front of that pipe in the floor and it'd lift the river at you. You'd not stop the river." He looked at the pipe going down into the stone. "You'd do it on the push. On the push it's only got what's in the drum. What it's just lifted. That's all it's got. A drum's worth."

"And if you took the drum's worth," said Callie. "On the push. Every time. So it pushed and nothing got up the pipe."

"Then it'd lift the river again," said Pell. "And push it at you again. And you'd take that." He was quiet. "Till the river ran out. Or you did."

"And if somebody shut the river?"

Pell looked at her properly then.

"Then it'd have a drum's worth," he said. "And no more. And it'd push it at you, and you'd take it, and it'd lift nothing, because there's nothing to lift. And push again. Less." He was quiet for a long time. "It'd pump itself dry. It doesn't know how to do anything else. A pump doesn't stop because the water's gone. It stops when somebody stops the wheel."

Callie put the lantern down on the stone bed of the pump.

"Nobody stops its wheel," she said.

"Then it'd go on," said Pell. "Pushing nothing. Into whatever was taking it."

---

She went out to the fire.

They were all there. Dee had changed Rook's pad and was sitting with her book. Jab was sitting with his right hand palm up on his knee. Toren had the paper. Rook was on the canvas with his eyes open. Nobody was talking. They had been not talking since *tomorrow we decide*.

"Pell," said Callie. "Say it again. The pump."

Pell said it again, from the cart, while she stood by the fire with her hands open. Lift and push, and the flap, and the river. On the lift it's got the whole river. On the push it's only got what it's lifted.

Jab had looked up at *the push*. He did not look away from Pell until Pell had finished.

"On the push," said Jab.

"That's what I did wrong," he said. He said it to his hand. "I put it on the rope in the ten. On the lift. And it went past, because it had the whole hill behind it and it was going somewhere. And then the three came, and it pushed, and I was a hole." He turned the hand over. "And there was nothing shut. The whole hill was behind the push. So it kept pushing."

"If somebody shut the river," said Callie.

"The lee," said Toren.

He had put the pencil down.

"The lee stopped the floor," he said. "At the door. It stopped everything crawling in. It didn't stop the rope." He was looking at the fire, not at her. "But the rope's a thing. It's a rope. It's lying there. If the rope's cut—"

"It mends in the three," said Jab.

"It mends in the three if it can reach." Toren looked up. "If it's cut at the door, and Callie's lee is in the door, in the cut, it can't reach. It can grow up to the lee. It can't grow through it." He stopped. "Then what's in the room's all there is. That's the drum. The river's on the other side of Callie."

Nobody said anything.

Dee had opened her book. She was not writing. She was looking at Toren.

"Say it in order," she said. "All of it. From the stair."

---

He said it in order.

He said it the way he had said everything since the forty-third, the way he had said the siding and the wash. He did not have the answers. He only had to ask for them in the right order, and put them where everybody could see them, and see if they stood.

It would be in the room. Everybody agreed with that. Rook said it, from the canvas: "It goes where the grey is." The grey had been lying in the first room all afternoon, and the heap in the corner, and the husks killed and killed again. And the carrier was there. They did not think it wanted the carrier. But it would be there.

There were two ways into the room from the corridor for the rope. The floor, through the door, where the rope would lie across the threshold. And the ceiling, through the hole above the door, and down the wall.

"I cut both," said Toren. "The ceiling first, outside, before it goes in the hole. Then the floor at the threshold. The heel. In a ten. Before it pushes."

"And me in the door," said Callie. "On the threshold. In your cut." She put her hand out flat and tipped it back. "The lee off the curve, standing up into the corridor. And leaned back. Up the wall over the door. Over the hole. Like a lean-to, the wrong way up."

"Can you?"

"It's a door," said Callie. "And a bit. It's still. It's on concrete." She thought about it. "A door's the cheapest thing I know. A door and a bit's more. And if there's husks in the corridor, and there will be, they'll come at it, and that's more again." She looked at Dee. "I want two. Settled. Tonight."

Dee did not answer at once.

"Two's more than you've ever carried," she said.

"I settle well. You said. It's the only thing I do better than Toren."

"It's not the only thing," said Toren.

"It's the only thing tonight," said Callie.

Dee looked at her for a long time. Then she wrote it.

"Two," she said. "You'll settle it slowly, and I'll sit with you, and if it slops I'll stop you at one and a half." She looked up. "And you'll be in the room."

"I'll be in the door."

"You'll be in the room," said Dee. "With your back to it. You'll be facing the corridor with the lee in front of you, and everything that's in that room will be behind you. You'll not see it. You'll not see any of it."

"I know," said Callie.

She did know. She had known since she said *in the door*. She had known it the way she knew a husk line.

"Jab," said Toren.

"On the push," said Jab. "Not the lift. When you cut it, it'll mend. That's all it does. It'll push into the cut at the three. I put my hand on the cut, on the room side, at the three, and it pushes into me. And I take a drum's worth. And I put it in a vent jar, and stopper it. And I take my hand off." He looked at Dee. "Before the ten. Every time. Off before the ten."

"I'll say *off*," said Dee. "At the end of every three. Every one. You'll not wait for me to say it twice."

"I'll not wait."

"And what's in the room is all it's got," said Toren. "And every three it pushes some of it into Jab, and Jab puts it in a jar. And every ten it lifts what's lying in the room. And it's less." He stopped. "Pell said. It'll pump itself dry."

"It'll not stand there and let you," said Rook.

He said it from the canvas. He had not moved, but his voice was clear.

"It had a husk on your strut all afternoon," said Rook. "It'll make more. It'll put them in your way. It'll throw what it's got at whatever's worked, and Jab's hand is worked, and so is Callie's back. And so is you." He looked at Toren. "You'll not be cutting. You'll be keeping it off them."

"Then I'll keep it off them," said Toren. "And cut when I can." He looked at the paper. "Every rope it's got in the room is a way it moves. Every one's a way it throws. I cut them, one at a time. Every cut's a hole. Every hole, it pushes, at the three. And Jab's there."

"And when it's got nothing left to push with?" said Dee.

"It opens," said Jab. "At the three. In the middle. The light." He did not look at anybody. "I saw it open. It opens every time it pushes. That's where the push comes from."

"Then that's where I'll put the point," said Toren.

---

"Say what it costs," said Dee.

Callie said it.

"Twenty-nine," she said. "Inside. One jar open, with five in it. Three whole." She did the sum out loud, because Dee would want it out loud. "Settling tonight. Two in me, that's a Handful and three quarters more. A Handful and a half in Toren. Half in you, Miss Wren, to make you one. That's three and three quarters." She stopped. "Twenty-five and a quarter. The line's sixteen. Nine and a quarter to work with."

"Nine and a quarter," said Toren.

"It's not twelve," said Callie.

"It's not twelve," said Toren. "It's nine and a quarter. That's what I'll pay." He wrote it. "If it's not done at the line, we come out, finished or not."

"And if it's nearly done?" said Dee. "At the line. If it's lying there with nothing left and you've got the point up."

Toren did not answer at once.

"Then I'll say it," he said. "Then. Out loud. What we're doing and what it costs and how long. And you'll write it. And it'll be mine."

"It'll be mine as well," said Dee. "It's my line."

"And the home three don't cross that bridge," said Toren. "Whatever happens. Whatever it's lying there with. They're for the road." He looked at the cart, at the rack behind the driver's bench, where the three jars stood grey to the shoulder in the dark. "Say it, somebody who's not going in."

"The home three don't cross the bridge," said Pell. "I'm sitting on them."

She settled by the fire with Miss Wren beside her.

It was the most she had ever taken. Dee weighed it out of the open jar onto the square of paper on her knee, a Handful and three quarters, grey and fine. Callie put her palm flat on it and let it come.

It came. It always came well, for her. It came up into her palm slow and cold, and went down, and lay at the bottom of the well on the quarter that was there, and she felt the stone get bigger. She did not haul. She let it come at the rate it came, and she thought about nothing, the way Dee had taught them at the long table.

She did not think about nothing.

She thought about the door. She thought about standing in it with the hook on the threshold and the lee up, facing the corridor, and the room behind her. Toren behind her, and Jab, and Miss Wren, and the lifter, and she would not see any of them.

She had always held what she could see. That was what Rook had taught her on the forty-fourth. *Watch who's inside. Put your eyes on the people and hold them there.* She had held Jab's shoulders on the old line, and Jab and Pell under the lee at the wash, and taken Toren in and out of it with her eyes. The lee went where she looked. It had always gone where she looked.

Tomorrow she would be looking at a corridor.

The lee would not be over anybody. It would not be shaped like where they were. It would be a wall across a door, standing between a corridor and a room, keeping the corridor out. Nobody would be inside it. Everybody would be behind it.

*You can't have all of us,* Miss Wren had said on the forty-fourth. *You'll have to decide who's worth the lee.*

She understood it now with her palm on the paper. It was not about who was worth it. Tomorrow the lee was not for anybody. It was for the thing that was not them. It was for keeping the rest of the hill off, so that the three people behind her could do what only they could do, where she could not see them do it.

She would not be carrying them. She would be holding the door so they could carry it.

*Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.*

Her mother had said that with her words half gone, on the edge of the bed, with Callie's fingers round her wrist. She had opened Callie's fingers off her wrist, one at a time.

"One and a half," said Miss Wren quietly. "It's lying still. Go on."

Callie went on. The last quarter came up into her palm and went down and lay there.

"Two," said Miss Wren. "Is it still?"

Callie went down to look. The stone was big at the bottom of the well, bigger than it had ever been, and it was still. It was not slopping. It was not in the hall. It was lying where she had put it.

"It's still," she said.

"Then it's yours," said Miss Wren. She wrote it. "Road, none. Inside, twenty-five and a quarter. One open, with a Handful and a quarter in it. Three whole." She looked up. "Say it back."

Callie said it back.

He sat on the cart with his back to the rack and the atlas shut on his knee, and she stood by the cart wheel with the hook and the counter. The fire was down. Across the river the fence stood black against the dark. The hill went up beyond it. Over the flat top of it there were no stars, and round the place with no stars there was the light the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened and thinned.

"I'm counting it," said Pell. "In case anybody asks."

"Can you see the falls?"

"No. It's too dark. I can see the edge." He was quiet. "I'd like to see the falls. I'd like to know if it's more at night."

"I know."

"I'd say it again."

"I know you would."

"I said it so it had been said," said Pell. "By somebody who wasn't going in. Somebody has to say it, on a road, or you go in without anybody having said it, and then you don't know you chose." He looked across the river at the hill. "It's said now. It's written down. So now you can go in and do it properly."

Callie looked at him.

"That's all," said Pell. "That's what saying it's for."

She stood by the wheel and did not say anything for a long time.

"Pell," she said. "Your father. With the pump."

"What about him?"

"Did he mend the flap? Every spring?"

"Every spring," said Pell. "Cut a new one out of an old boot and put it in. Took him ten minutes." He was quiet. "Took me a year to learn how, and he let me do it wrong for most of that year. He'd stand there and watch me pump water down the pipe for an hour and not say anything. Then he'd say *now go and look at the trough.*"

"That's cruel."

"It's a good way to learn about flaps," said Pell. "You never forget which way they go."

In the morning Rook was cooler.

Not cool. Dee had her fingers on his wrist at first light and her face said so. But the sweat had stopped, and his eyes were clear, and when she lifted the pad the red round the stitches had not gone any further out in the night. It had not come in either.

"It's holding," she said.

"Like the post," said Rook.

"The post was in lead," said Dee. She gave him the second of the four papers in boiled water, and he drank it and made the same face.

He did it in three movements, with a hand on the cart wheel and a hand on Pell's good knee, and Pell said "Mind the leg" and Rook said "I'm minding it." He stood with his coat over his shoulders and walked, slowly, down the gravel to the near end of the bridge, and sat down at the foot of the first post with his back against it. He had not asked anybody.

"Sitting's allowed," he said, when Dee looked at him.

They ate. They drank the boiled water and a little of the full skin. They said the rules at the bridge end, and *pull the door* at the end of them.

Rook said the rules with them from the post. At the end he looked up at Callie.

"You'll have your back to them," he said.

"I know."

"When I shut the road at the Sag," said Rook, "I never once looked round at the lifters behind me. Not once, in six weeks. I looked at what was coming up out of the bottom." He was looking at the hill. "If I'd looked round, I'd have dropped it. You can't hold a thing and look behind it. You hold what's in front. They'll hold what's behind." He looked back at her. "You'll want to look. Don't."

"I'll not look," said Callie.

"You will," said Rook. "Once. Everybody does. Then you'll not do it again."

They went in faster the second time.

There was nothing to drag. The sledge was in the first room, under a hill. They went along the pipes on the north side of the blocks, and up the Z, and round the heap at the first bend. Miss Wren drew them at the top of the road at the twentieth turn, off the open jar, and Callie weighed it. A Handful and a quarter had been in it at the gate.

Toren wrote it.

The lanterns in the hall. The yellow rail. The stair. The long grey slide of the husk's ash down the steps from the top, where Toren had killed it against the hook, lying on the stair in a streak and going down it in threads. They went down beside it. At the landing the counter said fifty. At the bottom it said sixty.

"Open," he said.

"Good," said Miss Wren. "That's thirty breaths we don't have to spend."

The corridor had changed.

The tray on the right-hand wall was bowed all along. The rope in it was as thick as a leg, grey, heavy, crawling. It had bent two of the brackets so that the tray hung down at a slant between them. # Chapter 38 — Jab: Off Before the Ten

The first thing Jab did in the room was take the frame off.

He had never done it inside a fence. Nothing clean goes on the ground. But there were the cabinets along the right-hand wall, big grey steel ones as high as his shoulder, standing in a row with flat tops. He backed up to the nearest one the way he backed up to the tailboard at every halt, and let the rack come down on its top, and slid his thumbs out of the chest band and stepped out from under.

"It's a shelf," said Dee, beside him. "Not a floor."

"It's a shelf," said Jab.

His back came up. He had not known how much of him had been holding the frame until it was gone. Four full jars and two empties and a broken socket, and the lantern hanging off the side of it, lighting the room yellow from the top of a cabinet.

Dee put the canvas bag down on the next cabinet with a sound like a bell. Three vent jars. She had the open jar in the crook of her arm, and the glass in her fist, and Callie's counter on its strap over her own shoulder now. She had taken it off Callie's hip in the corridor before Callie turned round.

"Ten," said Dee. "Seven. Eight."

Jab crouched by the cut.

It was the big rope. Toren had dragged the heel through it on the threshold, and the grey had spilled out of it onto the concrete at the cut. The end of it on the room side lay across the floor from the threshold toward the lifter, three paces, as thick as Toren's thigh. At the far end it went into the grey hump that lay over the carrier.

At the near end, a pace from Jab's knee, it stopped. There was the spill, and then the concrete, and then the curve of Callie's hook lying on the threshold. Callie stood behind the curve with her back to him.

He could not see the lee from here. It stood on the other side of the curve, into the corridor. But he could see Callie's back, and her hands open on the conduit, and her shoulders. They were very still.

"Nine," said Dee.

Jab put his right hand out over the cut end of the rope. He did not put it down yet. He held it an inch off, the way he held it over a patient before he knew what was there. The white place on the heel of it caught the lantern.

Dee put the mouth of the jar to his left palm.

"Clean in," she said. "Three."

---

It pushed.

He felt it before he put his hand down. He felt the whole of the grey hump behind the rope do what it did at every three, the thing he had felt through his palm on the rope by the door. It drew breath. It vented. It pushed out into every place in the room where there was a hole.

The cut was a hole.

He put his palm flat on the end of the rope, round the white place, on the fingers and the side of the hand.

It came. It came up the rope at him from the hump like something shoved down a pipe, all at once, warm and gritty and loose, and into his palm. The clean cold came into his left hand from the jar and went through him and held it off his wrist. He held it high. It was not the hill. It was not the whole of everything lying where it fell, the way it had been on the rope by the door. It was one push. It was a palm's worth, and a little more, all at once.

It stopped.

It stopped because there was no more behind it. That was the thing he had not known until he felt it. On the rope by the door, the push had come and come. This came once, and it was all there was, and it stopped.

"Off," said Dee.

He took his hand off. He took it off with the grey held in it high and open, and turned, and Dee had a vent jar out of the bag with the stopper out. He put its mouth to his palm and tipped it. The grey went down out of him into the glass.

Dee put the stopper in with her thumb.

"One," she said.

Jab looked at the cut.

The end of the rope lay on the floor a pace from Callie's heels. It had not grown. It had not reached for the threshold. It lay where Toren had left it, with its spill, and the spill was going in threads back along the rope toward the hump. The ten had begun.

"It's where it was," he said.

"It's where it was," said Dee. She wrote it with the vent jar under her arm. "Jab. It's where it was."

He had heard the other things while his hand was on the rope. He looked at them now.

One of the arm-ropes was up.

It had been lying across the heap of boxes, as thick as an arm, flung out from the hump. It was not lying now. It had come up off the boxes at the three the way a wet rope comes up when somebody lifts the end of it, heavy and slow, and swung. It had swung across the room at the door. At the worked thing standing in the door with its back turned.

And Toren had been there.

He was standing a pace behind Callie on the room side with his left arm in its cuff against his chest. There was a strut going up from the concrete beside his boot at a slant, over Callie's shoulders, the way you lean a plank over a door. The arm-rope lay across it. "Holding," said Toren. His teeth were together.

Callie had not turned round. Jab saw that. He saw her shoulders move at the sound of it hitting, and not turn.

He went at it. He did not go at it with the heel. He did not take his hand off.

Not yet. He knew he had to. He knew the ten was coming a breath behind the three, and that it would draw, and that what he had in his palm was loose, and it would draw it back, and him after it. He knew all of that.

If he came off now, the ten would come, and it would draw what little was lying in the room, and at the next three it would have something to push, and it would shut.

"Toren," he said.

Toren was there.

"It's open," said Jab. "It's open and I'm on it. It's got nothing to shut it with."

"How long?"

"Till the ten," said Jab. "Then I'm off. Whatever you've done."

Toren lit the spike.

Toren lit the point.

He did not light the heel. He had nothing to light the heel with and the point both, and the heel was for hooking, and there was nothing to hook. He asked for the light at the very end of the spike, a finger's length of it, and only there. He asked it of his own, because the stone was gone. He felt the draw behind his breastbone, thin, like the last of the water going out of a barrel through a pinhole.

The lifter lay against his strut, knee-high, grey. The strut went down from its side into the concrete at a slant, and he was soft behind it, and he was paying for it by the breath out of what was left of his own. Beside it, on his knees, Jab had his right palm flat on the edge of the grey, a hand from the light.

The light was open.

It was a line along the top of the lifter, the colour of the bottom of a pond, wide now, as wide as two fingers. It had opened at the three and it had not shut, because it had nothing to shut with. Jab was taking what came out of it. Toren could see Jab's palm, held flat, and his face, and the face was very still. It was the face he had seen on the step of eleven when Jab put his hand on a drift in his sleep.

Everything was where it was.

Callie was in the door behind him with her back to the room and the lee standing out into the corridor off the curve. He could not see the lee from here. Jab drew them at the river, all of them, alone.

There was nobody else to do it. Miss Wren was at her floor. Rook was off, and hot, and lying on the canvas with his eyes open. So Jab stood on the gravel below the pump house in the last of the sun with a jar of the lifter's ash against his left palm and his right hand flat on one chest after another, and drew.

"Ninety," she said, at Toren. And later, "Twenty." And later, "Twelve. Off."

He did Toren. He did Callie, with his palm flat on the inside of her wrist, because the ribs were where the rest of it would have been. He did Miss Wren.

He did Miss Wren longest.

She stood in front of him with her hands at her sides and let him. Callie watched her face while he did it. It was white, and it was not steady now. It had been steady all morning under the hill, over the jar and the glass and Jab's back, nineteen times. It had stopped being steady on the bridge.

"A hundred and ten," said Callie, at Miss Wren's chest. "Ten breaths."

Jab did not say anything. He drew. He held it high and vented it into the gravel downstream of the pump house, away from the water, the road's and not the river's. He went back on.

"Sixty," said Callie, after a long time. "Thirty. Fourteen."

She thought about the door.

She had not let herself think about it all afternoon. There had been the stair, and the road, and the bridge, and the river, and the jars. Now there was only the dark and Pell and the cart wheel, and she thought about it.

She had held it. She had stood in it with her back to the room for twenty turns and more, with the stone going out of her into a door and a bit, and a husk pushed against the pale a foot from her face. She had never once seen what was happening behind her. She had heard it. She had heard the sack hit the wall. She had heard Toren say *holding* with his teeth together, and Jab say *busy*, and Miss Wren say *off*, over and over, and the glass going over. She had heard the line said. She had heard *two turns*, and said *I've got two turns, I've not got three*, and she had not known, saying it, whether two would be enough. She had not been able to do anything about it if it was not.

She had looked round once. Rook had said she would.

She had turned her head and seen Jab on the floor with his hand out, and Miss Wren behind him with the jar, and the room behind that, grey. And Jab had looked at her and said *busy*. Just that. The word he said to tell you he was working and how long you had to hold.

And she had turned her head back and held.

She had not carried them. That was what she understood, standing by the cart wheel in the dark with her arm across her side. She had carried people all her life. She had carried her mother across four hundred miles in her head, every breath, every step, counting her. She had held a wagon on a hook with her mother in it. On the lip at the wash she had held a lee off her own stone over two bodies and taken a third in and out of it with her eyes. She had thought that was what it was for. Holding people.

Under the hill she had held a door. She had held the rest of the world off three people so they could do the thing only they could do, where she could not see them do it. She had not been holding them. She had been holding the edge of them. And they had held the rest.

*Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.*

She had thought, when her mother said it, that it meant letting go. It did not mean letting go. It meant choosing what to hold, and holding that, all of it, and trusting the people on the other side of it to hold their own.

She had not known that it would feel like this. It did not feel like forgetting. It felt like the ache in the palm after lifting, when you have put a thing down that was worth carrying.

Callie went out in front.

She did it without being asked, the way she had done it on the first morning on the old north road. She went out twenty paces in front of Duchess with the hook for a stick and her left arm strapped across her side under her coat. She began to read the road for the wheels.

It was the road they had come on, two days ago, at dusk. The old line going up from the river on its long bare slope to the ridge, and the cut through the ridge, and the wash behind it. She knew it. She read it anyway. She read it for the wheels. The loose stones on the bed. The place where the frost had heaved it. The mark where the cart had come down it on the forty-sixth with Pell on it and his foot on a coil of rope.

At the top of the slope, at the mouth of the cut, she stopped and turned round.

She looked back once. Everybody does.

The river lay low and dark in its stony bed, with the pump house on its near bank, roofless, and the two broken pipes going into the water. The footbridge went across it on its posts, with two grey poles of wrack wood still lying along it over the crack. At the far end, the gate, with a twist of green wire through its chain, left so it would come.

And beyond the gate the yard, and the road going up the hill in its long Z, and the long building standing along the rim with its windows black. And the drum behind it with its broken top. And over the whole of the top of the hill, the sky.

The edge of it thickened. She did not count it. It thinned, and thickened at once, and thinned for a long time. It had no count. It was a thing breathing in its sleep, having a bad dream.

Under it, in a room under a hill, there was more ash lying on a floor than she could have lifted in a week. There were hundreds of boxes, and a third live one talking to the room, and three stoppered jars with a chalk cross beside them. She would put the number of every one of them on the Director's table.

In the cart behind Duchess there were two.

It was the forty-ninth day. At home it would be the ninth hour, near enough. Her mother would have walked to the ward for her pills at the eighth hour, with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. Senna would be on the fourth bed on the right, with five days to the narrow bed and the machine that sang flat. Tonk would be in the fifth, with a cord round his fist. They were all breathing without her. They had been breathing without her for eight days, and nothing had happened to any of them because she was not counting.

She did not know what would happen on the fifty-fourth. She did not know if the wind would be down tomorrow.

She knew what was in the cart, and what was in the frame, graded. She knew who was on the cart and who was walking. She knew the road to the cut, and she knew the wash.

She turned round and went on into the cut, twenty paces in front of the horse, and read the road for the wheels.

Behind her, at the back, she heard Toren counting them.
