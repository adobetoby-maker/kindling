# Chapter 41 — Jab: In Pieces

Jab woke in the dark at the mouth of the cut and knew where everybody was before he opened his eyes.

He had not always been able to do that. He could now, if they were near and he knew them. Pell was on the cart, awake, and trying not to move his leg. Dee was sitting up. Callie was standing, the way she stood every night now because lying down hurt more. Toren was asleep with his face in his good arm. Rook was on the canvas by the wheel.

Rook was hot.

Not the way he had been at the pump house on the first morning. Less than that. But it had come up in the night, the way a fire you have banked comes up again when the wind turns. Jab could feel it from where he lay. It was all through Rook, not in bits. It was him, fighting.

It was not a thing Jab could draw. He lay and felt it anyway, because he could not stop himself.

Then he felt for the other thing. He put his left hand flat on his coat, over the left side, and felt the shape through the cloth. The body. The tail. The place where the fin had been.

He got up.

---

The wind was down.

Callie said so before anybody asked. She was standing at the mouth of the cut with her arm strapped across her side under her coat and the hook in her other hand for a stick, looking east along the embankment. The sky over the marsh was going grey at the bottom. There was no sound at all from the reeds.

"It's down," she said. "It's been down since the fourth hour. I've been watching the reeds. They've not moved."

"And the drifts?" said Dee.

"Still." Callie did not look round. "I watched them too. They were still all night. Then about an hour ago the one nearest the gap slid. A hand's width. Not at a ten. Not at anything." She was quiet. "Then it lay still again."

Dee came and stood beside her.

"Once?"

"Once that I saw."

Dee looked at the drifts for a while. They lay along the north side of the embankment in the grey light, long and soft and pale, heaped against the bank like snow against a wall. Nothing moved on them.

"Write it," she said. "Then measure yourself. Then come and be measured."

---

She measured them at the cart, two fingers on each wrist.

"A hand and a bit," she said to Jab. "Floor's a finger." She turned his right hand over and looked at the heel of it by the first light. The white place was the size of a coin. The red round it had gone darker in the night, and the blister at its edge had filled. She had put a square of clean rag over it the evening before and tied it with thread. The rag was still clean. "Can you draw round it?"

"I did last night. On Duchess."

"I know you did. I was watching." She let go. "Round it. Not with it."

She did Callie, and told her three parts, and told her the ribs were not a thing she could measure with two fingers. She did Toren.

Toren had settled a Handful at the fire the night before, out of the lifter's second jar. Jab had watched him do it. He had sat with his palm flat on the square of paper for most of an hour, with his eyes shut, and let it come at the rate it came for him, which was slow. It had been full dark when the paper was clean.

"Stone's full," said Dee. "Your own's two turns. You've the bit back."

"I did nothing yesterday."

"You carried nothing yesterday," said Dee. "That's not the same thing, but it's near enough." She did her own last and did not say it, and wrote it.

"Say it," said Toren.

Dee looked at him.

"You said we'd say everything," said Toren. "Yours as well."

Dee was quiet for a moment.

"Two fingers," she said. "Over my floor. I can watch. I can draw leftovers off one person, once. I can't work." She shut the book. "That's what there is, Toren. It's not what I'd like."

"Then that's what we price," said Toren.

---

The gap was seventy paces from the mouth of the cut.

They walked out to look at it in the grey light, all of them but Pell, who looked at it from the cart. The embankment ran east from the cut, four paces wide on top, packed stone, with the drifts heaped up against its north side and the marsh standing black and still against its south. Iron posts stood along the north edge every twenty paces. Most of them leaned.

Then the embankment stopped.

It stopped the way a loaf stops where somebody has cut a slice out of it. The bed ran to an edge, and past the edge there was air, and then, four paces off, the bed began again. Between the two edges the bank went down on each side in a slope of loose stuff, stone and earth and grey, to the bottom. At the bottom the wash ran through, shallow and quick, from the drift side under where the arch had been, out to the marsh side. The arch was down in it. The stones of the crown lay in the water in a heap, and the water went white round them.

Lying across the bottom of the gap, half in the water and half out, was the iron post.

It had gone down with the crown. It lay at a slant from the foot of the near bank to the foot of the far one, with its lead collar still round it like a ring on a finger. The water ran under it.

Callie held the counter out over the edge at the length of her arm and waited ten breaths.

"Forty," she said. "Here. On the lip." She moved along the edge and held it out again, lower, over the slope. "Eighty. The bank. It's got grey drifted into it." She moved again. "A hundred. There. Where the drift comes into the gap from the north side. Where the crown went in."

Jab looked where she was looking. On the north side the drift came right down to the water. It had spilled into the gap when the crown fell, and it lay against the foot of both banks in a soft grey bank of its own, knee high, running up into the great heaps beyond.

"Seventy," said Callie. "At the water. The water's running. It takes it down."

Toren stood at the very edge with his left arm in its cuff against his chest and looked at all of it. He looked for a long time. Jab watched him look. It was the way he had looked at the rack in the north yard when Rook first put it in front of him, as weight, and where it went.

"Pell," said Toren, without turning round. "Say it again. How it goes."

Pell said it again from the cart, the way he had said it at the pump house. Wheels, axle, bed, shafts, the carrier on its board. Down one side on a rope round something that doesn't move. Up the other on a rope. Nobody below it.

"And the horse," said Pell. "The horse is the thing that lifts. Not you. None of you can lift anything this morning, and she can lift the lot. You put her on the top of the far side with a rope, and she walks away from the gap, and whatever's on the other end of the rope comes up. That's what a horse is for."

"And the near side?"

"The near side lets it down," said Pell. "Round a post. Slow."

Toren looked back along the embankment at the posts.

"Eight paces back," he said. "That one. It's standing straight." He looked across the gap. "And that one over there. Eleven."

"That'll do," said Pell.

---

Toren said who did what.

He did it at the cart, standing by the wheel with the paper in his hand, and he said it slowly, one name at a time, the way Dee said the rules at a gate. Jab listened to him do it. He had heard Toren give orders before. He had not heard him do this.

"I go first," said Toren. "Down the bank on steps, across on the post, up the other side on steps. I take the long rope over. I tie it round the post on the far side. Then there's a rope across, for hands."

"You'll be in the bottom," said Dee.

"I'll be in the bottom the most. That's mine. Somebody has to put a strut under things at the post, and that's me." He did not look at her. "Callie reads the banks for me. From the lip. Stone, soft, hollow. I don't set anywhere she hasn't said."

"Stone, soft, hollow," said Callie.

"Then Callie goes over with Duchess. Once. She leads her. Duchess knows her." He looked at Callie. "You don't come back. You stay on the far side with the horse and the counter, and you read the far bank for the loads. You don't lift. You don't take the rope."

Callie opened her mouth.

"I'll not lift," she said.

"Then Jab," said Toren. "With the frame. All the ash goes over on Jab. He stays on the far lip. That's where everybody comes up, and that's where everybody gets drawn." He looked at Jab. "You're the jar and the work. Dee's the watch. From here."

"From here?" said Jab.

"She can't be drawing and drawing," said Toren. "She's got two fingers. She stays on this side and lets the loads down round the post, and calls the glass. Nobody's in the bottom longer than she says." He looked at Dee. "And when the loads are over, she comes across with Rook. Last. And draws your leftovers. Once."

Dee did not say anything for a moment.

"That's right," she said. She wrote it.

"Rook," said Toren.

Rook was lying on the canvas with his eyes open.

"You're off," said Toren. "You come over at the end with Dee, on the steps, on the rope. You don't hold a load. You don't hold a rope. You don't help."

"I could sit on the post and count," said Rook. "Counting's not helping. It's counting."

"Then count," said Toren.

"And me," said Pell.

"You come over on the shafts," said Toren. "Like you said. On the canvas between them, with the horse pulling." He looked at the cart, at the rack behind the driver's bench. "And the home three with you. In your lap. You're sitting on them."

Pell looked at the three jars in the rack, grey to the shoulder.

"I'm sitting on them," he said.

---

Taking the cart apart took most of an hour.

Pell told them how from the boards. They set the axle up on two stones from the edge of the bed, one under each end, and knocked the linchpins out. Pell took the linchpins himself and put them in his coat pocket and buttoned it. "There's no finding a pin in grey," he said. They got the wheels off.

A wheel was as high as Callie's shoulder. It was ash wood with an iron tyre round it, and it was heavier than it looked. Jab walked the first one along the bed with his hands on the tyre, rolling it, and felt it try to go its own way at every stone.

They had the bed off the axle with Duchess on a rope, sideways, the way Pell said, and it came off with a groan and lay upside down on the embankment like a boat on a beach. The axle lay on its stones. The shafts came off their pins.

Pell watched them do all of it. He had his leg out on the canvas and his hands flat on his knees. When they lifted the shafts off, he took the canvas from under himself and folded it in four on his lap. Then he told Jab and Toren how to lash it between the two shafts, at the wide end, with the long sides round the poles and a rope through the eyelets.

"There," he said. "That's a bed and two poles and a bit of canvas. I said so."

"You said you'd not say anything about the camber," said Rook.

"I'll not," said Pell. "There's no camber on a drift."

They got him onto it. It took Jab and Toren and Dee, with Pell holding his own leg by the splint and saying *easy* and *easy* and *there*. When he was down, Toren brought the three home jars out of the rack one at a time. He put them into Pell's hands.

Pell set them round himself, one on each side of his hips and one between his knees, and put a rope round all three and round himself, and tied it.

"If I go in the wash," he said, "they go with me."

"If you go in the wash," said Dee, "I'll write it."

The carrier sat on its tailboard sledge on the embankment, with the lid in its groove. Jab stood and looked at it. Two boxes in it. The whole reason for everything.

"That comes after me," said Pell. "Not before. If something goes wrong on the first load, it wants to be me that it goes wrong with." He looked at the carrier. "Not that."

---

Toren went down first.

Jab watched him from the lip. He watched Callie crouch at the edge of the bank with the counter in her strapped arm and the hook's end out flat over the slope, pointing, the way she pointed at the ground in the north yard.

"Stone," she said. "There. Under a hand of grey. It's the edge of the old bed, where it was built up." She moved the hook. "Soft. Soft. Not there." She moved it again. "Stone. That's a block off the crown. It's sitting on something."

Toren set.

He put the foot of a strut down on the stone she had pointed at, under its hand of grey, and let it come up flat, a step, pale and hard, at the height of his knee. He put his boot on it. He stood on it for one breath, and it held him, and he stepped down onto the next one, which was already there, because he had set it while he stood on the first.

*Set. Step.*

He went down the bank that way, on steps nobody else could have stood on, with the long rope over his good shoulder, paying out behind him from the post. Each step went out when he left it. At the bottom he stepped off the last one onto the iron post where it lay across the water, and stood on the post, and the water ran under him.

"Eighty," said Callie, reading him from the lip. "A hundred, if you go to the north end of the post. Don't."

He did not. He walked the post to its far end, one-handed, with the rope going across behind him. Then he set steps up the far bank on the stones she called for him across the gap, and went up them, and stood on the far lip.

He tied the rope round the far post. He did it one-handed, slowly, with his teeth for the knot at the end.

Then he looked back across the gap at them. Jab could see his face. It was grey, the way it went when he had been paying.

"There's a rope," said Toren.

---

Callie took Duchess over.

She did not lead her down the steps. There were no steps for a horse. There was the bank, and it was loose, and Duchess put her front feet on it at the top and stopped, and put her ears back, and blew through her nose at the grey.

"She'll not," said Pell, from the shafts.

"She will," said Callie.

She stood below the mare on the slope. She did not pull the rope. She had her strapped arm across her side and the rope loose in her right hand, and she talked to Duchess. Jab could not hear what she said. It was low and flat, the voice she used for a number.

Duchess put one foot down. Then the other. Then she came down the bank the way a horse comes down a bank it does not like, sitting back on her haunches, in a slide of stone and grey. Callie went down in front of her, backward, reading every step, and did not once let the rope go tight.

At the water Duchess stopped again.

It was not the water. Jab saw that. It was the drift. The grey came down to the water on the north side of the post, a pace from her nose, and at the moment she reached it the edge of it slid. A hand's width, toward the water, and stopped. Not at a ten. At nothing.

Duchess threw her head up. The rope came tight all at once, and Jab saw Callie's arm go out after it, the right one, and her body turn with it.

She let go.

She opened her hand and the rope went out of it, and she stood in the bottom of the gap with her empty hand in front of her and her strapped arm across her side, and did not grab again.

Duchess stood with her head up and the rope hanging. She did not run. There was nowhere to run in the bottom of a gap. She stood and blew, with her eyes white.

"Let it run," said Callie, to nobody. "Lead's cheaper than you."

"*Horse*," said Pell. "She's a horse. Not lead."

"It's the same rule," said Callie.

She picked the rope up out of the water slowly, and talked to the mare again. After a while Duchess put her head down and followed her across the post-side stones and up the far bank in a scramble of hooves. Toren stood at the top with his good hand out, not on the rope, only there.

Callie came up after her on Toren's steps. At the top she stood with her hand on the mare's neck. She had her other arm tight across her ribs, and her face was the colour of the drift.

"I let go," she said to Toren. Jab heard it across the gap. "It pulled, and I let go."

"Good," said Toren.

---

Jab went over with the frame.

He went down Toren's steps with his hand on the rope. They came up one at a time in front of him, pale and hard, and went out behind him. Toren was on the far lip setting them, calling *step* for each one. Jab put his boot on them and they held him, and he did not look down at the grey.

He felt the bottom as he came into it.

It was not like Caul Hill. There was no pull in it. There was nothing under the grey drawing at anything. It lay there, the whole soft heap of it on the north side of the post, loose and cold and busy with the hill, and did nothing. It did not want him. It did not want anything.

He was glad of it. He had not known how much he would be.

On the post he stopped for a breath. He did not know why. He stood on the iron with the water running under him and the frame on his back, with every jar they had in it, and looked north along the wash at the drifts going away toward the hill, and at the edge of the sky over it.

It was thickening and thinning. It had no count.

"Jab," said Toren, from above. "Step."

He went up.

---

The far lip was the station.

That was what Toren called it, and Jab did not argue. He stood on the embankment at the top of the far bank, four paces back from the edge, with the frame on his back and nothing to rest it on. The tailboard was on the other side of the gap under the carrier. So he stood.

Callie read the ground under him. "Forty," she said. "Clean enough. It's the road's."

"Then I vent into the road," said Jab.

"Not into my floor," said Callie. Somebody had to say it. Dee was on the other side.

He had the lifter's second jar, and the first. He took the second out of the rack by the neck and pulled the stopper and held it in the crook of his left arm, against his palm. Three and a half in it. It was the lifter's. It was cold, all of it, with nobody in it.

He drew Toren first.

Toren came and stood in front of him on the lip with his arms at his sides, the good one and the cuffed one, and Jab put his right hand flat on his chest, round the white place, and felt.

"Busy," he said. It was the bottom, and the steps, and the bank. It was in the chest and a little in the legs, where the grey had come up off the bank as Toren walked in it. Warm grit. The hill.

He drew. It came up into his palm. The clean cold came through him from the jar into his left hand and held it off his wrist, and he held it high, and turned, and put his palm flat on the stones of the road and let it go down out of him.

"Clean," he said.

"How much?"

Jab weighed the jar on his palm, the way Callie did.

"A quarter," he said. "And the vent."

"That's one crossing," said Toren. He looked back across the gap at the carrier on its board and the bed upside down and the axle on its stones and the two wheels and Pell on his canvas between the shafts. "There's nine more."

---

The wheels went first, because Pell wanted to see how a load behaved before it was him.

Dee let them down. She had the long rope round the near post twice. Jab could see her across the gap, standing back from the post with the rope over her hip and her feet set, the way she had stood at the rail's post on the landing with the ramp going down in front of her. Rook sat on the embankment beside the post with his knees up and his coat over his shoulders.

"One," he called. Jab heard it across the gap. "One wheel. Going down."

The wheel went down the bank on the rope, rolling, with Dee paying out slow. At the bottom it came up against the post, and Toren was there. He had gone back down his steps to the post, and he stood on it and put a strut under the rim of the wheel where it met the iron. It came up at a slant, a short ramp, and the wheel went up it and over the post and down onto the stones on the far side.

"Rolling," called Pell.

Then Callie walked Duchess away from the far lip along the embankment, east, with the other end of the rope on the mare's collar, and the wheel came up the far bank behind her. It came up the whole slope, bumping, rolling where it could and sliding where it could not, with Jab at the top guiding the rope off the edge. It came over the lip onto the bed. Callie stopped the mare.

"One," said Jab.

It had worked. He saw Toren see that it had worked, down in the bottom on the post. He saw him breathe out.

The second wheel went wrong.

It went wrong halfway down the near bank. The rim hit a buried stone that Callie had called *stone* and meant for a boot, not a wheel, and it jumped. It came off the ground and turned in the air and came down on its edge, and the rope jerked round the post, and Jab saw Dee go forward a step with it and set her feet.

The wheel twisted on the rope. It came round broadside. Its weight went sideways off the line of the rope, and the rope slipped up the post, off the stone it had been sitting on.

"Let it run," said Pell. He said it loud.

Dee let it run.

She opened her hands on the rope, and it went out round the post with a hiss, and the wheel went down the rest of the bank on its own. It went fast. It came off the bottom of the slope and hit the stones of the fallen crown, and bounced, and went over the water and over the end of the post. It went into the drift on the north side.

It went in three paces, and fell over on its side, and lay there in the grey with half its spokes showing.

Nobody said anything.

"A hundred and ten," said Callie, beside Jab, with the counter held out over the edge. "Where it's lying."

---

Toren was on the post.

Jab saw him look at the wheel. He saw him look at the grey round it, knee deep and soft, and at the far end of the post, and at his own boots.

Then he saw Toren look up at the far lip. At Jab.

"It's a wheel," said Toren. "We can't go home on one wheel."

"No," said Pell.

"Who goes in?" said Toren.

He did not say *I'll go*. Jab heard him not say it. He heard it the way he heard Callie not say a number when she did not like it. Toren stood on the post and asked it, out loud, to everybody.

"You're in the bottom already," said Dee, from the near lip. "You're the one that's in it."

"I'm in it at eighty," said Toren. "That's a hundred and ten."

"Callie," said Dee. "Read it."

Callie crouched on the far lip. She held the counter out, and looked for a long time at the grey on the north side of the post, the way she looked at a floor. Jab watched her read it.

"The water's in it," she said. "At the edge. Look." She pointed with the hook's end. "Where the drift comes down to the stream, it's dark. It's wet. The stream's been running into the foot of it all night. The wet grey doesn't lift. It's like wet flour. It lies." She moved the hook along. "There's a line of it. Wet. From the end of the post along the water to where the wheel is. A pace wide. If you walk in the wet, you're walking in the stream's side of it, not the drift's."

"How much?" said Toren.

"Eighty on the wet," said Callie. "A hundred and ten on the dry. Don't touch the dry."

"How long?"

"As long as it takes you to put a rope through a wheel with one hand," said Callie.

Toren looked at the wheel.

"Jab," he said. "Throw me the other rope."

---

Jab threw it.

It was the back rope off the carrier's sledge. Pell had had them take it off. It went out over the gap and Toren caught the end of it one-handed against his chest and looked at it.

Then he stepped off the post into the stream.

Jab watched him walk the wet line. It was a pace wide, as Callie had said, a dark band along the foot of the drift where the stream had soaked it. He walked in the water where he could and in the dark grey where he could not. He did not touch the pale grey above it. He went slowly. He went the way Rook had gone up to the drift on the forty-fifth to look, alone, and been told off for it after.

"Glass," said Rook, from the near lip. Jab heard him say it. "Somebody's got the glass."

"I've got it," said Dee. "One."

At the wheel Toren stopped. He was in the stream to his shins. The wheel lay on its side a pace from him, half in the wet grey and half in the dry. He bent. He could not kneel. He put the rope through between two spokes near the hub, one-handed, and brought the end back, and put it through again. Then he stood up and held the end in his teeth and tied it round itself with his good hand.

It took a long time.

"Two," said Dee.

"It's through," said Toren round the rope. He let go of it with his teeth. "It's tied. Take it."

Callie turned Duchess and walked her away along the embankment.

The rope came up tight over the lip beside Jab. It came tight down the far bank, across the stream, to the wheel. The wheel moved. It dragged out of the grey on its side, plowing a furrow through the wet edge, and came across the water and up against the foot of the far bank. Jab went to the edge and guided the rope off the stones as it came.

It came up the bank on its side, like a sledge. It came over the lip.

"Two," said Jab.

"Three turns," said Dee, across the gap. "Toren. Out."

Toren came out. He came up the far bank on his own steps, slowly. At the top he stood in front of Jab without being asked.

"Busy," said Jab, with his palm on his chest.

It was. It was more than the first time. It was in the chest and the belly and the legs to the knee. The drift had come up off the wet edge onto him after all, the way it always did, a little, however careful you were.

He drew it. He held it high and vented into the road, and drew again.

"How much?" said Toren.

"Three quarters," said Jab. "That was a Handful, that wheel."

"That wheel's the way home," said Toren.

Jab weighed the jar.

"Two and a quarter in it," he said. "Out of three and a half."

---

The axle went down and across and up. Then the bed, upside down, on two ropes, with the horse pulling it up the far bank like a sledge and its planks grating on the stones. Then the carrier.

Pell would not let anybody hurry the carrier.

"Slow," he called, from his canvas on the near lip. "Slower than that. It's lead. It doesn't care. Let it not care slowly."

It came down the near bank on its board with Dee paying out round the post, and at the bottom Toren put a strut under the front of the board at the post, a ramp, and it went up and over the iron, and the horse took it up the far side. Jab went to the lip and guided the rope, and did not put his hands on the board, and it came over.

He looked at it on the bed of the embankment. The carrier, grey and dull, with the lid in its groove. Two boxes in it.

"That's the boxes," he said, to nobody.

"That's the boxes," said Callie, beside him. She had the counter out at it. "Nine. At a hand. Same as it was."

He drew Toren again after the carrier, because Toren had been on the post for it. He did not say how much. He weighed the jar and said a number, and Callie said it back.

"One and a half," she said. "In the second."

---

Pell came last of the loads.

She took him up the far bank the way Pell had said a horse would, walking away from the gap along the embankment with her head down and the rope on her collar, and the shafts came up the slope behind her with the canvas between them and Pell in the canvas. "Home," he said. "Three. Not touched."

He drew Pell the rest of the way. He drew Duchess, with his palm flat on her neck where the collar had been, while she stood with her head down and let him. Dee came behind him with the glass in her fist and her eyes on his back.

"Rook," said Dee. "Walk."

He walked. He came up the far bank on Toren's steps, one at a time, slowly, and at the top he stood on the embankment and did not sit down. He was shaking. Jab could see it from four paces off. It was all through him. It was the fever, and the cold, and the bank.

"Come here," said Jab.

Rook came. He stood in front of Jab and let Jab put a palm flat on his chest.

It was two things. Jab could feel them both. There was the grey off the bank, a little, busy, in the chest and legs. He could draw that. And under it, all through, hot, not in bits, there was Rook. There was the thing in his back that was not grey at all, and Rook fighting it.

Jab took his hand away. He held the draw high and vented it into the road.

He had not taken it out since the gate. He had felt it through the cloth every day, and every night, and in the sink, and in the fight. He had not once taken it out to look at it. It lay on his palm now, small and dark and smooth, with its tail and the place where the fin had been. It was the room, Dee had said, at the pump house, with the counter. It was clean.

Rook looked at it.

"He said he's minding me," said Jab. "On the hill. That was his job." He looked at it. "The hill's done. He's only borrowed. I've to bring him back."

"Then that's five days as well," said Rook.

"Five days."

"And don't eat inside," said Rook.

Jab looked up at him. Rook's mouth had moved at the corner.

"He told you that," said Jab.

"He told the whole ward," said Rook. "Twice. Senna told me at the gate, word for word. She said it was the best thing anybody had said in that ward all winter." He looked away east along the embankment, toward the end of it, and the slope beyond, and the road going away over the grey country toward the old north road and the Sag and the gate. "He's four. He'll be doing that chair on his own by now, and ordering the nurses about. You'll come in and he'll not look up."

"I know," said Jab.

He put the fish back inside his coat, on the left side, and buttoned it. He could feel it through the cloth. Home was not a direction. It was four years old and it had a cord wound round its fist, and it was waiting to see if he was asleep.

Dee came last.

She came up Toren's steps to the far lip and stood in front of Jab with the glass in her fist. She held out her hand, palm up, for the jar. He gave it to her.

"Stand still," she said.

He stood still. She put her palm flat on his chest and the jar against her other hand, and he felt her find what was in him. It was the leftovers, the little warm grit that had come into his wrists from every draw. It was not much. She drew it, and held it high, and turned, and put her palm on the road.

"Clean," she said.

He watched her face. It had been white for three days, and it was white now. She took her fingers and put them on her own wrist, the crooked one, and looked at nothing.

"A finger and a bit," she said. "Over." She let go of her wrist. "That's once. That's what I had."

"Toren," said Jab.

Toren came up out of the gap last of all.

He came up the far bank on his own steps with nobody to call them. Each one came up in front of him, pale and short, and he put his boot on it and stood for a breath, and it went out behind him. At the top he stood on the lip and looked back down into the gap at the post, and the water, and the drift with the furrow in it where the wheel had been dragged out.

Jab drew him one more time. He did not ask. Toren stood.

"Busy," said Jab. And then, after a while, "Clean."

"Callie," said Jab.

Callie had the book on her knee on Pell's canvas. She did not have to weigh the jar. She had been counting the whole time.

"The second's done," she said. "Three and a half out of it, and Toren's Handful for the steps last night. The first's open. It had eight." She looked at the jar in Dee's hand. "It's got two left. Six out of it. On Pell, and Duchess, and me, and Rook, and Toren, and Jab, and the vents."

"Say the whole of it," said Toren.

"Ten and a half," said Callie. "For the wash." She wrote it. "I said ten, maybe twelve."

"You said an hour."

"I said an hour," said Callie. She looked at the sun. It was well up over the marsh now, and yellow. "It was an hour and three quarters. The wheel."

"Then write that," said Toren. "The price was right. The time wasn't."

The gap in the embankment, four paces wide, with the post lying across the bottom of it and the water running under. The furrow in the drift. The near lip, with nobody on it now, and the near post with the rope still round it, twice, going across to the far post. The mouth of the cut beyond, seventy paces off. And beyond the cut, over the ridge, a mile off, the pump house, and the river, and the bridge with two poles on it, and the gate with a twist of green wire through its chain, and the hill.

Jab looked at him.

*50th. The wash, in pieces. Out: 6. In: 6. And a horse. And the boxes. 10½ of the lifter's (the price). 1¾ hours (not the price).*

Pell on the canvas between the shafts, with his hands on three jars nobody had opened. Dee with the glass, white. Rook, shaking, standing up because nobody had told him he could sit. Callie on the canvas beside Pell with the book on her knee and her arm across her side. Duchess with her head down, eating nothing, because there was nothing to eat on an embankment.

And Jab, with the frame on his back and a fish buttoned over his heart.

"Six," said Toren. "And a horse."

"Now put it back together," said Pell.

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-42.md

# Chapter 42 — Toren: Backward

The cart lay on the embankment in six pieces, and Toren wanted to pick every one of them up himself.

He knew it was stupid. He knew it the way he knew his arm was in a cuff. He stood on the stone bed of the embankment with the gap behind him and the marsh on his right and the drifts on his left, and looked at the pieces, and his good hand kept closing on nothing.

Two wheels, one of them grey to the hub. The axle on the ground. The bed, upside down, like a boat. The shafts, with the canvas still slung between them. And the carrier on its board, with the lid in its groove.

"Well," said Pell, from the canvas. "Don't stand there looking at it. It won't go together on its own. I've asked."

---

"Tell me how," said Toren.

"I told you how at the pump house."

"Tell me again. In order. Slow." Toren sat down on his heels on the stones beside the canvas, so that his face was near Pell's. "And I'll say who does it."

Pell looked at him.

"That's a new thing," he said.

"It's not new," said Toren. "I did it at the gap."

"You did it at the gap for a gap," said Pell. "This is a cart. It's worse. A gap's only ever the one thing."

He told them. Bed right way up first, on the rope, with the horse, so it's not in the way of anything. Then the axle under it, and the bed onto the axle on its pins. Then the shafts. Then the wheels, last, on the axle arms, and the pins through, and the axle up off its stones.

"And the carrier on last of all," said Pell. "When it's a cart. Not before. You don't put a load on a thing that isn't a thing yet."

Toren said who.

He said Callie at Duchess's head for the bed, because Duchess knew her, and because leading was not lifting. He said Jab and Dee on the axle, one at each end, walking it under. He said Rook on nothing.

"I could hold a pin," said Rook.

"Hold it, then," said Toren. "Sitting down."

He did not say himself. He stood back from the bed with his good hand at his side and watched.

It was the hardest thing he had done since the gap. It was harder than the wheel in the drift. In the drift he had known what to do with his hands. Here there was nothing for them, and he stood with them empty while Callie walked the mare away along the embankment, and the rope came tight, and the bed rolled over onto its wheels-side with a crash of boards, right way up.

"That's the bed," said Pell. "Now the axle. Walk it. Don't carry it. It's iron in the middle."

Jab and Dee walked it. They went crabwise along the bed of the embankment with the axle between them, lifting one end and then the other, the way you walk a heavy chest across a room. Dee's face went white at every lift. Toren saw it. He saw Jab see it too, and take a little more of the next lift, and Dee let him.

He wanted very much to be at the end Dee was at.

"Toren," said Pell. "The pins. Where they go. Show her."

It was a thing Toren could do with one hand. He went to the bed and showed Dee the holes in the bolsters where the pins went down through the bed into the axle's blocks. He held the pins while she knocked them home with a stone. It was a small thing, and slow, and nobody would have looked at it twice.

"There," said Pell. "Now it's a bed on an axle. That's half a cart."

---

The shafts went on. Duchess stood between them while they did it, with Callie's hand on her neck, and blew through her nose at the grey on the canvas.

Then the wheels.

The first wheel was clean. It had come down the bank and up the other side on the rope with nothing worse than a knock. Jab rolled it along the embankment to the near end of the axle, and he and Dee lifted it, and walked it onto the axle arm, and it went on. Pell took one of the linchpins out of his coat pocket and held it out, and Rook took it off him, sitting, and pushed it through the hole in the end of the arm with his thumb.

"There," said Rook. "I've held a pin."

"You have," said Pell. "I saw you."

The second wheel was the one from the drift.

It lay on its side on the embankment where Duchess had dragged it out. The grey was on it still, in the spokes near the hub where the rope had gone through, and all along one side of the tyre where it had lain in the drift. The furrow it had made was grey. The rope was grey.

Callie held the counter to it.

"Sixty," she said. "At the hub. Forty on the tyre. It's the spokes. The grey's gone in round them where they go into the hub. Into the cracks."

"Nobody puts a bare hand on that," said Dee. "Not till it's been in water."

"There's water," said Callie. She looked back at the gap. "At the bottom."

Nobody said anything.

Toren looked at the wheel, and at the gap, and at the stream running white round the fallen stones at the bottom of it, under the post.

"No," he said.

"It's a wheel," said Pell. "It's the other half of the way home."

"It's going on the cart," said Toren. "With cloth on hands. And it gets washed at the first water that isn't in a hole under a drift." He looked at Pell. "When's that?"

Pell thought.

"The new water," he said. "Where the siding goes round. Two days. There's the stream at the washout before that. No. That's after." He shut his eyes. "The new water. The fifty-second. It's not clean. It's not drift."

"Then the fifty-second," said Toren.

---

They put it on with the cloth.

It was the last of the canvas strips from Pell's splint, the ones Dee had cut and never used. She wrapped them round Jab's hands and her own, and they lifted the wheel off the ground, grey side away from them, and walked it onto the axle arm. Rook reached for the second pin from Pell. Dee said, "Not that end. Not your hands," and Rook took his hand back.

Toren pushed the pin through himself. He did it with his right hand, which had a strip of canvas round it now, and he felt the grit in the hole as it went through.

"There," he said.

Then they had to lift the axle off its stones, which was the part Pell had not said.

"How?" said Toren.

"With the cart," said Pell. "You lift the shafts, and the axle comes up off the stones at the back, like a see-saw. Then somebody kicks the stones out. Then you put the shafts down. It's how you tip it, the other way."

"Callie," said Toren.

"I'll kick," said Callie. "I'll not lift."

Jab and Dee lifted the shafts. The bed tipped. The axle came up a hand off its stones. Callie hooked the first stone out with the end of the hook, not her foot, and then the second, and Jab and Dee let the shafts down slowly, and the cart came down onto its wheels.

It stood.

It stood on the embankment on its two wheels with its shafts on the stones and Duchess between them, looking exactly like a cart.

"Now it's a cart," said Pell.

Toren went round it. He went round it once, the whole of it, with his hand out an inch off the boards the way Jab held his hand over a patient, and looked at every joint. Then he stopped at the second wheel.

It was not straight.

He could see it. He crouched and looked along the tyre, and the tyre was not in line with the other one. It leaned out at the top. Not much. A finger's width, at the rim.

"Pell," he said.

"I see it." Pell had turned on the canvas to look. "It's the axle arm. It came down on its edge on the stones in the bottom. It's bent it. Or it's sprung a spoke." He looked at it for a long time. "It'll walk."

"What does that mean?"

"It means it'll go out at the top and in at the bottom every time it goes round," said Pell. "Like a man with a bad knee. It'll rub the hub. It'll sing. It'll wear." He was quiet. "It'll do five days, if nobody asks it to go fast. And if we grease it at every halt. And if somebody looks at it every halt and tells me if the rub's got worse."

"Callie," said Toren.

"I'll look at it," said Callie.

"Every halt," said Toren. "And say it. The same as the ash."

"The same as the ash," said Callie.

---

Loading took a long time, because the pieces of the load had to come to the cart instead of the cart going to them. They tipped it again for the carrier, the way they had at the pump house, with the shafts up and the tail down and the sledge pulled up the bed on its front rope. When the shafts came down the carrier sat on the bed behind the driver's bench, on its board, with two boxes in it.

The home three went into the rack. Pell watched every one of them go in.

Pell went on the cart. Toren had thought about that. He had thought about it on the post with the water going under him. Whether a man with a broken ankle should ride a cart with a walking wheel for five days, or be dragged on two poles behind it. But there was no choosing, because there were only two poles, and they were the shafts.

"I'll ride," said Pell. "I'll ride and I'll listen to it. I'll know before you do if it's going."

Rook went on the canvas beside him, on his side, with his back off the boards.

Jab's frame went into the rack behind the driver's bench, beside the home jars. Dee said so. "It's not coming off your back and onto the ground for five days," she said to Jab, "and you're not carrying eight jars for five days on a palm like that." Jab did not argue.

It was not eight jars now. Toren counted them, because he was counting everything. The fence's fourth, with five in it, and the fifth, whole, with eight. The lifter's first, with two. Callie's clean one, with eight. Callie's dirty one, with four, in the socket where jar B had been. The lifter's second, empty now, and his own lifter's jar, empty. And the socket at the end.

"Say it," he said.

Callie said it. She stood at the tail of the cart with her arm across her side and said it flat.

"The fence's, thirteen. Five in the fourth, eight in the fifth. The lifter's, ten, clean. Two in Jab's first and eight in my first. Four, dirty, in the end. Home, twenty-four, not touched. Settled, none." She stopped. "Water, a skin and a half. Wood, none. Meal—" She looked at the sack in the bottom of the rack. "I'll count the meal tonight."

"Count it now," said Toren.

She looked at him.

"You count everything," said Toren. "Count that. I don't know how much there is. I've been eating it."

---

The meal was barley, ground coarse, in a flour sack from the long building's kitchen. The bread had gone on the forty-seventh. The cold meat had gone the same day. There was a twist of salt in a paper, and there was Duchess's oats, in her nosebag and in a smaller sack, and there was the meal.

Callie measured it in the tin cup, cup by cup, into the pot and back into the sack, with Pell watching and saying nothing.

"Fourteen cups," she said. "And a bit."

"How long's that?"

"At a cup each a day," said Callie, "two days." She put the cup down. "At half a cup, four and a half."

"And we've five," said Toren.

"We've five," said Callie. "If the wheel goes five."

Nobody said anything.

"Half a cup," said Toren. "From tonight. And the rest on the last day." He looked at the sack. "Write it."

"And the horse?" said Pell.

"The horse eats what's growing," said Callie. "There's grass on the old line. Thin. There's more on the north road." She looked at Duchess, who was standing in the shafts with her head down, eating nothing. "Her oats are three days, at what she had coming out. Six at half."

"Half," said Pell. "She'll hate us."

"She already hates us," said Rook, from the canvas. "Since the drift."

"She's right," said Pell. "She's always right. She's a horse."

It was the same thing he had said at the pump house. Toren heard Callie hear it, and her mouth move.

---

They went east along the embankment at the pace the wheel would go.

It sang. Toren heard it at the first turn. It was a long low sound, like a finger drawn round the rim of a cup, and under it, every turn, a small dry knock where the hub rubbed. He walked at the back and listened to it the way he had listened to Callie count a sky, and after a while he could tell the knock was the same every time.

Callie went out in front, twenty paces ahead of Duchess, reading the bed for the wheels. She had the hook for a stick. She did not walk fast. She stopped every twenty paces and turned round and looked at the second wheel, and said, "Same," and turned back.

At the east end of the embankment the old line went up the last slope, long and bare, to the top where they had camped on the forty-fifth. Callie held the counter out at the foot of the slope.

"Fourteen," she said.

"That's the road," said Dee.

Toren stopped at the foot of the slope and turned round.

He could see the whole of it from there. The embankment going away west, two hundred and fifty paces of it across the bottom of the wash, with the drifts on its right and the black marsh on its left and the iron posts leaning along its north edge. The gap, seventy paces short of the far end. The ridge beyond, low and bare, with the cut through it.

And over the ridge, a long way off, the flat top of the hill, and the sky over it.

He did not count it. He did not have to. He could see from here that it had no count. It thickened, and thinned, and thickened again at once, like a man breathing in his sleep.

He thought about the ash lying on the floor of a room under that hill. Knee deep. More than they could lift in a day with nobody hurt. And three stoppered vent jars under the racks, with a chalk cross beside them, and a live box half open, talking to the room. And a gate with a twist of green wire through its chain, the ends left loose.

Somebody would go back there. He did not know who. He did not know when. It would not be tomorrow.

He took the paper out and wrote on it, under the morning. *The wash, behind us. 2 in the afternoon. The second wheel walks (the fall). Meal: 14 cups, half a cup a day. Senna's cycle: the 54th.*

He looked at the last line.

He had not written it before. He had said it at the pump house, the day they decided. *That's Sowerby dosing her off her blood, badly, a day past the cycle.* He had not written it anywhere, because writing it made it a number, and he had not wanted it to be a number.

It was a number. It was four days off.

He folded the paper and put it back in his coat, on the side with the plate, and went up the slope behind the cart.

---

They camped at the top of the slope where they had camped on the forty-fifth, the night before the wash.

It was the same place. Toren knew it by the black ring of their fire on the stones and the thorn they had cut and not burned, still lying where Rook had thrown it. It had been five days. It felt like a year.

He did not start the fire.

He wanted to. He stood by the black ring with his good hand at his side and thought about thorn, and a pot, and water, and who would fetch it. Then he took the paper out, and the pencil, and sat down on a stone, and wrote.

He wrote the evening down the back of the paper the way he had written the ifs at the stove in eleven. One to a line. And after each thing, a name.

*Fire. J. (thorn, the pile by the ring).*
*Water. Boiled. D. W. says how much.*
*The meal. C. counts it into the pot.*
*Duchess. Rubbed down, fed half, hobbled. C. (not lifting).*
*The wheel. Grease. P. says how. J. does it.*
*Rook's back. D. W.*
*Pell's foot. D. W.*
*The draw. None tonight unless D. W. says.*
*Settling. None. (T. at a turn and a bit, own. No stone.)*
*Watches. Two awake. 1st: P. and D. W. 2nd: C. and T. 3rd: J. and P. (if awake).*
*The count. T.*

He looked at it. It was a long list, and there was only one thing on it with his name, and that was the count.

He read it out.

He read it standing by the cold fire ring with the paper in his hand, and they listened, and when he had finished nobody said anything for a moment.

"You've given yourself the count," said Dee.

"I've given myself the second watch as well."

"And the count," said Dee. "That's all."

"I'm at a turn and a bit," said Toren. "Of my own. My stone's gone. My arm's in a cuff." He looked at the paper. "I can't lift an axle. I can't carry a pot. I can count."

Dee looked at him for a long time over the book.

"That's right," she said.

She said it the way she had said it at the gap. It was the second time today. Toren did not know what to do with it. He put it in his pocket with the stone and the plate.

---

Jab lit the fire. He did it with the thorn from the pile, and his flint, and his left hand, and he did not light the knife to do it, though Toren saw him think about it.

Callie counted the meal into the pot. Three cups, for six. She did it with the tin cup and the flat of the knife across the top of each one, level, the way her grandfather had leveled a measure of grain.

Dee did Rook's back by the firelight.

Toren watched from the stone. He watched her take the pad off and look, and put her fingers near the red and take them away. He watched her face.

"It's more," she said.

Rook did not say anything.

"It's not come out further," said Dee. "It's hotter in the middle. There's a place at the bottom of the stitches that's gone yellow." She put the back of her fingers against his neck. "You're hotter than yesterday."

"I walked across a bank," said Rook.

"You walked across a bank," Dee agreed. "On steps. With a hole in your back."

She opened her small bag and took out the last paper. It was folded small and white, tied with its bit of thread. She held it in her fingers and looked at it.

"Keep it," said Rook.

"You said for when you're worse."

"I'm not worse. I'm the same, and hotter." He shut his eyes. "Worse is when I can't get up. I can get up. Keep it for that."

Dee held the paper a moment longer. Then she tied the thread round it again and put it back in the bag.

"I'll give it you tomorrow," she said, "if you're hotter in the morning than you are now. I'm not asking you. I'm telling you what I'll do."

"That's fair," said Rook.

Toren wrote it. *Rook: the back's worse. Yellow at the foot. The last paper kept. D. W. gives it tomorrow if he's hotter in the morning.*

He had not been asked to write it. He wrote it because it was what the paper was for.

---

He had the second watch with Callie.

Pell was awake when they came on. He was always awake. He sat up on the cart with his back to the rack and his leg out, the way he had sat since the forty-sixth, and he did not lie down when Dee did.

"I'll sit," he said. "I'll not sleep anyway. It's the foot."

So there were three of them awake at the second watch: Callie standing by the cart wheel with the hook, because she could not lie down; Toren on the stone by the fire, feeding it thorn, an armful at a time; and Pell on the cart.

It was a long time before anybody said anything.

"You asked who goes in," said Pell.

Toren looked up.

"At the gap," said Pell. "The wheel. You stood on the post and asked it. Out loud. *Who goes in.*"

"I went in."

"You went in," Pell agreed. "I know. I was watching." He was looking at the fire. "But you asked first. You didn't just go. Your father would have just gone."

Toren did not say anything.

"I'm not saying he was wrong," said Pell. "He went first his whole life, and he got a lot of people a long way. I'm saying you asked. And Dee said you were the one in the bottom already. And Callie read the wet. And then you went, because it was yours to go." He moved his leg on the boards and made a small sound. "That's not the same as going because nobody else will. It looks the same, from the lip. It isn't."

"How is it different?"

"The second one, you'd go in anyway," said Pell. "If it was a hundred and forty. If there was no wet. If your arm was off. You'd go because you couldn't stand at the top and not." He looked at Toren. "The first one, you'd not. If it had been a hundred and forty, and no wet, you'd have said *nobody*, and cut the rope, and we'd be going home on one wheel and a drag. And you'd have written it."

Toren looked at the fire.

He thought about the post. About standing on it with the water going under him and the wheel lying in the drift. He had wanted to go. He had wanted it the way he wanted to pick up the axle. And he had asked.

He did not know why he had asked. He had not decided to. It had come out.

"I didn't know I was going to ask," he said.

"No," said Pell. "That's how you know it's yours."

---

The hound came at the end of the second watch.

Toren did not see it. Callie did. She said, "Toren," very quietly, and he looked where she was looking, which was east, into the dark past the edge of the firelight where the old line went on over the top of the slope.

"Two eyes," said Callie. "Low. At the thorn."

He could see it now. It was at the edge of the dark, low down, a shape the colour of the dark with two points of green in it where the firelight caught.

He had built the fire where he had built it on the forty-fifth. Open to the south, where the ground went down to the road. Lit on the south. The dark lane on the east, between the end of the thorn pile and the edge of the light, three paces wide. He had built it without thinking. His hands had done it.

The hound went along the edge of the light. It did not come in. Hounds did not come in at a light. It went round to the east, to the dark, to the lane, the way they always went.

He stood up.

He had a turn and a bit of his own, and no stone. He could light the heel of the spike for as long as a turn and a bit would hold it, and it would fence one hound for most of that. It would put him at his floor before morning, with the washout four days off and the Sag after it.

"Jab," he said.

Jab was asleep by the fire. He was not asleep by the time Toren said it. He was up on one knee with his hand on his coat before the word was finished.

"There's a hound," said Toren. "At the lane. I'm at a turn. You're at a hand and a bit." He did not say anything else.

Jab looked at him. Then he looked at the lane.

"Mine," said Jab.

"Yours," said Toren.

Jab went to the lane. He stood in it, with the end of the thorn pile at his left shoulder and the edge of the firelight at his right, where Toren had stood on the forty-first. The knife came on his left fist, dark. He lit the hook of it and nothing else, an inch of pale grey light at the tip.

The hound came up the lane at the dark and met the light, and stopped.

It stood a pace from the end of the knife with its head low and its green eyes on the hook. It did not come on. It did not go back. It stood there. Jab stood there. Neither of them moved.

"It's only the hook," said Callie quietly. "He's lighting a finger of it."

"I know," said Toren.

It went on for a long time. Toren counted it. He could not help counting it. At two hundred breaths the hound put its head down lower and went back into the dark, east, the way it had come, and did not come round again.

Jab let the hook go dark.

He came back to the fire and sat down by it and put his left hand on his coat.

"A finger of the hook," he said. "For two hundred breaths." He looked at his fist. "I'd have lit the whole belly, once. The first year."

"You told me," said Toren. "Your aunt stood behind you at the racks."

"And said I was lighting a whole knife to cut a string," said Jab. "She'd have liked that hound. It never got cut at all."

Toren wrote it. *2nd watch. A hound at the lane. J. (the hook only). 200 breaths. No kill. No ash.* And under it, because it was true: *T. asked. J. took it.*

Pell had watched the whole of it from the cart without saying anything.

"There," he said, when Toren had put the pencil away. "That's two."

---

In the morning Rook was hotter.

Toren knew it before Dee said it, because Jab was already kneeling by the canvas with his palm an inch over Rook's back and his face gone still.

"It's him," said Jab. "All of it. More than yesterday."

Dee put two fingers on Rook's wrist and the back of her other hand against his neck. She did not say anything. She opened the bag and took out the last paper, and untied the thread, and stirred the powder into a cup of the water she had boiled last night.

"You said you'd tell me," said Rook.

"I'm telling you," said Dee. "Drink it."

He drank it. He made the face.

"That's the last of Sowerby's," said Dee. She put the empty paper back in the bag, folded, as if it were worth keeping. "There's nothing after that but getting you to him."

"Four days," said Rook.

"Four days and a bit," said Toren. "Pell?"

Pell had the atlas open on his knee in the grey light.

"The fifty-fifth," said Pell. "If the wheel goes. If the siding's dry. If the washout's no worse than it was." He shut the atlas. "If."

Toren wrote it.

*51st. The camp above the wash. Out: 6. Rook: the last paper. Home the 55th, if.*

Then he stood up and counted them. He did it out loud, which he did not always do.

"Pell," he said. "Rook. Dee. Jab. Callie."

He looked at Duchess, in the shafts, with her ears back at the second wheel.

"Six," he said. "And a horse. And a wheel that walks."

"Say the rest," said Callie.

He looked at her.

"You said *out*," said Callie. "Every day since the gate. You've never once said where to."

Toren looked east along the old line, where it went away over the grey country toward the siding, and the new water, and the junction with the iron posts, and the old north road, and the Sag, and the gate. Four days. Four days and a bit. Somewhere at the end of it an old woman was lying on a pillow on the fourth bed on the right, and in three days a man with a white hand would sit down at a machine that sang flat. He would warm it, and warm it, and guess.

"Home," he said.

Callie nodded once and went out in front, twenty paces ahead of the horse, reading the road for the wheels.

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-43.md

# Chapter 43 — Callie: Half a Cup

The second wheel said the same thing all day.

Callie listened to it from twenty paces in front. She could not help listening. It was a long low note, the sound of a wet finger round the rim of a cup, and under it, once every turn, a dry knock where the hub came round and rubbed. She had counted the knocks for the first mile without meaning to. Then she made herself stop, because there was no end to counting a wheel.

At every halt she went back and looked at it.

She crouched by it, with her arm across her side and the hook for a stick, and looked along the tyre against the other one, and put her palm flat on the hub to feel how warm it was. Then she said it. She said it the same way she said the ash.

"Same," she said, at the first halt. "It's the same."

"Same," she said, at the second.

At the third halt, in the middle of the afternoon on the fifty-first, she crouched by it for longer.

"It's warmer," she said. "The hub. It's not leaning more. It's warmer."

"Grease," said Pell, from the cart.

Jab greased it. There was a pot of grease in the rack, black and thick, the size of a fist. Pell told him how much, which was less than Jab wanted to use. Jab put it on the end of a stick and worked it in round the hub while Pell listened.

"Same," said Callie, at the fourth.

---

It was the fifty-first day, and it was cold, and the wind had come round to the north in the night.

She read the road for it. She could not read the road for anything else. She could not carry. She could not lift anything heavier than the counter or Miss Wren's book. Miss Wren had given her the book to carry in a canvas bag on her right side, because her left arm was strapped across her ribs under her coat and would not go out without the ribs saying so.

She could read.

She went twenty paces in front of Duchess with the hook for a stick and read the old line for the wheels. It was the road they had come out on. She knew it. She had read it going west, a stone at a time, with the lee rolling beside her on the back of the curve. She read it now going east, and it was the same road, and it was not.

The first thing was the drift.

It was the one Rook had walked down to alone on the forty-fifth, at the foot of a long slope, in the low place where the old line went over a dip. She had read it then at twenty and thirty-one. She saw it from the top of the slope, grey and soft and long, lying in the dip to the north of the road like a sleeping animal.

"Wait," she said.

They waited. She went down the slope ahead of the cart with the counter out.

"Twenty-six," she said, at the edge of the road nearest the drift. And then she looked at the drift itself, for a long time.

On the forty-fifth it had lain still. Then on the afternoon of the forty-sixth, at the wash, all the loose grey in the world had moved west, up the grade toward the hill, ten and three. She had not seen this drift then. She did not know what it had done.

She could see what it had done now.

The top of it was not smooth. It was combed, the way the yard inside the fence had been combed, in fine lines. But the lines did not all go one way. Most of them ran west, toward the hill, where they had been going when it stopped. Some of them had slumped across the others. And at the east end of the drift, nearest the road, the lines went east.

Not many. A few. Fine, like hairs. They went east off the drift across the edge of the road, and stopped.

She watched. She watched for a hundred breaths, because Pell had taught her that two samples were not a trend, and one look was not even one sample.

Nothing moved.

Then one of the east lines slid. A hand's width. East. It stopped.

"Callie," said Toren, from the cart.

"It moved," she said. She did not turn round. "One line. East. Not at a ten." She watched a while longer. "It's stopped."

"Write it," said Toren.

She could not write it and hold the counter. So she said it again, slowly, and Toren wrote it on the back of the paper, walking. *The drift where Rook went (45th). Combed, most lines west. Some east, at the road end. One slid east, once, not at a count.* She heard the pencil.

She did not say what she thought, because she did not know what she thought. She walked on past the drift, reading the road for the wheels.

---

They ate at dusk, at a place with no walls, on the bare top of a rise.

Half a cup each. Callie counted it into the pot, three cups for six, level, with the flat of her knife across the top of the tin. Jab had the fire going with the last of the thorn from the wash camp. Miss Wren boiled the meal with the salt and a little water, and it came up thick and grey and hot, and she put it into six cups and handed them round.

It was not enough. Callie knew exactly how much it was not enough. She had counted the sack.

"Eleven cups," she said, when she had put the sack back. "And a bit."

"Say it," said Toren.

"Eleven, and a bit. Three a night." She did the sum. "Three nights and two cups."

"And then?"

"And then it's the fifty-fifth," said Callie. "And we're home. Or we're hungry."

"Or both," said Rook, from the canvas.

He had eaten his half cup lying on his side, slowly, with Miss Wren holding it. The last paper had gone into him that morning. He had slept most of the day on the cart. His face was wet and grey, but he had eaten.

"Sowerby's kitchen does a porridge," said Rook, "that's worse than this. With lumps in it you could build a wall with." He shut his eyes. "I'll be eating it in four days. I'll think of this and weep."

"You'll complain about it," said Miss Wren.

"I'll complain about it," Rook agreed. "Weeping."

Pell laughed. It was the first time Callie had heard Pell laugh since the drift. It was short, and it hurt his leg, and he stopped.

---

On the fifty-second it rained.

It came in the night, out of the north, and it did not stop. Callie heard it start. She was standing by the cart wheel on the second watch with Toren, because she could not lie down. It came in fine and cold, and then heavier, and put the fire out, and ran off the brim of her hat in a line.

"The skins," said Miss Wren, from under the cart.

She was already up. She had the canvas off the rack, not the one Pell was on but the old one they had used for the carrier, and she held it out flat by two corners with the middle sagging. Jab took the other two corners. The rain came down into it and ran to the middle and lay there. Miss Wren held the first skin's mouth under the lowest corner, and Jab tipped, and the water ran in.

"That's clean," said Miss Wren. "Sky water. It's the only clean thing we've had in a week."

"Read it," said Callie.

She held the counter to the canvas. Seven. The room. She held it to the skin. Seven.

"It's clean," she said.

By light they had both skins full, and the pot, and Pell's hat, which he had taken off and held out upside down in the rain without being asked, and passed down to Miss Wren full. Miss Wren drank out of it.

"It tastes of your head," she said.

"It tastes of rain," said Pell. "My head's been in there thirty years. It's clean."

The rain did not stop. They went on in it, because there was nothing else to do. The old line went east over the grey country with water standing in its ruts, and the wheel sang, and the rain ran off everybody. Duchess walked with her head down and her ears flat. Pell sat on the cart with the canvas over him and the atlas under his coat. Rook lay under the canvas beside him.

By the middle of the morning, Callie's ribs had started to shake.

She did not know it was the ribs at first. She thought it was her. She was cold, and wet through, and walking, and her whole body was shaking the way it shook in the pen after the red. But it was the ribs. The cold had got in under the strapping and every shiver went into the place where they were cracked, and the place said so.

She walked. She read the road. She did not say anything about it.

"Callie," said Miss Wren, at the halt.

"The wheel's the same," said Callie.

"I didn't ask about the wheel." Miss Wren put the back of her hand against Callie's neck, under the collar, and took it away. "You're shaking."

"Everybody's shaking."

"Everybody's cold. You're shaking in the side." Miss Wren looked at her. "You'll ride."

"There's no room."

"There's room at the tail, if Rook moves his feet."

"I'll not ride," said Callie. "If I ride I can't read. If I can't read, the wheel goes in a hole."

Miss Wren looked at her for a long moment in the rain.

"Then you'll walk," she said. "Beside the cart. Not in front. Toren reads."

"Toren can't read ground."

"Toren can read a hole in a road in the rain as well as anybody," said Miss Wren. "He's been watching you do it for fifty days. It's not the pen. It's a road." She did not raise her voice. "You'll walk beside the cart with your hand on it, and you'll tell him if he's wrong. That's a job."

Callie looked at Toren.

He had heard. He was standing at the back of the cart with the rain running off his hat and his arm in its cuff. He did not say anything. He did not look away.

"I'll tell you if you're wrong," said Callie.

"I know you will," said Toren.

He went out in front. She walked beside the cart with her right hand on the rail, and every time she started to go forward to look at the road, she stopped herself. He read it. He read it slowly, stopping more than she would have, crouching at the soft places with his good hand flat on the ground. He did not read it the way she did. But he did not put a wheel in a hole.

At the fourth soft place, she saw him stop and look at a stone in the road and then walk round it on the high side, the way she would have. She had not said anything.

She did not say anything then, either. She held the rail and walked, and the cart went on, and her ribs shook.

The siding came in the afternoon.

It was where the old line had gone into the new water on the forty-third, a mile-wide bottom of grey water with the rails going into it and not coming out. They had gone round by the siding, north, on a raised bed, and camped where it rejoined the line west of the water. Now they came to the west end of it from the other side.

The new water was higher. Callie could see that from the cart. The rain had been running into it all day. It lay across the bottom of the country, grey under grey sky, with the rain making rings on it.

"It's come up the siding bed," said Toren, from in front. "A hand. It's over the bed at the low end."

She wanted to go and look. She stood at the cart rail with her hand on it.

"Is it moving?" she said. "The water. Over the bed."

Toren crouched. He looked at it for a while.

"No," he said. "It's lying on it."

"Then it's a hand of water on a stone bed," said Callie. "The wheels'll go through a hand."

"The wheel," said Pell. "The one that walks. Will it go through a hand?"

"It'll go through a hand of water," said Callie, "if nobody asks it to go fast."

"Then nobody's asking," said Pell.

And that was where they washed the wheel.

Pell had said the new water. It was not clean, but it was not drift. They stopped at the edge where the siding bed went into the flood, and Jab and Miss Wren wound the canvas strips round their hands. Toren held Duchess. They walked the cart forward until the second wheel stood in the water to the hub, and then Jab and Miss Wren went into the water beside it and scrubbed it. They did it with a handful of reeds from the edge, spoke by spoke, round the hub where the rope had gone through and the grey had gone into the cracks. It came off in the water in a thin grey cloud and went away.

Callie held the counter to it when they had finished.

"Twenty," she said. "At the hub. It's in the wood. It'll not come out of the wood." She held it to the tyre. "Twelve."

"Twenty's a wheel," said Miss Wren.

"It's a wheel," said Callie. "It's not a wheel I'd put my hand on."

"Nobody's asking you to," said Miss Wren. She came out of the water with her hands held out from her sides, and stood in front of Jab. "Draw me. And then I'll draw you. Off the first jar. And then we'll be clean, and it'll be a quarter, and Callie can write it."

Jab drew her. She drew him. Callie wrote it.

*52nd. The new water. The second wheel washed (D. W., J.). Hub 20, tyre 12. ¼ off the lifter's first (J. and D. W.). It's in the wood.*

The ditch was on the siding, where they had left it.

Callie knew the place before they got to it. There was the bend in the siding bed, and the long straight after, and on the north side of the bed the ditch, with water standing in it now. On the edge of the ditch, where they had left it on the forty-third, there was a flat stone.

The chalk cross had gone off it in the rain. There was only the stone, grey and wet, with the rain running off it into the ditch.

Under it, she knew, there was a vent jar, sealed. And in the ditch beside it, three coils of copper wire that a man with no hands had stripped out of somewhere near the wash and dragged forty miles on a handcart with a girl, because he did not know what else to do.

"Forty," she said, at a pace from it. "It's the coils. They're still hot."

She stood by the cart rail and looked at the stone. Nobody would know, now, that there was anything under it.

"Toren," she said.

He had stopped. He was looking at it too.

"The chalk's gone," she said.

He did not say anything. He took Pell's chalk out of his coat pocket and went to the stone and crouched, with the rain running off his hat, and drew the cross on it again, big, with the chalk held flat so it went on thick. The rain started to take it off at once. He drew it again over the top.

"It'll go," said Callie.

"I know it'll go," said Toren. He stood up. "Somebody might come before it does."

That night Callie was wrong.

She knew she was wrong while she was doing it, which was the worst part. She did it anyway.

They were camped at the east end of the siding, where it left the old line at the edge of the new water. They had stood there at noon on the forty-third and chosen it. There were no walls. There was nothing to burn but reeds, and the reeds were wet. Jab got a fire going with them in the end, a small smoky one that would not boil the pot, and they ate the meal half cooked, three cups for six.

"Eight cups," said Callie, putting the sack away. "And a bit."

Nobody said anything.

"If we went on," said Callie.

Toren looked up.

"If we went on tonight," she said. "It's not raining now. It's stopped. There's a moon behind the cloud. I could read by a lantern. We'd be at the junction by morning. That's half a day." She heard her own voice. It was not flat. "That's the fifty-fourth, at the north road, not the fifty-fourth at the washout. Half a day nearer."

"To what?" said Miss Wren.

Callie did not answer.

"To the fifty-fourth," said Miss Wren. "At the shed. At the ninth hour."

"Half a day nearer," said Callie.

"Half a day nearer to being a day late," said Toren.

He said it quietly. He had the paper on his knee with the pencil on it and he did not pick the pencil up.

"We're not there on the fifty-fourth," he said. "Not if we walk all night. Not if we walk all tomorrow night. The fifty-fourth's gone. You said it. At the pump house. *Nothing's the fifty-fourth.*"

"I know what I said."

"Then why?"

She did not know why. She stood by the smoky fire with her arm across her ribs, shaking a little still from the day, and she did not know why. She only knew that the whole of her wanted to be moving toward the gate. That standing here at the west end of the siding, doing nothing, was the worst thing she had done since the door.

"Because I can't count it from here," she said.

It came out before she decided to say it.

"I've counted everything," she said. "The ash, and the meal, and the wheel, and the sky, and the drifts, and the knocks in the hub. I've counted the lot. I can't count her. I can't count any of them. Not Senna on the bed. Not Tonk. Not my mother." She stopped. "I can't count them from here, and I can't get there. And if we walked, at least I'd be doing the thing that gets there."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"The wheel," said Pell.

She looked at him.

"You walk that wheel at night," said Pell, "on the old line, with the siding under water and ruts full of rain, and you'll not see it go. You'll hear it. I'll hear it. And then we'll be at the junction on the fifty-fourth with a cart on one wheel and Rook lying in a rut and me sitting in the road." He did not say it unkindly. "You'd not be doing the thing that gets there. You'd be doing the thing that doesn't."

"I know," said Callie.

"I know you know," said Pell. "I'm saying it so it's been said."

It was what he had said at the pump house. Callie heard it. She stood by the fire and felt it land, the way the push had landed on the lee.

"Your mother's been counting herself for fifty-two days," said Rook, from the canvas. He did not open his eyes. "She did it before you were born. She'll have done it every morning you've been gone. And the weighman, and Katori. They've all been counting her." He shifted his shoulders on the canvas and made a small sound. "It's not in your hands. It's never been in your hands. It's been in theirs the whole time. That's why you could go."

Callie did not say anything.

She thought about her mother on the edge of the bed, on the fortieth, opening Callie's fingers off her wrist one at a time. *Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.* She thought about the door, and her back to the room, and *busy*.

She had done it once. She had held the door and not looked round. She had thought, doing it, that she had learned it.

She had learned it once.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"For what?" said Toren.

"For asking."

"You didn't ask," said Toren. "You said *if*. That's allowed. That's what the paper's for." He picked up the pencil. "I'll write it. *C.: walk tonight? No. The wheel.* And you can say it again tomorrow night if you want. And we'll say no again."

"You'll say no again."

"We'll say no again," said Toren. "Every night, if you want. It's cheaper than a wheel."

Jab laughed. He was sitting by the smoky fire with his hands held out to it and his face black with the reed smoke, and he laughed, short, and looked surprised at himself.

"What?" said Callie.

"*It's cheaper than a wheel*," said Jab. "Everything is. We've found the one thing on the whole road that's dearer than Rook."

"Nothing's dearer than Rook," said Rook, with his eyes shut. "Write that."

Toren wrote it.

The junction came on the fifty-third, at noon.

There was the place where the old line came in from the west across the fields and met the old north road beside its line of iron posts. They had camped against its bed on the forty-second, the night before Toren wrote *Tomorrow's Gran's*. They turned south onto the north road, and Callie felt the whole party turn with it, the way a sledge feels a rope come round a post.

South. The gate was south. Two days.

The rain had stopped in the night. The road was wet and the ditches on either side of it were full. The sky had lifted and gone pale.

And the grey in the ditches was lying wrong.

She saw it at the first culvert south of the junction. They had come over it on the forty-second, going north, an hour short of the junction, and she had read it then because she read every culvert. There had been two husk lines there on the forty-second, crossing the road from the culvert, fine, like the lines a snail leaves. She had counted them.

There were six now.

She stopped in front of it and held the counter out.

"Thirty," she said.

"What was it?" said Toren.

"Twelve," said Callie. "On the forty-second. At the culvert. I said it at the halt." She was looking at the lines. They came up out of the culvert mouth on the west side and crossed the road and went into the ditch on the east. Six, in the wet. And in the ditch, where the water lay, there was grey. It lay in the water in fine combed lines, like the yard at Caul Hill.

They pointed south.

Not all of them. Most. They lay along the bottom of the ditch under the standing water, pointing down the road, the way the road went.

"Toren," she said.

He came and stood beside her.

"It's lying south," she said. "In the ditch. Like it was lying west at the hill."

"Is it moving?"

She watched. She watched for a hundred breaths. The grey lay in the water, combed, pointing south, and did nothing.

"No," she said. "Not while I'm looking."

"Then it's lying south," said Toren. "That's all we know."

"That's all we know," Callie agreed.

He wrote it. *53rd. The first culvert south of the junction (42nd: 2 husk lines, 12). Now: 6 lines, 30. Grey in the ditch lying south.*

She went on. At the second culvert there were four lines, and twenty-eight. At the third there were five, and the grey in the ditch lay south, and while she was watching, one line of it slid. A hand's width. South. It stopped.

Not at a ten. At nothing.

"That's three," she said.

"Three what?"

"Three culverts," said Callie. "Three times more than it was. Three ditches, lying the same way." She did not look at him. "That's enough to say it's more. I'll not say what it is."

"Then say it's more."

"It's more," said Callie. "Between here and the fold. And it's lying the way we're going."

Toren looked down the road. South, where the north road went on over the grey country in the pale afternoon, toward the drovers' fold at the twentieth stone and the ruined corner on the rise, and the Sag, and the gate.

He did not say anything for a while.

"Dessa's got a watch on the Sag," he said.

"I know."

"She'd have had it every day. Since the forty-first." He looked at the ditch. "Katori's blade, and Ines, and Otto, and the lifters. And Wyck teaching them to stand."

"I know," said Callie.

"Then that's what's there," said Toren. He put the paper away. "We'll know tomorrow night. We'll know when we see it. I'll not have us walk a day expecting a thing because of what's in a ditch."

"I'll not say it again," said Callie.

"Say it at every culvert," said Toren. "The number. Not what it is."

She said it at every culvert. There were four more before dark. She said the number at each one and the number of lines, and which way the grey lay, and it lay south every time.

At the halt before dark Pell said the knock had changed.

Callie had not heard it. She had been reading culverts. She went back to the wheel and crouched and put her palm on the hub. It was hot. Not warm. Hot, like a pot lid.

"It's gone further out," she said. She looked along the tyre. "Two fingers. At the rim. It was one."

"A spoke's sprung," said Pell. "Near the hub. I can hear it go every turn. It's loose in its hole, and the wheel's leaning on it."

"Can we mend it?"

"We can wedge it," said Pell. "If we'd a wedge. And we'll grease it. If we'd grease."

Callie looked at the grease pot. There was a smear of black in the bottom of it.

"There's none," she said.

"There's tallow," said Jab.

They looked at him.

"The lantern wicks," said Jab. "They're in tallow. There's a pot of it in Dee's bag for the lanterns. Tallow's grease. It's what they grease a hub with at home when there's nothing better."

"Then we'll have no light," said Miss Wren.

"We'll have less light," said Jab. "We've two nights. We'll have a fire."

Miss Wren looked at the tallow pot for a moment. Then she gave it to him.

Rook made the wedge.

He had been lying on the canvas all day with his eyes shut, hot, and he sat up to do it without anybody asking. He took the stub of wood Toren had brought him, a piece split off the end of a thorn root at the fire, and his own knife. He sat with his back against the rack and whittled, slowly, with the shavings falling on his coat.

"You're off," said Miss Wren.

"I'm whittling," said Rook. "It's not working. It's a knife and a stick." He turned the wedge over and looked at it. "My mother could do this in the dark. I'm doing it with the light and a fever and it's worse than hers." He held it out. "There."

Jab drove it in with a stone, beside the loose spoke where it went into the hub. He greased the hub with tallow off his finger. Pell listened.

"Better," said Pell. "Not good. Better."

Callie put her palm on the hub.

"Same," she said.

"Say it at every halt," said Pell. "Tomorrow."

"Every halt," said Callie.

The fifty-fourth was the washout.

She knew it when they got there, the way you know a word before you have finished reading it. It was the place where a culvert had gone on the forty-second and the stream came across the road eight paces wide. Toren had set his first strut into mud there and it had sunk, and he had fallen like a sack and kept his left arm in. And Pell had taken the empty cart down laid over to one side, with everything off it on stones and canvas on the far bank.

The stream was higher. It had been raining into it for two days.

"Stone," said Callie. She had gone to the edge. She was allowed that. She held the hook out flat over the water and pointed. "The shelf's there. Under the brown. Where it was. It's deeper on it. A hand more." She moved the hook. "Soft, on the near side. Soft. Stone. The far side's stone all along."

"Then we do it the same," said Toren.

"The carrier," said Pell.

Toren looked at it. On the forty-second the carrier had been empty. Now there were two boxes in it, and it was lead, and it did not go on anybody's back.

"It stays on," said Toren. "The rest comes off. The frame, and the home three, and the meal, and Rook." He looked at Pell. "And you."

"I'll stay on," said Pell. "I'll sit on the high side. I'll lean. You'll want weight on the high side." He looked at the water. "It's what I'd tell anybody."

Toren looked at him for a long moment.

"The high side," he said.

"I've a broken ankle," said Pell. "I've not a broken head."

So they took everything off but the carrier and Pell. Jab and Miss Wren carried the frame over between them on a pole, and the home three one at a time in their arms, through the water to their knees, and set them on the canvas on the far bank. Callie did not carry anything. She stood at the edge with the counter and the book and watched them go, and said *stone* at every step.

Rook walked over by himself, slowly, with Miss Wren at his side, not touching.

Then Toren set.

He had settled half a Handful the night before at the fire, out of the lifter's first jar. Callie had watched him do it. It had taken him most of half an hour. Miss Wren had asked him why, when there was nothing to hold. He had said, "The washout," and then, "I'm the first one full. The night before. You said." Miss Wren had looked at him and not answered. She had said it in the shed on the thirty-second, when he took a tenth in a hundred and four breaths and she told him he would never take ash in a hurry.

He stood in the stream now, on the stone shelf, with the water to his knees, and waited for the cart.

Pell took Duchess's rope from the cart himself, sitting on the high side, leaning. Jab led her. The cart came down into the water with Pell's weight on the high side and the carrier in the middle, and the walking wheel on the soft side, going round, singing.

"Now," said Callie. "It's at the soft."

"Set," said Toren.

The strut came up out of the stone shelf at a slant under the axle, beside the walking wheel, where the wheel would be. The wheel came down onto the soft and began to go, and the strut was there.

"Holding," said Toren.

It held. The cart went on. The wheel came up off the soft onto the stone. Toren let the strut go. At the end. Not after.

"Rolling," said Pell.

And that was the whole of it, going this way. Four breaths. The cart came up out of the water on the far side with water running off its bed and the walking wheel singing, and Pell sitting on the high side with his face grey, leaning.

---

She had tried to help.

She had not told anybody she was going to. When the cart came down into the water, she had crouched at the edge on the stone. She had put the back of the hook's curve down on it, still, where the stream came round the end of the shelf, and reached for her own reserve. It was three parts back. She had meant to put a lee in the water, on the upstream side of the cart. Only a small one. Only enough to take the push of the current off the walking wheel while it was on the soft.

The Ward went into the curve.

The lee started to stand up. She felt it start, the pale wall beginning off the outside of the steel.

And her ribs went.

Not broke. Went. The lee wanted her whole body behind it, the way a door did, the way it always had. Her body was not there. It was across her side under the strapping, shaking, and the moment the Ward asked for it the place where the ribs were cracked said *no*, very clearly, all the way down.

She counted. She did not know why. Four. Five. Six.

"I'm stopping," she said.

She let it go. The lee went out before it had ever properly stood up. It had not done anything. The current had gone round it the whole time.

Nobody had seen. Toren was in the stream with his back to her. Miss Wren was on the far bank.

Jab had seen.

He was standing in the water at Duchess's head, and he had turned round, and he was looking at her. He did not say anything. He looked at her crouched on the stone with the hook and her arm across her side and her face doing whatever it was doing.

"Six," she said to him, across the water. "Six breaths. That's what I've got."

Jab looked at her a moment longer.

"That's six more than Toren needed," he said.

And he turned back to the horse.

At the ninth hour Toren stopped.

They were a mile south of the washout, with everything back on the cart and the walking wheel singing. The sun was out, pale and thin, and the road ran south in front of them over the rise toward the drovers' fold. Callie was twenty paces in front of Duchess again, because Miss Wren had said the road was dry now and she could read it.

She heard him stop. She heard the cart stop because Toren had stopped. She turned round.

He was standing at the back of the cart with his good hand at his side, looking south.

He did not say anything. Nobody asked him. Callie knew what hour it was. She had known it all morning, the way you know a thing you have been trying not to count. The ninth hour on the fifty-fourth. At the shed, at the yellow line, two orderlies would be lifting an old woman off a bed into a chair. One of them would push her down the long slope while she complained about the camber. And at the bottom, a man with a white hand would sit down at a machine that sang flat, and warm it.

He would warm it and warm it. Or he would not warm it at all, and Doctor Sowerby would take the blood out of her arm and do the other thing, badly.

Callie did not know which. Nobody on the road did.

She stood in the road twenty paces in front of the horse and looked at Toren, and he looked south, and after a while she turned and looked south with him. There was nothing to see. There was the road, going over the rise, and the grey country, and the pale sky.

She did not count anything.

She thought about her mother on the stool in the weighbridge hut. The ninth hour was the hour of the morning lift, when there was a lift, which there had not been since the twenty-fifth. So the ninth hour would be the watch's jars, the dirty ones, which Otto brought up from the lip. Her mother would be sitting on the stool with the tally board on her knee and her stick against the wall. She would read the beam left-handed, and say the number, and write it in big careful figures, and read it back to the old man in the apron.

Or she would not. She might be in the ward, with the right side gone. She might be having a morning where the words came and a morning where they did not.

Callie did not know. She stood in the road and did not know, and she let herself not know it. She did not count it.

It felt like the ache in the palm after lifting.

"That's the ninth hour," said Toren, after a long time.

"I know," said Callie.

He took the paper out and wrote on it. She did not see what. He put it away and walked on, and the cart went on, and she turned and went on in front of it, reading the road.

They camped that night at the ruined corner on the rise.

It was the same corner as the forty-first: two walls, north and west, meeting, and open to the south and east. The cart went in against the north wall with the jars to the stone. Duchess was tied short to the wheel, facing out. The fire went out to the south-east. The dark lane was the four paces between the east end of the north wall and the edge of the light. Toren built it without looking at the paper. His hands built it.

"Two cups," said Callie, at the meal sack. "That's the last. That's tonight."

"And tomorrow?" said Rook.

"Tomorrow's the gate," said Toren.

"If," said Pell.

"If," said Toren.

Callie counted the two cups into the pot, and the flat of the knife across the top of each one, and then she turned the sack inside out and shook it over the pot. Barley dust came out of the seams, a pinch, grey.

"That's all of it," she said.

Miss Wren measured them at the fire after they ate. She did it with two fingers on each wrist, the way she always did. Callie watched her face. It was not white any more. It had not been white since the new water.

"Your own's all back," she said to Callie. "The ribs aren't." She held the wrist a moment longer. "You want to settle."

"I want to settle half," said Callie.

"For what?"

Callie did not answer at once.

"For the Sag," she said. "For tomorrow. In case."

"In case what?"

"I don't know," said Callie. "Seven culverts. All lying south." She looked at the fire. "I'll not hold anything on these ribs. I know that. I had six breaths at the washout."

Miss Wren looked at her.

"I saw," she said. "From the bank."

"Then you know I'll not hold anything." Callie looked at her hands. "But I'd like to have it. If there's a thing to hold. Half. So it's there."

Miss Wren was quiet for a long time.

"Half," she said. "Off your first. And you'll not spend it unless Toren or I say. And if you spend it, you'll stop when the ribs say, not when the stone says."

"When the ribs say," said Callie.

Toren settled a Handful.

He did it without being asked, on the square of paper at the fire, out of the last of the lifter's first jar. It took him most of an hour. When it was done the first jar had a quarter in it, and Callie weighed it, and said it.

"The lifter's first, a quarter. My first, seven and a half. That's seven and three quarters, clean. The fence's, thirteen. Dirty, four. Home, twenty-four, not touched." She stopped. "Settled: a Handful in Toren. Half in me. Water, two skins. Meal, none."

"And the wheel?" said Pell.

Callie went and crouched by it and put her palm on the hub.

"Same," she said.

Toren wrote it all. Then he wrote the watches, and the names after them, and read them out. Then he stood up and counted them, out loud.

"Pell. Rook. Dee. Jab. Callie." He looked at the mare. "Six. And a horse."

"Say where to," said Callie.

"Home," said Toren. "Tomorrow."

She had the second watch with him again.

The fire was down. Pell was awake on the cart, because Pell was always awake. The others were asleep: Rook on the canvas, hot, Jab by the fire with his hand on his coat, Miss Wren under the cart with her book.

South of them, a long way off, a day's walk, was the Sag. She could not see it. She knew exactly where it was. It was over the rise, and over the next rise, down the long straight of the north road past the culvert where a boy had once knelt in the angle of a wall, and then the dip.

"Toren," she said.

"What."

"The last culvert. Before dark." She did not look at him. "It had nine."

"Nine lines," she said. "And forty. And the grey in the ditch lying south." She waited. "I said the number."

"You said the number."

"I'm saying the number again," said Callie. "I'm not saying what it is."

"We'll know tomorrow," he said.

"We'll know tomorrow," said Callie.

She stood and let it be what it was, and the fire burned down, and Pell on the cart shifted his leg and made a small sound. Somewhere a long way off a bird called once, and did not call again.

# Chapter 44 — Jab: The Wrong Movement

There was nothing to eat on the fifty-fifth, so nobody pretended there was.

Dee boiled the pot anyway. She put the last of the salt in it, a pinch, and they drank it hot out of the cups, salt water, and Rook said it was the best thing Sowerby had ever cooked. Duchess had the last handful of oats out of her nosebag and looked at them all afterward as if they had taken the rest.

Jab drew nobody. There was nobody busy. He stood by the cart at the ruined corner with the frame in the rack behind the driver's bench, and his palm under its rag, and felt round them all.

Rook was hot, still. Less than the fifty-first. The last paper had held it, Dee said, and it had not come on further, and the yellow at the foot of the stitches had not spread. "Holding," she said. "It's always holding. One of these days I'll say it's better, and you'll not believe me."

"I'll believe you," said Rook. "I'll ask you to say it twice."

Callie was standing by the wheel with her hand on the hub. "Same," she said.

Toren counted them and said where to.

---

The drovers' fold was at the twentieth stone.

Jab remembered it from the forty-first. It had been Senna's camp, and it had been no good: the road-side wall slumped, a spring burst out of the hill and flooding it, and two husk lines coming up out of the culvert beside it. Duchess had put her ears back. They had gone on to the ruined corner instead.

They passed it at the second hour.

The spring was still running. The fold was still a bog, with the wall down and the water standing brown in it. And on the road beside it, coming up out of the culvert and crossing to the ditch on the far side, there were lines.

Callie did not have to count them. Jab saw her not count them. There were too many. They came up out of the culvert mouth like the threads of an unravelling rope and went across the road and down, and the grey in the ditch lay along the bottom in the brown water, combed, pointing south.

"Forty," said Callie. "Forty-four."

She did not say anything else.

Jab put his hand on his coat, on the left side, over the fish. He did not know why. He had started doing it without deciding to, the way Toren started counting.

And he felt it.

It was a long way off. It was south, down the road, past the rise and the next rise, farther than he had ever felt anything. It was not a person. He knew people. People had a shape to them when he felt them, a weight, a place where they stopped. This had no place where it stopped. It was wide, and low, and busy.

He stood in the road with his hand on his coat.

"Jab," said Dee.

"It's south," he said. "There's something busy. South."

"How far?"

"I don't know. Far." He shut his eyes. He had felt a lean at a mile on the forty-third, a man with bad hands in a ditch. This was farther than that and it was bigger. "It's not a thing. It's not like the lifter. There's no pull in it." He opened his eyes. "It's like the drifts at the wash, when you stood on the lip. The whole bottom. Busy."

Toren had come up from the back of the cart.

"The Sag?" he said.

"I don't know what it is," said Jab. "I know which way."

Toren looked south down the road. He did not say anything for a while.

"Is it moving?" said Callie.

Jab went down to feel. He did it the way he felt a floor, with his eyes shut, not reaching. It was there, wide and low and far, the way a fire is there on the other side of a hill when you can see the glow and not the flames.

It did not do anything for a long time.

Then it heaved.

That was the only word he had for it afterward. The whole wide busy thing, far off, all at once, came up. The way the heap in the corner of room one had come up at the three. But there had been a count to the corner. Ten, and three. This had no count. It came when it came.

Then it went down again.

"There," said Jab. "It pushed. Just then." He looked at Dee. "Not at a three. At nothing."

Dee looked at the glass in her fist. She had not turned it.

"Say when it does it again," she said. "And I'll turn it. And we'll see if there's a count to it that you can't hear."

---

They walked. He said when it pushed.

He said it at the third hour, walking at the tailboard with his hand on his coat, and Dee turned the glass. He said it again a long time after, and she counted the turns. Eleven. He said it again after four. Then after nine. Then after two, and again after two, and then nothing for a long time.

"Eleven, four, nine, two, two," said Dee. She wrote it. "That's not a count. That's a man breathing in his sleep."

"That's what Pell said about the sky," said Callie.

"I know what Pell said about the sky."

"I'm saying it's the same."

"I know it's the same," said Dee. "I'm not saying it's the same thing. Nobody on this road can say that." She shut the book. "It's the same shape. That's all anybody can write."

Toren had not said anything since the fold. He was walking at the back, the way he always did. Jab could feel him there without looking. He was doing a sum. Jab knew the weight of Toren doing a sum.

At the fourth hour they came to the culvert.

It was the one below the long straight, where the north road came down toward the Sag. There was a wall there, the end of a wall, standing in a field beside the road. Jab had knelt in the angle of it once, on the road with Tonk, before the gate, with a child behind him and a hound in front. It had been a long time ago. It had been fifty-five days.

He did not stop. He looked at the angle of the wall as they went past. There was nothing in it. There was grass.

The husk lines came up out of the culvert beside it. Twelve. Callie said the number.

And he felt the other thing.

It came up out of the wide busy thing to the south like voices out of a crowd. He knew them before he knew he knew them. They were people. They were a long way off, farther than a mile, and he could feel them anyway, because he knew them, and because they were so busy.

Otto. He knew Otto. He had felt Otto's hand on his collar on the east track and never once felt him look back. Otto was south, and he was busy all through, the chest and the arms and the belly, warm grit, like the man in the ditch with the copper.

Katori. He had never felt Katori before, not properly. She was hard to feel. She was like a stone in a stream. But she was there, south, and she was busy too.

And under them, a long way under, the wide thing heaved again.

"They're on the lip," said Jab.

He heard his own voice. It was not steady.

"Otto. Katori. I can feel them. They're busy. The grey's in them. They've been standing in it." He shut his eyes and felt for the rest. It was harder. There were others, but he did not know them well enough. The big quiet yard lifters were only a weight. The weighbridge girl, a small quick shape, busy in the arms. Somebody sitting still, at the edge of it all, not busy. That would be Dessa.

"All of them," he said. "They're all busy. Except one."

"Which one?"

"The one sitting down."

Toren stopped walking.

"How long?" he said. "How long have they been in it?"

"I don't know how long," said Jab. "I can feel how much." He opened his eyes. "More than the copper man. Less than Pell in the drift. All of them. It's on them like dust on a coat."

Toren looked down the long straight to where the road went over the last rise. Beyond it, Jab knew, the road went down into the dip, and across the bottom, and up the other side to the south lip, and two hundred paces of made road to the yard gate.

"Pell," said Toren. "How far?"

"Two miles," said Pell. "An hour. At the wheel's pace."

"Then an hour," said Toren. He looked at them. "Nobody runs. The wheel doesn't go fast. We get there the way we get there." He looked at Jab. "Tell me every time it pushes."

---

They came over the last rise at the fifth hour, in the afternoon, and saw the Sag.

Jab had seen it a hundred times. He had stood in the door on the west shoulder every morning for two weeks, and he had knelt in its bottom with his hand on a woman's chest. He knew its shape the way he knew the shape of the east track. He stood on the north lip beside the cart and did not know it.

The bottom was full of fog.

Not white. Not the milk fog of the mornings, lying still in the dip until the sun got to it. This was grey. It was the grey of the drifts at the wash, of the floor of room one, thin and pale, lying in the bottom of the Sag over the slabs and the black water between them. It lay there, and it moved.

It did not move with the wind. There was no wind. It moved on its own. It thickened in one place, over the joints on the east side, and heaped up, and slid down again. It crept up the lower slope toward the east track a few paces, and slid back. It lay flat for a long time, and then all of it heaved up at once, a hand, two hands. The white post with the six on it was not standing in it any more. Jab could not see the post at all.

He felt it heave. He had felt it heave from two miles off. From here it was under his feet.

"Now," he said. "That's a push."

He watched it come.

Out of the fog, on the east side of the bottom, where the joints were, things stood up. He counted them without deciding to. Three. Four. The colour of old wasp nests. They stood up out of the grey as the grey heaved, the way the husk had stood up out of the heap in the corner. They turned. They went up the lower slope at the lamps.

The lamps were still there. They stood on their poles in their line along the upper edge of the east track, lit, though it was day. There were more of them than there had been. There had been a line of them on the forty-first. Now there was a second line below it, closer to the bottom, and two of those were dark.

The husks went up at the lit ones.

Something came down off the west rim, fast and thin. One of the husks stopped and came apart into grey on the slope. That was Ines. Jab looked for her and found her, on the west rim above the pipe's west mouth, a small shape with a bow.

The other three reached the lamp-line.

There were people on it. Big shapes, standing, with long iron bars, between the lamps. The yard lifters. And a smaller one between them, broad, with his feet set. That was Otto. Jab could feel him. The husks came at the lamps and the bars met them, one, and another, and knocked them down the slope.

And a quick shape came down the lip path from above, one-handed, with a straight grey blade lit on its fist, and put it through the third husk's back as it got up.

The weighbridge girl.

The grey of them lay on the slope below the lamps. Nobody went down to it.

"It lies where it falls," said Dee quietly, beside him. "Katori's rule."

At the top of the dark lane, between the third lamp and the fourth, there was a figure standing with a grey blade on her fist, lit, lying flat along the edge. She did not move. There was nothing in the lane. Hounds went for the dark. There were no hounds, this time.

"Katori," said Callie.

Jab did not need to look. He could feel her.

---

And at the south lip, on a crate, with her bad leg out straight and a book in her lap and her eyes shut, there was Dessa.

Jab could see her. It was three hundred paces across the bowl. He could see the crate, and the book, and the small straight shape of her on it, not moving. Behind her, on the gravel of the lip, there was something new. A table. It was a board on two trestles, with jars on it, and a canvas on the ground beside it with somebody lying on it.

A yard lifter. One of the big quiet men. He was lying on his side on the canvas behind the lamp-line, and somebody was kneeling beside him.

"He's sick," said Jab. He could feel it. "That one. It's in his belly."

He looked at the rest of it. He could not help looking, the way you look at a room you have come back to after a long time, to see what has moved.

The lamps had moved. There were more.

There was a rope along the lip path now, knotted to iron stakes, for hands. It had not been there on the forty-first.

The weighbridge girl's left arm had no plaster on it. It had a leather binding from the wrist to halfway up the forearm, laced tight.

Wyck was on the lip above the lamps. He had the bar. He had it in his left hand, the way he had since the gate, and his right arm was still strapped across him. He was standing where he could see everybody's feet. Jab watched him turn his head and say something down the line to Otto, and saw Otto shift his weight back into his heels.

The day holder's hollow at the south lip was empty.

The long building stood beyond the berm, low and white, with the windows of the ward along its side. Somewhere behind those windows there was a fifth bed on the right.

Jab put his hand flat on his coat and felt the fish.

Three hundred paces. And a Sag.

---

"They've seen us," said Callie.

The figure on the west rim had stopped. Ines. She was standing on the rim with her bow down, looking across the bowl at the north lip. At them.

She turned her head and shouted something. It came across the bowl thin and flat, the way voices do over water.

"*Six!*"

Jab saw the whole line turn. Otto, on the lamp-line. The yard lifters. The weighbridge girl on the lip path. Wyck.

Katori did not turn. She stood at the top of the dark lane with her blade lit and did not move her head. But Jab felt her know.

Dessa did not turn either. She sat on the crate with her eyes shut.

It was not loud. It did not have to be. It was flat, and it carried, the voice nobody argued with, and it came across three hundred paces of grey fog in the still afternoon as if she were standing beside them.

Toren stepped forward to the edge.

"*Dessa!*" He did not shout the way he shouted at a gate. He shouted the way he said a count. "*Six! And the cart! And the boxes!*"

"*I know,*" said Dessa. "*I can hear you. Stay on the lip. Don't come down.*"

"She's using it," said Toren.

"Using what?"

"*Everybody back.*" Toren did not take his eyes off the far lip. "It's the call. For a push. Everybody back behind the lamps. And let it come to the light." He was quiet for a moment. "I wrote it on the step of eleven. The night before we went."

"She's made it work," said Pell, from the cart. He had pulled himself round on the boards to see. "That's a different thing. You wrote a thing. She made it a call. That's better than yours."

Dee did Jab. "A hand and a bit. Floor's a finger." She turned his palm over. "That's still a blister."

"*Dessa!*"

"*I'm here.*"

Two breaths before.*" He stopped. "*I've a stone, and two turns.*" He stopped again. "*And the boxes. In lead. On a cart with a wheel that walks.*"

"*Two breaths?*" said Dessa.

"*Two breaths.*"

Another pause.

"*What do you want?*" said Toren.

"*Both.*"

"*Then say where you want us.*"

"Now," he said.

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-45.md

# Chapter 45 — Toren: Everybody Back

The top half was quiet, the way Dessa had said.

Toren walked beside the walking wheel with his good hand an inch off the rim. The made road went down the north slope of the Sag in a long straight line, cracked and tilted, with grass in the cracks. Dee had Duchess's head. Pell sat on the high side of the cart, leaning, with his leg out and his hands on the rail. Rook lay on the canvas behind him on his side, not asleep, looking at the fog.

Jab walked at the tailboard with the frame on his back and his hand flat on his coat.

Callie walked behind the cart. Not in front. Dessa had said from the corner, and the corner was at the bottom, and she was not going to be at the corner until the cart was. So she walked behind it with the hook for a stick and the counter held out, and read the road over the tailboard, and said the numbers.

"Thirty," she said. "Forty. Fifty, at the edge of it."

The fog began a third of the way down.

It did not have an edge the way a wall does. It had an edge the way water has one on sand, thin at first and then not thin. Toren walked into it. It came up round his boots, grey, and then round his shins. It was not wet. It was not anything. It was dust that had not decided to lie down.

"Sixty," said Callie. "Seventy."

"Jab," said Toren.

"Nothing," said Jab. "It's lying there."

The walking wheel knocked. Toren heard every knock. It sounded very loud in the bottom of the Sag, like a man tapping on a door.

---

"Now," said Jab. "Pushing."

Toren stopped. He put his hand up without looking, and the cart stopped behind it. Dee stopped Duchess.

"*Pushing,*" he said. Not loud. It went across the fog.

"*Everybody back!*" said Dessa.

He heard the lamp-line move. He could not see it well from down here. He could see the lamps, a line of yellow blurs up the east slope, and dark shapes stepping back from them.

And he felt the bottom heave.

It came up under the fog all round them, not a sound, a movement. The grey round his shins rose to his knees and sank back. And off to the left, east, on the far side of the road, where the slabs dropped away toward the ditch and the joints, something stood up.

It stood up out of the fog twenty paces off. Grey on grey. It turned.

Toren did not move. Nobody moved. That was the rule. *You stand still where you are.* He stood beside the wheel with his good hand an inch off the rim and his stone full at the bottom of him and did not reach for it. Nothing on the cart was worked. Nothing on the cart was lit.

The husk turned toward the lamps.

It went up the east slope at the yellow blurs, away from them, through the fog, into the light. Something came down off the west rim, thin and fast, across the whole width of the Sag. The husk stopped. It came apart.

"*Down,*" said Dessa.

"Go," said Toren.

They went on.

---

The bottom was slabs.

He had crossed it on the forty-first with the watch on the lip and nothing coming up. He had knelt in it on the twenty-fifth with a woman bleeding in the fog. He knew it. He walked across it now with the fog to his knees, and it was like walking across a floor you know in the dark.

The slabs were tilted. Some of them had dropped at one edge into the water, and some had risen. The road ran over them in a line of concrete, cracked into blocks, with black water in every crack. The cart went over them a slab at a time, rocking, with the walking wheel singing and knocking and Pell leaning on the high side saying *easy*.

"Eighty," said Callie. "Eighty-five."

"There's the corner," said Dee.

Toren saw it.

It was where the white post had been. The post was not there. It lay flat in the water on the east side of the road, under the fog, with the *6* on it facing up. He could see the white of it through the grey. Beside it the slab under the road's east edge had dropped. It had dropped on the twenty-fifth, a hand, and folded the day holder's sheet. It had dropped again since. The whole east half of the road sloped down at that slab into the black water, two hands at its low corner. The water was over the lip of it.

And the walking wheel was on the east side.

"Callie," he said. "Ground."

She came up past the tail of the cart on the east side. She did not go in front of it. She crouched at the edge of the dropped slab with the hook's end held out flat, and read.

"Soft," she said. "The dropped one's rocking. It's sitting on water." She moved the hook. "The next one's stone. There. Past the corner. It's not moved. It's sitting on the old bed." She looked at him. "Under where the axle'll be when the wheel's in the middle of the drop."

"Set there," said Toren.

"Set there."

"And the ditch side?"

Callie turned her head and looked east. Past the dropped slab, past the post in the water, the ground went down in a long shallow slope to the ditch. The fog lay thicker there, heaped over the joints, and moved.

"That's where they come up," she said. "All of them. Look at it."

"Then that's yours," said Toren.

---

She did not stand.

He saw her decide it. She knelt on the stone slab past the corner, on the ditch side of the road, on both knees. She put the back of the hook's curve down on the edge of the slab, still, with the bend facing east. Then she did not take the conduit in both hands. She took it in one, the right, by the tail, where it ended. Her left arm stayed across her ribs. She knelt beside the hook, not behind it.

"The steel holds it," she said. She said it to him, or to herself. "Not me. I don't have to be behind it. It's in the steel."

"How long?"

"The half," said Callie. "And my own. And the ribs." She did not look at him. "Say when the wheel's past, and I'll let it down."

She reached for it. Toren saw her face go still.

The lee stood up.

It stood up off the outside of the curve, east, toward the ditch and the joints, pale. She did not let it stand straight. She leaned it back over the road, over the dropped slab, a lean-to. It went back and back, over the place where the cart would be when the wheel was in the drop, over Toren's place, over Duchess's head.

It was longer than a door. He could see that. It stood along the edge of the road from the corner past the stone slab, the length of the cart and the horse together, a pale wall with the fog heaped against its outside.

"It's a line," said Callie. Her voice was flat. "It's not a door. It's a line. Along the road." She breathed out through her teeth. "It's dear."

"Holding," she said.

"Bring her on," said Toren.

Dee brought Duchess on. The mare walked into the shelter of the lee with her ears flat and her eyes white and did not stop, because Dee was walking in front of her. The cart came on behind her. The good wheel rolled along the west side of the road on sound slabs. The walking wheel came to the edge of the dropped slab and went down onto it.

"Set," said Toren.

The strut came up out of the stone slab past the corner, at its slant, under the axle beside the walking wheel. The wheel went down into the drop, down the slope of the slab toward the water. The weight came onto the strut.

"Holding," said Toren.

It was a wheel. It was not the cart on the arch. It was a wheel on a drop, with Pell on the high side and a load of lead in the middle. He felt it come down the strut into the stone. He was soft behind it. He paid by the breath.

"Rolling," said Pell.

The wheel went on across the drop.

"Now," said Jab. "Pushing."

He did not have time to say it across the Sag.

He heard Jab say it at the tailboard, and felt the bottom heave, and he was holding a wheel on a strut in a drop with his stone going under it. He could not have said anything to anybody.

"*Pushing!*" That was Callie. Kneeling at the hook, with the lee up. She said it loud, flat, the voice for a number.

"*Everybody back!*" said Dessa, from the far lip.

The bottom came up.

It came up all round the dropped slab. The fog on the ditch side heaved against the outside of the lee, and the joints in the ground below the ditch opened in the grey. Things stood up out of them. Toren saw them through the pale of the lee. One. Two. Three. Grey on grey.

They did not turn toward the lamps.

The lamps were far off, up the slope. And here, two paces from them, there was a pale wall standing along the edge of the road, worked. And under the road there was a strut, worked. And a girl kneeling with her hand on the tail of a hook, and a boy holding a wheel.

They came at the lee.

The first one hit it. Toren felt it hit, through the stone, through the strut, up through his boots. It was the sound of a sack against a wall. The lee did not move. It stood. The husk stood against its outside, pushing, with no face.

"*Holding,*" said Callie.

She did not get up. She knelt with her right hand on the tail of the conduit and her left arm across her ribs, and her eyes were not on the husk. They were on the cart. On Duchess, and Dee at her head, and Pell leaning, and Rook on the canvas, and Jab at the tailboard. And on him.

The second husk hit the lee beside the first.

"The wheel," said Toren. "It's in the middle. Three more breaths."

"*Three,*" said Callie.

The third husk did not hit the lee. It went along its outside, toward the end of it, where the lee stopped at the tail of the cart.

"*End,*" said Callie. "It's going round the end."

Toren could not move. He had the wheel.

"Mine," said Jab.

He went. He went past the tailboard to the end of the lee with the frame on his back, and the knife came on his left fist, dark, and he lit the hook of it and nothing else. The husk came round the end of the pale wall at the worked thing and met the lit hook and came apart. The grey fell on the slab at the end of the lee and slid off into the water.

Something came down across the Sag from the west rim. Thin and fast, it came down at a long slant over the road and struck the first husk against the lee's outside. It came apart. The grey slid down the outside of the pale wall into the fog.

"*Ines,*" said Callie. She had not moved her eyes.

"Past," said Pell. "The wheel's past."

Toren let the strut go. At the end. Not after.

The walking wheel came up off the dropped slab onto sound stone on the far side of the corner, singing. The cart came level.

The last husk stood against the lee's outside, pushing.

Ines's second arrow came down across the bottom and took it.

"*Down,*" said Dessa, from the far lip.

"Down," said Callie.

The lee went out.

She knelt on the slab with the hook lying flat, and her right hand still on the tail of it, and breathed. Toren saw her breathe. She breathed out through her teeth, slowly, the way you let out a rope.

"How much?" he said.

"All the half," said Callie. "And some of mine." She did not get up yet. "It held. I didn't have to be behind it. It held on the steel." She looked at her hand on the tail of the conduit. "I didn't have to be the wall."

"No," said Toren.

"I thought I did," said Callie. "At the washout. I thought I had to be behind it. Every time. My whole life." She got up, one knee and then the other, with the hook for a stick. "I don't."

They came up the south slope in the next quiet.

The lamps were there. The lamp-line stood along the upper edge of the east track, and the made road came up past the end of it to the lip. As the cart came up the last of the slope, the line opened. Otto came down from his place with his big hands out and took Duchess's rope from Dee without a word. One of the yard lifters took the other side of her head. They walked her up the last of the slope over the lip and onto the gravel, and the cart came up behind her. The walking wheel knocked, and Pell leaned on the high side with his face grey, and Rook on the canvas lay with his eyes open.

"Behind the lamps," said Dessa.

They were behind the lamps.

Toren stood on the gravel at the south lip and turned round and looked back across the Sag. The fog in the bottom. The dropped corner with the post lying in the water. The north lip, three hundred paces off, empty.

He counted them. He could not help it.

"Six," he said. "And a horse."

"I know," said Dessa.

She was on the crate. She had not moved. She sat with her bad leg out straight and the big book in her lap and her eyes shut.

He went and stood in front of her.

She opened her eyes.

He had not seen her face since the forty-first. It was thinner. It was white, with marks under the eyes, and set, the way Dee's had been set at Caul Hill over the jar. She looked at him for a long moment. She looked at his arm in its cuff, and his face, and his coat, which was still grey in the seams.

"You're late," she said.

"Four days," said Toren.

"I know how many days. I've been counting." She shut her mouth hard. "I've got six lines. I've not written in them."

"Not yet," said Toren.

"Not yet," said Dessa. She looked past him, at the cart, at Pell, at Rook on the canvas, at Jab with the frame and Callie with the hook, and back at him. Then she shut her eyes again.

"Draw them," she said. "Now."

The table was two boards on trestles behind the lamp-line, with jars on it. The watch's jars, Toren saw. Otto's dirty ones, the sweepings from the lip. On the canvas beside it the yard lifter lay on his side, grey in the face, with a basin by his head. The man kneeling beside him with a wet cloth was one of the gate crew.

"He's the worst," said Dessa, with her eyes shut. "He was on the lamp-line below the others since first light. He was sick at the fourth hour. Then Otto. Then Katori, and she'll not say. Then the other one." She did not open her eyes. "The girl's in her arms. Ines is the least. Wyck's been on the lip." She stopped. "I've not been anywhere. I've sat."

"You're not busy," said Jab. He had come to the table with the frame. "I felt you from two miles. You're the only one that isn't."

"It came up to thirty feet," said Dessa. "It's not come nearer than that all day. I'd have known."

Jab swung the frame off his back onto the table. He took out the lifter's first jar, with its quarter, and Callie's clean one with seven and a half.

"Dee," he said.

"I'm the watch," said Dee. She stood at his left shoulder with the glass. "Work."

He knelt by the yard lifter on the canvas. He could kneel here. It was the lip. He put his right palm flat on the man's belly, round the white place, and the jar against his left, and shut his eyes.

"Busy," he said. "It's in the belly. He's swallowed it. It's all through the belly and the chest." He drew. Toren watched the man's face as Jab drew. It did not change for a long time. Then it did.

"Vent," said Dee.

Jab held it high and turned and put his palm flat on the gravel at the edge of the lip, where the grey from the watch's kills had been lying all day.

"Not into my floor," said Callie. She had come to the table. She was standing, with the hook for a stick. "Into the road's."

"It's the road's," said Jab.

Dee took his leftovers. Jab went back on.

---

Toren stood at the table and looked at the line.

He had been going to take a place on it. He had been going to walk down to the lamps and stand in somebody's place so they could come up and be drawn. He had been going to do it without deciding, the way his hands built a fire.

He stopped himself.

"Dessa," he said. "Who comes off, and who takes their place?"

Dessa did not open her eyes.

"Otto next," she said. "Wyck takes Otto's place. Wyck." Her voice went up, flat, across the lip. "*Otto's coming off. Take his bar.*"

Wyck came down the lamp-line from his place on the lip. He had the bar in his left hand. His right arm was strapped across him, and his face was set in a way Toren knew from the road, the way it went when he was doing a thing Keel had told him not to.

"I'm cleared tomorrow," he said, to nobody. "It's one day."

"*Taking,*" said Wyck, at Otto's shoulder.

"Yours," said Otto.

"Mine."

"*Letting go,*" said Otto, and stepped back, and Wyck stepped into his place with the bar planted and his weight back in his heels. Otto came up the slope to the table with his big hands hanging. He was grey and wet in the face and he had been sick. Toren could see it on his coat.

"Otto," said Jab, from the canvas.

"I'm all right."

"You're busy," said Jab. "Stand there."

Otto stood there. Toren watched Jab draw him. Otto looked down at Jab's hand on his chest the whole time, and went red about nothing, and did not say anything.

*Taking. Yours. Mine. Letting go.*

They had learned it in the north yard with a rack and a sack. Callie and Toren, the first time, on the thirty-fourth. He had not known the whole watch was saying it now.

"Who taught them that?" he said.

"You did," said Dessa. "It was in the book."

---

"Katori," said Dessa. "Next."

Katori did not come.

She was at the top of the dark lane between the third lamp and the fourth, with her grey blade lit and lying flat along the edge like a stove across a room. She had not moved since Toren came over the north lip. He did not think she had moved since noon.

"*Katori,*" said Dessa. "*Off.*"

"I don't come off," said Katori. She did not turn her head. "Nobody's got the lane."

Toren looked at the lane.

It ran down from the gap between the third lamp and the fourth, dark, toward the ditch. The hounds came up it. That was what it was for. They went for the dark, and the dark was the lane, and at the top of the lane was Katori with a blade, and nobody else.

"I'll take the lane," said Toren.

Katori turned her head then.

She looked at him. She looked at his arm in its cuff and his face and the spike, which was not on his fist. She looked at him the way she had looked at him on the step of eleven, weeks ago. Then she had been deciding whether he was worth telling something to, and had decided not yet.

"With what?" she said.

"A strut," said Toren. "And the spike. Not together."

"How much have you got?"

"A stone," said Toren. "Full. And two turns of my own and a bit. I've held a wheel in the bottom. That was four breaths." He did not look away. "I can hold a hound off a strut. I've held a husk on one at Caul Hill for the whole of a thing I'll tell you about. I can't hold it and kill it. It's one or the other."

Katori looked at him for a long moment.

"Don't block it," she said.

"What?"

"The lane. Don't put the strut across it." She turned back to the dark. "You block it, they go round. Round the lamp. Into the light, where Otto is. Otto's got a bar and no blade." She was quiet. "Narrow it. Put the strut in at a slant, from this side. Make the lane a pace wide at the top. Then they come one at a time. Or they don't come."

"One at a time," said Toren.

"And when one comes, you don't kill it," said Katori. "You hold it. On the strut. And you say so." She looked at him again. "And somebody else kills it."

"Who?"

"Me," said Katori. "When I've been drawn."

Dessa's voice came across the lip, flat.

"*Katori. That's mine to say. You're off.*"

Katori looked at Toren a moment longer. Then she lifted the blade off the lane, still lit.

"*Taking,*" said Toren.

He stepped into the top of the lane beside her, with the third lamp at his left shoulder and the fourth two paces off at his right.

"Yours," said Katori.

"Mine."

"*Letting go,*" said Katori, and let the blade go dark, and stepped back. She walked up the slope to the table with her cap pulled down, and did not look back once.

Toren stood at the top of the dark lane, with the fog in the bottom of the Sag below him and nothing on his fist. He was waiting.

---

He waited a long time.

He heard Jab at the table behind him. *Busy.* And after a while, *Clean.* And Dee saying *vent*, and the soft sound of a palm going flat on gravel. He heard Callie say the jar.

"The lifter's first's done," she said. "The quarter. My first's open. Seven and a half."

He heard Katori say, "It's in my chest." He heard Jab say, "It's all through you. You've been in it since noon," and Katori not answer.

The fog lay in the bottom. It crept up the lower slope and slid back. It heaved a little, and lay down.

"*Pushing,*" said Jab, from the table. Two breaths before.

"*Everybody back!*"

The line stepped back behind the lamps. Toren did not. He was at the top of the lane, and the lane was the dark, and the dark was where they came.

He felt the bottom heave. The grey came up the slope toward the lamps. Things stood up in it and went up at the light. Two. Three. The bars met them. The girl came down the lip path with her blade and killed one.

And something came up the dark lane.

It came low and fast, the colour of the dark, with two green points in it where the lamplight caught. It came straight up the lane at the dark top of it, where he was standing.

"Set," said Toren.

He put the foot of the strut down at the top of the lane, on the third-lamp side, at a slant, leaning out over the lane toward the fourth. It came up out of the gravel pale and hard, as high as his waist. The lane was a pace wide at the top now. A pace between the end of the strut and the fourth lamp's pole.

The hound came up the lane and hit the strut.

It did not hit it the way a husk hits. It hit it the way a dog hits a gate, all teeth and weight and scrabbling claws, and fell back, and came again. It could not get past the strut. It went along the end of it, into the pace of dark that was left. It came through the pace at him.

"*Holding,*" said Toren. "*It's on the strut. It's coming through the end.*"

He could not kill it. He had the strut.

He let the strut go.

It went out. The hound came through the whole width of the lane now, at him, low. He lit the heel. He did not light the rest. He put the lit hook of the spike down across its path, low, and it went into the light because it was going for him and the light was in the way. He dragged the hook round against the third lamp's pole and put the point in.

It came apart. The grey fell on the gravel at the top of the lane.

He let the heel go dark.

"Set," he said, and put the strut back in.

His breath was going. He could feel it. The stone had paid for the hound on the strut, for as long as it was on it, and for the heel. The stone was less than half.

"That's one," he said.

"*Down,*" said Dessa.

---

Katori came back.

She came down from the table without being told, with her cap pulled down and her blade dark on her fist. She was not grey in the face any more. She was white. She stood at his right shoulder, on the fourth-lamp side of the lane, a pace off.

"I'm drawn," she said.

"You're drawn."

"You let the strut go and killed it," said Katori. "I watched." She did not look at him. "That was right. It was the only thing you could do with nobody else here." She was quiet. "There's somebody else here."

"You hold," she said. "I kill."

"I hold," said Toren.

He understood it all at once, standing at the top of the lane. He had held and killed, held and killed, all the way from the Breach. He had done it because there had been nobody else to do the other half. Callie had held the door at Caul Hill so the others could do what only they could do. And he had given her the lean. He had given it away.

He could give this away too.

"I'll hold," he said. "You kill."

"That's the lane," said Katori.

---

The big push came at dusk.

Jab felt it first. Toren heard him at the table. He did not say *now* this time, or *pushing*. He said, "*Dee*," and there was a thing in his voice Toren had not heard since the rope by the door at Caul Hill.

"*It's all of it,*" said Jab. "*The whole bottom. It's coming. It's more than two breaths. It's—*"

"*Everybody back!*" said Dessa.

The whole bottom of the Sag came up.

It came up the way the drifts at the wash had come up in the gust, only there was no gust. The fog heaved up out of the bottom and up the slopes. It came up past the lamp-line on the east track and onto the lip path, and up the made road toward the lip. Things stood up in it everywhere. Toren could not count them. He tried. He stopped.

The lamp-line was behind the lamps. Nobody was below them. The things came up at the light.

Otto and the yard lifter met them with bars. Wyck was on the line, where Otto had been. He stood with his bar planted and his weight in his heels, and a husk came at him, and he did not strike it. He took it on the bar, low, the way you take a sack, and let it come, and turned it off the end of the bar down the slope, and stepped back into his place.

"*Heels,*" said Wyck, down the line.

Ines was loosing from the west rim. Toren could hear the bow. The girl was on the lip path with her blade.

And up the made road, toward the lip, toward the table where Jab was drawing, the fog came, and husks in it.

"*Callie!*" said Dessa.

"*I've got the road,*" said Callie.

Toren could not look. He heard it. He heard the hook go down on the concrete at the top of the made road, where it came up over the lip. He heard her say, "*Holding,*" flat, the voice for a number.

And the dark lane filled.

They came up it three at a time. Hounds. They came up the lane at the dark top of it, where he was, and the first hit the strut and the second hit the first and the third went along the end of the strut into the pace of dark.

"*Holding,*" said Toren. "*Three. One through the end.*"

"*Mine,*" said Katori.

She did not step into the lane. She reached across the end of the strut with her blade lit, lying flat, and the hound coming through the pace of dark met the edge of it. It came apart.

"*Two on the strut,*" said Toren.

The strut took them. They hit it and scrabbled and fell back and hit it, and it went down into the gravel and did not come up into him. He was soft. His breath was going under it.

"*One coming round,*" he said. "*The end.*"

"*Mine.*"

Katori killed it.

"*One.*"

It came through the pace at the end. Katori was there. It came apart.

The lane was empty.

He stood at the top of it with the strut up and nothing on it, and his breath going, and his stone going. He felt the stone go. It went out from under him the way it had gone at the steel door, like water out of a basin, and he was on his own. He was two turns of his own and a bit, and the strut on that.

"*Stone's gone,*" he said. "*I'm on mine.*"

"*How much?*" said Katori.

"*Two turns.*"

"*Then let it down when it's empty,*" said Katori. "*Not before. I'll say.*"

He held it. He held a strut with nothing on it, at the top of a dark lane, while the fog heaved behind the hounds. It was not free. There was no free strut. But it was cheaper than a hound.

---

He heard the table.

He heard it in pieces, the way Callie had heard Dee call the turns from the cut at the wash.

"*Jar,*" said Jab. Before it was dry.

"*The fence's fourth,*" said Dee. "*Not the home three.*"

"*Not the home three,*" said Callie. From the top of the made road. Holding.

He heard Jab's voice go on. *Busy.* He heard it a long time. *Clean.* He heard Jab say *next*, and the other yard lifter's slow voice answer.

And then he heard Jab stop.

"*Off,*" said Jab.

Toren could not look round. He had the lane.

"*I'm warm,*" said Jab. His voice was very steady. "*The palm. Round the mark. It's to the wrist. It's not the draw. It's mine.*" A breath. "*I'm off. Vent.*"

"*Vent,*" said Dee.

He heard the soft sound of a palm going flat on gravel. He heard it for a long time. Ten breaths. Twenty.

"*The girl's not done,*" said Jab. "*Nor Wyck. Nor Ines.*"

"*I know who's not done,*" said Dee. "*You're off. Sit on the crate. I'll draw you.*"

"*Dessa's on the crate.*"

"*Then sit on the ground,*" said Dee. "*It's the lip. You can sit.*"

Toren held the strut. He did not know how long. He held it and listened, and he heard Jab not go back on. He heard Dee drawing him. He heard nothing from Jab at all for a long time, which was the loudest thing he had ever heard him do.

At the top of the made road, behind him, he heard Callie.

"*I'm at my floor,*" she said.

Her voice was thick. There was blood in it.

"*I'm at my floor. I'm stopping.*"

"*Down,*" said Dessa. "*Callie. Down.*"

He heard the hook lie flat on the concrete.

He looked down the lane. It was dark, and empty, and below it the fog in the bottom of the Sag was lying down.

---

It went down the way it had come up, with no count.

The fog sank back down the slopes into the bottom and lay there. It heaved once, a little, and lay down again. Things that had been standing in it came apart on their own, the way the husks in the corridor had come apart when the lifter died, and lay as grey. Or they went back down into the joints. Toren could not tell which. Nobody could.

"*Down,*" said Dessa. "*Stay on the line.*"

Nobody moved off the line.

He let the strut go. At the end. Not after. He stood at the top of the dark lane with nothing on his fist and his own down to a turn, and his arm aching in its cuff, and did not turn round yet.

"Callie," said Katori, beside him. "Read it."

Callie came down the lip to the top of the made road. She had the counter held out. Her face was white and there was blood on her lip and down her chin.

"Forty," she said. "At the lip. Seventy on the slope. It was a hundred and ten in the push." She watched the counter. "It's coming down."

Toren turned round.

At the table, Jab was sitting on the ground with his back against the trestle and his right hand held palm up on his knee. Dee was kneeling in front of him with her palm flat on his wrist. The rag was off the white place. The blister had broken. The skin round the mark was red and wet.

"Cool," said Dee. She took her hand away. "You're cool."

Jab looked at the hand.

"I stopped," he said.

"You stopped," said Dee. "You said *off* before I did. You vented long. You sat."

Jab did not say anything for a moment.

"The girl's not done," he said. "Nor Wyck. Nor Ines."

"No," said Dee. "They're not."

"I'm cool," said Jab.

"You're cool," said Dee. "And a hand. Over your floor. I measured you."

Jab got up. He did it slowly, one knee and then the other, the way Rook got up.

"I'm back on," he said. "If you'll watch."

Dee looked at him for a long moment. Toren watched her do the sum. The one with a price on every line, and the other one.

"I'll watch," she said. "Three. Then you're off for tonight, whatever's left."

"Three," said Jab.

He drew the girl. Toren watched him do it. She stood in front of him with her plait down her back and the leather binding on her left arm and her blade dark on her right fist. She looked at Jab's hand on her chest the whole time, frankly, the way she looked at everything.

"Busy," said Jab. "In the arms." And then, "Clean."

He drew Wyck. Wyck stood with his bar and his strapped arm and his face set, and when Jab said *clean* he said, "I'm cleared tomorrow," as if Jab had asked.

He drew Ines, last. She had come off the west rim with her bow and her empty quiver and stood in front of him without a word.

"That's all," said Jab. "That's everybody."

"Jar," said Callie. She was at the table with the book, on her knees, because she could not stand. "The fence's fourth. It had five. It's got three. Two out of it." She wrote. "My first's done. Seven and a half. The lifter's first's done." She looked up. "Nine and three quarters. On the watch and us. For the Sag."

"And the home three?" said Toren.

Callie looked at the rack.

"Not touched," she said.

---

Dessa counted them.

She did it from the crate, with the big book open on her knee and a lantern the gate crew had brought hung on the trestle beside her. She did it the way she did the order, the way she had done it every night on the road from the old station: out loud, one name at a time, with her eyes open.

"Katori," she said. "Ines. Otto." She looked down the line at the two yard lifters. One of them was sitting on the lip with his trouser leg rolled up and a cloth tied round his calf, where a hound had got past a bar in the last of the push. "The two of you. The girl." She looked at Wyck. "Wyck."

Then she looked at the six of them.

"Pell," she said. "Rook. Dee. Jab. Callie." She looked at Toren last. "Toren."

"And a horse," said Toren.

"And a horse," said Dessa. She did not smile. She never smiled when she was counting. "Everybody back."

She turned the book to the back.

Toren knew the page before she found it. The departure page. *41st day. Morning. Out through the north yard gate, for Caul Hill, on the Director's narrow yes.* The names. The Flasks. *Home line: D. Cole.* And *S. V.: pull the door*, in her hand, because he had said it to her at the gate.

And under that, six lines. Empty.

She held the pencil over the first one for a long time.

"I said I'd write *back*," she said. "When you were back."

"We're back," said Toren.

Dessa wrote.

*55th day. Dusk. Back through the Sag, on a push, in the fog. Held by the home line and the six together:*

Then she stopped, and looked at the six lines, and at the names she had to write in them. She looked down the lip at the lamp-line, and at Katori at the top of the dark lane, and Otto, and the yard lifter with his calf tied up, and the girl.

"There's not enough lines," said Dessa.

Toren looked at the page.

"I left six," said Dessa. "One for each. I said I'd not need more than six." She did not look up. "I need more than six."

She wrote in the six lines anyway. She wrote the six of them, one to a line, the way she had said she would, in her small neat hand. *E. Marsh. D. Wren. P. J. C. T. Voss.* And then, under the last line, on the white of the page below it where there were no lines at all, she went on writing. *K. I. O. The weighbridge girl. The two yard lifters (one bitten, the calf, the last push). W. (cleared tomorrow.)*

And at the bottom, *D. Cole.*

She looked at it.

"That's everybody," she said.

"That's everybody," said Toren.

Dessa shut the book. She held it against her chest the way she had at the gate on the forty-first. She did not open her eyes for a while.

"Go and put them in the beds," she said. "All of you. Pell and Rook first. Sowerby's been standing at the yard gate since the fourth hour with a face like the end of the world." She opened her eyes. "The watch'll hold the night. It's lying down."

"Dessa," said Toren.

"What."

"It was yours," said Toren. "The push. *Everybody back.* It was the call."

"It was yours," said Dessa. "You wrote it."

"I wrote a thing," said Toren. "You made it a call."

Dessa looked at him in the lantern light for a long moment.

"Pell said that," she said. "Didn't he. At the north lip. I heard him. The whole Sag heard him." She almost smiled. It was the first time. "He's right. Go on. You're letting the cold in."

Toren went.

He walked up the made road from the lip toward the yard gate, at the back of the party, the way he always did. The cart went in front of him with the walking wheel knocking and Otto at Duchess's head. Pell sat on the high side, leaning. Rook lay on the canvas with his eyes shut, hot, and Jab walked at the tailboard with his right hand held palm up and open, cooling in the air. Callie walked beside the cart with her hand on the rail, and blood on her chin, and the hook for a stick.

At the yard gate there was a lamp, and under it a man in a white coat, standing with his arms folded. He had the face Dessa had said. Behind him, a long way up, in the long window on the second floor of the yard office, there was a light, and somebody small and square standing in it, not moving.

Toren counted them in through the gate. Out loud.

He did not say *out* this time. He did not say where to. There was nowhere to.

"In," he said. "Six."

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-46.md

# Chapter 46 — Callie: The Wheel in the Machine

They hosed them at the gate like arrivals.

Callie had known they would. She had thought about it on the road, on the nights when she could not lie down, and she had not minded thinking about it. It was the pen, and the gate, and the grey coats with the hose. It was the first thing Meridian had ever done to her. It was the thing it did to everybody who came in from outside, whoever they were, and she found she wanted it done.

The water was cold. It came out of the hose in a hard grey rope and went over her hair and her face and down the back of her collar, and her ribs took every shiver of it. She stood with her arms out from her sides the way the grey coat told her, the right one out and the left one only a little. She did not make a sound.

"Hook," said the grey coat.

She gave it to him.

He held it up under the lamp by the gate. He read the counter against the curve, and then against the grip, and then he looked at the paper tag tied round the conduit below where the rag had been. *pen, 35th.*

"Fourth time, miss," he said.

"Third was the pen," said Callie.

"Third was the pen." He took a stub of pencil from behind his ear and wrote on the tag, under the old line, holding it flat against the steel. Then he gave the hook to the other grey coat for the washing.

"When do I get it back?"

"Morning," said the grey coat. "Same as always."

She stood in the lamplight dripping and watched them take it away. She had not been without it since the forty-first. Her right hand did not know what to do.

Behind her, Hallet was at his window with his pen on its string. He was saying things to a young man about the time of night and the distance from the Sag to the north gate. He said them quietly and at length. He had his ledger open, and he was writing them in.

*Six.* She saw him write it. And then, after a long moment in which he looked at the cart, *and a horse.*

---

Doctor Sowerby had Pell off the cart before the cart had stopped.

He did not say anything to Pell. He said a great deal to the two orderlies with the stretcher, and to the lamp, and to the air in general. Most of it was about people who put broken ankles on carts for ten days. Some of it was about the splint, which he looked at for a long time without touching.

"Who did this?" he said at last.

"Miss Wren," said Pell.

"With what?"

"The tailboard rail," said Pell. "And the canvas off the cart."

Sowerby looked at the splint a while longer.

"It's not bad," he said, in a voice that meant it was very good and he would not say so. "Take him."

Then he got to Rook.

Rook was lying on the canvas on his side with his eyes open. Sowerby stood over him with his arms folded and his face like a thundercloud.

"Well," said Rook.

"Don't," said Sowerby.

"I've a hole in my back," said Rook. "And a dog in it. And she gave me all four of your papers. One a day. Not twice."

Sowerby knelt on the wet gravel of the gate yard and lifted the pad off Rook's back with two fingers. He looked. He did not say anything. He put the back of his hand against Rook's neck.

"How long?" he said. To Miss Wren.

"Since the forty-seventh," said Miss Wren. "It declared at dawn. The worst was the forty-seventh and the fifty-first. It's not spread since the fifty-first. It's yellow at the foot."

Sowerby put the pad back.

"Take him," he said to the orderlies. And then, to Rook, not looking at him: "You'll lie on your face for a week. You'll eat what I give you. You'll not get up without somebody counting."

"I've been counted for a week," said Rook. "It's the only thing I'm good at now."

The orderlies took him.

Callie looked for Jab.

He was not in the gate yard. The frame was leaning against the cart wheel with its jars in it, and Toren was standing beside it with his hand on the rack, the way you put your hand on a thing so it will not walk off. Jab was not there.

She saw him then. He was halfway across the north yard in the dark, walking. Not running. Nobody ran in the north yard at night, on the cinders, with the whitewash gone slick. He was walking very fast toward the long building, with his right hand held out a little from his side and his left hand flat on his coat. He was walking the way he had walked out of the room under the hill with the frame on his back, the lantern swinging, when Miss Wren had said *out*.

She did not call after him.

---

Her mother was at the weighbridge.

Callie had not expected that. She had thought her mother would be in the hut, at this hour, or in the ward. She came round the end of the berm into the south yard in the dark, wet through, with no hook and her arm across her side, and the weighbridge hut had a lamp in its window.

Her mother was on the stool.

She was sitting at the window of the hut on the high stool with the tally board on her knee and her stick against the wall behind her. The weighman sat on a crate beside her, in his leather apron. There were three jars on the plate. They were the watch's, from the lip, the dirty ones. Otto must have brought them up before the push. The weighman had not weighed them. He was sitting on the crate with his hands on his knees, not weighing them.

Her mother saw her come round the berm.

She did not get up. Callie saw her not get up. She saw her mother's right hand move on the board, and the board stay where it was, pinned against her knee by the heel of that hand, and her left hand lift off it and go up, flat, the way you lift your hand at a window to somebody across a yard.

Callie went to the window.

"Four days," said her mother.

"I know."

"I counted them. Don't look like that." Her mother looked at her through the window. "I'm allowed. You weren't here."

Her voice was slower than it had been. Callie heard it. She heard the words come one at a time, each one with a little space in front of it. They came, though. Every one of them came.

"Your ribs," said her mother.

"Two. The same two. Cracked this time."

"Show me."

Callie leaned in at the window. Her mother put her left hand out through it and laid it flat against Callie's side, over the wet coat, over the strapping. She did not press. She held it there.

"You've been holding something," her mother said.

"A door," said Callie. "And a road. And the top of the Sag."

"On those."

"On the steel," said Callie. "Not on me. I found out." She did not move away from the hand. "I found out I don't have to be behind it."

Her mother looked at her for a long time through the window of the weighbridge hut.

"No," she said. "You don't." She took her hand back. "What's in the frame?"

Callie told her.

She told it the way she had told it at every halt, flat, the whole of it. The fence's, eleven. Three in the fourth and eight in the fifth. Dirty, four. The lifter's, none. It had gone on the wash and the watch. Home, twenty-four.

"Not touched," said Callie.

Her mother looked at the three jars on the plate. Then she looked at the tally board on her knee, where she had written the watch's weights for fourteen days in big careful figures.

"I'll weigh them in the morning," she said. "Your three. I tied the stoppers." She looked at Callie. "My knots. I'll know."

"They're your knots."

"Then I'll know," said her mother. She looked at the weighman beside her. "And these. In the morning. He's not weighing tonight."

"I'm not weighing tonight," said the weighman.

"He sat down at the fourth hour when the fog came up," said her mother, "and he's been sitting since." She looked out through the window at the dark yard, toward the Sag. "So have I."

---

She did not sleep much. Keel strapped her ribs again in the ward, properly, with a long bandage and his mouth shut. Her mother sat on the edge of the bed in the fourth on the left and watched him do it. Then Callie lay on the bed in hut fourteen on her back with her arm across her side, and could not lie on either side, and did not sleep.

She lay and listened to her mother breathe.

She did not count it.

She noticed that she was not counting it. She noticed it the way you notice that a sound has stopped. Her mother's breath went in and out in the dark on the next bed, slow, with a little catch on the out-breath. Callie lay and let it go in and out, and did not put a number on it. After a while she was asleep.

---

The technician was waiting at the yellow line in the morning.

He was standing on the far side of it, in the treatment shed, with his left hand at his side and his right hand in a sling. The hand in the sling was bandaged to the wrist, with the fingers curled in a way that fingers do not curl on their own. Callie had last seen that hand on the thirty-fifth, in the pen, with the glove still on the concrete.

The carrier was on the near side of the line. Otto had brought it in on the sledge the night before, with Duchess, and left it where the man told him. The lid was in its groove.

"Sowerby says tomorrow," said the technician. He meant the hand. He did not look at it. "I know already. It's left-handed from here." He looked at Callie. "You read them. On the hill. The boxes. With the counter."

"I read about fifteen," said Callie. "At a hand."

"Then read them again," said the man. "For me. Here. I'd like to hear the numbers in the same voice."

Callie took the counter out of its bag. Her hook was in the gate washhouse still, but they had given her the counter back. Toren lifted the lid out of its groove with his good hand. There were the two boxes, in the lead bottom, grey steel loaves with the little dark windows and the plates beside them.

She held the counter to the first at a hand's width and waited ten breaths.

"A hundred and ten," she said. "Shut."

The second.

"A hundred and six. Shut."

The technician stood on his side of the yellow line and did not say anything for a moment.

"That's what you wrote," he said. "The boy read me the paper last night. Out of his coat." He looked at Toren. "He had it in his hand before the gate had shut behind him."

"You'd asked," said Toren.

"I'd asked," said the man. He looked at the boxes again. "Two," he said. "I asked for two, and three if you could. I didn't think I'd get one."

"There's a third," said Toren. "On the hill. It's open."

"Then it's the hill's," said the technician.

---

It took all of the morning.

He had been getting ready for it. Callie could see that. There were papers on the bench along the wall, the pages of a book copied out in his hand, in pencil, dozens of them. And on the steel of the rack, beside each box, there were numbers chalked in small figures. He had been writing down what every box ought to say, one by one, while he still knew.

"I did a month's work," he said, when he saw her looking. "Waiting. I didn't sleep much." He looked at the rack. "Now it's a night, maybe two, not a month. It learns the new one off the ones that still tell the truth. It doesn't need me to know, after that."

Nine.

# Chapter 47 — Jab: The Terms He Chooses

Mrs Lask was at the ward door when he got there, the night they came back, with the lamp turned down behind her.

"Visiting's over," she said.

"I know," said Jab.

She looked at him. She looked at his coat, wet from the gate, and his hair, and his right hand held a little out from his side with the rag off it and the broken blister shining in the lamplight.

"Wash that," she said. "In the basin. With the soap. Then you can go in."

He washed it in the basin by the door with the soap, round the white place and not on it. She watched him do it. Then she stood aside.

The ward was dark except for the lamp at the far end. Four beds on the right. The fourth had Senna in it, asleep, with her hands on the blanket. The fifth had Tonk.

Jab stopped at the foot of it.

Tonk was asleep on his back with the frame on his leg. The bar went across over the blanket and the four pins went down into the leg through the blanket's tent. The trapeze hung over him on its chain. His right fist was on his chest, closed, with the cord wound round it. Jab could see the coil of it, and the two ends. One end came out between the first two fingers, and the other between the last two. He was keeping both ends.

There was a chair by the bed. There had always been a chair by the bed.

Jab sat down in it.

He did not wake him. He sat in the chair with his hands in his lap and felt him. He could not help it. He did it the way he had felt the Sag from two miles off. Tonk was there. He was small, and warm, and asleep, and not busy. There was nothing in him that was not his.

The leg was the old leaning wall, all down the shin where the pins went in. Done. And near one of the pins, the lowest one, there was something else. A little warm. A little busy. Not grey. Tonk's own, fighting a small thing, the way Rook had fought the dog.

Jab sat and felt it and did not reach.

"You're not asleep," said Tonk.

He had not opened his eyes. He said it into the dark with his eyes shut and his fist on his chest.

"No," said Jab.

"You were a long time."

"Fourteen days."

"I *know*," said Tonk. "I counted. Senna helped. She knows the numbers after ten." He opened his eyes. "Give him."

Jab undid the button on the left side of his coat. He took out the fish and held it out on his palm.

Tonk took it with his left hand. He did not unwind the cord to do it. He held the fish up in the light from the lamp at the far end of the ward and turned it over. He looked at the body. He looked at the tail. He looked at the place where the fin had been. He put his thumb on the place.

"Did he eat inside?"

"No."

"Did you?"

"No. Nobody did."

"Did anybody sit down?"

"Callie," said Jab. "On her knees. She was hurt. She was allowed."

Tonk thought about this.

"That's allowed," he said. He put the fish on the pillow beside his head, where it had lived before. "Was he good?"

"He was good," said Jab. "He minded me. On the hill. There was a thing that wanted my hand and he was there the whole time." He did not know how to say the rest. "I didn't go to sleep."

"I can do the chair," he said. "All of it. By myself."

"And don't be asleep," said Tonk.

"I read it," said the Director.

She was quiet for a long time.

"Sixty-one Handfuls of the station's ash," she said. "Spent. And twenty-eight of a thing's that nobody owned, spent on six people and my watch." She put her pencil down. "Against two boxes."

"One box," said Toren. "The other's the proof."

"One box," said the Director. "In the rack. Green." She looked at him over her spectacles. "I went down and looked at it this morning. Before any of you were up. The technician showed me the light. He cried. I'd like that not repeated."

Nobody said anything.

"The sixty-one's the machine's," said the Director. "It's the station's spend. It's not a debt against anybody's household. I'm writing it so." She wrote it. "I lent it against your names so I'd know where it went. I know where it went."

---

"Now the other thing," she said.

She turned the paper over and put her finger on the back of it, near the middle. On the place she had read twice.

"*Under the line on purpose,*" she read out. "*Inside two turns. Three Handfuls. Jar four. T. and D. W.'s call. Once. For the Director.*"

She looked up.

"Say it," she said. "Both of you. In your own words. Not the paper's."

Toren said it first. He said it the way he had said it in the room, Jab thought, with his teeth together. It was the line, and the jar, and the lifter against the strut. Jab was on the push, and Callie was in the door with two turns. And it was nearly done, and if they had come out it had the door and the river.

"I said it out loud," said Toren. "Before. What it would cost and how long. And Dee said it was hers as well. And we did it, and it cost three, inside the two turns. And I wrote it that night."

"Why?"

"Because it was the rule," said Toren. "And I broke it. And you'd have to know."

Dee said it after. She said less.

"It's my line," she said. "I drew it on the thirty-seventh and I drew it again at the pump house. I watched it cross. I said *inside two turns, not a breath more*, and I turned the glass." She did not look away from the Director. "I'd do it again. The same, in the same room. I'd not do it anywhere else. That's the difference and I don't know how to write it better."

The Director looked at the two of them for a long time.

"I've had people break my rules," she said. "Every week of my life. I've had them break it and come home and say nothing. And I've found out, because I always find out. And I've had them off the register and out of the gate the same day, whoever they were." She put her hand flat on the paper. "Not for breaking it. For hiding it. A rule broken in secret is a rule that's gone. There's no rule after that, there's only what people think they can get away with."

She looked at the paper under her hand.

"You wrote it on the day," she said. "You said it to me before you said it to anybody in this station. You put both your names on it." She took her hand off. "That's an exception. It's owned. I can put it in a book."

She wrote. Jab watched the pencil. She wrote for a long time.

"This is what I've written," she said, and read it.

"*D+48. The signed line at Caul Hill crossed, on purpose, by T. Voss and D. Wren together, said aloud and priced beforehand, three Handfuls, inside two turns. Owned the same night. Censured, both names. Not praised. Not a model. The red-call rule and the abort line stand as signed, unchanged.*" She looked up. "*D. Wren draws nobody for fourteen days from today, on Doctor Sowerby's word and mine. T. Voss leads no party past the Sag under his own name before the spring review.*"

Toren did not say anything.

"You'll lead a lane," said the Director. "You'll lead a watch. You'll not take anybody out of my sight until I've seen a winter of you." She put the pencil down. "That's not a punishment. That's what it costs to have done it and told me."

"Yes," said Toren.

"And you," she said to Dee.

"Fourteen days," said Dee. "I'd have said twenty."

"Then I'll write twenty," said the Director. "Since you're offering."

For the first time Jab saw Dee's mouth move at the corner.

---

"The reviews," said the Director.

She took two sheets out of the drawer of the table. Jab knew them. They were the review sheets, the ones with the clocks on them, stopped at the gate on the forty-first. One had his name on it, and Tonk's. The other had Callie's and Tilda's.

"Held," said the Director. "Clocks stopped at the gate. I said they'd start again when you came back. You're back."

Jab felt Callie go still beside him.

"Twenty-eight days," said the Director. "That's the station's review. Every household on residency, every twenty-eight days, until they're on a contract or out." She looked at the sheets. "I've been sitting at this table for eleven years reading them. It's the worst thing this station does and I can't stop it with one hill."

She took the pencil.

"*C. and T.*," she said, and wrote. "Resident. A year. On T.'s tally work at the weighbridge, which the weighman has put in his book as paid. And on C.'s defender standing, which comes after. Reviewed next autumn." She did not look up. "Not every twenty-eight days. Once."

Callie did not say anything. Jab heard her breathe out through her teeth.

"*J. and T.*," said the Director. She wrote. "The boy's settled until he's done growing. That's written already, on the thirty-eighth, as a thing the station owes. It's not on this sheet and it'll never be on this sheet. It's not yours to trade and it's not mine to take back." She looked at Jab over her spectacles. "Do you understand me?"

"Yes," said Jab.

"Say it."

"Tonk's not on the paper," said Jab. "Whatever I say."

"Whatever you say," said the Director. She went on writing. "Your own residency's yours. Not on any service. Reviewed once a year, like hers." She put the pencil down. "The rest of the yard's still on twenty-eight days. Forty households. I'll have them on my desk next week, the same as always."

"Can you change that?" said Callie.

"Not with this," said the Director. She looked at the slate on its easel. "With the next one, perhaps. Or the one after. I've been trying for eleven years." She looked back at the sheets. "This is what one hill buys. Two households. Not forty."

---

"Now the roll," she said, to Jab.

He had known it was coming. She had said on the thirty-eighth she would ask again when he was back. He was back.

"The register," said the Director. "It has a line with your name on it. It says what you can do. On the thirty-eighth it said *His own* in the margin, in your words." She looked at him. "I'm asking you the thing I asked you on the twenty-seventh. The medical roll. On call. Sent for. Paid."

"No," said Jab.

She did not look surprised.

"Say why," she said.

"Because then it's not mine," said Jab. "It's whoever sends. And I'll go every time I'm sent, because I can't not. I found that out." He looked at his hand, the white place on the heel of it. "On the hill. I put my hand on a thing that needed everything, and I couldn't take it off. Dee took it off. I'd have kept drawing till there was nothing of me. Because it needed it, and I could." He looked at the Director. "If I'm on a roll, it's the same. It's a hill every day. There's nobody to say *off*."

The Director did not write anything.

"Then what?" she said.

Jab had thought about it. He had thought about it in the chair by the fifth bed, all night, and in the morning while Tonk did the chair, and on the way up the stair to this room. He had not thought about it in words. He found the words now, standing at the table.

"The table," he said. "Once a week. On the other side of Dee's. Teaching whoever comes. I said yes on the thirty-eighth. I'm saying it again. That's mine, and I'll do it."

"Yes," said the Director. She wrote it.

"And the Sag," said Jab.

She looked up.

"Two watches a week," said Jab. "This winter. With Dee or Rook as the watch, when they're off their fourteen days. Never on my own. Never without somebody to say *off*." He heard his voice. It was steady. "At the table behind the lamps. Drawing the watch when they've been in it. The way I did on the fifty-fifth." He stopped. "And my floor's in the book. Dessa's book. Every watch. Where it was and where I stopped."

The Director looked at him for a long time.

"Nobody asked you for that," she said.

"No," said Jab. "That's why I can say it."

She wrote it. She read it back.

"*J. Two watches a week at the Sag, this winter, as a Sustain worker, with a named watch, never alone. Floor recorded each time. His own offer. Not on call. Not on the roll. Not sent for.*"

"And one more," said Jab.

She waited.

"Supper," said Jab. "At the fifth bed on the right. Every day. With Tonk." He did not look away. "That's first. Before the table and before the Sag. If there's a day it's one or the other, it's supper."

Callie made a small sound beside him. Katori, by the window, turned her head.

The Director looked at him over her spectacles.

"That's not a term," she said. "That's a supper."

"Tonk said to put it in," said Jab. "This morning. After the chair. He said I was to put it on the paper first, or I'd forget." He was quiet a moment. "I'll not forget. He's right to want it written."

The Director looked at him a moment longer. Then she took the pencil and wrote at the top of the page, above everything else, in small square letters. She turned the page round on the table so he could read it.

*First: supper at the fifth bed. Daily. (T.'s term.)*

"Sign it," she said.

Jab took the pencil. He signed it at the bottom. *J.* He looked at it. It was the second time he had signed anything in this room. The first time he had signed a thing that said *His own*. This one said what his own was.

---

"The rest of you," said the Director.

She took another sheet out of the drawer. It was not a review sheet. It was blank at the top and ruled down the page, and at the head of it, in her hand, it said:

*Defender standing. Provisional. Reviewed in the spring.*

"The Sag needs more hands than it's got," she said. "It needed them before you went. It needs them more now. Fifty-eight this week." She did not look at the slate. "I've got two registered holders, and one of them's lying on his face and the other's in a chair in the east corridor. And I've got a yard full of young people who've done more on that lip in fourteen days than I'd have asked a crew of grown men to do in a month." She put the pencil down beside the sheet. "I'll not pretend I don't need you. And I'll not pretend it's right that I do."

Nobody said anything.

"So it's written," she said. "Standing. Not a contract. Each of you signs your own line or doesn't. There's an adult on every watch you stand. Mr Marsh, or Miss Wren, or Katori." She looked at Katori by the window. "Your floors in the book. Your injuries in the book. You're reviewed in the spring, and anybody who wants off, is off. No debt. No clock." She pushed the sheet across the table. "And if I find anybody on this sheet standing a watch alone, I'll have the whole sheet up and burn it in that stove."

Toren signed first. *T. Voss.*

Callie signed. *C.*

Dessa signed from the chair, with the big book under the sheet for a desk. *D. Cole.*

Jab signed. *J.*

Katori brought the sheet from the table to the door, where Otto was standing with his big hands in his pockets, going red. Wyck was behind him with his bar, cleared since yesterday, and the weighbridge girl with her plait and her leather binding. Otto signed. Wyck signed, left-handed. *W.*

The weighbridge girl took the pencil.

Jab watched her write. She wrote a whole name, carefully, first and last, in a clear round hand. It was the first time he had seen it. He did not read it. He looked away while she wrote it. It was hers, and she had put it on a paper, and that was enough.

She gave the pencil back.

"Seven," said Dessa, from the chair, with her eyes shut. She had counted the pencil going round. "On the sheet."

"Seven," said the Director. She took the sheet and looked at it, and put it in the drawer. "And the ones who aren't on it. The yard lifters. Ines." She looked at Katori. "You."

"I'm not young," said Katori.

"No," said the Director. "You're the watch." She took her spectacles off. "That's all. Go and do something useful. Dessa, I'd like the ledger at the end of the week, every page. Toren, I'd like the paper copied into it before you lose it."

"I'll not lose it," said Toren.

"Everybody loses paper," said the Director. "Copy it."

---

He went to the fifth bed at the supper hour.

He went straight from the east shed, where he had sat at Dee's long table for the first time on the other side of it. He had not taught anybody. Nobody had come. He had sat there for an hour with a jar and a square of paper and nobody on the far bench, and Dee had sat beside him and not drawn anybody, because she was not allowed.

"They'll come," Dee had said. "When they know the table's there. Next week. The week after."

"I'll be here," said Jab.

"I know you will," said Dee. "That's the whole of teaching. You're there when they come."

Mrs Lask was at the ward door. She did not say visiting was over. She looked at his hand, and he showed her the clean dressing, and she stood aside.

Tonk was in the chair by the bed, with a tray on his knee. It was porridge. There was a lump in it you could have built a wall with. Rook's bed was across the ward, the second on the left, and Rook was lying on his face in it with his arm hanging down, and Jab could hear him complaining about the porridge from the door.

There was a second tray on the end of the bed.

"You're late," said Tonk.

"I was at the table."

"The table's *after*," said Tonk. "It's on the paper. Supper's first."

"It's on the paper," said Jab. "I wrote it."

"Then you're late," said Tonk.

Jab sat down in the chair on the other side of the bed and took the second tray on his knee. The fish was on the pillow. The cord was round Tonk's fist, both ends. Jab ate the porridge. It was grey and thick and hot and there was not much of it, and it was the best thing he had eaten in fourteen days, and he did not say so, because Rook was already saying it the other way.

Tonk ate his, slowly, with his left hand, because his right had the cord in it.

"Did you put it first?" he said.

"First," said Jab. "Above everything. She wrote it herself."

Tonk looked at him over the tray.

"The lady upstairs?"

"The lady upstairs."

Tonk thought about this for a long time. He ate a spoonful of porridge and looked at the lump in it.

"Good," he said.

Jab finished his porridge. He put the tray on the floor. He sat in the chair by the fifth bed with his hands in his lap, one with a clean dressing on the heel of it, and watched his brother eat. He did not count anything. He did not reach for anything.

He was not asleep.

## CHAPTER — manuscript/chapter-48.md

# Chapter 48 — Toren: The First Page After

The weighman put the test weights on before he weighed anything.

Toren had seen him do it on the tenth day, walking past. He had not stood and watched it. He watched it now. The old man in the leather apron came out of the hut with an iron block in each hand and set them down in the middle of the plate one after the other, carefully, as if they might wake. Then he went back into the hut and looked at the beam for a long time. He moved the little weight along it with one finger, and looked again.

"Fifty," said Tilda, from the stool.

"Fifty," said the weighman.

He came out and took the blocks off the plate and carried them back into the hut, one at a time.

"Now it's honest," said Tilda. "Put them on."

---

It was the fifty-eighth, the third hour of the morning, and the whole of the ash was on the gravel beside the plate.

Otto had brought it from the east shed on the barrow, where it had been locked since the fifty-fifth. Toren had walked beside the barrow with the Director's paper in his coat. Jab walked on the other side with his hand an inch off the jars, not touching. Callie walked in front, with the counter.

Three home Flasks, grey to the shoulder, with Tilda's knots on the stoppers. The fence's fifth, whole. The fence's fourth, with three in it. Callie's dirty jar, with four. And the empties, six of them, clinking in the straw.

Otto put the first home Flask on the plate.

Tilda read the beam. She read it left-handed, with the tally board pinned against her knee by the heel of her right hand. She said the number. The weighman said it back. She wrote it in big careful figures and read it back to him.

"Eight," she said. "Clean. My knot."

"Your knot," said the weighman.

She did it three times. Eight, eight, eight. Each time she leaned forward on the stool and looked at the stopper through the hut window, at the thread round its neck and the knot in the thread. It was a knot Toren did not know, small and hard and folded back on itself.

"My grandfather tied it on grain sacks," said Tilda, when she saw him looking. "At the scale house. So nobody could say a sack had been opened when it hadn't." She wrote the third eight. "Or hadn't when it had."

"They hadn't," said Toren.

"I know they hadn't," said Tilda. "It's my knot. I'm saying it for the board."

---

The board was on the wall of the hut, painted black, with names down one side in white paint and numbers down the other in chalk. Callie's was on it, and Katori's crew, and a dozen others. Near the bottom there was the line that had been there since the thirty-ninth, in the weighman's chalk:

*LENT. CAUL HILL. 12 FLASKS, CLEAN. J., 8, IN THE FRAME. P., 4, ON THE CART. ROAD 1. HOME 3.*

And above it, still, boxed with a ruler, *SUSSTAIN*.

The weighman took the chalk off the ledge. He did not rub anything out. He drew a line through the LENT line, straight, with the ruler. Then he wrote under it.

*BACK: HOME 3, NOT TOUCHED (T.'S KNOTS). FENCE 11 (1 WHOLE, 3). = 35 CLEAN.*

He stood back and looked at it.

"And the rest?" he said.

Toren took out the Director's paper.

He had it in her hand, not his. She had written it on the fifty-seventh after they left the long room, and sent it down by Hallet to eleven that evening, folded twice. *For the board, and the book. Say it at the plate.* He unfolded it and read it out. He did not look up while he read, because he did not want to get any of it wrong.

"*Spent: sixty-one, clean, of the store's. The machine's. Not against any household.*"

The weighman wrote it.

"*The hill's: twenty-eight clean, lifted, spent on the party and the watch. Four dirty, to the dirty shelf, marked THE HILL, for the yard. The rest where it lies.*"

The weighman wrote it. *THE REST WHERE IT LIES.* He looked at it for a while before he went on.

"*Of the thirty-five back,*" read Toren, "*to the store: thirty-two. The fence's fourth, three, to the east shed, for the treatment and the table.*" He turned the paper over. "*From the store, by my order: two Flasks to the Sag, for the table behind the lamps, this winter. Half a Flask clean to the north yard, for the lanes, with the four dirty. Every jar against a name. Every jar chalked.*"

He looked up.

"*D. C.*," he said. "*For the Sag.* That's Dessa's name. And *Wyck, for the yard.*" He looked at the paper. "And her cross. In a circle."

The weighman wrote it all. When he had finished, the board had a new block of chalk under the struck line, as long as the line had been. He stood back.

"That's the most ash I've ever chalked in one morning," he said, "that I haven't weighed."

"You weighed the thirty-five," said Tilda.

"I weighed the thirty-five," said the weighman. "The rest I've chalked because she says so." He looked at the struck line. "I'll believe the sixty-one when I've seen that machine sing."

"It sang yesterday," said Callie. "Once."

"Then I'll believe it," said the weighman. He did not smile. "Once."

---

He wanted a name.

He said so while Otto was loading the store jars back onto the barrow to go down under the plate. He said it to Toren, with the chalk still in his fingers, looking at the board.

"Whose is it?" he said.

"Whose is what?"

"The crew." The weighman pointed with the chalk at the struck line, and the new block under it. "Every jar on that board goes against a crew. That's how it's kept straight. A crew's got a name. It's up there." He pointed up the board at the white-painted names down the side. "Katori's. The east lifters. The old north crew, that were before you came, that are in the ground now. So when I say *so-and-so's*, you know whose jars and whose debt and whose name the Director's cross goes under." He looked at Toren. "What do I paint?"

Toren looked at the board.

He knew what it was. He had heard it at a fire on the road, before the gate, with Rook sitting across it. A company, sitting in a yard, hiring. A man behind a table with a box. Sections named off whoever ran them, so the pay was kept straight. A brass chit, stamped with four letters and a number. *Tull's sixth.*

"Not mine," he said.

The weighman waited.

"Not mine," said Toren again. "We're not named off anybody."

"Then whose?"

Toren did not know.

He looked at Callie. She shook her head once. She did not want it either. He looked at Jab, who had his hand on his coat, over the fish that was not there any more, because it was on a pillow in the fifth bed. Jab looked back at him and did not say anything.

"It's got a name," said Dessa.

She was in the barrow. Otto had brought her up in it from the Sag at the end of the night watch, with the big book in her lap and her bad leg out straight. She had been sitting in it at the edge of the plate the whole time, with her eyes shut, listening.

"It's got a name already," she said. "It's in the book. It's been in the book since the thirty-seventh." She opened her eyes. "You wrote it. Toren. In your hand. On the page with the ledger."

She opened the big book on her knee. She turned the pages back, a long way, past the departure page and the six lines and everything under them, to a page Toren knew. The ledger page. The ash and the rotation and the lines, and at the bottom of it, in his own hand, because he had written it there that night at the table in the shed:

*Home line, command: D. Cole.*

"That was the ones who stayed," said Toren.

"That was the ones who held," said Dessa. "While you were gone. And then on the fifty-fifth, in the fog, it was the ones who held and the ones who came back, together. I wrote it. *Held by the home line and the six together.*" She did not look away from him. "And then I ran out of lines. And I wrote the rest under them, with no lines. Everybody."

"*Home line*," said Tilda, from the stool. She said it slowly, one word and then the other, with the little space in front of each. She was trying it. "That's a name."

"It's not anybody's," said Callie.

"It's everybody's," said Dessa. "That's why." She shut the book. "Paint it."

The weighman looked at Toren.

Toren looked at the board, at the white names down the side. Katori's. The east lifters. The old north crew, in the ground. He thought about a boy of thirteen on a road with his father's Ember gone cold in his fist and nobody behind him and nobody ahead. He thought about a company that burned a yard and broke up and took another name at another wall. He thought about a Sag at dusk, and a girl on a crate with her eyes shut, and a whole line stepping back behind the lamps at once because she had said *everybody back*.

"Home line," he said.

The weighman went into the hut and came out with a pot of white paint and a brush. He painted it on the board, down the side, under Katori's, in square white letters. He took a long time over it. When he had finished he stood back.

*HOME LINE.*

And beside it, in chalk, where the numbers went, he wrote *D. C.*

"Her command," he said. "Not yours."

"Not mine," said Toren.

"Good," said the weighman. He put the brush in the pot. "I don't like crews named after boys. They've no idea what they're doing."

"He's right," said Tilda, from the stool, and wrote nothing, and looked at the board for a long time.

---

Rook was lying on his face in the second bed on the left.

He had been lying on his face for three days. He said it was the longest he had lain still since he was born, and that it was worse than the hole. Sowerby had opened the foot of the stitches on the fifty-sixth and let the yellow out, and put something in it that Rook would not describe, and closed it. The red had not gone further since. It had begun, Sowerby said, to come in. He said it the way he said everything, as if it were bad news.

Toren went to see him on the fifty-ninth. Hallet was already there.

He was standing at the end of the bed in his grey cardigan with his pen on its string, and he had something in his hand. He was saying things, quietly, at length, about the south gate and the fifty-sixth and an arrival party of four, from the east road. He stopped when Toren came in.

"Go on," said Rook, into the pillow. "He's all right. He's heard worse."

Hallet looked at Toren. Then he held out the thing in his hand to Rook, low, by the side of the bed, where Rook could see it without lifting his head.

It was a piece of brass. It was small, flat, the size of a thumbnail, with a hole punched through one end.

"One of the four paid his intake with it," said Hallet. "At my window. On the fifty-sixth. He put it on the ledge and said it was good anywhere on the east road." He was quiet. "The gate doesn't take brass. I told him so. He took it back and paid in the station's coin, which he had. Then he went off to the south huts." He looked at the brass in his hand. "He left it on the ledge after. I don't think by accident. I've kept it since."

"Why?" said Rook.

"Because of the stamp," said Hallet.

Rook put out his hand and took it.

He did not lift his head off the pillow. He held the brass in his fingers in front of his face and turned it over, once, to the stamped side. Toren could see it from where he stood. There were letters stamped in it, and a number. He did not know the letters. They were not four. The number was not six.

Rook looked at it for a long time.

His face did nothing. It did nothing so completely that Toren looked at it twice. He had seen it do that on a gravel bar at midnight, four feet from a disk on a cord. He had seen it at a low fire, across from a girl with a warm stone in her hands.

Rook turned the brass over, face down, on the blanket by his hand. He put his palm flat over it.

"It's the same brass," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"Thank you," said Rook, to Hallet. "I'll keep it now."

"I'd like it back," said Hallet. "For the ledger. It's evidence of a thing."

"It's evidence of a thing," Rook agreed, into the pillow. "You'll have it back. When I can sit up and look at it with the right face on." He shut his eyes. "Not now."

Hallet looked at him for a moment longer. Then he nodded, and went, with his pen swinging on its string.

Toren stood at the end of the bed.

"Don't," said Rook, without opening his eyes.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to ask me what it said," said Rook. "It said a name I don't know yet, and a number I don't know yet. That's all it said." His hand lay flat on the brass on the blanket. "It's the same brass. That's all I know. And four men from the east road, in the south huts, since the fifty-sixth."

"What will you do?"

"Lie on my face," said Rook. "For four more days. Sowerby says." He was quiet. "Then sit up. Then stand. Then I'll ask Hallet where the south huts are, as if I didn't know." He opened his eyes and looked at the wall. "Then we'll see."

Toren did not say anything.

"Go on," said Rook. "You've a lane at the fourth hour. Katori said. You'll be late, and she'll look at you."

---

The lanes were there.

He had not been sure they would be. Nobody had been in the north yard for fourteen days but the watch, going to and from the lip. The whitewash had gone grey on the cinders with the frost and the rain and the boots, and in places it had gone altogether.

The weighbridge girl was repainting them.

She was doing it with Rook's bucket and his long brush, bent over, walking backward along the line of the first lane where it ran along the shed wall. Otto held the bucket and walked with her. She did not look up when Toren came into the yard. She painted the line the whole way to the post, straight, sixty paces, and then went back and started the second.

"Who told you?" said Toren.

"Nobody told me," said the girl. "It was gone." She kept painting. "You can't have a lane with no line. You'd not know where to stand."

There were three.

The first lane ran along the shed wall, with Rook's posts and the rope and the frame of uprights with the brackets that did not match. Nothing in it was straight, and nothing was where you expected it. That was the point. The second lane went round the end of the sand heap, and it was Wyck's. The third went out to the east shed, to the long table, and it did not have a post in it. It had a bench and a stove.

Katori was at the end of the first lane with her cap pulled down.

"You're late," she said.

"I was with Rook."

"Then you were with Rook," said Katori. She did not ask. "It's your lane. I'm the adult." She said *the adult* as if it tasted of something. "The Director's paper says there's an adult. I'm standing here. That's all I'm doing."

"You're not teaching?"

"You're teaching," said Katori. "I'm watching you do it. If you do it wrong I'll say."

---

He did not know how to teach a lane.

He stood at the head of the first lane with his arm in its cuff, and looked at them. The weighbridge girl, finished painting, with her blade dark on her right fist and her plait down her back. Otto, with his big hands. The two yard lifters, one with a bandage on his calf where the hound had got past his bar on the fifty-fifth, standing on it anyway. Three of the young ones from the south huts, whom Toren did not know, who had come to the yard gate that morning because they had heard. Their names were in Dessa's book already. He did not know them yet.

He thought about Rook, on the first day, painting the lanes. He thought about the first drill. *One obstacle, three answers.* And Rook making them swap problems, not shapes.

He did not do that. He did not know how to yet.

He did the only thing he knew.

"This is the lane," he said. "There's a post at the end. You walk to it. When I say, something comes at you from the side. A sack, off the frame." He nodded at the frame with the brackets that did not match. "It's not a husk. It's a sack. You don't have to stop it."

"Then what?" said one of the young ones.

"You say what you're doing," said Toren. "Out loud. Before you do it. *Holding.* Or *taking.* Or *letting go.* And somebody beside you says *yours*, or *mine*." He looked at them. "That's the lane. Saying it out loud. Even when it's awful."

The weighbridge girl looked at him.

"That's on the first page," she said. "Of Dessa's book."

"I know," said Toren. "It was Milo's."

He did not say who Milo was. She did not ask. She walked to the head of the lane with her blade dark and stood there, and he let the sack go off the frame, and she said *holding* before it reached her, and Otto said *mine*, and stepped in with his weight in his heels. The sack hit Otto's shoulder and stopped.

"That's the lane," said Katori, from the end of it. It was the first thing she had said in an hour. "Do it again."

---

Senna was in her chair in the corner by the east shed, where he left it for her, out of the wind with its back to the stove pipe.

She had been lifted into it by two orderlies at the third hour and pushed down the long slope, complaining about the camber, and brought across the yard. Nobody had asked her to come. She sat in it with her hands in her lap, the fingers not quite closed, and a blanket over her knees. She watched the lanes.

Dessa was on a crate beside her with the big book open.

"She's giving reasons," said Dessa, when Toren came to the corner at the end of the morning. "For every drill. Why it's that way. I'm writing them."

"Whose reasons?"

"Hers," said Dessa. "And Rook's, that she's heard him say. And yours, that she's heard you say. And mine." She did not look up from the page. "It's not what you'd call a lesson. It's why."

"Read me one," said Senna.

Dessa read. "*The lane with the crooked posts. Why: because the road isn't straight. S. V.*"

"That's it," said Senna. "That's the whole reason." She turned her head on the back of the chair and looked at Toren. "Somebody in a hundred years will read that and think it's stupid. They'll straighten the posts."

"And then?"

"And then somebody'll fall over on a crooked road," said Senna, "and go and read the book. And find out why." She shut her eyes. "That's what a book's for. So you can find out why after the person who knew is dead."

Toren looked at the page.

It had columns. He had seen Dessa rule them on the fifteenth, on the first page after Milo's line. *Exercise. What went wrong. Who was hurt. What it cost. How long to come back. Second go.* They ran all the way down the page, in her small neat hand, and the page after that, and the page after that. The whole morning was in them. The girl's *holding*. Otto's *mine*. The yard lifter with his bandaged calf, who had stood on it and should not have, with *K. sent him to the bench. 3rd go.*

"There's a column for reasons now," said Dessa. "I've ruled it in. On the right. Hers."

"That's seven columns."

"There'll be more," said Dessa. "I keep finding things that want writing down that I didn't know were things."

---

They chose it on the evening of the fifty-ninth, on the steps.

Nobody had said anything about it. Toren had not been waiting for anybody to. The Director had given them standing and put them on a sheet, and Dessa had the watch rota to make for the winter, and she would make it, and they would stand where she put them.

He was on the step of eleven with the paper on his knee. He was copying it into the big book, a line at a time, because the Director had said to and because everybody loses paper. Callie was on the step of fourteen with the hook across her knees and her mother beside her. Jab came down the row from the long building in the last of the light, from supper at the fifth bed, and did not go into twelve. He sat down on its step.

The lamp at the end of the row had not come on yet.

Dessa came along the row in the barrow with Otto pulling it and the rota on a board in her lap. She stopped between eleven and twelve.

"I've the winter," she said. "The watches. I've put you where you're wanted. Tell me now if it's wrong, because once it's on the board it's on the board." She held it up. "Toren, the lane, mornings. The Sag, the first watch, three nights in seven. Jab, the table behind the lamps, two watches. Callie—" She stopped. "Callie, I've put on the lip with me, reading. Not holding. Keel says six weeks for the ribs. The second watch."

"Put us on the same watch," said Callie.

Dessa looked at her.

"The three of us," said Callie. "The same one. When Jab's on the table, put me on the lip, and Toren on the lane. The same night."

"Why?"

Callie did not answer at once. She sat on the step of fourteen with the hook across her knees.

"Because I know where they'll be," she said. "Without looking."

Dessa looked at Jab.

"I can feel where they are," said Jab. "If they're near. I can feel if they're busy." He looked at his hand, with the clean dressing on the heel. "It's cheaper. If it's them."

Dessa looked at Toren.

He had not been going to say anything. He had been going to let Dessa put them where she put them. He found he had put the pencil down.

"Because that's the line," he said.

Dessa did not say anything for a moment. She looked at the three of them, one at a time, the way she did the order at night. Then she took the pencil from behind her ear and made a change on the board, and another.

"The second watch," she said. "All three. With Dee when she's off her twenty days, and Rook when he's off his face." She put the pencil back. "It'll mean you're tired together. And hurt together. And if there's a bad night, it'll be a bad night for all three of you at once."

"Yes," said Toren.

"That's not cheaper."

"No," said Toren. "It's what we are."

Dessa looked at him for a long moment in the dusk.

"I'll write that," she said. "Not on the board. In the book. In the reasons." She looked at Otto. "Go on."

Otto pulled the barrow on down the row.

---

He sat on the step of eleven after dark with the big book shut on his knee.

The lamp had come on at the end of the row, flat and white. Down the row Callie had gone in, and her mother. The door of twelve was shut, and there was no light in it. Jab had gone back to the long building after Dessa, to the chair by the fifth bed. He would sleep there tonight, and tomorrow night, until Tonk told him to stop.

Toren put his hand on his shirt.

He did it without deciding to. He did it the way his hand had gone there at the yellow line in the shed, on the fifty-sixth, when he did not know it had. He knew now. He put it there on purpose, flat, over his breastbone, on the shirt, under the coat.

It was there. It was always there. A disk the width of two thumbs, near black, on a cord of braided boot-lace. There were nine fine grooves on each face, from the centre out to the rim. It was cold. It had always been cold. It had been cold on a gravel bar with a man looking at it who knew what it was and would not say. It had been cold on the post at the wash with the water going under, and cold at the top of the dark lane. It had been cold under his hand in the shed, at the yellow line, beside a round seat in a steel rack with nine ribs in it and nothing sitting there.

*Released.*

He did not take it out.

His grandfather had worn it forty years and never once let anybody see it. His father had said it to him on the road, plainly, as a piece of information. *Under the shirt, always. You don't show it. Not at a gate, not at a wall, not to a friend.* He had not known why. Neither had his father, or his grandfather. They had done it anyway, and they were still there. Or they had been.

Callie had seen his hand. He knew she had. She had looked at it twice at the yellow line and said nothing, and put it wherever Callie put things.

He did not take it out. He did not write anything on the paper. He sat on the step with his hand flat on his shirt and the disk cold under it, and thought about a line in a book, in pencil, in a hand nobody knew. *Bearer.* Whoever had it in his hand.

He had it.

He took his hand away.

---

In the morning of the sixtieth he went down to the Sag with the first watch, because the rota was not on the board yet and Dessa said he could.

It was cold. The fog lay in the bottom of the dip, white, milk, the ordinary fog of a morning, lying still until the sun got to it. The lamps stood along the east track on their poles. The dark lane ran down between the third and the fourth. The hollow at the south lip had frost in the bottom of it.

The day holder was in her chair at the lip.

She had asked Sowerby nine times, Dessa said, and he had said no eight. She sat in the chair in her grey yard coat with a blanket over her knees and did not hold anything. She could not hold anything. She sat and looked at the bottom of the Sag, where she had stood for two years.

"Morning, boy," she said, when he came to the lip.

"Morning," said Toren.

Callie was on the crate beside Dessa's, with the counter. She had come down with the first watch too. She was not holding anything either. She was reading.

"Twenty," she said. "At the lip. Forty at the bottom." She watched the fog. "It's lying still."

"Is there a count?" said Toren.

Callie watched for a long time. The fog lay in the bottom of the Sag, white, with the black water under it between the slabs. Then, on the east side, over the joints, it thickened. Not much. A hand. And lay down again.

She did not say a number.

"No," she said. "There's no count."

Toren looked north, across the Sag, past the north lip. He looked up the long straight of the north road where it went over the rise, toward the drovers' fold and the old line and the siding and the wash. A hundred miles and more. And at the end of it, a hill with its top taken off, and a sky over it.

He could not see it from here. He did not need to. He knew what it was doing.

Under that hill there was a room with a slope of grey on its floor, knee deep, more than anybody could lift in a day with nobody hurt. There were three stoppered jars under the racks, with a chalk cross beside them. There was a live box half open, talking to the room. There were ten more rooms behind a door, off a corridor going west, toward a round wall with its top broken off. Nobody had looked in them. And there was a gate with a twist of green copper through its chain, the ends left loose, so they would come.

Somebody would go back there. He did not know who, or when. Not before the spring. Not under his name.

And in the south huts there were four men from the east road who paid in brass.

And at the end of this row of huts there was a woman with a cap pulled down who had something she had not said yet. She had said so on the forty-first at the gate, to Rook. *I've not said the rest.*

He stood on the south lip of the Sag in the cold with his arm in its cuff and did the sum. He could not help doing it. It was a long one. It did not have an answer at the bottom. It had ifs, one to a line, as many as he could think of.

And under all of them, the one he had written first, on the step of eleven, the night before they went.

"Toren," said Dessa. She had her eyes shut. "You're doing a sum. I can hear it."

"I'm doing a sum."

"Then write it down," said Dessa. "Later. Not on the lip." She did not open her eyes. "Count them first."

He counted them.

He did it out loud, standing at the lip, the way he did it every morning now. The day holder, in her chair. Dessa, on the crate. Callie, beside her, with the counter. Katori, at the top of the dark lane, with her blade dark. Ines on the west rim. Otto on the lamp-line, between the two yard lifters, with his weight in his heels. The girl on the lip path. Wyck, cleared, on the lip above the lamps, where he could see everybody's feet.

And up the made road behind him, coming down from the yard gate in the grey light, not running, a figure with a frame on its back and its right hand held a little out from its side. It was coming to the table behind the lamps for the first of its two watches, because it had said so, on a paper, in its own words.

Jab stopped at the table and looked at the lip, and at the lamps, and at the two of them. He lifted his hand.

Toren lifted his.

"Everybody," he said.

Dessa did not open her eyes.

"Everybody back," she said, and wrote it in the book.
