# Chapter 25 — Callie: Two Steps

By the end of the first mile Callie knew the order was wrong.

She had walked it in the north yard a dozen times. Pell at Duchess's head, then Miss Wren, then Rook, then Jab with the frame, then Callie, then Toren last. They had walked it round the whitewash lanes with sand in the jars and the cart creaking empty, and it had looked like a line. It had looked like something that would hold.

The old north road was not the north yard.

It ran north out of the Sag in two ruts with a hump of frozen grass between them, and the ruts were full of ice that had set in ridges overnight. Duchess did not care about the ridges. She walked the way a sound horse walks on a cold morning when she has been kept in a stable for six weeks. She walked as if the road had been built for her and she meant to see the end of it by noon. Pell walked at her head with the lead rope loose in his hand, and the cart came after her with the wheels cracking the ice, and the gap between the tailboard and Rook opened a pace, and then another.

Callie watched it open. She could not help watching it. It was a thing that was happening on the ground.

Jab was two paces in front of her. He had the frame high on the bone of his hips the way Wyck had shown him, with the chest band under the yoke. The eight jars stood in their rack where Tonk's seat had been, grey to the shoulder, and the curved rail at the top had the top row leaning on it. He walked well. He had walked four hundred miles with a frame on his back. But he had walked them with his brother in it, and his brother had weighed less than eight Flasks of ash and the jars they were in. Callie could see the difference in his stride. It was shorter than it had been at the gate. He was taking the ruts carefully, one foot and then the other, like a man going down a stair with a tray.

Rook was beside him on his left, walking a careful straight walk with his hands at his sides. He did not look back.

Behind her, Toren said nothing at all. She knew he was counting.

"Pell," said Miss Wren, at the second milestone.

Pell stopped. He did it without looking round, the way he did most things, with one word to Duchess and a hand on her neck, and the cart creaked to a halt forty paces ahead of Rook.

---

They came up to it in ones and twos. Nobody said anything about the forty paces. Miss Wren stood by the tailboard with her book under her arm and watched them come, and when Toren had arrived last and stopped, she turned the brass glass over on the flat of the tailboard.

"Ten turns," she said. "Then we'll talk about what just happened. Sit down if you can sit."

Jab could not sit.

Callie saw him work that out. He looked at the frost-white verge, and at the jars on his back, and at the ground. Nothing clean went on the ground. It was the first thing Miss Wren had said about the jars in the shed, and she had said it every day since. Clean on a rack or a bench or a shelf, dirty on the floor, and the road was a floor. If he took the frame off, eight jars of lent ash would sit on the frozen grass of the old north road, and every time he did it, somebody would have to lift the whole of it back onto him. So he stood in the ruts with the weight on his hips and his thumbs hooked in the chest band and did not say anything.

Pell looked at him for a moment. Then he went round to the back of the cart and dropped the tailboard.

It came down on its chains and hung level, at about the height of a man's belt. Pell tested it with his hand. He moved two rolls of canvas to one side of it and made a gap.

"Back up," he said.

Jab looked at him.

"Back up to it," said Pell. "Slowly. Let the rack come down on the board. Don't take the belt off." He put his hand flat on the tailboard. "She'll take the weight. The frame stays on you. The jars don't touch the road."

Jab backed up to the cart. The bottom of the rack met the edge of the tailboard, and he let his knees go a little, and the board took the frame. Callie saw the weight go off his hips. She saw his shoulders come down half an inch and his face change, very slightly, the way her mother's face had changed at the weighbridge the first time she sat on the stool.

"Oh," said Jab.

"Every halt," said Pell. "You come to the tailboard. Nobody else uses it." He went back to Duchess.

Callie looked at the tailboard, and the chains, and the frame resting on the edge of it.

It was a thing her grandfather would have done. It was the kind of thing that had been done at the scale house at Thistle Ford a hundred times a day by people who did not think about it. Get the weight onto something that is not you. Don't put down what you'll have to pick up again. She had thought of the cart as the thing that carried the lead box and the home jars and the camp. She had not thought of it as a thing a person could lean on.

---

"It was forty paces," said Miss Wren.

She said it at the eighth turn of the glass, with all of them round the tailboard. She did not say it to anybody in particular.

"It'll be sixty by noon," said Pell. "The horse walks at the horse. She's been in a box six weeks."

"Then the horse is wrong," said Rook.

"The horse is never wrong," said Pell. "The horse is a horse. You don't argue with her. You put her where she can't be wrong." He looked at Jab resting against the tailboard. "A column walks at its slowest. That's him, with eight on his back. Everybody walks at him. The horse walks at him too."

"And me," said Rook.

Nobody answered that.

"And you," said Miss Wren, after a moment. She did not look at him. "I watched you. You were keeping up with him and you were paying for it. You kept your shoulders square for a mile so nobody would see the back." She opened her book. "You'll walk beside him. Not in front. Beside."

Rook looked at her. Then he looked down the road and did not argue, which Callie had never seen him do.

"And Callie in front," said Pell.

Callie looked up.

"You read the road," said Pell. "That's on the paper. The floor inside the fence and the road outside it. You can't read the road from behind a frame." He put his hand on Duchess's neck. "Twenty paces in front of her. Look at what the wheels will go over, not what you'll go over. You'll know the difference."

"I'll know it," said Callie.

So they made a new order, standing in the ruts at the second milestone with the glass running. Callie out in front, twenty paces ahead of Duchess. Pell at Duchess's head. The cart. Jab at the tailboard, so that every halt was already there when it came. Rook on Jab's left. Miss Wren behind them with the glass and the book, where she could see every one of them without turning her head. Toren last, as runner.

Pell wanted halts by the milestones. Every second stone, he said, and ten turns of the glass. Miss Wren wanted halts by the glass. Rook wanted to stop when the frame said so. They argued about it for two turns, not loudly, while Jab rested his rack on the tailboard and watched them.

"Two stones," said Toren, from behind.

They looked at him.

"Two stones and ten turns," he said. "And if Jab wants a halt before two stones, he says *board*, and we stop, and nobody asks him why." He was not looking at anybody. He was looking down the road. "That way it's a number and it's him. Both."

Miss Wren looked at him for a moment. Then she wrote it down.

"Say it," she said to Callie. "Before we go."

Callie did not ask what. She had known since the gate that it would be her. It was the only kind of counting she was allowed now, and she was good at it.

"Road," she said. "One Flask. Eight Handfuls. On the cart. None gone." She looked at Jab's back. "Inside, eight Flasks. Sixty-four. In the frame. None gone. Home, three Flasks. Twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." She stopped. "Settled. A Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in you."

"And the counter."

Callie took the counter out of its canvas bag and turned the switch on the side. It ticked. One, and nothing, and two close together. She held it at the level of her chest and counted ten breaths.

"Seven," she said. "It's the room."

"It's the road," said Miss Wren, and wrote it. "Every halt. The same words. Out loud."

Callie put the counter back in the bag. She knew, putting it back, whose words they were. They were her mother's, on the floor by the stove with the store book open on her knee when she could not sleep. *Thirty-one Flasks and a Handful. Four of them thin.* The same words every night, read out so they were said somewhere and not only in her mother's head.

She went out twenty paces in front of Duchess and began to read the road.

---

It was not hard reading, on the first day. That was the strange thing.

The old north road was a road people had used. Callie could see it in the ruts, which were old and deep and full of ice, and in the stones that had been put into the worst holes at some time and pressed down by wheels until they were part of the road. Somebody had cleared a fallen branch off it and dragged it into the ditch. The branch was grey and soft with a winter or two of rot. Nobody had been this way since, not with a cart.

She read it for the wheels. That was what Pell had said, and he was right, and it changed what she saw. For herself she would have seen a road. For the wheels she saw where the ruts went off at an angle round a frost heave. She saw where the hump between them was higher than the axle, and where the verge was soft under the white.

At the first bridge she stopped.

It was not a bridge, really. It was a culvert under the road with a low stone parapet on each side. The road went over it on a hump. She walked out onto the hump and stood on it, and looked down at the stones on the downstream side, and at the water coming out under them.

The water was brown. It came out fast and noisily, too fast for the size of the ditch, and there was a fan of bright gravel spread out below the mouth of the culvert that had not been there long enough to grow anything.

She walked back off the hump.

"Pell," she said, when Duchess came up. "Go on the left of it. The high side. The right-hand wheel wants the parapet."

Pell looked at the culvert and then at her.

"Why?"

"There's been a lot of water through it, lately, and fast. There's new gravel below it. The stones on the downstream face are standing proud of each other, the way they do when something's come out from behind them." She pointed with the hook's handle. "It'll take the cart. I think it'll take the cart. But if it's going to go, it'll go on the right."

Pell looked at the stones for a long moment. Then he took Duchess over the hump on the left, with the right-hand wheel a hand's width from the parapet, and it took the cart. Nothing moved.

"Right," said Pell, and walked on.

That was all. Callie went out in front again. She found that her face was hot, and she did not know why, and then she did. It was the first time since the plain that she had read a piece of ground for somebody who had simply done what she said. He had not argued or asked her to be sure. He had only asked *why*, which was different. At the Sag there had always been Katori, and Katori had always looked at the ground herself first.

She thought about her mother on the stool at the weighbridge, reading the beam before the old man could.

It was the ninth hour of the morning, near enough. At the long building the doors would be open, and Mrs Lask would be at her list. The fourth bed on the left, where her mother came every morning at the eighth hour for her pills, would be made up tight again and empty. Her mother had walked into the ward on her stick this morning, and let go of Callie's hand first. Then she would have walked back out again past Mrs Lask, with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. And now it was an hour past the pills and Callie did not know how the hand was. She did not know if the right leg had held on the step of fourteen.

She did not know.

She found, walking out in front of the cart on the old north road, that she was holding the strap of the counter bag. She had it in her left fist where it crossed her chest, tight, the way she had held the conduit on the hill with the wagon on it. She had not known she was doing it.

She opened her hand.

The strap fell back against her coat. Her hand hung at her side, open, with the cold on the palm.

It was harder here. She had not expected that. She had thought, on the step of fourteen the night before with her mother asleep behind her, that the hard part was the letting go, and that once it was done it would be done. It was not like that. On the stool at the weighbridge she could have seen the step of fourteen. On the old north road there was only the next culvert, and the next, and the whole of the road between her and a bed she could not see, and she had to let go of it again every few hundred paces. Every time, it was a little further off.

She read the road. It was the one thing she could do about any of it.

---

At noon Miss Wren measured their floors.

She did it at the tailboard with Jab's rack resting on it and the glass running, two fingers on the inside of each wrist in turn, the way she had at the gate. Callie held her hand out without thinking.

She held it out palm up.

It was her mother's way. It was how her mother held her hand out on the ledge of the hut window, or on the blanket, when she wanted Callie's hand in it. Callie had held her hand out like that at the stove every night for a month, and on the road for four hundred miles before that. She did it now for Miss Wren at the tailboard without deciding to. Then she saw what she had done and felt her face go hot again.

Miss Wren looked at the hand. She did not say anything about it. She put two fingers on the inside of the wrist, where she would have put them anyway, and looked away down the road while she counted.

"Where it was," she said. "Your own and the stone." She let go. "You've been holding something."

"The strap."

"I saw," said Miss Wren. She wrote something in her book. "Carry it in your pocket if you have to hold something. Not across your chest. If you go down with a strap across your chest, you go down on the counter." She moved on to Toren.

They ate at the tailboard. It was bread and cold meat from the long building's kitchen and a twist of salt, and water from the skins, and it was good. Duchess ate out of a nosebag with her eyes half shut. Rook sat on the edge of the tailboard beside Jab's rack, very carefully, and got up again very carefully when it was time. It took him two movements. Nobody counted them out loud. Callie counted them in her head and was ashamed of herself, and then was not, because Toren was counting them too. She could see him doing it.

---

In the afternoon the wind got up from the west.

It came across a long field on the left of the road that had been ploughed at some time and never sown, and had gone back to dirt and thistle. The wind took the top off the dirt and brought it across the road in a low brown haze. It got into the eyes and the teeth and the folds of the coat. Duchess put her head down and walked into it. Callie turned her face half away and read the road out of the corner of her eye.

Rook came up beside her.

She had not heard him come. He had left Jab at the tailboard and come up past the cart and past Duchess at his careful straight walk, and he was beside her now, twenty paces in front of the horse, with his collar turned up against the grit.

"Miss Wren said beside Jab," said Callie.

"Miss Wren's watching me do it," said Rook. "She'll tell me when I'm to go back. I wanted to see what you see." He looked at the brown haze going across the road. "That's what it'll be like up there."

"Dust?"

"Dust off a place that's worse than a field." He put his thumb to the corner of his eye and took it away grey. "The kind you don't get off with spit. Senna's crew washed at the river every night because of it. It's what's on the ground that gets onto you. That, and what you breathe." He was quiet for a few steps. "Your Ward stops a hound. Does it stop that?"

Callie looked at the haze.

"I don't know," she said. "I've never tried it on dust."

"Try it."

"Miss Wren says every hold's paid for."

"She does," said Rook. "And she's right. And we've got six days of road to learn on, and no yard at the end of them." He looked at her. "Ask her."

Callie stopped and let Duchess come up to her, and let the cart come up to her, and walked back along it to Miss Wren.

Miss Wren had heard every word. Callie could see she had. She walked behind the cart with the glass in one hand and the book under her arm, and she did not stop walking.

"Off the stone," she said. "Not your own. A little, and then stop, and you'll say how much, out loud, before you do anything else. Tonight you'll settle back what you spent, out of the road jar, and Callie will say that too." She looked at Callie. "That's the price of learning on a road. It's paid in the same ash you'll want at the fence. Every hold you practise here is a hold you haven't got at the stair. I'd like you to know that before you decide."

Callie thought about it for the length of the cart.

"I'll try it," she said.

---

She did it on herself first, because that was the one she knew.

She walked beside Jab at the tailboard, on his right, on the windward side where the grit came across. She put the hook over her shoulder out of the way, and she reached for the stone at the bottom of the well and did not shut her hand over it. She let the Ward come up out of the stone and into her. Not into the steel. Into her.

It came up through her heels, the way it always came, and over her.

The grit stopped.

It stopped hitting her face. She could see it going across the road in its low brown haze and not getting to her. It met something a hand's width from her skin and went round it, the way water goes round a stone in a stream. She could hear it. It made a small dry hiss against the Ward, like sleet on a window.

She took one step.

The Ward held. She felt it hold, the whole shape of it round her, with the weight of her coming down through her front foot into the road.

She took another.

And as her back foot came up off the road, the Ward tore.

It did not go out. It tore, low down at the back, the way a sheet tears when you pull it off a line one-handed and it catches on the peg. She felt it go ragged behind her heel and then all up the back of her, and the grit came in through the tear and hit her in the back of the neck. The whole shape of it went out like a lamp.

She stood in the road with the grit in her eyes.

"Two steps," said Toren, from the back.

"I know it was two steps."

"It was two steps in the yard," said Toren. "On the twentieth. Dessa wrote it."

"I *know*, Toren."

Jab, at the tailboard, made a small sound that might have been a cough.

Callie did it again. This time she did not lift her back foot. She kept both feet flat on the road and slid them forward, one and then the other, like somebody going across a frozen pond who does not trust the ice. The Ward came up. The grit went round her. She shuffled one foot forward, flat. It held. She shuffled the other. It held. She shuffled the first.

It held for three shuffles. On the fourth her heel came up a fraction of an inch off a ridge of ice, and it tore.

Rook laughed.

He did not laugh unkindly. He laughed the way he had laughed once at the gate, low and careful, the way a man laughs with a hole in his back. He was walking beside Jab with his collar up and his hands at his sides, and he laughed at the sight of her shuffling up the old north road like an old woman on a wet floor. After a moment Callie laughed too, because she could see it. She could see exactly what it looked like.

"It wants ground," said Rook.

She stopped laughing.

"It's always wanted ground," said Rook. "Everything you've ever held had ground under it. The hook on the scarp. The rail at the jam. The pipe. The rack. Your own feet in the lane, standing. The minute there's no ground under a bit of it, that bit goes." He looked at her. "You're standing in it, and then you're lifting half of what it's standing on."

"The wagon was on a hill," said Callie.

"The hill was ground."

Callie walked for a while with the grit in her teeth.

"How much?" said Miss Wren, from behind.

Callie went down to look. She did not like the answer, and she said it anyway.

"A quarter," she said. "Out of the stone. For about five steps, all told. On me." She heard it. It was more than a turn and a half of the glass in the hook, for five steps. "Settled, three quarters."

"Three quarters," said Miss Wren, and wrote it. "It's three times the price on you. You knew that."

"I knew it."

"Now you know it on a road," said Miss Wren. "That's different. That's enough for today."

"I want to try it in the hook."

"Tomorrow," said Miss Wren. "One thing a day. You've learned the thing today. It wants ground. Sleep on it." She turned the glass. "And tonight you'll settle a quarter out of the road jar, and you'll say it, and the road'll be seven and three quarters. That's the day's price."

Callie walked in front of the cart for the rest of the afternoon and did not try anything. She read the road for the wheels. She did not hold the strap. She thought about her own two feet on the road, one of them always coming up, and about the Ward, which did not like it.

---

At dusk they came to the place where Senna had said to camp.

Pell had it on his route sheet. He had read it out at the gate in the north yard and at the noon halt, in the flat voice he used for a distance. Senna had dictated it on the thirty-second day, and Toren had written it in the book as she said it, not tidied. *The twentieth stone. A drovers' fold on the left, stone, with a spring above it. Good water. We slept there the first night going up and the last night coming down, and nothing came.*

The twentieth stone was there. It was a short grey post at the side of the road, leaning, with *20* cut in it and moss in the cuts.

The fold was there too. It was on the left, fifty paces off the road, a square of stone wall about the height of a man's chest, with the dark of a wood behind it on the rising ground. It was the right shape and the right size and in the right place.

It was not a place anybody could sleep.

The wall on the road side had come down. It had not been knocked down. It had slumped outward all along its length, the way a wall goes when the ground under it has gone soft, and lay in a long low spill of stone. The spring above the fold had burst at some time in the last twenty years and come down through it. Callie could see the water standing in the fold in the last of the light, flat and black, with the tops of old nettles sticking up out of it.

And along the ditch below it, between the fold and the road, there was a line of grey.

Callie stopped.

She did not have to go closer. She had seen that grey every morning of her life on the plain from the age of eleven. It lay in a thin crooked line along the bottom of the ditch, in the silt, the way it lay where something had come up out of the wet and moved along a bottom and come apart there, or gone on. A husk line. And another beside it, fainter, older. And beyond the fold, where the ditch went under the road in a culvert, there was a darker place in the ditch where the silt had been turned over and not settled.

Duchess stopped.

Nobody had asked her to. Pell had not said a word. She stopped in the road twenty paces short of the fold with her head up, and put her ears back flat against her skull, and would not go on.

Pell put his hand on her neck. He did not pull on the lead.

"She doesn't like dogs," he said quietly. "She'll hate hounds. If she stops and puts her ears back and won't go on, it's not her being difficult." He looked at the ditch. "It's something in the ditch."

Callie heard the weighbridge girl say it. She heard it word for word, standing by the gate of the north yard in the frost that morning with her plaster held against her chest. *Bring her back.*

"Husk lines," said Callie. "Two. One of them from the last day or so, one older. They come up out of the culvert, there, and go along the ditch past the fold. There's been something in the fold." She looked at the water. "It's a place where they come up. Like the Sag. A little one."

Nobody said anything for a while. The light was going. The dark of the wood above the fold had gone from green to black while they stood there.

Pell took the route sheet out of his coat and looked at it. He did not need to. He knew what it said. He looked at it anyway, at Senna's line in Toren's careful hand, and at the fold in the dusk with the water standing in it.

"Twenty years," he said.

"We don't sleep here," said Miss Wren.

"No," said Rook. He was standing at the tailboard beside Jab with his hands at his sides, looking at the ditch. "We don't sleep anywhere we haven't made ourselves. Not on this road. Not with what's on that cart." He looked up the road, past the fold, to the north.

Callie looked where he was looking.

A quarter of a mile on, the road went up over a low rise. On the top of the rise, against the last grey in the sky, there were two black lines standing up at an angle to each other. They were the corner of a building that was not there any more. It was two walls meeting, with nothing on the other two sides.

"Where, then?" said Callie.

Nobody answered her at once. Behind her, at the back of the cart, she heard Toren begin to count them under his breath. He did it the way he had at the north lip of the Sag. *Pell. Dee. Rook. Jab. Callie.* And after a moment, *Duchess*, as if the horse were one of them, which she was.

"Six," he said. "And a horse."

Pell turned Duchess's head toward the rise, and she went, with her ears still back, walking faster than she had walked all afternoon. Callie went out twenty paces in front of her into the dusk and read the road for the wheels.

# Chapter 26 — Toren: The Dark Lane

The corner on the rise was all that was left of a house.

Toren saw that when they came up to it in the last of the light. Two walls of dressed stone stood at right angles, each about ten paces long and higher than Pell's head, with the black holes of windows in them and the stumps of floor beams sticking out at the height of a man's chest. The other two walls had gone somewhere, long ago, stone by stone. There were grass and thistle growing where the floor had been. On the open sides the ground fell away gently to the road on the south and to a long rough field on the east. On the west, beyond the end of the wall, it ran on level for a hundred paces to a line of thorn.

The wind came from the west, over the thorn, into their faces.

"Here," said Rook.

He had stopped at the corner with his hand on the stone. He did not say it the way he said most things. He said it the way Pell said a distance.

"It's got two sides," said Pell.

"It's got two sides nobody has to watch," said Rook. "And the wind's in our faces. Whatever comes, the horse smells it before it gets here. And we're on the top of something." He looked back down the rise at the road, and the fold, and the ditch running away south-east in the dusk. "Everything that comes has to come up. That's worth more than a roof."

Nobody argued with that. Toren did not think anybody could.

They argued about everything else.

---

It was not a loud argument. It was the kind they had had on the second floor on the day of the reviews, and in the shed on the twenty-second, and at the long table on the thirty-seventh. Everybody was right about one thing and wrong about the thing next to it.

Pell wanted the cart across the open side, as a wall.

"It's the only third wall we've got," he said. "It's six foot long and four high with the boards up. Put it across the east side, chock the wheels, and you've got three sides and a gap."

"It's got four Flasks on it," said Dee. "And the lead box. You're putting the thing we most need to bring home in the one place anything will come at first."

"It's got four Flasks on it wherever it stands."

"Then it stands where nothing comes at it."

"Then where's my third wall?"

Rook said nothing. He was sitting on a fallen stone at the foot of the corner with his hands on his knees, and Toren could see from the way he sat that the back was bad. It had been bad since the noon halt. He had walked the afternoon beside Jab at the frame's pace and paid for every step of it.

Jab stood with his rack resting on the tailboard and looked from one of them to the other.

Toren looked at the corner. He looked at the two walls and the open ground and the line of thorn to the west, and the dark coming down over all of it. He thought about the north lip of the Sag in the fog, and lamps on poles along the east track, and a gap between the third lamp and the fourth where there was no lamp at all.

"Katori's watch," he said.

They stopped and looked at him.

He had not meant to say it out loud. He had meant to think it. But they were looking at him, all of them, and so he went on, because it was out now.

"The lamps along the track," he said. "Where the husks came up at the light, because husks go at anything lit. And the dark lane between the third and the fourth, where the hounds came up, because hounds go at the dark. Katori put the dark where she wanted them." He looked at the corner. "We haven't got lamps on poles. We've got a fire."

"Go on," said Rook.

"The cart inside the corner," said Toren. "Against the north wall. With the jars against the stone, like Dee says, where nothing gets at them without coming through us. Duchess between the cart and the fire, on a short rope, where she can see the open side. And the fire out here." He walked out from the corner onto the open ground, three paces, four, toward the south-east. "Here. So it lights the end of the south wall, and the open ground in front of it, and the way up from the road." He turned round. "And not the end of the west wall. Not out there, by the thorn. We leave that dark."

"A lane," said Rook.

"A lane," said Toren. "Between the end of the west wall and where the firelight stops. About four paces wide. If there's hounds, they'll come up there, because it's the dark. So that's where we stand when they come." He stopped. "And husks come at the fire. On the lit side. Where the south wall's between them and the cart."

Nobody said anything.

Pell looked at the corner, and at the ground, and at the place where Toren was standing. He looked at it the way he looked at a road on the atlas. He looked for where the numbers went wrong.

"The cart's still my third wall," he said at last. "It's just facing the other way."

"It's facing the stone," said Dee.

"I heard him," said Pell. He went to Duchess's head. "I'm agreeing with him, Dee. I'm saying it in my own words so I can live with it."

---

They made the camp in the dark.

Pell backed the cart into the corner against the north wall, and chocked the wheels with stones, and got Duchess out of the shafts. He did it slowly, talking to her the whole time, and when she was out he walked her round the corner once, the whole thing, so that she saw both walls and the open ground and the fire-place and the dark at the end of the west wall. Then he tied her short to the wheel of the cart, facing out.

"She'll look at what she's been shown," he said. "If something's new, she'll know."

Toren got the fire going with wood from the thorn line. Callie brought water from the skins. Jab backed up to the tailboard where it stood in the corner now, and Pell and Toren lifted the frame off him together and stood it upright against the stone, jars to the wall, on two flat stones Callie had found so that it did not stand on the ground. Jab straightened his back for the first time since the morning and made a noise that was half a groan and half a laugh.

"That's the road," he said. "That's what the road was."

Pell called Toren over to the cart before he did anything else.

"Come here," he said. "I'll show you once."

He put his hand on Duchess's harness where it lay over the shafts: the collar and the hames and the long leather traces that ran back from them to the cart. He put his finger on a place on the traces, a hand's width behind the hames, where a buckle held the leather to a ring.

"If the cart goes," he said. "Off a road, or into a hole, or over. If it goes and she's still in it, it takes her with it." He put his finger on the place. "You cut here. Both sides. Here, and she's free, and the cart's still yours, and the shafts don't come down on her legs." He moved his finger forward, to where the reins ran. "Not there. There, you've cut the only thing you've got to hold her with, and she's still in the cart, and she's dragging you."

"Here," said Toren.

"Here," said Pell. "I've a knife on my belt for it. Everybody on a road with a horse has a knife on their belt for it." He looked at Toren. "I've never had to. I'm showing you so I'm not the only one who knows."

---

They ate at the fire. Afterward Dee said the lines.

She said them standing up with the glass in her hand. It was not the gate, and it was not the fence. But she had said at the long table that she would say them every morning at the fence, and she said them now, at night, on a rise in the dark, because it was the first night.

"Nothing to eat inside the fence. Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face." She did not look at anybody. "Red, and nobody trapped: everybody out, at once. Red, and a body trapped: the fewest who can do it stay, named, and not a breath longer. Two Flasks left inside, everybody comes out. Jab below his floor once, and I turn us round."

"And the road?" said Toren.

Dee looked at him.

"We've got a road Flask," said Toren. "One. It's for things on the road, like hounds at a culvert. You said that at the table." He looked at the fire. "Is tonight a thing on the road?"

"No," said Dee.

She said it at once. She had been waiting for somebody to ask.

"Tonight's a night," she said. "We'll have five more. If we spend the road jar on sleeping, we'll have nothing left for the road by the time we're anywhere near that hill, and the road near that hill is the road I'm frightened of." She turned the glass over in her hand without looking at it. "Nobody spends a grain of the road jar tonight. Not for light, not for struts, not for anything that isn't somebody bleeding. If you have to light, you'll light off your own. And the stone you've settled stays where it is. It's for the fence." She looked round at them. "Your own comes back with sleep. Ash doesn't."

"And if it's more than we can fence off our own?" said Rook.

"Then we'll wake you," said Dee. "You're not on a watch."

Rook looked at her across the fire.

"I'll take the dead one," he said. "Second to fourth. I always do."

"You'll sleep," said Dee. "From now till the light, flat, on the canvas, with your back on the ground and not on a stone. That's not advice, Elias. That's the Director's word on this road, and it's in my mouth." She did not raise her voice. "You walked twenty miles beside a frame today with your shoulders square so nobody would see. I saw. You'll not do it again tomorrow if you spend tonight sitting up."

Rook sat very still.

Toren watched him. He had seen Rook sit that still once before, in a chair at the foot of Jab's bed in the ward, when Sowerby came in. He watched him decide whether to argue.

"The Director's never slept on a floor," said Rook.

"Then she's wiser than you," said Dee.

After a moment, Rook laughed. It was the low careful laugh. He got up off the stone, in two movements, and went to the canvas by the cart and lay down on it on his back, in his coat, with his hands folded on his chest. He shut his eyes.

"Wake me if it's a Barrel," he said.

---

The watches were Toren's, because he asked for them.

He had not known he was going to ask. He found himself standing by the fire with the paper out, the signed one, and the pencil. He was writing them down on the back of it before anybody else had said anything. It was like the ledger in the shed. He did not have the answers. He only had to ask for them in the right order.

"Two awake, all the time," he said. "One who can fight and one who can see."

"See what?" said Callie.

"What the other one can't." He looked at them. "Duchess sees the ditch. That's hers. Whoever's on watch watches Duchess's ears. When they go back, we stand up." He looked at Jab. "And you can see us."

Jab looked at him.

"You said once," said Toren, "on the road in, with your brother. You said when you're tired you can feel where everybody is. Like counting in the dark." He did not know if he had it right. "I thought you could count us. Asleep. Without looking."

Jab was quiet for a while.

"I can feel people I know," he said slowly. "If they're near. If I'm not trying." He looked at the canvas where Rook was lying. "I can feel him. He's not asleep."

"He's not asleep," said Dee, without turning round.

"Then that's what you can see," said Toren. He wrote it. "How long's a watch?"

"Two turns of the glass," said Pell, from the cart, "is two minutes. You'll turn it sixty times and go out of your mind." He came over to the fire. "Two armfuls of the thorn. That's a watch. When you've burned two armfuls, wake the next."

So Toren wrote it like that, by the fire, on the back of the paper with the Director's signature on the other side. Pell and Callie first, two armfuls. Toren and Jab in the middle, two armfuls, the dead hours. Dee and Callie last, till the light, because Dee said she woke at the fourth hour whether anybody let her sleep or not.

"You're on two," said Dee to Callie.

"I'll sleep at the fence," said Callie.

"You won't," said Dee. "Nobody sleeps at the fence. That's why you'll sleep now."

---

Before he lay down Toren settled.

He had spent nothing out of the stone that day. The Handful he had taken in at the stove in eleven the night before still lay at the bottom of him, warm and faint. But Callie had spent a quarter of hers on the road in the afternoon, and Dee had weighed a quarter out of the road jar for her at the fire, and Callie had put her palm on it and taken it in in seventy breaths with her shoulders down. And Callie had said it, out loud, the way she said everything now. *Road, seven and three quarters.* Toren had found himself sitting by the fire watching her do it, and counting her breaths, and thinking about his own.

He would never take ash in quickly. Dee had said so in the shed, and it was true. A tenth took him a hundred breaths and more. A quarter took most of the time it took a pan to boil. If he needed more than he had, he would have to have taken it in the night before, sitting down, slowly. He had to be the first one full.

He was full. He did not need to settle anything tonight.

He sat by the fire anyway, with his palm flat on his knee and nothing under it, and went down once to look. Not to check. To know. The stone was there, a long way down, warm. Above it lay his own reserve, the two turns and a bit of the brass glass that Dee had measured on the seventeenth day and he had never measured again.

He came back up and lay down on the canvas by Jab, with his coat on and his left arm in its cuff against his chest. He counted them before he shut his eyes. He could not help it.

Pell and Callie by the fire. Dee rolled in her coat by the wall with the glass under her hand. Rook on his back by the cart, not asleep. Jab beside him. Duchess tied to the wheel, facing out, her ears forward, eating.

Six. And a horse.

---

Callie woke him at the second hour by putting her hand on his shoulder and not saying anything.

He was up at once. It was very dark. The fire had burned down to a red bed with a few flames running along the top of the thorn. The cold had come down hard while he slept, and the grass was stiff with it, and when he breathed it went out of him in a cloud that the firelight turned orange.

"Nothing," said Callie. "Two armfuls. Nothing. Her ears haven't moved."

She went and lay down by Dee. Pell was already down, by the cart. Jab was sitting up by the fire with his coat pulled round him, and he looked at Toren and nodded once, and they sat.

For a long time there was nothing.

Toren put thorn on the fire. He watched the light go up the end of the south wall and out over the open ground to the south-east, the way he had said it would, and stop. He watched the dark at the end of the west wall, where the light did not go. It was four paces wide, and then the wall's end, black against the grey. Beyond it the ground ran level to the thorn line, a long way off, where nothing moved.

He watched Duchess's ears.

"You're counting," said Jab.

"Every armful."

"I can feel you doing it." Jab had his hands in his coat. "You do Pell first. Then Dee. Then Rook. You always do Rook third."

"He's always third."

"You do him longest." Jab looked at the canvas by the cart. "He's asleep now. Properly. About an hour."

Toren looked at the long dark shape of Rook by the cart. He could not see anything but a coat. He believed Jab anyway.

"Good," he said.

---

It was the third armful when Duchess's ears went back.

Toren did not see it happen. He was looking at the fire. He heard Jab stop breathing beside him, and looked up, and the mare was standing with her head up and her ears flat against her skull. She was looking west, past the end of the west wall, into the dark.

Toren stood up.

He did not do anything else for a moment. He stood by the fire and made himself look at what was in front of him, the way Rook had told him on the north road. Not what he was afraid of, or what he wanted to see. What was there.

The dark at the end of the west wall. The level ground beyond it, grey with frost. The thorn line.

Nothing.

And then two low shapes came along the thorn line, from the north, and stopped.

They were hounds. He knew the way they moved before he could see them properly. They moved together, one a little behind the other, low, with that way they had of stopping all at once as if they had both heard the same thing. They stood at the thorn line a hundred paces off, grey against the grey, and he knew they were looking at the fire.

"Two," said Jab, very quietly.

"I see them."

They came in. Not straight. They came along the thorn line toward the south, and then down the slope toward the road, and then back up toward the lit side, where the firelight lay on the open ground in front of the south wall. At the edge of the light the first one stopped. It stood with its forefeet on the frost at the very edge of the orange, and did not come any further. The second came up beside it. They stood there.

Then they went round.

Toren watched them do it. It was the Sag. It was the north lip in the fog with the lamps in their line and the hounds going along below it, testing, and not coming up at the light. They went round the edge of the firelight, along it, keeping just outside it, back up the slope, and along the front of the south wall at a distance. They came round the corner of the light toward the west.

Toward the dark.

"Wake Rook," said Jab.

"No."

Jab looked at him.

"Dee said," said Toren. "Wake him if it's a Barrel." He was already moving. "It's two hounds. It's a lane. That's what it's for."

He went to the end of the west wall.

---

He stood in the dark lane with the stone wall at his back and the level ground in front of him. He could hear the hounds. He could not see them for a moment, because they were in the dark too now, and then he could. They were two grey movements coming along the outside of the firelight, fast now, low, into the one place where nothing was lit.

Into him.

He let the spike come on his fist.

It came the way it always came now, heavy and crooked and ugly, dark, with the thick knob of the heel sticking out at its bad angle at the root. He held it up in the dark.

And he asked for the light at the heel, and only at the heel. He did not ask the stone for it. He did not reach down past his own reserve to where the Handful lay warm at the bottom. He reached for what was his, the thin draw behind his breastbone like water going out of a barrel through a pinhole. He looked at the heel. He kept his eyes on it.

It lit.

It lit a hand's width of thin white-grey along the hooked curve of the heel, and nothing else. The rest of the spike stayed dark. In the black lane at the end of the west wall it was very bright. It was the only light between the wall and the thorn.

The first hound stopped as if it had hit a rope.

It stopped four paces from him, all at once, on its forefeet, with its hindquarters coming round. The second came up beside it and stopped too. They stood there, in the dark lane, with the little light at the end of his fist between them and him.

He did not move.

He could feel the draw. It was not much. It was the trickle Jab had shown him in the first lane, not the pinhole, and it went on, and on. He stood with the light up and watched them. Their heads were down and turning, left and right, the way they did. They went left. They went along the front of him to the end of the wall, where it met the dark open ground on the north side, and looked at the cart and the jars and Duchess tied to the wheel with her ears flat. And Toren moved.

He did not go at them. He took two steps to his right, along the end of the wall, and held the light up where they were looking.

They stopped again. They came back.

They tried it four times. Right, to the edge of the firelight, and the firelight stopped them. Left, round the end of the wall toward the cart, and the little light at the heel of his spike stopped them. Right again. He moved with them each time, a step or two, and held the light where they wanted to go, and they did not go there.

The fifth time they did not try. They stood in the lane in front of him for a long moment with their heads down. Then they turned together, as if one of them had said something, and went away across the frost toward the thorn line at a lope, and along it to the north, and were gone.

Toren stood in the dark lane with the light on his fist.

He let it go.

He had been holding it, he found, for a long time. He did not know how long. His arm was shaking. The draw behind his breastbone stopped, and something in his chest let go with it, and he found he was breathing very hard.

---

When he got back to the fire Jab was standing by Duchess with his hand on her neck. The mare's ears were coming forward, slowly, one and then the other.

Rook was lying on the canvas by the cart with his eyes open.

He had not moved. He was lying on his back with his hands folded on his chest the way he had lain down, and he was looking at Toren. He had been looking, Toren understood, the whole time. He had heard Duchess stamp, and Jab say *two*, and he had lain there on his back on the frozen ground with his eyes open and watched a thirteen-year-old go out alone into the dark lane with one arm in a cuff. And he had not got up.

He did not say anything now either. He looked at Toren for a moment longer. Then he shut his eyes.

Toren sat down by the fire.

"How long?" he said.

"About eleven minutes," said Jab. "I counted Duchess. She stamped every so often. It was eleven, near enough." He sat down beside him. "You lit the heel the whole time."

"I didn't know how else."

"You only lit it when they were looking at you. Did you know that?" Jab put a piece of thorn on the fire. "When they went away from you, to the lit side, it went down. Not out. Down. And when they came back, you lit it again. You didn't do it on purpose. I watched it."

Toren had not known that.

"It's cheaper that way," said Jab. "Light what you'll use. When you'll use it." He looked at the fire. "You'll be tired in the morning. That's all it'll cost. Your own."

He wanted to go down and look. He wanted to see how much of the two turns and a bit was left. He sat by the fire and did not go, because Dee would measure him in the morning, and because it would be what it was whether he looked or not.

Instead he took the paper out of his coat, and the pencil, and wrote on the back of it, under the watches.

*41st night. The corner on the rise past the 20th stone (the fold's gone). Out: 6. In: 6. 2 hounds, 3rd armful, the dark lane, T. lit the heel off his own about 11 min, they went. No ash spent. R. slept (J. says about an hour).*

He looked at it. His grandmother had told him the man who ran her crew had written the count on his wrist with a burnt stick. *Write it somewhere better than your wrist.*

It was not much better than a wrist. It was the back of a paper he would carry all the way. But the Director had signed the other side, and his grandmother's cross in a circle was there, and all of theirs. He thought that was better.

---

Rook could not get up in one in the morning.

Toren saw him try. He was lying on the canvas with the frost white on the blanket over him, and when Dee said *light* he folded his arms across his chest and leaned to sit up without his hands, the way he had done it on the forty-first morning in eleven. He got halfway and stopped. He lay back. Then he did it the other way, with a hand on the ground and a hand on the wheel of the cart. It took him three movements, and at the top of the third he stood with his hand on the cartwheel and his face gone the colour of the frost.

Nobody said *three*. Dessa would have. Dessa was a day behind them on a crate at the south lip with her eyes shut.

"Ground," said Rook, to nobody. "It's the ground. The cot in the shed doesn't get this cold."

"I know," said Dee. She was at the fire, measuring floors. "It'll be the ground every night for twelve nights. I'll put the canvas double tonight." She did not look at him. "Wrist."

Rook came and gave her his wrist. She counted with two fingers on it and her eyes on the thorn line, and did not say anything, and wrote in her book.

Then she did Toren.

"Your own's down," she said. "A turn, near enough. The stone's where it was."

"The heel."

"I know it was the heel. Jab told me before you were awake." She let go of his wrist. "Your own comes back. You'll feel it by noon. If you'd lit off the stone, you'd have it back at the fence in a hundred and four breaths a tenth, sitting down, the night before, and you'd not have had the night." She wrote. "That was right."

It was the first time Dee had told him something he did was right. He did not know what to do with it, so he put it in his pocket with the stone.

---

On the forty-second day the road went down into a valley and the valley had eaten it.

It was not a big valley. It was a fold in the land with a stream at the bottom, and the old north road had crossed the stream on a culvert like the one Callie had sent the cart round on the first morning. The culvert was gone. There was a gap in the road, a channel eight paces wide and waist deep in the middle, with the stream running brown and fast through the bottom of it. The broken ends of the culvert's pipe stuck out of the banks on either side like the ends of a bone. The banks were raw mud and gravel, not grown over.

Pell stood on the edge and looked at it for a long time.

"Spring," he said. "This spring or last. The pipe choked and it went round and took the road with it." He looked up the valley and down it. There was nothing up it but more valley and a stand of dark trees. Down it the stream went into a bog. "We cross here or we go back to the last stone and look for a way round, and there isn't one on the atlas."

"We cross here," said Dee.

"We cross here," said Pell.

He did it himself first. He walked Duchess down the near bank, slowly, with his hand on her neck, and let her look at the water and put her nose in it. Then he took her through it with the water to her knees and his own boots filling, and up the far bank. The far bank was steeper. Toren watched Duchess's hind feet dig into it and the gravel go out from under them and her go up anyway, the way a horse goes up a bank, all at once, in a scramble. Pell led her back again. He did it three times. The third time he stopped in the middle of the stream with the water round his knees and looked at the far bank.

"The near one's fine," he said. "The far one's soft on the left. The right wheel'll go up it. The left'll sink." He came back up. "She'll pull it through. She'll not pull it out of a hole."

Toren looked at the far bank.

He had been thinking about it since Pell's first crossing. He had been thinking about it the way he thought about Wyck's sack at seven paces, with the whole of him. A wheel going down into soft ground, and the weight of a loaded cart on the wheel, going down. A weight coming, and something to put under it.

"I'll put a strut under it," he said.

---

It did not work.

He stood on the far bank in the mud up to his ankles, on the left, where Pell had said the wheel would sink. Duchess came down the near bank and into the water with Pell at her head, and the cart came after her, lurching, with the water up to its hubs and Jab's rack on the tailboard and Jab walking in the stream behind it, holding the tailboard with both hands. The right wheel went up the far bank on the gravel. The left wheel went into the soft and sank.

Toren put the foot of the strut down outside his left boot, at a slant, into the mud, and let it come up from there toward the axle.

It came. It came up thin and bright from the mud and met the axle beside the hub. He felt the weight of the cart arrive on it, and he was soft behind it, the way he had learned. And the foot of the strut went down into the mud like a stick going into porridge. It did not break. It went down, and down, and the strut went down with it, and the axle went down on top of the strut. The wheel sank to its hub.

He let it go. There was nothing to hold.

"No ground," said Callie.

She was standing on the near bank with the hook, where she could see the whole of the crossing. She said it the way she said a tick.

"There's no ground under you," she said. "It's mud all the way down on that side. Two feet of it, maybe more. The strut's standing on nothing." She looked at the water. "But there's stone under the gravel on the right. A shelf. Where the water's clear over it, look. It's running smooth there and it's not bringing any mud down. There's a flat stone under the gravel, a foot down." She pointed with the hook's handle. "There. If you put the foot of it there, it's standing on something."

Toren looked at the place. It was a pace to the right of where he was standing, in the stream, where the water ran clear and fast over gravel.

"It'll be at a worse angle," he said.

"It'll be at an angle with ground under it," said Callie.

He went into the stream. The water came up over his boots, so cold it was like being hit. He stood in it on the right, where she said, and felt with his boot through the gravel, and there it was. It was flat and hard, a foot down, like a step.

"Pell," he said. "Hold her till I say."

"I'll hold her," said Pell.

Toren put the foot of the strut down through the water and the gravel onto the stone, and let it come up. It came up at a long slant from the stone to the axle, longer than he had ever built one. It met the axle. It stood.

"Set," he said. He did not know why he said that. It was what came out. "Pull."

Pell said a word to Duchess, and she leaned into the collar.

The cart came up.

It came up out of the mud onto the strut, and the weight of it came onto the strut, and down the strut into the stone under the gravel. He was soft behind it. His knees gave a little and took the last of it. And the wheel came up out of the hole and rolled, and as it rolled, the axle went forward along the top of the strut. The strut had been built for a weight that stood still. The load was moving along it and up it, toward the end.

He held it too long.

He held it after the axle had gone past the top of it, and the strut had nothing on it, and he was braced for a weight that had gone. He went forward. His boots went out from under him on the gravel, and he went down into the stream on his side.

He did not put his hand out.

He felt himself start to. He felt the left arm start to come out from his chest to take the fall, the way an arm does, the way it had on the Sag slabs in the first days. And he heard Wyck on the step in the dark with the bar across his knees. *Not that hand. Not for anything.* He kept it against his chest. He went down like a sack, on his shoulder and his hip, into the freezing water. He rolled once in the gravel and came up on his knees with the water running out of his coat and the arm still against his chest where it had been.

"Toren," said Callie, from the bank.

"I'm up."

"You're in the river."

"I'm up in the river," said Toren. His teeth were going. "The arm's where it was."

On the far bank the cart stood on dry gravel with water running off its wheels. Duchess was blowing. Pell was looking back at him with his hand on her neck.

"She's out," said Pell. "That was a good hold." He looked at Toren in the stream. "You held it past where it was any use. When the wheel's gone by, it's gone by. You let go when it's past you."

"I know."

"You'll know it better next time," said Pell. "Get out of the water. You'll be no use to anybody with no feet."

---

There was a second crossing, because there were two wheels.

Pell had thought about that before Toren had. He had the cart through the first time with the left wheel on the strut. Now he took Duchess back to the near bank and turned her and brought the cart through again from the other side, backward, so that the right wheel would come up the soft side this time. He said it was for the practice. Toren did not believe him, and then he did.

"It's two wheels on a cart," said Pell. "And you've got one arm. You'll want to know how to do the other side."

The other side was the left strut.

Toren stood in the water and knew it before he began. The strut that would go under the right wheel from where he stood was the one that came from the left, off the side where the arm was in its cuff, where nothing had ever come as fast as it should. It was the late one. On the whitewash, on the seventeenth day, it had been the one that came up out of the ground a beat behind and held when nothing else had. He had been building struts from the ground ever since because of it.

He put its foot down on the stone.

It came late.

He felt it come late, the way it always did. It came a beat after he asked for it, up out of the stone at its slant, while the wheel was already coming. For a moment there was nothing under the axle at all but water. Then it met the axle as the weight arrived. It met it from the ground up, because it could not have come any other way, and it stood.

"Set," said Toren. "Pull."

The cart came up the bank. The wheel rolled up out of the soft and along the top of the strut. This time he watched the axle go along the strut. When it came to the end he let the strut go, at the end, not after. He let it go the way he had let the rack down on the second try, soft, a little at a time. He stood in the water behind nothing, and did not fall.

"There," said Pell, from the bank.

"It was late."

"It was on time for the wheel," said Pell. "That's the only time that counts."

Toren came out of the stream. He stood on the gravel with the water running out of his boots and went down once to measure. The stone was smaller. He had asked it twice, for the two wheels. The first time had been long, too long, past where it was any use.

"How much?" said Dee. She had come through behind the cart with her coat hitched up round her and her book held over her head.

"A quarter," said Toren. "Near enough. Out of the stone."

"Say the rest."

"Settled, three quarters," said Toren. "Tonight I'll take a quarter back out of the road jar. It'll take me till the second armful." He looked at the far bank, where the cart stood. "Road, seven and a half."

"Seven and a half," said Callie, from the far side, and she said it the same way.

At dusk on the forty-second day they came to the place where the iron posts began.

Toren saw them from a long way off, because the road came up out of the valley onto a long bare ridge. From the top of the ridge he could see a line of short dark posts coming in across the fields from the west. They were iron, set in the ground every twenty paces, the tops of them bent and rusted, some of them leaning, some of them down. There was a road beside them, or there had been, a raised bed of stone with grass growing through it. "The old line," said Pell. He had the atlas open against Duchess's neck. "Red. There." He put his finger on it. "Four days west. Then the hill."

They made camp at the place where the line met the road, against the stone bed of the old line where it came up out of a cutting. It had one side of good stone and the wind in their faces. It took them less than half the time it had taken on the rise. It took a long time. He did not haul. He sat by the fire with his palm flat on the paper and let it come at the rate it came, and Callie beside him said *road, seven and a half* for Dee's book.

Tomorrow was the forty-third.

He thought about it with his palm on the ash. At the ninth hour, two orderlies would lift his grandmother from the bed into a chair, and one of them would push her down the long slope to the yellow line while she complained about the camber. A man with a white hand would warm the machine with his left hand, slower. He would be walking west on the old line, beside the iron posts, when it happened. He would not know which hour it was, not exactly, because there was no clock on the old line. He would know it anyway.

When the paper was clean he wrote on the back of the other paper, under the watches.

*42nd. The iron posts, where the old line meets the road. Out: 6. In: 6. Rook up in three. The washout: T. set twice (the second one the left), a quarter. C. read the stone.*

He looked at the count. Then he wrote one more line under it, because it was true and because there was nowhere else to put it.

*Tomorrow's Gran's.*

He folded the paper and put it in his coat with the plate. Then he lay down by Jab with his arm against his chest and counted them, the way he always would now.

Pell. Dee. Rook, longest. Jab. Callie. And Duchess at the wheel with her ears forward, eating.

Six. And a horse. And everybody in the beds.

# Chapter 27 — Jab: Choosing

The old line was good walking. That was the first thing Jab learned about it, and he learned it with his hips.

Somebody a long time ago had wanted it to stay where it was put. The bed of it was raised a man's height above the fields in most places, and it was made of broken stone, sharp-edged and grey, packed down so hard that seventy years of weather had not moved it. Grass grew through it in tufts. Thistles grew through it. But under the grass and the thistles the stone was still there, and the cart went along it without a lurch, and a man with eight jars on his back could put his feet down without looking.

The iron posts went along the north edge of it, one every twenty paces. They were as high as his chest, square, rusted to the colour of old blood, with a bent arm at the top where something had once been hung. Some of them leaned. Some had fallen and lay in the grass beside the bed. Most of them stood.

He counted them. He did not mean to. It was what the road did to people. Toren counted the party and Callie counted the ash, and Jab found by the middle of the morning that he was counting iron posts. Twenty paces, a post. Twenty paces, a post. At every hundredth post, Pell said, there had been a number, and there was, sometimes, on a little plate that had mostly rusted away.

At the second halt he backed up to the tailboard and let the rack come down on it, and put his thumbs in the chest band, and felt the weight go off his hips.

His hand went to his coat.

It went there on its own, the way it had gone to the cord on the road in, a hundred times a day, to feel whether it was taut. There was no cord now. There was a small hard shape under the cloth on the left side, over the place under his breastbone. He put his fingers on it through the coat and felt the shape of it. The body. The tail. The place where one fin had broken off before Tonk was born.

He took his hand away.

It was not a charm. He knew that. Tonk had not given it to him so that nothing would happen. Tonk had given it to him so that he would bring it back. It was the other end of the cord, the end Tonk had kept on his own fist, and it did not make him braver. If anything it made him more careful, which was, he thought, what Tonk had meant.

*Don't eat inside.*

He would not eat inside.

Jab thought about the ward.

He thought about the fifth bed on the right with the chair at the foot of it, and the sixth bed, empty, with the rail bare where the knot had been for sixteen days. "Go on," said Dee.

Jab went down into the ditch.

He knelt beside the man in the wet grass, on the side away from the wire, and put his right hand flat on the man's chest, over the breastbone. The man flinched. Then he did not.

Dee put the mouth of the jar to Jab's left palm.

---

It was not like the pen.

That was the first thing he knew. In the pen it had been a flood, a man full to the arms, a seed in his lap, and Rook's hand over his and Dee's voice and the counter buzzing. Everything had happened at once and he had learned it in the same breath he did it. This was one man sitting in a ditch with his back to a cart wheel, and a thin busy warmth in him, lower down. There was time.

He found it. He did not reach for it. He put his attention down under his palm, the way he put it on a table and took his hands off it, and let himself feel what was there. The heart, going slow and tired. The breath. The cold coming up out of the ditch. And the busy thing, not his, in the chest a little and the belly more, warm and fine.

The clean cold came into his left hand from the jar and went through him and out of his right. It paid.

The busy thing came loose, a little, near his hand. It came up into his palm like hot sand. He held it high. He did not let it into his wrist. Ten breaths.

"Vent," said Dee.

He lifted his hand and put it flat in the vent jar by his knee, on the pale dust, and felt it go out of him into nobody. Three breaths.

"Back on."

He went back on. He did it again. And again, lower, with his palm on the man's belly now, where the most of it was, and the man lay back against the wheel with his eyes shut and let him.

"Forty," said Callie, after the fourth.

He did it again. Draw, ten breaths, hold high. Vent, three. Back on. It was like lifting. It was the east track at first light with his palm on a drift, and the ash coming at the rate it came, and nobody hauling. It was like nothing he had ever done that mattered, and it was the most ordinary work he had ever done.

"Twenty-six."

He went down the right arm, from the shoulder to the elbow. It was thin there. Then the left.

"Nineteen."

He came to the hands.

---

He stopped at the wrists.

He could feel the hands under the rag, the way he could feel a fire he was not looking at. They were done. Most of them. The skin and the ends of the fingers had the old leaning wall in them, the thing that had happened and was not happening any more. And under it, a little, there was still something busy, the warm water in the bottom of the broken pot. It was not much. It was all through the done part, the way water gets into a wall.

"Go down the hands," said Dee.

Jab did not move.

"Go down the hands," said Dee again. "There's some left in them. I can see it on the counter. Callie. The hands."

"Twenty-four," said Callie. "At the hands. Higher than the chest."

"No," said Jab.

Dee looked at him.

"It's in the done part," said Jab. He did not take his hand off the man's wrist. He kept it there, feeling. "It's in the wall. There's a little busy in it, all through, and a lot done round it. If I draw it I'm drawing through what's done. I'd have to pour a jar into it to get a spoonful out." He heard himself. "It's the old wall in Dessa's leg. It's the technician's hand. It's not worth a jar. He'd not keep the hands if I emptied the road into them. He'd keep a spoonful less of what's in them." He looked at Dee. "No."

Nobody said anything.

Dee looked at him for a long moment. Then she took the counter out of Callie's hand and held it to the man's wrapped hands herself, close, and watched the needle for ten breaths. Then she gave the counter back to Callie. She put the stopper back in the jar with her thumb and held her own palm flat over the man's hands, an inch above the rag, not touching. She shut her eyes.

She opened them.

"Leave it," she said.

That was all. She did not say *good*. She did not say *you're right*. She wrote something in her book, and then she said, "Off. Vent. Give me your hands," and Jab took his own hand off the man's wrist and vented and held his hands out to her.

She took them, flat, palm to palm, and drew. He felt her find the little warm grit left in the creases of him and take it up into her own palm, and turn, and put her hand in the vent jar.

"Clean," she said. "You'd have carried that."

---

Callie read the jar.

She did it before anybody asked, the way she had in the pen. She took the road jar out of Dee's hand and weighed it on her own palm, with her head a little on one side the way her grandfather must have held a sack.

"It's light by two," she said. "Near enough. Two, and a bit for the vent and a bit for Miss Wren's draw." She looked at Toren. "Road, five and a quarter."

Toren wrote it. Jab saw him write it on the back of the paper, under something else. He did not say anything about it.

"And the man," said Dee. "His chest."

Callie held out the counter.

"Sixteen," she said. "From here. It was sixty."

The man in the ditch opened his eyes.

He looked at Jab, kneeling beside him in the wet grass. He did not say thank you. He looked at Jab's face for a long time with his red-rimmed eyes, the way you look at a thing you do not understand and are not sure you want to.

"My hands," he said.

"I can't do the hands," said Jab. "They're done. I'm sorry. They were done before we got here." He did not look away. "The rest was busy. It's not busy now. Most of it. You'll be sick again, a bit. Be glad when you are. It's your body getting the rest out."

The man looked down at his hands in their dark rag.

"I pulled a cart of wire for four days," he said, "for hands I'll not be able to use to spend what it fetches."

Nobody answered that. There was no answer.

---

The girl told them about the wash while Dee sealed the vent jar.

She told Dee, not Jab. She had put the bar down at last, on the handcart, and she stood with her arms round herself in the cold and told it the way she had told them about her father, fast and hoarse and all at once.

"Don't go over the wash," she said. "On the line. Where it crosses. There's a bridge in the bank, a stone one, where the water goes under, and it's going. The water's eaten under the far side of it. He went over it with the empty cart and it shook. It shook under a *handcart*." She looked at the cart and the horse. "It'll not take that."

"How far?"

"Two days. Less. Where the line goes down to the river, before the hill." She pulled her arms tighter. "And there's grey all over there. On the ground. In heaps, like snow that won't melt, all along the wash and up the bank of the line. That's where he went down for the wire. Into the grey." She stopped. "And things come up out of it. At dusk. We had a lamp on the line and two came up at it out of the grey, the first night, and he killed them with the bar and they went to more grey." She looked at the hill that was not there yet, west, over the trees. "And the sky's wrong over the hill. At night. It's like looking down a well."

Dee wrote all of it.

"Wash in running water," she said to the girl. "Every night. Both of you. Hands and face and hair, before you eat. Nothing goes in your mouth that's touched that wire." She looked at the handcart. "And leave the warm coils."

The man's head came up.

"No."

"Some of them," said Dee. "Callie will tell you which."

Callie went down the side of the handcart with the counter at arm's length, coil by coil, and said the numbers out loud. Twelve, and fourteen, and twenty-eight, and eleven. The three that were twenty and over she pointed at with the hook's handle without touching them.

The man looked at the three coils for a long time. Then he nodded, once, and the girl pulled them off the handcart with her sleeves over her hands and rolled them into the ditch. She did not look at them after.

They did not give their names. Dee did not ask, and the girl did not offer, and when the man opened his mouth as if he might, the girl said, "Don't," and he shut it again.

Toren and Pell got the handcart out of the ditch and onto its two wheels on the siding bed, pointing east. Dee put the sealed vent jar in the ditch by the three coils, with a stone on its lid, and Toren chalked a cross on the stone with the chalk from Pell's pocket. *Don't touch.*

Then the man got up. He got up on the girl's shoulder and the cart's handle, and stood. He stood with his hands held out in front of him, and the girl took the handles, and they went.

They went east along the siding toward the old line and the water and the north road and whatever was beyond it. Jab watched them until they were in the trees. The girl pulled. The man walked beside the handcart with one wrapped hand on the pile of wire, not holding, only there. Neither of them looked back.

---

That night they camped at the far end of the siding, where it came back up to the old line on the west side of the water.

Jab sat by the fire with his hands on his knees. They were steady. They were tired in a way he did not have a word for, not in the muscles. In the palms. As if he had been holding something hot for an hour and had put it down, and the holding was still in them.

Dee measured him.

"A hand," she said. "And less of a bit than this morning. Your floor's where it was." She let go of his wrist. "You didn't touch your own. The jar paid. You're tired because you worked."

"I know."

"I know you know." She wrote. "Two Handfuls and a quarter, with my draw. For a man we'll never see again, who'll not keep his hands." She did not look up. "I'd have said no, if it was mine to say. I'd have said it for the right reasons. I'd have said it for the fence." She shut her book. "It wasn't mine. I'm writing that as well."

She went to the cart.

Rook was sitting on the canvas with his back against the wheel, where Dee had put it double. He had not said anything all evening. He had not said anything at the ditch either, except *he's feeling*, and he had not taken his hand off Jab's shoulder the whole time, and it had not done anything but be there.

"That was yours," he said, when Jab looked at him.

"It was theirs," said Jab.

Rook thought about that.

"Yes," he said. "That too."

Jab looked at the fire. He thought about two Handfuls. He thought about a man walking east with his wrapped hand on a pile of wire he would not be able to spend, and a girl pulling the cart. He thought about hounds on the fifth day, and five and a quarter in the road jar, and eight jars on his own back that were not his, and a fence.

He did not know if he had been right. He knew that the man's chest was sixteen and had been sixty. He knew that he had said no to the hands, and that Dee had put her own palm over them to see, and then said *leave it*. He knew that Toren had stood at the back of the cart with a pencil and not said *go on* or *don't*.

He did not know what it would cost on the fifth day.

Toren came and sat by him with the paper.

"I wrote it," he said. He held it out. Jab did not take it. He read it where it was, in the firelight, in Toren's careful slow hand, under the day's count.

*43rd. The siding (the line's under water). A man and a girl, copper off the line near the wash. J. chose. Road jar 2 and a bit. His chest 60 to 16. The hands were done; J. said no to them and D. W. checked and said leave it. The girl says the wash bridge is going.*

Under it, in the same hand, smaller:

*Gran's was today.*

Jab gave it back.

"You didn't say don't," he said.

"No," said Toren. He folded the paper. "It wasn't mine." He looked at the fire. "I wanted to. I had the sum. I'd have said it if you'd asked me. You didn't ask me."

"I was waiting for you to say it."

"I know," said Toren. "I was waiting for you not to need me to."

Jab sat with that. After a while he put his hand on his coat, over the small hard shape on the left side, and left it there.

*Bring him back.*

He would. He was going to try very hard. He sat by the fire with his tired hands and the fish under one of them, and thought about the grey on the ground at the wash like snow that would not melt. And about the sky over a hill he had not seen yet, that a girl of twelve said was like looking down a well.

# Chapter 28 — Callie: The Still Point

On the forty-fourth morning Callie walked into her own hook.

She had thought about it all night, between her watch and the light, lying on the canvas with the stone settled at the bottom of the well and the hook on the ground beside her where she could put her hand on it. *It wants ground.* The Ward on her tore the moment one foot came up. So she would not put it on her. She would put it in the steel, where it was cheap, where it had held a rack and a stair and the mouth of a pipe. She would carry the steel.

She tried it at the first halt, on the old line west of the water, with the wind coming across the bare fields from the west and bringing the grit with it again.

She held the hook out in front of her by the conduit, with the curve at the end of the four feet of pipe, up off the ground at the height of her waist. Curve outward. She reached for the stone and let it go into the steel.

The hook went pale.

It went fixed. It went exactly as fixed as it went across a doorway or a pipe mouth. It stopped being a thing she was holding and became a thing that was simply there, in the air, four feet in front of her, at the height of her waist, and nothing on the earth was going to move it. The grit hissed against the pale steel and went round it.

She took a step forward.

The hook did not.

She walked into it. She walked into the end of her own conduit, which was fixed in the air like an iron bar set in a wall. It stopped her at the ribs, on the left side, on the place that had been healing since the scarp. She heard herself make a noise. She stood with the conduit against her ribs and her hands still on it and her eyes watering.

Rook laughed.

He laughed out loud this time, not the low careful laugh. He was at the tailboard beside Jab, and he laughed and then put his hand on the tailboard and stopped, because laughing hurt his back. He laughed anyway, a little, into his collar.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. I'm sorry."

"You're not," said Callie.

"I'm not," Rook agreed. "I'm sorry about the ribs."

She let the Ward go. The hook came alive in her hands again, heavy and ordinary, and she stood holding it with her other hand pressed flat on her side.

Miss Wren had not laughed. She had watched it with the glass in her hand and her face perfectly still, and when Callie let go she came round the tailboard.

"Ribs," she said.

"Same place."

Miss Wren put her hand flat on Callie's side through the coat and pressed, not hard. Callie breathed in and it hurt, and breathed out and it hurt less.

"Bruised," said Miss Wren. "Not cracked again. You'll know about it for a week." She took her hand away. "How much?"

"Nothing. It was there for a breath."

"Then it cost you a rib and nothing else. That's cheap." She looked at the hook. "It's still. That's what it is. That's what it's always been. You put it in the steel and the steel stops. You can't walk a thing that's stopped."

"I know that now."

"I knew it yesterday," said Miss Wren. "I didn't tell you. You'd not have believed me till you'd walked into it."

---

She walked in front of the cart all morning and thought about it with her side aching.

The Ward was being still. That was what it was, in the steel. Not strong. Still. The rack had not been held up by strength. It had been held by the hook being somewhere and not moving. On her it was the same. It was her being still, and the moment she lifted a foot she was not still any more, and it tore.

So a Ward that moved was a Ward that was not a Ward.

She tried it in her head every way she could think of. Plant the hook, walk up to it, let it go, carry it forward, plant it again. That was what Toren had done with the lead box in the pen, walking it on its edge. But in the gap every time she let go there would be nothing at all. The handoff gap was two, with somebody under the load, and three without. Two, every few steps, with nothing. It would be a fence with holes in it, walking.

She tried it anyway, at the next halt, three times, with the hook. Plant, walk, let go, lift, plant. It worked the way a fence with holes works. The grit came through every time she lifted, in a gust, and hit Jab in the face.

"That's worse than nothing," said Jab mildly, wiping his eyes. "That's nothing with a wall in it."

"I know."

"How much?" said Miss Wren.

"A little. Every time I plant it, it takes a bit." Callie went down to look. "An eighth. Near enough. For three tries."

"Settled, five eighths," said Miss Wren, writing. "You're down to less than a Handful and you've not got a thing for it."

"I've got that it doesn't work."

"That's a thing," said Miss Wren. "It's not a thing you can hold a stair with."

---

At noon Pell greased the axle.

He had been listening to it all morning. Callie had heard him listening. The right wheel had begun to sing at the hub, a thin dry note on every turn. It had started when the cart came out of the washout on the forty-second, and Pell had been walking at Duchess's head with his head a little on one side, the way her grandfather had held a sack when he was listening to it.

At the noon halt he got down on his knees on the stone of the old line beside the wheel. He took the grease pot out of the box under the driver's bench and a stick. He worked the grease in round the hub where the axle went through. He did it slowly, the way he did everything, with the wheel turning under his hand a little at a time as he rocked the cart.

Callie sat on the edge of the bed with her side aching and watched him.

She watched the wheel. She did not mean to. It was a thing that was moving, and she watched moving things. It was a big wheel, as high as her shoulder, with twelve spokes and an iron tyre. There was a puddle under it where the frost had melted on the stone in the sun. When Pell rocked the cart, the wheel rolled forward a hand's width into the puddle and back again. The bottom of it went into the water and out of the water.

"It's the washout," said Pell, to the hub. "She got grit in there in the stream. It'll sing till it's out."

"It goes into the puddle," said Callie.

Pell looked up at her.

"The wheel," said Callie. "When you rock it. It goes into the water and out again." She was looking at the bottom of the tyre, where it met the stone. "It doesn't splash. When you roll it forward it goes into the water and it doesn't splash at all."

"It wouldn't," said Pell.

"Why not?"

Pell sat back on his heels with the grease stick in his hand and looked at her the way he had looked at her on the first morning, when she had told him to take the high side of the culvert. The way you look at somebody who has asked the right question.

"Because a wheel doesn't move where it touches," he said.

Callie looked at him.

"The bit of the tyre that's on the ground," said Pell. "Right there. Where it's in the water. That bit's not going anywhere. The top of the wheel's going forward twice as fast as the cart. The middle's going as fast as the cart. The bottom—" He put his hand flat on the stone beside the tyre where it touched. "The bottom's still. For a moment. Every bit of it, in turn. It comes down onto the ground and it stops, and then the next bit comes down and stops, and the one before it lifts off. It never slides. That's why it doesn't splash." He put the grease stick back in the pot. "That's all a wheel is. A lot of still, one after another. My father told me that when I was ten, and I thought he was mad, and he wasn't."

Callie sat on the edge of the stone bed of the old line and did not move.

*A lot of still, one after another.*

She looked at the hook across her knees. At the curve of it, a foot of it, the grain hook her grandfather had used at the hopper gate, bent like a new moon. She looked at the bottom of the curve.

"Pell," she said.

"Mm."

"If I rolled it. The hook. On the ground, on the curve, like the bottom of a wheel. The bit that was on the ground—"

"—would be still," said Pell. "For a moment. Then the next bit." He looked at the hook, and then at her. "I don't know anything about your Ward, Callie. I know about wheels."

"The Ward wants still," said Callie. "It wants ground."

"Then give it both," said Pell, and went back to the hub.

---

She tried it before the glass ran out.

She stood up on the old line with her side aching and turned the hook over in her hands. She held it by the end of the conduit, with the four feet of pipe going down and away from her at a slant and the curve at the bottom, the way you might hold a long-handled hoe. She put the curve on the stone. Not the point of it. The back of it, the outside of the bend, the way the bottom of a tyre sits on a road.

She pushed.

It rolled. It did not roll the way a wheel rolls, all the way round. It was only a foot of curve. But it rocked forward along its own bend, and the part of it that touched the stone moved along the bend from the back of the curve toward the front, like the bottom of a rocking chair. The rest of the hook went forward with her hands.

She did it again. She pushed it forward and let it rock along its curve, and when it came to the front of the curve she lifted it a hand's width and set the back of the curve down again, further on. Rock, lift, set. Rock, lift, set. Like a man walking on one long foot.

Then she reached for the stone and did not put the Ward in the hook.

She put it in the part of the hook that was touching the ground.

She did not know if she could. She had never asked the Ward to go into part of the steel and not the rest. She had always asked it for the hook, and got the hook, all of it, fixed. But she looked at the place where the curve met the stone, the way Toren had looked at the heel of his spike in the first lane. She kept her eyes on it, the small bright line where the back of the bend sat on the grey. She asked for the Ward there.

It came.

She felt it come up out of the stone at the bottom of the well and down through her arms and along the pipe and into the curve. Into the bottom of the curve, where it touched. The steel there went pale. Not all of it. A hand's width of the bend, where it was on the ground.

And from that hand's width something stood up.

It stood up off the curve of the hook like a sheet of frost standing on its edge. It was pale, and she could see through it, and it went up from the steel where the steel touched the stone to the height of her shoulder. It was a wall, a short wall. It stood on the outside of the curve, the side the bend faced, and it leaned a little over nothing.

She pushed.

The hook rocked forward along its curve. The pale hand's width at the bottom went with it, sliding along the bend as the touching part moved. It stayed where the steel met the stone, and the wall stood up off it and went with it. It did not tear. It did not tear because there was always a part of the steel that was on the ground and still. The Ward was always in the still part.

She walked.

She walked one step, rocking the hook forward on its curve, and the wall went with her. Two steps. She lifted the hook at the front of the curve and set it down again on the back of the bend a hand further on. For the length of a breath the wall shivered and thinned, and did not go, because she set the back down before the front came up.

Three steps. Four.

The grit came across from the west in its low brown haze and hit the pale wall and hissed against it and went round, and did not get to her.

Five. Six.

"Callie," said Miss Wren.

She did not look round. She did not dare.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

On the tenth the back of the curve came down on a loose stone in the bed of the old line. It skidded. The hook rocked sideways instead of forward, and for a moment there was no part of it that was still on the ground. Only a part of it that was sliding.

The wall went out like a blown lamp.

She stood on the old line with the hook in her hands and the grit in her face.

"Ten," said Toren, from the tailboard. He said it in the voice he used for a number he was writing down. "That was ten."

---

Nobody said anything else for a moment.

Then Rook said, "Again," and Miss Wren said, "How much?" in the same breath. Pell, on his knees by the wheel with the grease pot, said, "Mind the loose stones," without looking up.

Callie went down to look.

"About a tenth," she said. "For ten steps." She heard it. It was more than the hook held still across a door. It was much less than on her. "Somewhere in between."

"Settled, a half and a bit," said Miss Wren. She wrote it. "Somewhere in between is a price, Callie. It's not a number. Again, and we'll find the number."

She did it again. She did it nine times that afternoon, walking in front of Duchess on the old line with the grit coming across, and she found the number, and the other numbers that went with it.

She found that it wanted the stone of the old line. On the packed grey stone of the bed the curve rocked true and the wall stood. Where the bed had gone to earth and grass, the curve sank a little and rocked slow, and the wall thinned. Where it had gone to loose sand, at a place where the old line crossed a dry stream on a bank of it, the curve skidded on the first step and there was no wall at all.

"Footing," said Rook, from the tailboard. "Same as everything. Same as the strut."

She found that it wanted her slow. At the frame's pace, at Jab's pace, it held. When she walked faster the curve came off the ground at the front before the back was down, and there was a moment of nothing, and the wall shivered and went. She tried to run with it once. She got two steps.

"Speed," said Rook.

She found that it stood on one side only. It stood off the outside of the curve, the side the bend faced, and nowhere else. If she turned the hook to face the other way, the wall stood on the other side. It did not go round. It was not a room or a dome or the sheet the day holder had laid across the whole bottom of the Sag. It was a wall, and it went where the curve was pointing.

"It's a lee," said Pell, at the fourth halt. He was standing behind it with his collar down for the first time all day and the grit going past a foot from his face. "That's all. It's the lee side of a hedge. It'll keep what's blowing off whatever's behind it. It'll not keep anything off that comes round the end."

"How much?" said Miss Wren.

"A tenth," said Callie. "For fourteen steps."

"Fourteen," said Miss Wren, writing. "It's getting cheaper."

"I'm getting better at it."

"That's what cheaper is," said Miss Wren.

---

At the fifth halt she put people in it.

She had not done it on purpose until then. The wall had stood beside her and kept the grit off her, and anybody who happened to be walking close behind it had got the lee of it too. Pell had, and Jab once. Now she wanted to know.

"Jab," she said. "Walk behind it. Close. On this side."

Jab came off the tailboard and walked behind her, a pace back, on the lee side, with the frame on his back. She rocked the hook forward on its curve along the stone and the wall stood up off it. She could feel it stand. She could feel it standing between the grit and Jab's face, and she knew where his face was, because she could see his shoulders out of the corner of her eye.

"Rook," she said. "You too."

Rook came. He walked beside Jab, on his left, the way he always walked. Callie felt the wall change.

She did not ask it to. It changed on its own. It got longer at the back, to put Rook's shoulders behind it as well as Jab's. As it got longer it got thinner, like dough being pulled. She could feel the grit hissing against it harder, as if the wind had found where it was weakest.

She held it for eight steps. On the ninth it tore at the far end, behind Rook, and the grit came round the end and hit him in the side of the head.

"That's two," said Rook, wiping his ear. "Two's shorter."

"Two's shorter," said Callie.

She tried the cart. She knew before she tried it that it was stupid, and she tried it anyway, because she had to know. She stepped out wide on the windward side of Duchess and the cart. She rocked the hook forward on the stone of the bed and asked the wall to stand up off the curve and go along the whole side of them, horse and cart and all, to the tailboard where Jab walked.

It went.

It went long and thin and pale, along Duchess's shoulder and her flank and the whole side of the cart, like a sheet of ice on a pond in the first night of frost. It held for four steps. On the fifth it went from end to end all at once, like a pane going in a window.

Callie stopped. She put the back of her hand to her lip, and it came away red.

"Nose," said Miss Wren, already beside her with a cloth.

"I know."

"Head back. No. Head forward. Pinch it." Miss Wren held the cloth. "That's the widening. Same as the twentieth, with Wyck. Eleven seconds and a nosebleed." She looked at Callie over the cloth. "You can't have the whole cart. You can't have all of us. I'd like that said out loud, by you, now, so it's said."

"I can't have all of you," said Callie, through the cloth.

"How many can you have?"

Callie thought about it with her head forward and the blood going into the cloth.

"Two," she said. "Close. On good stone. At his pace." She nodded at Jab. "For a while. Not long. Or one and the frame. Or—" She stopped. "It's who's behind it. Every body I add, it's thinner. It's shorter. I've got to choose."

"Then choose," said Miss Wren. "Every time. Before you put it up. Who's inside." She took the cloth away and looked at it. "That's the whole of it. That's what nobody who's ever thrown up a wall over a room has had to learn, because they could have the room. You can't. You'll have to decide who's worth the lee."

---

Rook and Miss Wren argued about it at the sixth halt, and Callie heard it because she was sitting on the tailboard's end next to Jab's rack with her head forward and the cloth to her nose.

It started because Rook wanted her to go again.

"She's got it," he said. "She's had it since noon. She needs to do it a hundred times, not fifteen. She needs to do it till it's in her feet. It's a road. It's the only yard we've got."

"It's the fence's ash," said Miss Wren.

"It's the road's ash."

"It's ash she'll settle out of the road jar tonight," said Miss Wren, "because she's spent half of what she's been carrying since the gate. And the road jar's got five and a quarter in it, because Jab put two into a man in a ditch. And we're two days from a bridge a girl of twelve says shakes under a handcart." She did not raise her voice. "Every try she makes today is a minute of a stair she hasn't got at the fence, Elias. Every one. I'll give her three more. Not a hundred."

"Three's not learning."

"Three's what the road's got."

Rook was quiet. Callie watched him. He was standing by the tailboard with his hand on it, and his face had the look it had when he was deciding whether to argue with somebody who had the right to tell him no. She had seen it on the first night on the rise, when Miss Wren had told him to sleep.

"Three," he said.

Miss Wren looked at him.

"Three," said Rook again. "It's your word." He turned to Callie. "Then listen to me, because you've only got three, and I'd like one of them to be different."

Callie took the cloth away from her nose.

"You're watching the hook," said Rook. "All afternoon. You're watching the bottom of the curve, where it touches. I've seen you. You look at the steel like it's going to run off." He put his hand on the tailboard. "Don't. The curve'll look after itself. Pell's right, a wheel doesn't need watching. It's still where it touches whether you look or not." He nodded at Jab. "Watch who's inside. Watch his shoulders. Watch mine. Put your eyes on the people and hold them there, not the steel." He was quiet a moment. "That's what I did with Senna for three hundred miles. I didn't watch my hands. I watched her."

Callie looked at him.

"You've been holding the hook," said Rook. "Hold them."

---

She did the first of the three with her eyes on Jab's shoulders.

It was the hardest thing she had done all day. The curve rocked along the stone in front of her, and every part of her wanted to look down at it, at the bright line where it met the ground. She did not look. She looked back, out of the side of her eye, at Jab walking in the lee with the frame on his back and the eight jars in the rack. She looked at the top of his shoulders where the chest band came over them. She held them there, in her eyes, the way she had once held a wagon on a hook with her mother in it.

The wall stood up.

It stood up and it was different. She felt it be different. It did not stand straight up off the curve like a board any more. It leaned. It leaned back over Jab, a little, like the roof of a lean-to. And it was shorter at the front and longer at the back, where he was, and thicker there, over his shoulders, where she was looking. It was the shape of where he was.

She walked twenty-two steps before the curve found a loose stone.

"Twenty-two," said Toren.

She did the second with Jab and Rook. She put her eyes on both their shoulders at once, the way you look at two things on a table. The wall leaned back over both of them. It went thin at the far end over Rook's head, and she felt it go thin, and she did not look at the hook. She looked at Rook's head. It came back.

Sixteen steps.

She did the third one alone, with nobody in it, to see.

It stood straight up off the curve like a board, the way it had at noon, and it cost what it had cost at noon, and it held twelve steps. It held them for nobody.

She let it go and stood on the old line.

"Say it," said Miss Wren.

"It's cheaper with somebody in it," said Callie slowly. She did not know if that was the right way to say it. "Not cheaper. It goes further. When I'm looking at who's in it, it goes where they are and not where they're not. It's not wasting. When there's nobody, it stands up everywhere, because it doesn't know where to be." She looked at Rook. "It's the same as the light. The heel. It goes where you're looking."

"Jab said that," said Toren, from the tailboard. "In the first lane. On the thirty-first."

"I know he said it. I heard it on the step." Callie looked at the hook in her hands. "I didn't know it was true for this."

Miss Wren wrote for a long time.

When she had finished she turned the book round and showed Callie what she had written. It was in her small upright hand, under the day, under all the tenths.

*C. — a moving lee. Rolls the curve on the ground and puts the Ward in the part that touches (Pell's wheel). One side only, like a hedge. Wants stone, and her walking pace or less. One person, 22 steps; two, 16; nobody, 12; the whole cart, 4 and a nose. Goes further when she watches who's inside (R.). She has to choose who. It keeps off what's blowing. It won't keep off what's in the ground, and it won't keep off what comes round the end.*

"Is that right?" said Miss Wren.

Callie read it twice.

"That's right," she said.

"Then it's in the book," said Miss Wren. "Settle. A half out of the road jar. Tonight. And say it."

---

She said it at the fire that night.

"Road, four and three quarters."

She said it the way her mother read the store book, flat, for somebody else. Then she put her palm on the half-Handful on its square of paper on her knee and let it come at the rate it came, with her shoulders down, and her side aching where she had walked into the conduit.

It took her a hundred and fifty breaths, near enough. She did not count them. She knew.

When it was done she sat with her hand on her knee and did not know what to do with it.

It was the forty-fourth day. She knew that without asking. She had known it all day, the way she knew where the ash was. But she found she wanted to hear it said.

"What day is it?" she said. "At home."

Toren looked up from the paper.

"The forty-fourth," he said.

"I know it's the forty-fourth."

"I know you know," said Toren. He did not ask why she had asked. He looked back down at the paper. "The pills were at the eighth hour. Your mother'll have walked to the ward. Katori'll have walked on one side of her, and Ines on the other, not holding. The old man'll have had the stool warm." He did not look up. "That's what's on the back of Dessa's six lines. What they're doing while we're doing this."

Callie did not say anything.

She had not thought about her mother for three hours.

She had found that out at the fourth halt, standing behind the lee with her nose bleeding and Miss Wren holding the cloth. She had looked up and seen the light going over to the west and known it was the afternoon, and known that she had not once thought about the fourth bed on the left or the stool in the hut window since before noon. Since the wheel. She had felt it like a step missed on a stair. She had thought, *I let go of her*, and it had gone through her cold.

She sat with it now by the fire.

Her mother had said it. *Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.* And Callie had nodded on the edge of the bed with her hand open on the blanket, and she had thought she understood. She had not understood that it would feel like this. It did not feel like trust. It felt like forgetting. It felt like the thing she had been most afraid of her whole life, which was looking up from a sum and finding that the person she had been counting had gone on breathing without her.

She had gone on breathing without her. Her mother had. For three hours, on the forty-fourth day, eighty miles off, her mother had walked to the ward and sat on her stool and read the beam and said *that's a dirty one*, probably, to Otto. Nobody had been counting her. And nothing had happened to her because of it.

Nothing had happened to her because of it.

Callie sat by the fire with her hand on her knee and let that go through her, slowly, the way cold goes out of a stone brought in from the yard.

---

Miss Wren sat down beside her when the others were at the cart.

She did not say anything for a while. She had the book on her knee and the glass in her hand, and she turned the glass over and watched it run, the way other people watch a fire.

"You looked like somebody who'd lost a jar," she said at last. "At the fourth halt. After the nose."

"I'd not thought about her for three hours."

"No," said Miss Wren. "You were working." She watched the sand. "That's what working is. It's the first thing Vell ever told us at the table. You can't draw with half your mind on your mother. You'll draw your mother into the dish." She turned the glass. "It comes back. When you stop. It always comes back. It's still there. It's just not in your hands."

Callie looked at her.

"Have you got anybody?" she said. "At home. That you don't think about when you're working."

Miss Wren did not answer at once.

"I've got him," she said.

She did not look at the cart, where Rook was lying on the canvas doubled under him with his hands on his chest. She did not need to.

"I've had him since I was fourteen," she said. "Since the wire. He walked me away from it for two months with a broken wrist and no shoes. Then for a long time I didn't see him. I thought about him every day I wasn't working." She turned the glass. "I'm not his. He's not mine. It's not that kind. But I've watched him do what he did in the Sag, and in the pen, and I know what he'll try to do at that fence. He'll try to spend himself. He'll do it because there's somebody in front of him who needs it and he can't not. He's always been like that. It's the best thing about him and it's going to kill him."

The glass ran out. She did not turn it.

"The Director's word is the only thing he's ever let stop him," she said. "And only because it's mine, in her mouth. He'd not stop for hers. He'd not stop for his own." She looked at the fire. "I've never been anybody's word but my own. On this road I'm the Director's. I don't like it. I'm using it anyway." She picked up the glass. "That's what I'm holding. Since you asked."

Callie sat beside her.

"You told him to sleep," she said. "On the rise. And he slept."

"He lay down," said Miss Wren. "Jab says he slept an hour. That's an hour more than he'd have had." She turned the glass over. "It's a start."

---

At dusk on the forty-fourth day the old line came up onto a long low rise, and from the top of it Callie saw the hill.

It was a long way off. It was at the edge of the world, west, where the land went flat and grey toward the last of the light. It was the only thing on the whole of that flat that was not flat. It stood up out of it alone, a long, low, heavy shape, like a loaf of bread with the top cut off. It was flat along the top where a hill should have been round. Somebody had taken the top off it. Senna had said so, in the ward, in the book. *A hill with its top taken off.* Callie had not believed it would look like that. It looked exactly like that.

Pell stopped Duchess. Nobody had asked him to. Everybody stopped.

Over the flat top of the hill, the sky was wrong.

Callie did not know how else to say it. It was the end of the day, and the sky in the west was going the colour it went, grey and pale yellow and then grey again, with long thin clouds lying across it. And over the hill, over the flat top, there was a place where the sky did not go the colour it went. It was a patch, round, the size of her thumb held up at the end of her arm. It was darker than the sky round it. Not black. Bruised. The clouds that lay across the sky went into it and came out of the other side of it not quite where they should have been, the way a stick looks bent where it goes into water. Round the edge of it the light was a colour she did not have a word for. It was a little green, a little grey, like the light in the bottom of a pond.

It did not move. She watched it and it did not move. But she had the feeling, watching it, that it was not still either. The way you have the feeling that a sleeping animal is breathing before you can see its side go up and down.

"Two days," said Pell.

Nobody else said anything at all.

Callie read the land in front of her, because it was what she did, and because she could not look at the sky over the hill for long.

The land between the rise and the hill was flat and bare and long. The old line went across it straight as a ruler, a thin grey stripe with its posts along it. And in the low places, all across the flat, there was grey. It lay in the hollows and along the bottoms of the ditches and against the sides of things, in long soft drifts. It lay the way snow lies a week after a fall, when the rest has gone and only the drifts are left in the places the wind put them. But it was not white. It was grey. It was the grey she had lifted every morning of her life off the plain, the grey in the jars on Jab's back.

There was a great deal of it.

"That's ash," she said. "In the low places. All the way to the hill."

"Lying where it fell," said Rook.

"Nobody's lifted it," said Callie. "Nobody's been near it to lift it."

Beside her, Jab had put his hand on his coat, over the left side. He did not say anything. He was looking at the patch of wrong sky over the flat top of the hill, and his face was very still, the way it went when he put his attention down on a table and took his hands off it.

"Jab," said Callie.

"I don't know," said Jab. He did not look away from the hill. "It's a long way. I don't know what it is. I don't feel anything." He was quiet. "I don't like it."

"Nobody likes it," said Pell, and said a word to Duchess, and walked on down the rise toward the flat. Callie went out twenty paces in front of him with the hook and read the road for the wheels. She did not look up at the sky again until it was dark, and then she could not see it.

# Chapter 29 — Toren: Set and Step

On the forty-fifth morning Dee made them say the rules at the start of the day, as if the old line were a gate.

She did it at first light by the fire, with the frost on everything and the hill already there in the west, flat and dark against a sky that was only beginning to be a colour. Nobody looked at the hill. Everybody knew it was there.

"Two days," said Dee. "Say them now. I want them in your mouths before we're anywhere near a fence. I want them said so often you'd say them in your sleep."

They said them. Nothing to eat inside the fence. Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, every time, coat off, shake it, wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat. Never carry one of the boxes. Not on your back. Not in your arms.

"And the rest," said Dee.

"Red, and nobody trapped," said Callie. "Everybody out, at once."

"Red, and a body trapped," said Toren. "The fewest who can do it stay. The watch says their names. They stay till the person's out and the source is covered. Then out. Nobody back in till the counter's under the red."

"Two Flasks left inside," said Jab. "Everybody out. A healer at their floor twice. A healer still warm after the watch. And me below my floor, once." He did not look at Dee. "And you turn us round."

"And me," said Rook. "Half a turn. Ten minutes. Every time."

"And me," said Pell. "Outside. At the cart. Writing down who went in." He looked at the hill. "And the three Flasks don't go inside for anything."

Dee wrote nothing. She had written it all before.

"Good," she said. "Now the road."

---

The siding had brought them back to the old line on the forty-third, and the forty-fourth had been good stone all day. On the forty-fifth day the old line went on west across the flat toward the hill, and it began to break.

It did not break all at once. It broke the way ice breaks on a pond in a thaw, a crack here, a tilted slab there. The bed of the old line had been packed stone for three days, hard and grey and good for the wheels. Now the frost had got into it. It had heaved it up in places into long humps, and dropped it in others into dips with water standing in them. In one place it had split it clean across, so that the near half of the bed was a hand's width higher than the far half, with a gap between them the width of a boot.

Pell stopped Duchess at the first of the gaps and looked at it.

"She'll take it," he said. "The wheels won't. Not loaded. The near wheel drops in the gap and the whole cart comes down on the axle." He looked along the line. There were more gaps. There were more humps and dips, as far as he could see. "It'll be like this all day."

"I'll set under it," said Toren.

Pell looked at him.

"Like the washout," said Toren. "Only more times. Under the axle when the wheel's over the gap, and hold, and let go when it's past. Then walk up and do the other wheel." He heard himself say it. It sounded simpler than it was going to be. "I'll do it every time."

"There'll be forty of them," said Pell.

"Then I'll do it forty times."

"You'll not," said Dee, from the tailboard. "You'll do it as many times as there's ash for. Your stone's a Handful. A tenth's four holds, if you're soft." She looked at the line. "Forty gaps is eighty wheels."

Toren thought about that.

"Then I'll learn to do it for less," he said.

---

It did not go well at first. He had known it would not.

The first time he set too far ahead. He stood on the far side of the gap and put the foot of the strut down on the far slab and let it come up toward where the axle was going to be. The wheel came to the gap and dropped into it, a hand's width, and the axle came down short of the top of the strut and hit the near end of it. It knocked the strut sideways. It went out. The wheel banged down onto the far slab and the cart jolted, and the four jars in the cart's rack rang against each other in their straw.

"*Easy*," said Jab.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry to me," said Jab. "Be sorry to them. They're lent."

The second time he set right under the axle and locked. The wheel came into the gap and the weight came onto the strut, more of it than he had expected, and everything in him went hard at once, the way it had under the sack at seven paces on the thirty-second. The weight came up through the strut into his knee and his hip and his arm. He felt it go into the arm. Not a break. A jar, up from the wrist into the elbow inside the leather cuff, deep and dull, like a door slammed in the next room.

He let go. He stood on the slab with the arm against his chest and breathed through his teeth.

"Where?" said Dee.

"Same place. Not as bad."

"Soft," said Pell, from Duchess's head. "You locked."

"I know I locked."

He did it a third time. And this time, before he set, he did not look at the axle. He looked at the ground.

He looked at the slab on the far side of the gap. It was grey and flat and heavy, sitting on the bed with its edge a hand's width lower than the near one. He looked at where the foot of the strut would go. Not the middle, which was cracked. The near edge of it, where it sat on the stone of the bed underneath, solid, the way the stone shelf had sat under the gravel in the washout.

"Callie," he said.

She was twenty paces in front of Duchess. She came back.

"Tell me the ground," he said. "Where the foot goes. Tell me as I'm doing it."

Callie looked at the slab.

"Near edge," she said. "Left of the crack. It's sitting on stone. The middle's sitting on water." She crouched and looked. "The right-hand end's hollow. Don't go there."

"Near edge," said Toren. "Left of the crack."

He put the foot down there. He let the strut come up from the ground at its slant, soft behind. It came up under where the axle would be when the wheel was in the gap. It did not come up under where the axle was now. He stood there and waited with it, soft, the way you wait for a sack you can hear coming down the lane.

"Set," he said.

"Rolling," said Pell, and Duchess leaned.

The wheel came to the gap. It dropped. The axle came down onto the top of the strut and the weight went into it. It went down the slant into the near edge of the slab where Callie had said. It did not come up into him, because he was soft, and his knees gave a little and took the last of it. The wheel rolled across the gap on the strut the way it had rolled up the bank in the washout. The axle went along the top of the strut toward the end.

He let it go at the end. Not after. At the end.

The wheel came down on the far slab without a jolt.

"Step," said Toren, and walked up the side of the cart to the other wheel.

"Stone," said Callie, already crouching at the gap. "Same place. Near edge."

"Set," said Toren.

"Rolling," said Pell.

It went across.

---

By the middle of the morning they were not saying anything else.

It had become the thing they said. Toren had not meant it to. He had said *set* in the washout because it was what came out, and he said it now because it was what came out, and Pell had said *rolling*. Callie had begun saying *stone* and *soft* and *hollow* before Toren had even asked her, crouching at every gap before the cart came to it. By the eighth gap they were not deciding who said what. It was only a thing that happened on the old line: *stone*, *set*, *rolling*, *step*, and the cart going over.

On the fifteenth gap Callie was not there, because she had gone ahead to look at a hump in the bed. Toren looked at the slab himself, and found the near edge sitting on stone, and set, and Pell said *rolling*. It went over.

He stood on the far side of it with the strut gone. He had not even thought about it until it was done.

"That was without her," said Pell.

"I know."

"You looked at the ground yourself." Pell looked at him over Duchess's neck. "You'll not always have her. Up there she'll have the stair."

"I know," said Toren.

At the second halt Dee made him go down and measure.

"Half," he said. He heard himself say it, and it was less than he had thought. "A half of the stone. For twenty-two gaps."

"Twenty-two gaps is forty-four wheels," said Dee.

"Some of them I didn't set. Some of the gaps were small." He thought about it. "About thirty sets. Half a Handful."

"That's fifteen to a quarter," said Dee. "At the washout it was two to a quarter. At six paces in the yard it was ten." She wrote it. "You're not holding them as long. You're letting go at the end."

"Pell said."

"Pell was right," said Dee. "When the wheel's gone by it's gone by." She shut the book. "Settle a half tonight. Out of the road jar. That's four and a quarter left in it, then." She looked at him. "And don't do it forty times tomorrow. Tomorrow you'll do it once, and it'll be the one that matters."

---

In the early afternoon they came to the first drift.

It lay in a hollow on the north side of the old line. The line ran along the edge of a long shallow dip in the flat, and the wind off the hill had filled the bottom of the dip with grey. It lay against the north side of the bed, the side the wind came from, in a long soft heap like a snowdrift, grey to the colour of the jars. The top of it had a crust on it, darker, where the frost and the wind had set it. Along the edge of it, where it met the stone of the bed, there was a thin line of grey going up over the lip of the bed and onto the stones, blown there.

Callie stopped twenty paces short of it.

She did not go closer. She took the counter out of its bag and switched it on and held it out in front of her, and the ticking went from rain starting to rain falling while Toren watched her face.

"Twenty," she said. "From here. Ten breaths." She took two steps. "Twenty-six." Two more. "Thirty-one. At the edge of the bed." She stopped. "It's warm. Not like the wire. It's thinner than the wire. But it's all of it warm." She looked at the drift. "It's off the hill. It's the grey the girl said. Like snow that won't melt."

They all looked at it.

"It's loose," said Pell. "Nobody's lifted it. It's lying where it fell." He looked along the line. "And there's the wind."

The wind was coming off the hill from the west-north-west, across the flat, the way it had come for two days. Toren saw it take the top off the drift. It did not take much. It lifted a thin grey haze off the crust, very fine, and brought it across the bed of the old line in a low smoke. The smoke crossed the stones and went on over the south side into the flat.

"That's what the girl's father walked into," said Dee. "Going down to the wash for the wire. That, only more."

"Where's Rook?" said Callie.

---

Rook was on the drift.

Not on it. At the edge of it. He had come round the front of the cart while they were all looking at Callie, and gone along the north edge of the bed at his careful straight walk, and down off the bed onto the flat at the foot of the drift. He was standing there with the grey to his shins, with his head on one side, looking at the crust. He crouched, slowly, in two movements, and put his hand out flat over it, not touching.

"Elias," said Dee.

Her voice did not go up. It went very quiet.

Rook stood up.

He came back up onto the bed. He did it slowly, and when he got to the top of the lip Toren saw his boots and his trousers to the shins. They were grey. It was not much grey. It was the fine grey of a man who has walked through ash that has been blown and is lying loose. He stood on the bed with his hands at his sides and looked at Dee.

"I wanted to see how it lies," he said. "Whether it's lying like a drift or lying like something's been through it. Whether it's been lifted, or turned—"

"Callie," said Dee. "His shins."

Callie held the counter out toward Rook's legs. She did not go nearer than her arm's length.

"Forty," she said. "Ten breaths. At his shins."

"You went to look," said Dee.

"I'm the one who looks," said Rook. "I've always been the one who looks. That's what I'm for."

"Not with my ash," said Dee.

It was very quiet on the old line. Duchess shifted her feet. The wind brought the thin grey smoke across the bed and past them.

"I'm not your ash," said Rook.

"On this road you are," said Dee. "Every bit of you. You're ten minutes a turn, half a turn, in my book, signed. And every grain that's on your legs now is a grain Jab has to draw off you before you've done anything at all, and that's out of a jar that was for the road." She did not look away from him. "You went to look alone. At a drift. Two days from the fence. Because you wanted to see."

Rook looked at her for a long moment.

Then he looked down at his own grey shins.

"Yes," he said. "I did."

"Then we'll do it properly," said Dee. "Now. Here. As if it was the fence." She turned. "Jab. You've got a patient."

---

It was the first time they did it all together.

It was not planned. Toren understood that afterward, sitting by the fire. Nobody had meant it to happen on the forty-fifth day on the old line by a drift nobody needed to go near. It happened because Rook had gone to look, and Dee had decided to turn it into something instead of just being angry. She stood at the tailboard with the glass and gave them their places the way she had in the pen.

"Rook stands where he is," she said. "On the bed, at the edge, by the drift. He's the patient. The wind's bringing that across him and onto whoever's working on him." She looked at Callie. "You. The lee. Between the drift and them. On the stone."

Callie had the hook already.

"Jab. You draw his legs. Off the road jar. Toren brings it." She looked at Toren. "And the cart's got to go past him on the bed while you're doing it, because we're not stopping on this road for the afternoon. There's a gap in the bed there by the drift." She pointed. There was, a hand's width, where the bed had split. "So you set under the wheel while they work."

"All at once?" said Toren.

"All at once," said Dee. "That's what it'll be."

So they did it all at once, and the first time it did not work.

---

Callie went to the north edge of the bed between the drift and Rook. She put the back of the hook's curve on the stone, and rocked it, and the lee stood up off the bottom of the curve, pale, with the grey smoke hissing against it. It stood between the drift and Rook's legs. She walked it along the edge toward him, a step, two, with her eyes on his shoulders the way Rook had told her.

Jab came off the tailboard with the frame on his back and knelt in front of Rook on the bed in the lee, and put his right hand flat on Rook's shin. Toren went to the tailboard for the road jar.

And Pell brought the cart up the bed toward the gap by the drift, with Duchess walking slowly. Toren came round the side of the cart with the road jar in his good hand, and saw where the gap was, and saw where Callie was. They were in the same place.

The gap in the bed was on the north side, by the drift. The north wheel would drop into it. And Callie was walking the lee along the north edge of the bed toward Rook with the curve of the hook rocking on the stone, right across the place where Toren had to put the foot of his strut.

"Callie—"

"I'm here—"

He put the jar in Dee's hand, and went to set, and the foot of his strut came down on the stone at its slant exactly where the back of Callie's curve was coming down to rock forward. It did not hit it. It came down next to it, and the strut and the curve were in the same hand's width of ground. The curve came down on the foot of the strut instead of the stone. It skidded on it.

The lee went out.

The grey smoke came across Rook and Jab and Jab's hand on Rook's shin, and Jab coughed, and Dee said "Sleeves," and everybody put their sleeves over their mouths. The north wheel came to the gap with Toren's strut standing in it, and he held it, and it rolled over. And Callie stood on the north edge of the bed with the hook in her hands and no lee at all.

"Stop," said Dee. "Everybody stop."

They stopped.

"That's what it'll be," said Dee, into her sleeve. "That's exactly what it'll be. Two of you wanting the same ground." She took her sleeve away. "Sort it out. Now. Before we do it again. Out loud."

---

They sorted it out standing on the old line with the grey smoke going past.

"You're on the outside," said Toren. "Always. The windward edge. Where the wind comes from and the drift is."

"And you're under," said Callie. "Under the cart. On the downhill side, or the inside. Never the edge." She looked at the gap. "If the gap's on the edge, you set from the inside, slanting out. Your foot's never where my curve is."

"And if it has to be?"

"Then you say *ground*," said Callie. "And I lift for a breath. And you say *set*. And I put it down again past you."

"And Jab's inside," said Toren. "Always. Behind you. In the lee."

"Behind me, in the lee, with whoever he's working on," said Callie. "They're who I'm looking at."

Jab, kneeling on the stone with his hand on Rook's shin and his sleeve over his mouth, said nothing for a moment.

"And I say when it's busy," he said. "When I've got it. And when it's clean." He looked up. "So you know how long you've got to hold it. So you're not holding the lee over nothing."

"*Busy*," said Toren. "*Clean*."

"And *jar*," said Jab. "Before it's dry. Not after."

Dee wrote it all down on her knee.

---

The second time it worked.

Callie went out to the north edge on the windward side with the hook and said "Lane." It was the first time she had said it. She did not seem to have decided to. The lee stood up off the curve between the drift and Rook, pale, and she walked it along with her eyes on Rook's shoulders. It leaned back over him and Jab, the way it had over Jab on the forty-fourth, the shape of where they were.

Jab put his hand on Rook's shin. Dee held the road jar to his left palm.

"Busy," said Jab.

Pell brought the cart up. The north wheel came toward the gap. Toren came round on the inside, the south side of the wheel, and did not go near the edge. He looked at the ground on the inside of the gap and found stone, and put the foot of the strut there, slanting outward under the axle.

"Set," he said.

"Rolling," said Pell.

The wheel came into the gap and onto the strut and rolled across it, and Toren let it go at the end. The curve of Callie's hook rocked along the stone of the edge a pace outside where his strut had been, and nothing touched anything, and the lee held.

"Vent," said Dee.

Jab lifted his palm off Rook's shin and put it flat on the stone of the bed on the downwind side, where the ground was already grey. He did not put it in a jar. Dee had told him on the first day on the old line that on a road where the ground was already dirty, you could vent into the ground, and only there. *Not into my floor. Into the road's.* He held it there for three breaths.

"Back on," said Dee.

"Busy," said Jab. And then, after two more draws: "Clean."

"Down," said Callie, and let the lee go.

It had taken eleven turns of the glass. Callie held the counter to Rook's shins at arm's length.

"Nine," she said. "The room."

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

Rook stood on the bed of the old line with his clean shins and his hands at his sides and looked at the three of them. He did not look at Dee. He looked at Callie with the hook, and Toren by the wheel, and Jab still kneeling on the stone with his hand flat on the bed and his face quiet.

"Well," he said.

That was all. He did not say anything else. But Toren saw his face, and it was the face he had had in the first lane on the thirty-fourth day, when the rack came down onto the cinders where nobody was. It was the face of a man who has watched something happen that he did not do.

"Callie," said Dee.

Callie read the jar.

"A half," she said. "Out of the road, for Rook. With the vent." She went down to look. "A quarter of mine, for the lee. Eleven turns." She looked at Toren.

"Two sets," said Toren. "Hardly anything."

"And mine," said Dee. "I'll draw Jab's hands and his, and that's a quarter of mine I'll settle back tonight." She wrote. "So tonight the road jar gives a half for Toren and a quarter for Callie and a quarter for me, and a half's gone out of it on Rook already." She looked up at Rook. "That's a Handful off the road because you went to look. Toren's half is the gaps."

"I'll not go to look again," said Rook.

"You will," said Dee. "You'll go to look again, because it's what you're for. You'll take Callie with the counter when you do, and you'll stand on the line and let her read it from there. And you'll not put a foot off the bed." She shut the book. "That's the rule now. Nobody goes to look alone."

Rook looked at her.

"That's better than mine," he said.

"It's better than both," said Dee.

---

The line came down off the flat in the late afternoon onto the last long slope, and at the bottom of the slope there was the wash.

Toren saw it from the top. The old line went down the slope on its raised bed, straight, with its posts along the north side. At the bottom of the slope the flat opened out into a wide shallow bottom, grey from one side to the other. The line ran out across it on a long embankment, higher than it had been anywhere, a man's height and a half above the bottom. At the far side the embankment went into a cut through a low ridge, a notch with steep walls. And beyond the ridge, a mile off, there was a river, low and dark in its bed. Beside the river there was a square stone building with no roof.

The pump house.

And beyond the river, the fence, running away north along the far bank, a line of posts with wire on them. And the hill, near now, so near that it filled half the west, flat-topped, dark. And over the flat top of it, the place in the sky that was wrong.

"The wash," said Pell.

They camped at the top of the slope, on the bed of the old line, a mile and a half from the embankment, with the wind in their faces and the sun going down behind the hill. Dee would not go nearer to sleep. And in the last of the light, Toren and Callie and Pell went down to look.

They did not go alone. Dee had said so. They went together, down the bed of the old line to the start of the embankment, with Callie in front with the counter held out at the end of her arm, reading it out every ten breaths. Toren walked behind her and counted. Pell walked behind him with his hands in his coat, looking at the embankment the way he looked at a road.

"Fourteen," said Callie, at the foot of the slope where the embankment began.

They stopped there. Dee had said not a step onto the embankment. They stood on the last of the old line where it came off the slope and went out onto the embankment, and looked.

---

The wash came down from the north-west, from the direction of the hill.

It was a wide shallow channel across the bottom, dry in the middle now except for a thin dark trickle, with banks of grey on either side of the trickle. The grey was the drifts. They were not like the drift by the old line in the afternoon. They were twenty drifts, a hundred. They lay all along the bed of the wash and up its banks, long and soft and crusted, grey to the colour of the jars. They were deepest against the north side of the embankment, where the wash came to it. There the wind and the water had piled them up against the foot of the bank in a long grey heap, half as high as the bank itself.

The embankment crossed the wash from east to west. It was about two hundred and fifty paces long, from where they stood to the mouth of the cut in the ridge. The bed of the old line went along the top of it, four paces wide, packed stone, with the iron posts along the north edge of it every twenty paces. On the south side the bank went down steeply into reeds, a marsh of them, grey-brown and dead, standing in black water, going away south to the river. On the north side the bank went down steeply into the drifts.

The wash went under the embankment through a culvert.

Toren could see it from where he stood. It was about three quarters of the way along, a hundred and eighty paces out, where the embankment dipped a little over the middle of the wash. There was a stone arch in the bank there, low, with the dark trickle of the wash going into it on the north side under the drifts. On the south side the arch was not there any more. It had fallen out of the face of the bank into the reeds, and there was a raw place in the south side of the embankment, like a bite. The bed of the old line ran over the top of the arch. There was an iron post on the north parapet of it, set in stone, leaning a little outward over the wash.

Across the top of the embankment, over the arch, there was a line in the stone of the bed. A dark line, crooked, from the north edge nearly to the south.

"That's the crack," said Pell quietly. "The girl was right." He looked at it for a long time. "The south face is old. It fell out years ago, and the arch has settled down onto what fell and packed it. That half's sitting on stone." He pointed with his chin at the north side, where the trickle went in under the drifts. "It's the north side that's going. The wash has been cutting in under it from the drift side all year. The crown's hanging on that side. That's why it's cracked."

"Will it take the cart?"

"It'll take the horse. It'll take people." Pell did not look away from it. "It might take the cart. I'd want both wheels on the south half, over the good stone. And the bed's four paces and the cart's two. With the south wheel on the south edge, the north wheel's still over the crack."

Callie had the counter out in front of her, pointed along the embankment.

"From here," she said. "Fourteen at our feet. I can hear it go up in the gusts." She waited. The wind came across the drifts from the hill and brought the thin grey smoke up off them and across the embankment in a long low haze. The counter went from rain falling to rain on a tin roof, and down again. "Forty, in that. Out there, halfway, it'll be more. On the arch, more. It's the drifts. They're against the north side, and the wind's coming off them across the top." She was quiet. "And at the bottom of the bank on the north side, by the arch, where they're deepest. I don't know. More than I want to say."

"Say it," said Toren.

"A hundred," said Callie. "Maybe. More, if you were in it. If you were down there." She put the counter away. "Nobody goes down there."

They stood and looked at the wash.

"The other ways," said Toren.

Pell told him. North up the wash, round the head of the drifts, toward the hill. It was nearer the fence and hotter all the way, and there was no bed for the cart. South down the side of the embankment into the marsh, and round through the reeds to the river, and up the river to the pump house. There was no bed for the cart there either, and the marsh was water to the waist. Or back.

"Or over," said Pell. "Once. Fast as the frame goes. Two hundred and fifty paces. And the arch."

Toren counted it. He could not help it. He did it the way his father had done a road, with the ifs.

*If the arch takes the cart. If the wind's down. If nothing comes up out of the grey at the lane, the way the girl said things came up at the lamp. If nothing comes out of the reeds.*

"Over," he said.

---

They made the plan at the fire. Dee made it. She said every part of it out loud and Toren wrote it on the back of the paper as she said it, under the day's count. Afterward he read it back, all of it, the way he had read the ledger in the long room.

Two hundred and fifty paces. One pass. At the frame's pace, Dee said, it was four turns of the glass, five with the arch. At forty ticks, fifty, a hundred in a gust, for five turns, it was a little in everybody. A quarter each, maybe, drawn out at the far end, in the cut, where the ridge was between them and the drifts and the counter would be low again. A Handful and a half, near enough, out of the road jar, for all six of them and the horse, if it went right.

"And if it doesn't?" said Rook.

"Then it's more," said Dee. "And we'll know how much when we're there, and not before."

The order.

Callie in front on the north edge, the windward side, on the stone of the bed, with the hook. She would not hold the lane the whole way. She could not. She would put it up in the gusts, when the grey came across, and let it down between them, and say *lane* and *down* so everybody knew. Behind the lane, in the lee, Jab at the cart's tail, with the frame on his back, and Dee beside him, on the north side of the cart. Dee would have the glass and the road jar out of its rack, in her arms, so it was to hand.

Pell at Duchess's head, on the south side, with his knife on his belt where he had shown Toren.

The cart in the middle of the bed. The four Flasks in the rack behind the driver's bench. The road jar was coming out of it into Dee's arms, so it would be three, the home ones. The lead box behind them, empty, on its straw. The vent jars in the straw at the tail, apart from everything, five of them now.

Rook at the tail on the south side. His job was the reeds. If anything came out of the marsh, it would come on the dark side, away from the lane and the light, and he would light his blade and fence it off the cart. Half a turn. Ten minutes. No more, and Dee would count it.

And Toren on the south side of the cart by the north wheel's axle, under, on the inside. At the arch, he would set under the north axle from the good stone on the south half of the crown, slanting across, and hold the cart while the north wheel went over the crack. Then *step*, to the south wheel, and set again if it wanted it.

"Callie's got the counter," said Dee. "She'll say the ticks every ten breaths while she can. When she can't, I'll take it off her hip."

"What's red?" said Pell.

"The line on the window," said Callie. "A hundred and fifty, near enough. When it's past that it doesn't tick. It buzzes." She touched the bag. "The needle sticks at the top of the red. He said. Tap it once. Not twice."

"Once," said Toren, and wrote it.

"Exits," said Dee.

"Back," said Toren. He had it on the paper. "East. Wherever we are, back is back the way we came, to the foot of the slope, where it's fourteen." He looked at the plan. "Until the arch. After the arch, forward's shorter. Seventy paces to the cut."

"Not the reeds," said Pell. "Nobody goes in the reeds. There's water in there to your chest."

"And not the drifts," said Callie.

"Nobody goes in the drifts," said Dee.

Toren read it all back to them, from the top. They listened. When he had finished, nobody said anything for a while. Duchess shifted at the wheel.

"And the ash," said Toren. "Before we go. Say it."

Callie said it.

"Road, three and a quarter. After tonight's settling. In Miss Wren's arms tomorrow." She looked at Jab. "Inside, sixty-four. On the frame. Not touched." She looked at the cart. "Home, twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." She was quiet a moment. "Settled, a Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in Miss Wren." And then: "We've spent four and three quarters of the road in five days. Two and a quarter on the man in the ditch. A Handful today because of the drift. The rest on learning." She did not look at Rook. "Not a grain on a hound."

"Not a grain on a hound," said Dee. "Write that, Toren. I'd like it written somewhere that we got to the wash with three and a quarter in the road jar and never spent a grain on a hound."

He wrote it.

---

He settled his half at the fire after the others had lain down.

It took him a long time. Longer than the washout's quarter. He sat with his palm flat on the square of paper on his knee and did not haul, and the ash came up into his palm at the rate it came, cold and slow, and went down. He sat with it. He felt it lie at the bottom of him and warm, and join the half that was there, until the stone was a whole Handful again. Warm and faint, a long way down.

The first one full. The night before.

He looked at the paper when he had finished. At the plan, and under it the count.

*45th. The top of the slope above the wash. Out: 6. In: 6. The drift by the line: R. went to look. First time all together: lane, set, draw. It worked the second time. Road, 3¼.*

He wanted to write something else. He did not know what. He sat with the pencil over the paper and thought about the arch, and the crack in the crown, and the grey heaped against the north side of the bank half as high as the bank itself.

He thought about his father, who had never once priced the way home. Behind them, east, was a road they had come along for five days. It was the only way home they knew. In the morning they would go out along the embankment, over the arch, and it would be behind them.

He did not write anything else. He folded the paper and put it in his coat with the plate. He lay down by Jab and counted them.

Pell. Dee. Rook, longest. Jab. Callie, with the hook on the ground beside her and her hand on it. Duchess at the wheel with her ears forward, not eating, looking west.

Six. And a horse.

---

In the morning the wind had dropped.

That was the first thing Toren knew. He woke at the fourth hour when Dee woke, because she woke, and lay on the canvas and listened, and there was nothing. No wind. The frost had come down hard in the stillness, and the grass on the bed of the old line was white and stiff, and the smoke from the fire went straight up.

"That's luck," said Pell, at the fire. "Don't anybody say it out loud."

They said the rules at first light, on the bed of the old line at the top of the slope, with the hill in front of them. It was very near now, dark against a sky going grey. The place over the flat top of it was darker than the rest of the sky, and round its edge was that light the colour of the bottom of a pond. Nobody looked at it for long.

Dee measured their floors. Callie's own and the stone, where they were. Toren's own, back from the night on the rise, and the stone full. Jab's hand and a bit, his floor a finger. Rook's.

She held Rook's wrist longer than the others.

"Half a turn," she said.

"Half a turn," said Rook.

Then they went down the slope in the order Dee had said, and at the foot of it, where the embankment began, Callie held out the counter.

"Twelve," she said. "No wind. It's lower than last night."

"Go now," said Dee. "While it's down."

And they went out onto the embankment.

---

Toren walked on the south side of the cart with his left arm in its cuff against his chest and his eyes on the north wheel.

He knew where everybody was. He knew it without looking, the way he had known it in the dark lane. Callie in front on the north edge with the hook, not rocking it yet, walking with the curve an inch off the stone. Pell at Duchess's head on the south side, a pace in front of him, with his hand on her neck. Duchess walking. The cart. Dee and Jab behind it on the north side, at the tailboard, Dee with the road jar in the crook of her arm and the glass in her other hand. Rook at the tail on the south side, behind Toren, walking his careful straight walk with his eyes on the reeds.

Six. And a horse.

The drifts lay against the north side of the bank below Callie, grey and crusted and still. The reeds stood on the south side below him, grey-brown, dead, still, in black water. Nothing moved anywhere. There was no wind. The smoke that had come off the drifts all yesterday was not coming.

"Twenty," said Callie. "Ten breaths."

Fifty paces. A post. Another.

"Twenty-six."

A hundred paces. The arch was ahead, eighty paces on, with the dark line of the crack across the bed over it and the iron post on the north parapet leaning out over the wash.

"Thirty."

And the wind came.

It came off the hill all at once, the way wind does after a still night. It came across the wash from the west-north-west, low, and it took the crust off the drifts below Callie as if somebody had blown on a plate of flour. The grey came up off them in a long low smoke and across the top of the embankment. Across Callie, across Duchess's head, across the cart.

"Lane," said Callie.

She put the back of the hook's curve down on the stone of the north edge and rocked it forward, and the lee stood up off it. It was pale, shoulder high. The grey smoke hit it and hissed and went along it and round the end of it. Toren saw it go round the end, in front of Duchess, and across Pell.

"Sixty," said Callie. "Ten breaths. In the lee. Ninety outside it."

"Keep walking," said Dee.

They kept walking.

A hundred and twenty paces. The arch was sixty paces on. The grey was coming across in waves, and Callie was walking the lee along the north edge at the frame's pace with her eyes on Jab and Dee behind her. It leaned back over them. Toren could see it lean. He was on the other side of the cart from it, and the grey was coming over the top of the cart onto him, fine, and he put his sleeve over his mouth.

Then Callie said, "Something," and her voice was different.

Toren looked past Duchess's head.

The drift at the foot of the bank below Callie was moving.

It was not the wind. The wind was taking the top off it in a smoke. This was underneath. The crust of the drift heaved, twenty paces ahead of Callie and below her, at the foot of the bank. It heaved up in a long hump the way the old line's bed had heaved in the frost. The crust cracked along the top of the hump. Something came up out of it.

It was the colour of an old wasp nest. It was the colour of the grey, because it was covered in it, and it came up out of the drift the way husks came up out of the ditch water at the Sag. All at once, as if it had been lying there waiting, it stood. It had no face. It turned toward the lee.

"Husk," said Callie.

Another came up behind it. And a third, further along, by the arch.

"They're coming at the lane," said Callie. Her voice was flat and loud. "At the Ward. It's worked. They go for anything worked. Rook—"

They came up the bank.

---

Rook came forward.

He came up the south side of the cart past Toren, at more than his careful walk. He went round Duchess's head and across the bed to the north edge in front of Callie. He was lighting his blade as he went. Toren saw it come on his fist, short and plain, and the light come along the edge of it, white. He met the first husk at the top of the bank, where it came up over the lip onto the stones of the bed, and put the blade through it.

It came apart.

It came apart the way husks did, all at once, into grey. And the grey did not fall. The wind had it. It went up off the lip of the bank in a burst, the whole of a husk's worth of loose grey, and the wind took it and brought it across the top of the embankment in a cloud, over Rook and Callie and Duchess's head, and over the cart. Over Toren.

"*Don't*—" said Dee, from the tailboard. "Not in the wind—"

The counter on Callie's hip went from rain on a tin roof to something faster.

"Ninety," said Callie. "In the lee. A hundred and twenty outside." She did not stop walking. She did not let the lee down. "Rook, don't kill them on the lip. The wind's bringing it over—"

The second husk came up over the lip. Rook met it, because there was nothing else to do with it, and it came apart, and the wind brought it over them.

The cart came to the arch.

---

Toren saw the north wheel go onto the crown. He saw the dark line of the crack in the stone of the bed under it. He was already moving.

He came round under the cart on the inside, on the south half of the crown, the good half, where Pell had said the stone was sitting on the part that was still there. He did not look at the husks or the grey. He looked at the ground, the way Pell had said on the forty-fifth. *You'll not always have her.* The south half of the crown. The dressed stone of the arch under the bed, sitting on its springing. A flat grey slab with its near edge sitting on stone.

He put the foot of the strut down on it, slanting outward under the cart, toward the north axle.

"Set," he said.

The north wheel came to the crack.

And the crown went.

It did not go all at once. It went the way the Sag had gone on the twenty-fifth day, a sound first, under his feet, a deep grinding like a mill wheel turning over. Then the north half of the bed over the arch dropped. It dropped a hand's width, and then another, away from the crack. The north wheel went down with it, into the gap, and the whole weight of the cart came over onto the north side.

It came onto the strut.

He was soft. He was soft behind it the way he had been at seven paces, and at the washout, and on thirty gaps on the old line. He felt the weight arrive. It was more than any sack. It was more than the rack or the box in the pen. It was a loaded cart going over into a hole, with four Flasks and a lead box and everything on it. It came down the strut into the slab on the good half of the crown, and into the stone under it. It did not come up into him. His knees gave, and took the last of it, and the cart stopped.

It stopped with the north wheel down in the broken crown to its hub, and the cart leaning out north over the lip of the bank, over the wash, over the drifts, on a line of light that ran from the south half of the arch to Toren's knee.

"Holding," said Toren.

In front of him, Duchess screamed.

# Chapter 30 — Jab: The Fence's Ash

Jab heard the horse before he understood what had happened to the cart.

He was at the tailboard on the north side with his hand flat on the edge of it, the way he walked now, and the lee was over him. He could feel it over him. He could not see it well. It was pale and it leaned, and the grey smoke from the drifts hissed along it and went round the end of it in front of Duchess. Dee was beside him with the road jar in the crook of her arm and the glass in her other hand. He had been watching the husks come up the bank at the lane and Rook going across to meet them, and the grey going up off the lip where Rook's blade went through them. He had been thinking *don't, not in the wind*, the same as Dee, a breath after she said it.

Then the ground under the cart made the sound the Sag had made.

The tailboard went out from under his hand. It did not go far. It tipped up and away from him, north, as the north side of the cart went down. He heard the jars on his own back ring against each other in their canvas and felt the frame shift on his hips. He put his feet wide on the stone and did not fall.

The cart stopped.

It stopped with a jolt, leaning out over the north side of the bank at a slant he had never seen a cart stand at. The north wheel was down in the bed to its hub, in a hole where the stone over the arch had been. The south wheel was up off the ground. And under the middle of it, coming up from the south half of the crown at a long slant to the axle, there was a line of pale light, thin and hard. Toren was at the bottom end of it on one knee on the stone, with his left arm against his chest and his face white.

"Holding," said Toren.

And Duchess screamed.

---

She was in the shafts. That was what Jab understood, a breath too late. The cart had gone over north and the shafts had gone with it, and the shafts were fixed to her. She was being pulled sideways across the bed by the whole leaning weight of the cart, toward the hole and the lip of the bank and the drop into the wash. Her hooves were going on the stone. She was screaming the way he had heard a horse scream once, on the road north of the valley, when it went through the ice of a ford.

And on the south side, in the reeds, something had answered her.

Jab saw it come out of the marsh. It came out of the grey-brown dead reeds at the foot of the south bank, low and fast, and it was a hound. Another came behind it. They came up the south bank in two long bounds toward the cart. Toward the dark under the cart where Toren was kneeling with his strut. Toward the south side, where there was no light and nobody, because Rook was on the north edge with his blade in a husk.

"*Rook*," said Dee.

Rook turned.

He was on the north edge at the lip with his blade lit and the grey of the second husk still going up round him in the wind. He looked across the bed at the south side and saw the hounds, and he came back. He came across the bed behind Duchess and past the front of the leaning cart, at a run, which he had not done since the Sag. His blade was lit on his fist. He put himself between the hounds and the dark under the cart.

The first hound stopped as if it had hit a rope. It stopped at his light. The second went round.

And Pell cut the traces.

---

Jab saw him do it. He saw it very clearly, the way he had seen every part of the pen afterward. He saw it in order.

Pell had not let go of Duchess's head. He had been pulled across the bed with her, holding the halter, with his boots sliding on the stone, and he was on the north side of her now, at the front of the leaning cart, near the crack. He had his knife out. He had had it out, Jab thought, since the stone made its sound. He put his hand on the harness a hand's width behind the hames, where he had shown Toren on the rise, and cut. One side. He reached under her neck for the other side, and cut.

Duchess came out of the shafts.

She came out of them all at once, the way a horse comes out of anything that has been holding her, and she went. She went east. Back the way they had come, along the bed of the embankment, past the tail of the cart and past Jab. Her hooves came down on the stone a yard from his boots. Her eyes were white and her ears were flat, and she was gone along the embankment toward the foot of the slope before he had turned his head.

And Pell was standing where she had been.

He was standing on the north half of the crown, by the crack, where the horse had pulled him. He had the knife in his hand. The north half of the bed over the arch had dropped a hand's width already, under the wheel. It was sitting on nothing much, the way Pell himself had said on the evening before.

It went under him.

It did not go far. It went the way a step goes when you put your weight on the edge of it and the edge is rotten. The stone of the bed tilted under Pell's boots toward the lip of the bank. He went with it. He went sideways off the north edge of the embankment, with his arms out and the knife going away from him, and down.

Down the north bank. Into the drift.

"*Pell*," said Toren, from under the cart. He did not move. He could not move.

Jab was already going.

---

He did not decide. He went the way he had gone off the made road into the fog after the day holder, because there was somebody down there, and he could feel them.

He went over the north edge of the bed past the tail of the cart, out of the lee. He felt the lee go off him. The grey smoke off the drifts hit him in the face like a hand, fine and dry. He went down the bank on his heels with the frame on his back and the eight jars in it. The bank was steep, and it was not stone. It was earth and grey, loose, and it went out from under his heels. He slid, and did not fall. He came down the last of it on his heels and one hand, into the drift at the foot, up to his knees.

It was soft. It was like walking into flour. It came up round his knees, grey and fine, and a little of it went up into the air where his boots went in.

Pell was lying in it on his side, three paces off, at the foot of the bank under the arch. He was lying half in the drift and half on the stones of the arch's wing wall where it came out of the bank. His right leg was under him wrong. His face was grey. It was not the grey of sickness. It was the grey of the drift, on his cheek and in his hair and round his mouth, where he had gone into it face first. He was coughing.

"Don't," said Jab. "Don't wipe your face."

Pell had his hand up to his mouth. He stopped with it there.

"My mouth's full of it," he said.

"Spit," said Jab. "Don't wipe. Spit." He got to him through the drift. "What's your leg?"

"The ankle." Pell spat. He spat again, grey. "On the stones. I heard it." He did not sound frightened. He sounded like a man reading a distance. "I heard it go."

Jab knelt beside him in the drift.

He did not have to put his hand anywhere to know. He could feel the ankle from where he knelt, the way he could feel a fire he was not looking at. It was a bad wrongness, fixed and bright, like the technician's hand. Something broken that was broken now and would be broken until somebody set it. He could not do anything about it. It was not busy.

And under it, all through, he could feel the other thing.

It was not much yet. It was new. It was in Pell's mouth and throat and the top of his chest, where he had breathed the drift going into it. It was on his face and his hair and his hands. It was going into him the way water goes into a boot, a little and a little. It was busy.

"Callie," said Jab, not loud. "Ticks. Here."

---

Callie was at the lip of the bank above them.

He could see her if he looked up. She was on the north edge of the bed with the hook, a pace from where Pell had gone over, and she had seen him go, and she had turned the hook round. She was trying to bring the lee down the bank.

He saw her try. She put the back of the curve on the lip of the bank where the earth went down, and rocked it forward, over the edge, onto the slope. She tried to walk it down the bank toward them the way she walked it along the stone. And the curve came down on the loose earth and the grey and skidded. It did not rock. It slid, sideways, a hand's width, a foot. There was no part of it that was still on the ground. There was only a part of it that was sliding.

The lee went out.

She tried it again. It went out again. He saw her face.

"It won't," she said. "It's loose. It's all loose. There's no still."

"Callie," said Dee. "The counter. Leave the lane."

Callie held the counter out at the end of her arm, over the lip, down toward them.

"From here," she said. "A hundred and ten. At the lip." Her voice was flat. "Down there I don't know. More. You're in it."

"Nobody else comes down," said Dee.

She was on her belly on the lip of the bank. Jab had not seen her get there. She was lying on the stone of the bed at the north edge with the road jar in her hands and the glass on the stone beside her. She looked down at him with her face white and absolutely calm, the way it had been in the pen.

"Jab," she said. "Stand up. Put your back to the bank."

He stood up in the drift with his back to the bank. The frame on his back came against the earth of the slope, and the top row of jars in the rack was a foot below the lip, below Dee's face.

"Put your right hand on his chest. Lean. Put your left hand up."

He leaned over Pell, with his back against the bank and his right hand flat on Pell's chest, and put his left hand up over his shoulder. Dee reached down from the lip at the full length of her arm and put the mouth of the road jar against his palm.

"Clean in," she said. "That's three and a quarter. That's all the road's got. Say when it's going."

---

He drew.

It was not the man in the ditch. It was not the pen. It was a man lying in a heap of the thing that was hurting him, breathing it, with a broken ankle and his face grey with it, and nobody over them. No lee, no lamp, nothing. It was the fog on the slab again, only he was not alone, and it was not his reserve on the other end. It was the jar.

"Busy," he said.

The clean cold came in at his left hand from the jar and went through him and out of his right, and paid. The busy thing in Pell's throat and chest came loose, the part nearest his hand, and came up into his palm like hot sand. He held it high. He did not let it into his wrist.

Ten breaths.

He could not vent into a jar. The vent jars were in the straw at the tail of a cart that was hanging off the edge of the bank on a line of light. He did what Dee had said on the first day of the old line. He turned his right hand over and put it flat on the drift beside them, downwind of Pell, where the ground was already the worst ground there was. He let it go out of him. Into the grey. Into nobody.

Three breaths.

"Back on," said Dee, from above.

He went back on.

Above him he could hear the rest of it. He could not see most of it with his back to the bank, but he could hear it. Rook's voice, on the south side, not words. A hound making the sound hounds made. Toren, under the cart, saying nothing, which meant the strut was still holding. Callie at the lip saying *a hundred and ten* and then *a hundred and twenty* in the same flat voice. The wind, coming across the drifts, bringing the grey.

"Busy," said Jab, and drew.

---

The third husk came along the foot of the bank.

He felt it before he saw it. He did not feel it the way he felt people. He felt it the way you feel somebody come into a room behind you. There was a thing that was not a person in the drift to his left, moving, and it was coming at him.

At him. Not at Pell. At the thing he was doing with his hands. It was worked. The drawing was worked, the way the lee was, and the strut, and Rook's blade. They went at anything worked.

He turned his head. It was coming along the foot of the bank toward them through the drift, grey on grey, low, with no face. It came from the arch, where the third one had come up, and it was coming fast.

He let the knife come on his other fist. He could not. His left hand was on the jar. He took it off the jar.

"*Jab*—"

He let the knife come on his left fist, dark, heavy in the spine, fat in the belly, and lit the last inch of it, the hook. He put the hook into the husk as it came at him, low, across the drift, the way he would have cut a rope.

It came apart.

It came apart into grey the way they did, all at once, a husk's worth of loose grey. And it went up. It went up off the drift in a burst in front of his face, fine and dry, and the wind took it and brought it back over him and over Pell. It came down on them like flour sifted over a table.

The counter above him on Callie's hip went faster. He heard it go.

"A hundred and forty," said Callie. "At the lip. That's near the red." And then, quieter, not flat: "Jab. That was on you."

"I know."

"Don't kill them down there," said Dee. "Every one you kill down there goes on him."

"I *know*." He let the knife go. He put his left hand back up for the jar, and it was there, against his palm. "It was coming at the hands."

"They'll all come at the hands," said Dee. "Draw."

He drew.

---

On the south side, above him, Rook made a sound.

It was not a word. It was the sound a man makes when something hits him from behind. It was short and deep and it stopped. Jab heard a hound snarl, close, under the cart. He heard something heavy go down on the stone of the bed. Then he heard Rook's blade go through something, the flat quick sound of it, and he did not hear the hound any more.

"Elias," said Dee.

She did not look round. She was on her belly on the lip with her arm down to Jab's hand and the jar against his palm. She could not look round.

"Elias. Say it."

"The second one," said Rook. His voice was different. "Went round. Under the cart. In the dark. I was on the first." He stopped. "It's dead. They're both dead."

"Say the rest."

"My back," said Rook.

Nobody said anything.

"It hit my back," said Rook. "High. Where it was. I felt it go." He was quiet. "It's bleeding. I can feel it going down."

"How long have you been lit?" said Dee.

"I don't know."

"I do. Twelve turns of the glass. Since the first husk." Dee still did not look round. "You're two over. You're off, Elias. Put the blade out and sit down on the stone where you are, on the south side, and put your hand on the cart wheel and don't move."

"There's—"

"You're off," said Dee. "That's the Director's word, and it's mine."

For a long moment Jab heard nothing at all from the south side of the cart.

Then he heard Rook's blade go out. He knew the sound of it going out, the small flat nothing it made. He heard Rook sit down on the stone, not in two movements. In one, heavily, the way a sack goes down.

---

The jar was going.

Jab felt it go. Not the way he had felt it in the pen, all at once, in the middle of a draw, with nothing to tell him. He was watching it this time. He had been watching it since the first draw, the way Dee had made him watch it at the long table two hundred times. Not only Pell's chest under his right hand. The jar against his left. He could feel the clean cold coming into his palm, and he could feel that there was less of it behind the mouth of the jar every draw. It was a pour that has got to the bottom of the jug and is coming thin.

He did not wait for it to stop.

"Jar," he said.

"That's the road," said Dee. "That's all of it. There's nothing else in it."

"Then the frame."

Dee did not say anything for a moment.

"That's the fence's," she said. "The frame's the fence's ash. Sixty-four. If I open one of those here, it comes off the forty-eight. The work at the fence gets shorter." She did not take her eyes off his face. "Callie's sum. You were there when she said it. It comes off the work, and the line doesn't move."

"I know," said Jab.

He looked at Pell.

Pell was lying on his side in the drift with his face grey and his eyes open, looking up at Jab. He was not coughing now. He was breathing, short and shallow, with his teeth together, the way a man breathes past a broken bone. Under Jab's right hand, in his chest, the busy thing was less than it had been. It was not gone. The grey from the husk had come down on him and put more of it back, on his face and in his mouth. It was going in again, a little and a little.

He thought about the fence. About two hours in the rooms at the bottom of a yellow stair, and an hour to get there, and an hour to get back out. He thought about forty-eight Handfuls to work with and every one of them priced, for five people inside and the venting and whatever they met. He thought about how many of those forty-eight he was about to take out of the fence and put into a man lying in a drift in the wash, before anybody had even seen the fence.

He thought about Pell outside the fence at the pump house with the lamp and the three Flasks, writing down who went in and who came out. Pell, who had put the tailboard down on the first morning so Jab would not have to take the frame off.

"Open it," said Jab. "I'm saying it. It's mine to say."

Dee looked at him a moment longer.

"It's yours," she said. "I'm writing it."

She put the empty road jar down on the stone beside her. Then she reached down over the lip of the bank, past his shoulder, to the rack on his back. He felt her hand on the top row. He felt her take one of the jars by its neck and lift it out of its canvas socket, and heard the stopper come out with a small sound.

She put it against his palm.

It was cold. It was very cold and very heavy, heavier than the road jar had been. It was full, and clean, and nobody was in it. It was eight Handfuls that had been for the stair.

"Clean in," said Dee.

---

He drew.

He drew for Pell's chest and throat, and then his face. The grey that had come down from the husk was on his face and in his hair, and some of it had gone in. He held it high. He vented into the drift downwind. He went back on.

On the fourth draw his palm went warm.

It was not the hot sand of the busy thing coming up. It was under that. It was in the palm itself. It was the way a pan handle goes warm after the pan has been on the stove a while, from the inside, a little. He knew what it was. He had watched it happen to Rook's hands in the pen, and heard Callie say *sixty, after he's vented, it should be ten.* He had felt Dee take it out of the creases of his own palms afterward. *You'd have carried that home.*

"Vent," he said.

He said it himself. Dee had not said it. He lifted his hand off Pell's chest and put it flat on the drift and held it there, not for three breaths. For five. For eight. He felt the warm go out of the palm into the ground, slowly, not all of it.

"I'm warm," he said. "My palm. After venting."

"How warm?"

"Like a pan. A bit."

"Then you're off for a turn," said Dee. She said it at once. "Vent, and rest, and I'll—"

She stopped.

He knew why she stopped. He knew before she did. There was nobody. It was work, reserve, watch, and turn it every twenty minutes, and the one who worked became the watch, and the reserve worked. Rook was the reserve, and Rook was sitting on the south side of the bed with his hand on the cart wheel and his back bleeding, and he was off. Dee was the watch. If Dee came down the bank into the drift, there was nobody on the lip who had drawn anything. There would be nobody clean enough to take the warm out of the one who had worked.

"I'm coming down," said Dee.

"No," said Jab.

She looked at him.

"No," said Jab again. "You're the watch. You come down, there's nobody clean. Then there's nobody to draw me, or you, or him." He did not look away from her. "I'll vent longer. Ten breaths. Then I'll go back on, and I'll say if it's worse."

Dee's face did not move.

"Ten," she said. "And you say."

He vented for ten breaths with his hand flat on the grey. The warm in his palm went down. It did not go all the way down.

"Back on," he said, before she could.

---

He did not feel the fourth one come.

He had his attention down under his right palm, in Pell's chest. He had the jar against his left and his back against the bank, and the busy thing coming up into his hand, and his eyes shut. He was not listening for anything in the drift. He was listening to Pell.

It came up out of the drift behind him on his right.

He did not know it was there until Callie said his name. She said it loud, from the lip, not flat, not at all flat. Then the husk hit him.

It hit him from the side. It hit him low, at the hip, with its whole weight, the way the one had hit Toren in the door at the knee on the twenty-fifth. It knocked him off his feet in the drift, and he went over.

He went forward. He did not know how he knew to. Toren had told him what Wyck said to him on the step in the dark, and he had watched Toren do it in the stream at the washout, going down. *Fall like a sack.* He went forward over Pell like a sack of barley going off a cart. He did not put his hands out. His right hand was on Pell's chest and his left had the jar, and he kept them there, and went down over Pell with his whole weight and the frame on his back. He came down on his knees and his chest in the drift with Pell under him.

The frame came down after him.

It came over his shoulders with the weight of eight jars in it. The rack swung forward on the belt and the chest band. The top row, where the jars leaned on the curved ash rail, came down with it. He felt the rail take them. He felt the rail stop them from going on over his head into the drift. It was the rail his brother's bad leg had ridden on for eighteen days, lashed tight and short.

And he heard one of them break.

It was the jar at the end of the top row. The one nearest the rail's end. It came down against the ash of the rail and he heard it go, a small hard sound like a cup dropped on a stone floor.

And then he felt it.

He felt it before he understood it. It was cold. It was very cold, all at once, on the back of his neck and between his shoulders, under his coat, down his back. It was fine and dry and heavy, and it was running down him the way water runs down you out of a bucket tipped over your head. It was clean. It was the clean cold that came into his left palm out of a jar, the cold with nobody in it. It was going down his back, over his coat and under it and out at his belt, and into the drift.

Into the grey. Into the worst ground there was.

It was gone before he could do anything at all.

---

Above him, the husk that had hit him was coming back.

He could feel it. He was lying over Pell in the drift with the frame on his back and his hands still where they had been. He could feel it turning in the drift beside them, coming at the hands again.

There was a sound over him like cloth tearing.

It was Callie. He knew it without looking. It was the sound her edge made coming off the curve of the hook, the long grey sweep of it, cold and colourless. She had let the lane go, if she had had it up. She had let go of whatever she was holding on the lip and put the edge on the curve on a count of two, and reached down over the edge of the bank with the whole four feet of conduit and a foot of curve and the edge on the end of it. He felt it go past over his back, over the frame, close. He felt the husk come apart beside them.

And the grey came down on them again.

It came down over his back and his neck and Pell's face under him, a husk's worth of it, sifting.

He lay over Pell in the drift with his face in the grey and did not move. He was not hurt. He did not think he was hurt. He could feel Pell breathing under him, short and shallow, through his teeth. He could feel the jar still in his left hand, cold and heavy and open. He could feel the busy thing under his right, in Pell's chest, going in again, a little and a little, all the way back to where he had started.

He could feel where the clean cold had gone down his back. There was nothing there now. The clean was gone into the drift, and the drift was the drift.

Above him, on Callie's hip, the counter had stopped ticking.

It had not stopped. It had gone past ticking. The ticks had run together into one sound, a thin high buzz, like a wasp shut in a jar.

# Chapter 31 — Callie: Who Is Inside

The needle was stuck at the top of the red.

Callie saw it through the little window in the side of the counter, with the buzz coming out of the grille against her hip like a wasp in a jar. It was lying hard over against the right-hand end, past the red mark, pinned there, not moving. It had not moved since the fourth husk came apart over Jab's back.

*It sticks at the top of the red. If it sticks, tap the glass with your nail. Once. Not twice.*

The old woman from the technician's shed had said it on the thirty-ninth, with the counter held against her chest like a sleeping animal. *He says you'll know when it's lying.*

Callie tapped the glass once with her nail.

The needle jumped. It came off the pin a hair's width, and trembled there, and went back. It lay against the right-hand end past the red mark and stayed, and the buzz did not change.

It was not lying.

"Red," said Callie.

She said it loud, the way the technician had asked her to on the bench by the yellow line. It went out across the top of the embankment and over the wash and came back off nothing.

"Red," she said again. "It's true. I tapped it. And there's a body trapped."

---

Miss Wren was on her belly on the stone of the bed at the north edge, a pace from Callie, with her arm still hanging down over the lip toward Jab.

She took her arm back. She got up onto her knees. She did it at exactly the speed it needed and no faster. Callie watched her face and saw her do the sum. Not the kind Callie did. The kind Pell did, with a price on every line.

"Red, and a body trapped," said Miss Wren. "Everybody not needed to get that body out goes, at once. The fewest who can do it stay, and I say their names." She did not raise her voice. "Jab. He's on Pell, and he's got the jar, and he can draw. He stays."

Down in the drift, face down over Pell, Jab said something that might have been *yes*.

"Toren. The cart's over them. If it comes down, it comes down on both of them. He stays."

Under the leaning cart, on one knee on the south half of the crown with his strut going up into the axle, Toren said, "Staying."

Miss Wren looked at Callie.

"You can't get it down the bank," she said. "I watched you try. Twice."

"Not down the bank," said Callie.

She had not known she was going to say it until it was out. But she had been looking at the bank while Jab drew, and at the lip, and at the bed of the old line where she stood. She had been looking at them the way she looked at anything. The bank was loose. There was no still in it. The lip was not loose. The lip was the edge of the bed, and the bed was stone, packed hard seventy years ago by people who wanted it to stay. She was standing on it.

"Along the top," she said. "On the stone. The curve on the lip. And lean it." She put her hand out flat and tipped it, like the roof of a lean-to. "Out over the edge. Down over them. I don't have to go down to them. It can lean down."

Miss Wren looked at her for as long as it took to breathe in.

"Callie," she said. "The lane. She stays."

Callie heard her own name said out loud by the watch, and it went into her like cold water.

"Elias," said Miss Wren. "Out. With me. Now."

---

Rook was sitting on the stone on the south side of the bed, by the tail of the cart, with his hand on the high wheel. He had not moved since he sat down. There was a dark patch on the back of his coat, high, between his shoulders, the size of a hand and getting bigger.

"Dee," he said.

"You're not named," said Miss Wren.

"I can—"

"You're not named," said Miss Wren again. She was on her feet. She had the glass in her hand and the empty road jar under her arm. "You're off, you're bleeding, and there's grey in your back where the hound opened it. If you stay you're a second body trapped, and I'll have to name somebody to get you out." She put her hand out to him, down, flat. "Up. With me. West, to the cut. It's seventy paces. The counter's under the red at the cut."

Rook looked at her hand for a moment.

Then he took it. He came up off the stone with his other hand on the wheel, in two movements, and stood, grey in the face. He did not look at Callie or at Toren or at the edge of the bank where Jab was. He looked at Miss Wren.

"West," he said.

"West," said Miss Wren. She turned her head to Callie without letting go of him. "I'll turn the glass at the cut. I'll call every turn so you can hear it. And I'll say his name, and his, and yours, every time, so you know you're named." She looked down over the lip one last time at Jab. "Get him out, and come out. Not a breath longer."

They went. They went west along the south edge of the bed, past the front of the leaning cart and past Toren on his knee under it, Rook with one hand on Miss Wren's shoulder and one on his own side. They went at Rook's pace, which was slow, along the top of the embankment toward the mouth of the cut, seventy paces off. There the ridge stood up on both sides of the old line and the drifts stopped.

Callie watched them go for as long as it took to count them. Two.

Then she turned round to the lip.

She was standing on the north edge of the bed at the lip of the bank, a pace east of the arch, where Pell had gone over. Below her the bank went down steep and loose, earth and grey, the height of a man and a half. At the foot of it, in the drift, Jab was getting up onto his knees over Pell. The frame was still on his back. There was an empty socket at the end of the top row of the rack, and broken glass in it, and a long pale stain down the back of his coat from the collar to the belt. That was where the clean ash had gone down him.

Pell was lying on his side in the drift under him with his face grey and his eyes open. His right foot was turned the wrong way on the wing wall of the arch.

Behind Callie, across the bed, the cart leaned out over her. It stood on its north wheel in the broken crown, tilted toward the lip, the whole long weight of it, with Toren's strut going up into the axle from the south side like a prop under a wall. The wind came across the drifts from the hill and brought the grey up off them and across the top of the embankment, across Callie and the cart and Toren. It went over the lip of the bank and down, onto Jab and Pell.

She did not look at the hook. She looked at Jab's shoulders, and the top of Pell's head, and the back of Jab's coat with the pale stain on it. She held them there, in her eyes, the way Rook had told her on the forty-fourth. *Watch who's inside.*

She held it there, still, on the lip. She reached for the stone at the bottom of the well, and let it go, and put the Ward in the bottom of the curve, where it touched.

She did not let it stand straight. She let it lean. She looked at Jab and Pell at the foot of the bank and let it lean out from the lip, over the edge, down, the way the roof of a lean-to leans off a wall. It went out over the loose earth of the bank, pale, like a sheet of frost standing out over nothing. It went down and over them. It stopped a foot above Jab's back. It was the shape of where they were, and nowhere else.

The grey came across the top of the embankment on the wind and hit it and hissed and went down the outside of it into the drift beyond them.

It did not get to them.

"Lane," said Callie.

Below her, Jab looked up.

He looked at the pale thing leaning over him like a roof, and at her on the lip above it with the hook, and his face did something she had never seen it do. Then he put his right hand back on Pell's chest, and put his left hand with the open jar against his own palm, and bent his head.

"Busy," said Jab.

---

It cost less than she had feared, standing still.

She felt that at once. She was not rocking the curve. It was sitting on the stone of the lip and not moving, and the Ward in the bottom of it was the Ward in a hook held still across a door. That was the cheapest thing she knew how to do. The lee leaning out over the bank off it was thinner and longer than a hook across a door, and she could feel it drawing on the stone. But not the way the moving lee drew, on the old line. Not the way the lee along the whole cart had drawn, the four steps and the nosebleed.

She went down to look, once, fast. The stone was less than half of what she had settled the night before. The gusts had taken it, all the way along the embankment, and the two tries down the bank, and the edge for the husk over Jab's back. There was less than half left.

Enough for now. Not enough for long.

"Toren," she said, without turning round. "I've got them. It's up. How long have you got?"

"Not long," said Toren.

His voice came from behind her and under the cart, tight.

"It's moving," he said. "The crown. Under the foot. It's still going." She heard him breathe. "I can feel it go under the strut. A little. Every time the wind comes."

Callie turned her head. She did not turn her body. She kept the hook on the lip and her eyes going back to Jab's shoulders every breath. But she turned her head and looked across the bed at the south half of the crown where Toren knelt.

She read it.

It was the part of the bed over the arch, where the stone of the old line sat on the crown. The crack ran across it from the north edge nearly to the south. North of the crack, the bed had dropped, and the north wheel was down in it. South of the crack, where Toren was, the bed had not dropped. But the slab under his strut's foot, a flat grey piece of the packed bed, was sitting on something that was moving. She could see it. There was a line of fresh bright dirt along its edge where it had shifted in the last breaths, a hair's width, and was not sitting where it had sat.

It was sitting on the bed. And the bed was sitting on the arch. And the arch was going.

But the arch itself was not the bed.

She looked past the slab, to the left of Toren's boot. Under the packed stone of the bed, where the edge had broken away when the north half dropped, there was a stone showing that was not the grey rubble of the bed. It was paler, and square, and dressed. It had a straight edge somebody had cut with a chisel a long time ago. It was a stone of the arch itself. The ring stone, the curved stone of the arch's own face, set on its neighbours the way it had been set when the arch was built. It was whole. It was sitting on the springing on the good side, the south side, where the old fall had packed under it. It was not moving.

"Ground," said Callie.

Toren looked up.

"Your foot's on the bed," she said. "The bed's moving. Left of your boot. Under the edge that's broken off. There's a stone of the arch, the ring stone. Pale, square, cut. You can see its edge." She held his eyes across the bed. "That's sitting on the springing on the good side. That's not going anywhere. Put the foot of it there."

Toren looked. She saw him find it.

"If I move the foot I've got to let this one go," he said. "For a breath. The cart'll drop."

"How far?"

"I don't know. A hand. The wheel's already in the hole."

Callie looked down at Jab and Pell under the lee, directly under the edge where the cart leaned over.

"A hand's all right," she said. "It'll sit down in the hole a hand. It'll not come over the lip for a hand. The wheel's got to come up out of the hole to come over, and it can't, it's in it." She did not know that. She knew it the way she knew a husk line. It was not a track so much as a direction. "Say it when you go."

"Letting go," said Toren.

---

The cart dropped.

It dropped a hand's width into the broken crown with a sound like a sack of stones set down hard, and the whole leaning weight of it shuddered. Callie felt it through the stone of the lip under the curve of her hook. The lee shivered. She did not look at the cart. She looked at Jab's shoulders and held them, and the lee held.

"Set," said Toren.

He had put the strut's foot down on the ring stone. She did not see him do it. She heard it in his voice. She heard the weight come onto the new strut, and not come up into him, because he was soft behind it, the way he had been at thirty gaps on the old line.

"Holding," said Toren. "On the arch."

And then, a moment later, differently: "It's not moving."

"It's the arch," said Callie. "It's whole."

"How did you see that?"

"It's cut," said Callie. "Somebody cut it. The rest's just stone."

---

"Busy," said Jab, from under the lee. And then, "Vent." He put his palm flat on the drift beyond Pell, downwind, and held it there, and put it back. "Busy."

Callie counted his vents. She could not help it. She could not see the counter's needle and did not try to. It was buzzing on her hip. It would buzz on her hip until she was out of the wash, because she was standing on the lip in the wind and the grey, outside her own lee. She had not put herself in it. She had looked at Jab and Pell, and it had gone where she was looking, and she was not where she was looking.

She had chosen. She knew she had. She had not decided it in words. She had decided it by what she had put her eyes on. It had cost nothing to decide, and it would cost her whatever the wind was bringing, and Jab would have to draw it off her at the cut.

*Who's worth the lee.*

Miss Wren had said it on the forty-fourth with the cloth to her nose. *You'll have to decide who's worth the lee.* Callie had thought, then, that it meant which of them. She understood now that it had always included her.

"Turn," said Miss Wren's voice, from the cut.

It came thin along the top of the embankment from seventy paces off, where the ridge stood up. "Turn. Toren. Jab. Callie."

Four turns of the glass since the red. Callie had not been counting them. She was glad somebody was.

---

"I can't hold this for ever," said Toren.

"I know."

"When they move him, I have to let it down. And if I let it down, it goes over." He was breathing hard. "It's leaning out over them. If the strut goes, the whole of it comes down the bank. On him. On them."

Callie looked at the cart.

It was over her head, nearly. It leaned out north over the lip at a slant, with the north wheel sunk in the broken crown and the high south wheel up in the air. The long weight of it was out over the edge of the bank, over the drift, over the place where Jab knelt on Pell. The front of it, by the driver's bench, was over the arch. And at the arch, on the north parapet, a pace in front of the front of the cart, there was the iron post.

It was one of the posts of the old line, like all of them, square and rusted. It was as high as a man's chest, with the bent arm at its top where something had once been hung. But it was not set in the bed like the others. It was set in the parapet of the arch, in the stone of it, in a square hole with a lead collar. It leaned a little outward over the wash, the way it had leaned when they saw it from the foot of the slope in the dusk.

It had leaned like that, Callie thought, for seventy years, and not gone any further.

"Toren," she said. "The post."

He looked.

"The post on the parapet," she said. "It's in the arch stone. Not the bed. It's set in lead." She looked at the front corner of the cart, where the driver's bench was, a pace from it. "If the cart were leaning on that, instead of on you."

Toren looked at the post for a long time. Callie could see him do the sum. The weight, and the lean, and a pace, and a strut from the ground.

"I'd have to walk it," he said. "A pace. Sideways. Onto the post."

"Can you?"

"Set, and step," said Toren. "Like a gap. Only it's the cart that steps, not me." He was quiet. "If it takes it, it takes it. If it doesn't, I'll have let go of the only thing I've got."

"It's in the arch," said Callie. "It's in the part that's whole."

Toren looked at her across the bed.

"Your call," she said. "It's your strut."

"I know it's my strut," said Toren. "I'm asking whether you think it'll hold."

Callie looked at the post. She looked at the lead collar in the square hole in the dressed stone. She looked at the lean of it, seventy years of the same lean and no more.

"Yes," she said.

---

He did it with two struts.

She watched him out of the corner of her eye, the way she watched the curve of her hook. She kept her eyes going back to Jab's shoulders every breath, and the lee held. But she watched.

He did not let go of the first. He left it under the axle, standing on the ring stone, holding. And he put the foot of a second one down, on the ring stone too, beside the first, a hand's width to the west. He let it come up from there at a different slant, not under the axle. Under the front corner of the cart, by the driver's bench. He was soft behind it. Callie saw him be soft behind both of them. She had never seen him hold two at once.

"Set," said Toren. "Two."

He let the first one go. Not all at once. The way he had let the rack down on the second try, a little at a time. The weight that had been on the first strut came over onto the second, and the second was slanting toward the post. As the weight came onto it, the front corner of the cart went where the strut was pushing it. A hand's width west. Another.

The front corner of the cart came against the iron post.

Callie heard it. It was a small sound, iron on wood, like a knock on a door.

"Step," said Toren.

He let the second strut go.

The cart leaned the last inch onto the post and stopped. It stood there. It stood with its north wheel in the broken crown and its front corner against the iron post on the parapet. The post was in its lead collar in the ring stone of the arch, and it leaned out over the wash at the same lean it had always had, and not a hair more.

Toren stood up off his knee on the south half of the crown with nothing in his hands.

"Post's got it," he said.

---

"Turn," said Miss Wren, from the cut. "Toren. Jab. Callie."

Eight.

"Now him," said Toren.

He came across the bed to the north edge, to the lip, a pace west of Callie, where she could see him. He looked down the bank at Jab and Pell under the lee.

"I'll come down," he said.

"Not down the bank," said Callie. "It's loose. You'll go down it like Jab did, on your heels, and you'll bring half of it down on them."

"Not on my heels," said Toren.

He put the foot of a strut down on the loose earth of the bank, a yard below the lip. It sank, the way it had sunk in the mud at the washout. He let it go. He looked at the bank the way he had looked at the far slab on the old line, and she saw him look for stone. There was a stone in the bank, a big grey one, half buried, a yard down and to the left. He put the foot of the strut on that. It came up from the stone at its slant toward the lip, toward him, and he stood on it. He stood on his own strut, the way he stood in the dark lane or at the washout, only now it was under his boot. It was a step.

"Set," he said, and went down onto it.

He found another stone below it and set another step. He went down, and let the one above go. He came down the bank that way, three steps, each one set on stone, with his left arm in its cuff against his chest and his right hand out for balance. He came down into the drift at the foot of it, beside Jab, under the lee.

Callie felt the lee change as he came under it.

She had not asked it to. It changed because she was looking at him, and he was under it now, and she could not stop looking at him. It went longer at the west end, to take his shoulders in. It went thinner. She felt the drawing on the stone go up, the way it had when she took Rook in on the forty-fourth.

Three.

She went down to look. A third of the stone. Less.

"Jab," said Toren. "How is he?"

"Busy," said Jab. "Still. Less." He did not look up. "The grey's off his face. It's in his chest, and I keep getting it, and the wind keeps—" He stopped. "It's less than it was."

"We have to move him," said Toren. "Out from under. West, to the cut. Seventy paces along the bottom of the bank. It's lower at the cut. We can get him up there."

"His ankle," said Jab.

"I know his ankle."

Pell spoke. It was the first thing he had said since the drift.

"Do it," he said, through his teeth. "It's a bone. It's broken already. It'll not be more broken for being carried." He looked up at Callie on the lip, past the lee, with his grey face. "I'll not say anything about the camber."

---

They moved him.

Toren got down on the drift on Pell's left and got his right arm under Pell's shoulders. He did not use the left. Callie watched him not use it. Jab got on Pell's right, with the frame on his back and the open jar stoppered and pushed into his own coat. They got him up between them onto his one good foot, with his arms over their shoulders and his broken foot held up off the drift. They went west.

Callie went with them along the top.

It was not the stone of the lip any more that the curve sat on. It was the lip going west, the edge of the bed, packed stone. She rocked the curve along it now, the way she had on the old line, rock and lift and set. She kept her eyes on the three of them below her. On Toren's head and Pell's grey hair and the top of Jab's frame with its empty socket. She walked, and the lee went with her, leaning out over the bank and down over them. It moved as they moved, at their pace, which was the slowest pace she had ever walked.

It cost. She felt it cost. Moving, it cost the way it had cost on the old line, and it was over three. It was thinner than it had ever been. The grey came across on the wind and hit it and hissed, and she could feel where it hit, all along it. It was like standing out in sleet in a coat that is not quite thick enough.

Twenty paces. Twenty-five.

"Turn," said Miss Wren, from the cut, nearer now. "Toren. Jab. Callie."

Thirty.

And the drift ahead of them, at the foot of the bank, between them and the cut, heaved.

---

It came up the way the others had, all at once, with the crust cracking along the top of a long hump in the grey. It came up twenty paces ahead of them at the foot of the bank. And another behind it, a little way out in the wash, turning.

Husks. Two.

They turned toward the lee. Callie felt them turn. They went at anything worked, and the lee was worked, and it was the brightest worked thing in the wash. They came along the foot of the bank toward it, toward the three of them under it.

"*Two*," said Callie. "In front. Coming at the lane."

Jab stopped. Toren stopped. Pell hung between them on his one foot.

"Don't kill them under it," said Jab. "Don't. Every one I killed down here went on him."

"I know," said Toren.

Callie looked at the husks coming. She looked at the lee, leaning out over the three of them off the lip, pale, with the grey from the wind hissing on the outside of it and sliding down.

Sliding down the outside.

*It'll keep what's blowing off whatever's behind it.* Pell had said that, on the forty-fourth, standing behind it with his collar down. *It'll not keep anything off that comes round the end.*

It kept off what was blowing. Whatever hit it on the outside slid down the outside, into the drift, away from them.

"Toren," she said. "Kill them against it."

He looked up at her.

"On the outside," said Callie. "Not under it. Outside it, on the wind side, with it between them and you. When they come apart, the grey goes against the lee. It'll slide down the outside." She heard her voice, flat and fast. "It'll go down the wrong side of it. Away from him."

"I'd be outside it," said Toren.

"You'd be outside it."

Toren looked at her. He looked at the husks, fifteen paces now, coming. He looked at Pell on his shoulder and Jab on Pell's other side.

"Can you hold them?" he said. "Without me. Him and Pell. Just the two."

Callie looked at Jab and Pell.

She took Toren out of her eyes.

It was the hardest thing she had done on the embankment. Harder than the ring stone, harder than the red. She was looking at the three of them, and she took her eyes off one. She put them on Jab's shoulders and Pell's head and nowhere else. She did it on purpose, the way she had once let her mother's breathing go past her without a number on it.

The lee went shorter at the west end. It went thicker. It came in over the two of them and left Toren out.

"They're inside," she said. "You're not. Go."

---

Toren let go of Pell.

Jab took the weight. He took it on his right shoulder, with the frame on his back and Pell's arm round his neck, and staggered, and did not go down. Toren stepped out from under the lee, west, into the wind and the grey. He went two paces along the foot of the bank toward the husks, and turned, so that the lee was on his right hand, between him and the others.

He let the spike come on his fist.

It came heavy and crooked, the ugly thing, dark. He lit the heel. Only the heel, the hand's width of thin white-grey along the hooked curve. He did not light the rest.

The first husk came at him. Not at the lee. At him, because he was lit now and nearer. It came along the foot of the bank through the drift with the grey going up round it. He put the lit heel into it low, hooking, the way he had put it into Wyck's sack in the first lane. He dragged it round, off its line. It turned against the outside of the lee.

He put the point in.

It came apart against the lee. The whole of a husk's worth of loose grey went up in a burst, and the wind took it, and threw it at the lee.

It hit the lee on the outside, all of it, and hissed like water thrown on a stove. And it slid down.

It slid down the outside of the pale leaning wall into the drift beyond it, away from Jab and Pell. Callie watched it slide. She felt it hit and felt it slide, a weight on the outside of the lee like sleet on a roof, and it did not come through. Not a grain of it came through.

It went over Toren.

He was outside. He was in the wind on the far side of the lee where the burst went up. It came down on him, on his face and his shoulders and his coat, grey. He put his sleeve over his mouth and did not stop.

The second husk came.

He did it again. The heel, and the drag, and the point, against the outside of the lee. It came apart and the grey hit the lee and slid, and went over him.

Then there was nothing in the drift at the foot of the bank between them and the cut but grey.

---

"Turn," said Miss Wren, close now, twenty paces. "Toren. Jab. Callie." And then, differently: "I can see you."

Toren came back under.

He did not ask. He put his shoulder back under Pell's other arm and took the weight off Jab. Callie put him back in her eyes, and the lee went longer at the west end and came back over him. It was thinner, and it drew. It drew so that she felt it at the bottom of the well like a hand scraping the bottom of a sack.

Forty paces. Fifty.

She went down to look, fast. The stone was nearly gone. There was a scrap of it. Below it, her own water, a finger and a bit above the floor, cold and clear.

Sixty.

The bank was lower here. It came down as the embankment ran into the ridge, until at the mouth of the cut it was only the height of a man's chest, loose earth, with the stone of the cut's wall beside it. Miss Wren was standing at the top of it on the bed of the old line, with the glass in one hand. Rook was sitting against the wall of the cut behind her with his coat off and a cloth held to his back.

"Up," said Toren.

He put the foot of a strut on the stone of the cut's wall where it came down into the drift, and let it come up at its slant to the top of the bank. He set it. It was a step and a rail at once, pale and thin, from the stone to the bed.

"Set," he said. "Jab. His good foot on it. Step."

Jab put Pell's good foot on the strut. Pell stepped. Toren held him from below with his right arm. Jab went up the bank on his heels and hands beside the strut with the frame on his back, and at the top he turned and got Pell under the arms. Miss Wren got Pell's other arm. They pulled. Pell came up over the edge of the bank onto the stone of the bed at the mouth of the cut, and lay there on his back, grey, breathing through his teeth.

Toren came up after him on his own strut, one step, and let it go.

Callie stood at the lip with the hook.

The stone at the bottom of the well was a scrap. Her own water lay above the floor, a finger and a bit, untouched. Nobody was under the lee any more. They were all out.

"That's the stone," said Callie. "I'm stopping before I start on mine."

She let it go.

The lee went out. It went out over nothing, over the empty foot of the bank, over a drift with nobody in it. The wind came across the wash from the hill and took the top off the grey and brought it across the lip, over Callie, and down.

She walked the last ten paces along the edge of the bed to the mouth of the cut.

At the fifth pace the counter on her hip stopped buzzing. It went back to ticking. It was fast, rain on a tin roof, but it was ticking. At the tenth, where the wall of the cut stood up on her right and the drifts were behind the ridge, it slowed.

She took it out of the bag and looked at the window. The needle was off the pin. It was lying well back from the red mark, trembling.

"Ninety," she said. "Ten breaths. At the mouth of the cut. Under the red."

"Out," said Miss Wren. "All three. Named, and out." She did not write it yet. She looked at the glass in her hand. "Fifteen turns. Fifteen minutes in the red, from the needle to the cut. I've got all of it."

---

Callie sat down on the stone of the bed at the mouth of the cut.

She had not meant to. Her legs did it. She sat with her back against the wall of the cut and the hook across her knees and her hands open on the conduit. Something warm ran out of her nose and over her lip. She put the back of her hand to it and it came away red.

"Nose," said Miss Wren, beside her, with a cloth.

"I know."

"Head forward. Pinch it." Miss Wren held the cloth. Her hands were perfectly steady. "Who was inside?"

Callie looked at her over the cloth.

"Jab and Pell," she said. "Then Toren. Then Jab and Pell. Then all three." She stopped. "Toren went out to kill them. I took him out. On purpose. So it'd hold on the two."

"And you?"

"No," said Callie. "I wasn't inside. I was on the lip."

Miss Wren looked at her for a long moment with the cloth held under her nose.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. So Toren can write it."

Toren was sitting against the other wall of the cut, grey from his hair to his boots, with the paper out already and the pencil. His hand was shaking. He did not wait to be asked.

"I'll write it," he said.

Callie said it again, slowly, all of it. Who was inside, and when, and who she had taken out and why, and who had not been inside at all. Toren wrote it on the back of the paper under the Director's signature, in his careful slow hand, with the grey on his fingers going onto the page.

When she had finished she took the cloth off her nose and looked at her hands on the conduit of the hook. They were open. They had been open, she found, the whole time. She had held the lee with her eyes, and not with her hands. Her hands had only been on the steel, the way you rest a hand on the rail of a stair.

She looked back along the embankment.

It lay across the wash in the grey morning, two hundred and fifty paces of it, with the drifts heaped against its north side and the wind taking the grey off them in a long low smoke. Halfway along it, over the arch, the cart stood. It leaned out over the wash with its north wheel in the broken crown and its front corner against the iron post on the parapet. The three home jars were in the rack behind the driver's bench, and the lead box on its straw behind them, and nobody near it.

Beyond it, at the far end, on the foot of the slope where they had come down in the stillness an hour ago, there was a grey shape standing on the bed of the old line. It had its head up, looking toward them.

Duchess.

She had stopped at the foot of the slope, where the counter had said twelve. She had put her ears forward, and she was waiting.

"Count," said Callie.

Toren looked up from the paper.

He counted. She could see him doing it. He did it the way he had at the north lip of the Sag, and at the corner on the rise in the dark, and every armful.

"Pell," he said. "Dee. Rook. Jab. Callie." He looked along the embankment at the grey horse standing at the far end of it. "Six," he said. "And a horse. Over there."

Nobody said anything.

Then Rook, sitting against the wall of the cut with his coat off and his back bleeding into a cloth, began to laugh. It was the low careful laugh, the way a man laughs with a hole in his back. It was the way he had laughed on the old north road on the first day, watching Callie shuffle up it like an old woman on a wet floor.

"She'll come," he said. "She'll come when she's ready. She's the only one of us with any sense."

# Chapter 32 — Jab: Ten and Three

Dee moved them twenty paces into the cut before she let anybody do anything.

"Here," she said, where the walls stood up on both sides of the old line higher than a man, and the drifts were all behind the ridge. "Callie."

Callie held out the counter with her other hand still pinching her nose.

"Thirty," she said. "Ten breaths."

"Then here," said Dee. "Nobody sits. Nobody touches their face. Nobody eats." She put the glass down on the stone of the bed and turned it over. "Rotation. From now."

Jab stood with the frame on his back and his hands held out a little from his sides, the way the technician had held his in the pen. He could feel his right palm. It was not hot. It was warm, from inside, like a pan handle. It had been warm since the fourth draw in the drift and it had not gone all the way down.

"I worked," he said. "I'm the watch now."

Dee looked at him.

"You worked," she said. "Fifteen minutes in the red and more before it. You're warm. I can see it from here." She did not argue with him. "You're the watch. I'm working. Pell first." She knelt beside Pell where he lay on his back on the stone at the edge of the bed. "Callie's the reserve, and she's got nothing, so she's a reserve with nothing, which is what we've got. Toren stood where he was. He was grey from his hair to his boots.

"Jar," said Dee.

Jab took the open jar out of his coat, where he had pushed it stoppered when they moved Pell. It was the one she had taken out of his rack from the lip. The fence's. He pulled the stopper and put it in her hand.

"How much is in it?"

He weighed it on his palm, the way Callie did.

"Five," he said. "Near enough. Three went on him in the drift."

"Then five," said Dee, and put the mouth of it to her own left palm, and her right hand flat on Pell's chest. "Watch."

He watched.

It was the first time he had watched anybody but Rook draw. He had been the one working in the pen, and in the ditch, and in the drift. He had never stood over it with his hands empty and seen it from outside. He saw Dee's face go quiet. He saw her palm lie flat on Pell's chest and not move. He saw her lift it after ten breaths and turn it and put it flat on the stone of the bed beside her, where the grey from their boots had already made the stone dirty, and hold it there for three.

"Vent," he said. A breath after she had done it.

"I know," said Dee. "Say it before. That's the watch."

"Vent," he said, at the next one, before.

"Better."

He watched her face. He watched her palms when she lifted them. He watched the jar in her hand get lighter every draw. He could not feel it, the way he felt it when it was against his own palm. He had to watch it, the way Callie watched a thing. He did not know until then how much of watching was being frightened for somebody and not being allowed to do anything about it.

Callie held the counter to Pell's chest, at arm's length.

"Forty," she said. And after two more draws: "Twenty-two." And then: "Fourteen."

"Off," said Dee to herself, and took her hand off. She held both her palms up to Jab. "Watch."

He held his own palm over them, an inch off, not touching. He had never done it before. He shut his eyes. He felt for the warm that was not hers, in the creases, the way she had felt for it in his.

It was there. A little.

"There's some," he said. "In the right. Not the left."

"Then take it."

He put his palm flat on hers and drew. It came up into his hand, a fine warm grit, and he held it high and turned and put his palm on the stone. It went.

"Clean," he said.

Dee looked at him over their two hands.

"Now you're a watch," she said.

---

They went round.

Dee worked on Toren next, because Toren was the worst. He had been outside the lee, killing husks against it, with the grey going over him. Callie held the counter to Toren's chest and said *sixty*, and Dee drew, and Jab watched, and at the end of twenty minutes by Dee's glass Jab said *off* before she did.

Then it was Jab's turn to work, and Dee's turn to watch. Callie was the reserve with nothing.

He worked on Callie.

She stood against the wall of the cut with the hook across her in both hands and her nose stopped bleeding. She held her left hand out to him palm up, the way she had held it out to Dee at the noon halt on the first day, before she knew she had done it. He put his palm flat on the inside of her wrist instead, because that was where it was, and drew.

It was on her face and her hair and her shoulders, where the wind had brought it over the lip. It was not much. It was more than it would have been if she had been inside.

"You weren't in it," he said, drawing. "Your own lee."

"No."

"You put us in it."

"I put who I was looking at in it," said Callie. "I wasn't looking at me."

He drew. He did not say anything else. He thought about the lee leaning out over him in the drift like a roof off a wall, pale, and the grey hissing on the outside of it. He thought about looking up and seeing her on the lip above it with the hook, in the wind.

"Twelve," said Dee, with the counter. "Off."

Then Rook.

---

Rook was sitting against the wall of the cut with his coat off and his shirt cut away at the back, and a cloth held to the place between his shoulders by his own hand, reached round. The cloth was red. His face was the colour of the stone.

"Grey in it," said Dee. She knelt behind him and took his hand away and the cloth with it, and looked. Jab looked too, from where he stood. The place where the hound had hit was the place where the old wound had been. It had opened again along the old line of it, and the edges were dark, and there was grey in it. It was fine grey, in the red, where the hound's weight had driven the dust from Rook's own coat into the hole.

"Water," said Dee.

There was one skin. It was Dee's own, the one she carried on her belt with the book and the glass and the small bag she had carried everywhere since the pen. Everything else was on the cart, and the cart was leaning on a post over the wash.

She used all of it. She poured it into the wound a little at a time and wiped it out with a clean cloth from her bag, and poured, and wiped. Rook sat against the wall with his teeth shut and his hands on his knees and did not make a sound.

"Jab," said Dee. "The grey. What's busy in it. Draw that. I'll do the rest."

Jab knelt behind Rook and put his palm flat over the wound, not touching it. An inch off.

He felt it. The torn place, bright and fixed, like Pell's ankle, like the technician's hand. It was done. It had happened when the hound hit. And in it, in the red and round it, the busy warm grit, a lot of it, where it should not have been.

"Draw my back," said Rook. His voice was very tired. "The pain. Take the edge off it."

"That's torn," said Jab. "It's not busy. I can't draw a tear." He did not move his hand. "I'll draw the grey out of it. I'll leave the tear to Dee."

Rook was quiet.

"You sound like her," he said at last.

"Good," said Dee.

---

It cost more than any of them.

Jab drew on Rook for twenty minutes by Dee's glass, and more, and the jar in his left hand went down and down, and ran low. He said *jar* before it was dry. Dee reached past his shoulder to the rack on his back, and took out another one, and pulled the stopper.

"That's two," she said.

"That's two," said Jab.

"Callie."

Callie said it from the wall, flat.

"Road, none. Inside, two opened on the road. The first one's done. The second one's open." She stopped. "And one broken. On the rail. That's three out of the frame. Five left in it, whole."

Nobody said anything.

Jab drew. It came up out of Rook's back slowly, through the torn place and round it, the busy grit. He held it high and vented it into the stone and went back. It was not like the drift, with the jar going and the husks coming and his back to the bank. It was slow and quiet in the cut with the walls up on both sides, and his palm getting warm again, and Dee saying *vent* before he needed it.

When he had finished, Dee stitched the back.

She did it with a curved needle from her bag and thread. She did it fast and small, the way Keel would have done it, while Rook sat with his hands on his knees and his eyes shut. Jab held the edges for her where she told him. When she had finished she put a pad on it and bound it round his chest with the last of the clean cloth.

"That was open to the air on a hill with the rip in the sky over it," she said. "It'll be hot tonight. Tomorrow we'll know." She tied the cloth. "You're off, Elias. Not half a turn. Off. Till I say."

Rook opened his eyes.

"That's two healers," he said.

"That's two healers," said Dee. "It was always going to be, some of the time. It's all of the time now." She sat back on her heels. "Write it, Toren."

Toren wrote it.

After that Jab drew Dee, off the second jar. She had lain on the lip in the wind for fifteen minutes with her arm down the bank, and it was on her sleeve and her face and in her hair. Then Dee took the leftovers out of his palms, flat, palm to palm, and he felt her find the warm that had not gone down in the drift and take it. His palms went cool for the first time since the fourth draw.

"That's the watch taking it off the one who worked," said Dee. "Before you rest. You'll rest now."

---

The wind dropped at midday.

It went the way it had come, all at once. One breath the grey was going off the drifts in the wash in a long low smoke, and the next it was lying there, crusted and still. Callie went to the mouth of the cut with the counter and held it out toward the embankment and waited ten breaths.

"Seventy," she said. "At the mouth. It was ninety." She waited. "Sixty."

"And the arch?" said Dee.

"I can't tell from here. Less than the red. Without the wind, it's the drifts on their own, lying there." She looked along the embankment at the cart leaning on its post. "Sixty, seventy. At the arch. Maybe."

Pell lifted his head off the stone.

"The horse," he said.

Duchess was still at the far end. She had not moved all morning. She stood on the bed of the old line at the foot of the slope, two hundred and fifty paces off, with her head up and her ears forward. She was looking at them, at the cut, where the people were.

"She'll not come past the drifts," said Pell. "Not on her own. Not with hounds on her nose." He looked at the cart. "And I can't whistle her with my face like this. I've grey in my mouth still."

"I'll fetch her," said Callie.

"Not on your own," said Dee.

So it was Callie and Toren, named, out loud, by Dee, at the mouth of the cut, and the time written. Callie to fetch the horse and read the crown. Toren to bring the cart off the arch. Jab stood at the mouth of the cut with Dee and watched them go out along the top of the embankment with the wind down and the drifts lying still.

He watched them the way he had watched Dee draw. From outside. With his hands empty.

---

It was while he was watching them that he saw the ash move.

He did not see it at first. He was watching Callie walk east along the bed with the hook over her shoulder and the counter held out. He was watching Toren go to the arch and stop on the south half of the crown and look at the ground. His eyes went down, the way eyes do, to the drift at the foot of the north bank under the arch. That was where he had been. Where Pell had lain.

There was a pale streak in it.

It was paler than the grey of the drift. It was where the jar had broken on the rail and the clean had gone down his back and out at his belt into the drift. It lay on top of the grey like flour spilled on a floor. It was a long pale smear a yard long, where he had lain over Pell.

It was moving.

He did not believe it. He looked away and looked back. It was moving. Not all of it. The edge of it, the west edge, the end nearest the hill. It was going out of the smear in threads. They were very thin, finer than hair, grey-white on the grey. They went out across the top of the drift toward the west, toward the hill, the way the first threads of smoke go out of a fire before the fire is lit.

They went, and stopped.

He watched. They lay still on the drift, the thin pale threads, going nowhere. And then they went again. They slid, all of them at once, a hand's width further west across the crust, and stopped again.

There was no wind.

The wind had dropped at midday. There was nothing moving anywhere in the wash. Not the reeds on the south side, not the dust on the drifts, nothing. And the threads were going west. They were going up. The wash came down from the hill. It ran from the hill down to the embankment and under it and on to the river. The threads were going the other way, up the wash, toward the hill, against the way the ground ran.

He counted.

He did not mean to. He counted the way he had counted Tonk's breathing for eighteen days on the road, and the ticks in the pen, and his own breaths holding a tenth of a Handful high in a quiet shed with a bucket going *tick*. The threads went, and he counted, and they stopped. He counted, and they went again.

Ten. And three.

Ten breaths going. Three breaths still.

Ten, and three.

---

"Dee," he said.

She was beside him at the mouth of the cut with the glass in her hand, watching Callie and Toren.

"Look at the drift," he said. "Under the arch. Where I was. Where the jar went."

She looked.

She looked for a long time. Jab watched her face. It did not move. It did not move the way it had not moved in the pen when the box came off the stool.

"It's going uphill," said Dee.

"It's going in," said Jab. "Toward the hill. Ten breaths, and it stops for three. Ten and three. I've counted it four times."

"Callie," said Dee. She did not raise her voice much. It carried. "Callie. The counter. Point it at the drift under the arch and don't move."

Callie had stopped on the bed halfway along the embankment. She turned round and looked at them, and then down at the drift. She held the counter out at the end of her arm toward the foot of the bank below her.

She did not say a number for a long time.

"It's not staying still," she said at last. "The needle." Her voice came along the top of the embankment flat and clear. "It goes up and it goes down. Eighty. Then ninety. Then eighty. It's not like the pen. It's not the wind. There isn't any wind." She was quiet. "It goes up, and it stays up, and then it goes down for a bit, and then up."

"How long up?" said Jab.

"I don't know. I've not—" She stopped. He could see her counting. "Ten. It's up for about ten. Then down for three. Then up."

"Ten and three," said Jab.

Toren, on the crown, had turned round. He was looking down at the drift below the arch, at the pale streak, and at the threads going out of it west. Then he looked past it, at the grey where the husks had come apart. There were the two smears on the lip where Rook had killed the first two, and the two on the drift under the arch, Jab's and Callie's, and the two further west where Toren had killed them against the lee. And past those, the two long smears on the stone of the bed on the south side where Rook had killed the hounds.

"All of it," said Toren. "Look. It's all going."

It was. Jab saw it when Toren said it. He had been looking only at the pale streak, at his own jar's worth. But the grey where the husks had come apart was going too. Thin grey threads came out of each smear on the drift and went west across the crust. So did the grey on the stone of the bed where the hounds had died, in threads, over the stone, and over the lip, and down, and west. All of it at once. Ten breaths, and all of it went a hand's width toward the hill. Three breaths, and all of it lay still.

Ten, and three.

"I've never seen ash go uphill," said Rook.

He had got up. He was standing against the wall of the cut behind them with his coat over his shoulders and his hand flat on the stone, grey in the face, looking out at the wash.

"I've seen it blow," he said. "I've seen it wash. I've lifted it for eleven years off every kind of ground there is. I've never seen it go uphill."

"Nobody touch it," said Dee. "Nobody go after it. Nobody."

---

Jab put his palm flat on the stone of the bed at the mouth of the cut.

He did not touch the ash. There was no ash where he put it. It was only the stone of the old line, packed and grey and a little dirty from their boots. He put his palm flat on it the way he put it on a drift on the east track at first light, and he did not ask anything. He let himself feel what was there.

It was not a lean.

A lean was a person. A lean was somebody, off there, bad, pulling a little, the way a rope pulls when the man at the other end of it leans. He had felt it for the girl's father at a mile. He had felt it for the day holder in the fog at forty yards. He had felt it for Tonk every day of his life.

This was not a person. There was nobody in it. It was like ash. It was like the cold that came into his palm out of a jar, with nobody in it.

But it was pulling.

It was not pulling at him. It was pulling past him. It was pulling at the ash on the ground, all the loose ash in the wash, everything that was lying where it fell. It came from the west, from up, from the hill. It was very far off and very steady. It pulled for ten breaths. It held for three. It pulled again.

It did not haul.

That was what he felt, with his palm on the stone, and it went through him cold. It did not haul. A husk hauled. A hound hauled, going at the dark. This did not. It pulled the way you pull a drift, flat, open, patient, at the rate it comes. And then it stopped for three breaths, the way you stop to vent. And then it pulled again.

It was doing what he did.

It was the long table. It was Dee's glass and the east track and the ditch and the drift. Draw, ten breaths. Hold. Three. Draw.

He took his hand off the stone.

He looked west.

---

The hill stood over them.

It was very near now. From the mouth of the cut it filled the west, dark and flat-topped, with the fence running away along the far bank of the river below it. Over the flat top of it, the place in the sky.

In daylight it was harder to see than at dusk. It was a patch, darker than the grey sky round it, bruised, with the clouds going into it and coming out of the other side not quite where they should. Round its edge there was the light the colour of the bottom of a pond.

He watched the edge.

It was not still. He had thought, on the forty-fourth at dusk from the rise, that it was not still, the way a sleeping animal is not still. He had not been able to see it breathe. He could see it now. The light round the edge of the patch thickened, very slightly, the green-grey going a shade deeper, for a while. Then it thinned, for a moment. Then it thickened.

He counted.

Ten, and three.

---

"It's the same," he said.

They were all looking at him. Dee beside him, and Rook against the wall. Pell on the stone with his head up. Out on the embankment Callie with the counter held out over the drift and Toren on the crown. None of them had spoken.

"It's the same count," said Jab. "The ash on the ground, and the needle, and the edge of that." He did not point at the sky. He could not make his hand do it. "It's all ten and three. It's all the same thing."

"What's the same thing?" said Dee.

Jab did not know how to say it. He said it the only way he had.

"Something's lifting it," he said. "Up there. Under that. At the middle of it. Something's got its hand flat on the whole hill, like a drift, and it's lifting the loose ash off the ground toward itself. All of it. Everything lying where it fell." He heard his voice go and let it. "It's not hauling. It's not a husk. It's lifting like we lift. Ten breaths, and it stops for three, like you taught me to vent. And then it lifts again."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't know what it is," said Jab. "I don't know what it's for. I can't feel it. There's nobody in it, the way there's nobody in ash." He looked at the pale threads going out of his own jar's streak in the drift, west, a hand's width at a time. "But I know what it's doing. It's doing what I do."

"It's doing what you do," said Dee slowly, "to ash."

"To ash," said Jab. "Only ash." He did not know that. He knew it the way you know the edge of a step in the dark. "The loose ash. Ours too. That's ours, going." He looked at the streak. "That was the stair."

---

Callie came back along the embankment.

She did not bring Duchess. She had turned round halfway, at Dee's voice, and she came back to the cut with the counter still in her hand and her face very still, reading them.

"The Director said lift at the edge," she said.

She said it to nobody. She said it the way she said a number.

"*Lift at the edge of it if the ledger says you have to lift.*" She looked at the pale threads on the drift. "She said it on the thirty-eighth. It's on Toren's paper. And we'd have had to, because of the stair. Because one room's all eight Flasks buys, and anything more comes off the ground up there." She turned the counter in her hand. "But there's no edge. Is there. There's nothing lying at the edge to lift. It's all going in."

"It's all going in," said Jab.

"Then the ash we'd need is in there," said Callie. "With whatever's lifting it. Under that." She did not look at the sky either. "And the boxes are under that."

Toren had come off the crown. He was standing at the mouth of the cut behind her, grey from head to foot. He had heard all of it.

"It's the same place," he said. "It was always the same place. You said. On the thirty-seventh. *It's only two things on paper.*"

"I didn't know it was this," said Callie.

"Nobody knew it was this," said Dee.

---

They brought the cart off the arch in the middle of the afternoon.

Callie fetched Duchess. She walked the whole length of the embankment to the foot of the slope with the counter held out, reading it out loud, *sixty*, *seventy*, *sixty*, and the wind down, and nothing coming up out of the drifts. She came back leading the mare by the halter, walking on the south edge of the bed with her own body between the horse and the wash. She talked to her the whole way the way Pell did, low, about nothing. Duchess came. She put her ears back at the drifts and she came anyway.

They could not hitch her. The traces were cut. Pell told them how, from where he lay on the stone at the mouth of the cut with his splint on. The splint was two pieces of the cart's tailboard rail that Toren had pried off with his good hand when he first went out to the arch, bound with Dee's last cloth. Rope from the cart, through the rings, round the hames, knotted this way and not that way. Toren did it one-handed and Callie did it with her teeth and the knots were bad and Pell said they would do.

Then Toren set under the north wheel where it sat in the broken crown. He put the strut's foot on the ring stone, on the arch, the way Callie had shown him, and let it come up under the axle.

"Set," he said.

"Rolling," said Callie, at Duchess's head, because Pell could not.

Duchess leaned into the rope, and the cart came up out of the hole onto the strut. The wheel rolled up the slant and out onto the good stone of the bed west of the crown. Toren let it go at the end. Not after. The cart came off the iron post and rolled west along the bed toward the cut, with Duchess walking and Callie at her head. The three home jars were in their rack behind the driver's bench, and the lead box behind them on its straw, and the vent jars in the straw at the tail. Nothing on it had moved.

Toren stood on the south half of the crown with nothing in his hands.

"Step," he said, and walked off it.

He was three paces off it, on the good stone west of the arch, when the crown went.

---

It went the way it had been going since the morning. There was the grinding sound under the stone first, like a mill wheel turning over. Then the whole of the north half of the bed over the arch slid. It went out and down, off the north edge of the embankment, into the wash. It took the crack with it, and the hole where the wheel had been, and the edge of the south half. And the iron post went, the post on the parapet in its lead collar in the ring stone, because the ring stone went too. The arch went. It went down into the wash under the drifts with a sound like a cart tipped over, and the grey went up off the drifts where it landed in a long slow cloud and lay down again.

Where the crown had been, there was a gap in the embankment. It was four paces wide, and the dark trickle of the wash ran through the bottom of it, and the drifts heaped on both sides.

Toren stood on the west side of the gap and looked across it at the east side, at the bed of the old line going away east along the top of the embankment toward the foot of the slope.

It was the way they had come. It was the only way home they knew.

"That's the road," said Pell, from the mouth of the cut. He had lifted his head to see. "That was the road."

Jab drew the mare at the mouth of the cut before they went on. She had been out on the embankment three times since the morning, once at a gallop in the wind. Pell said *do her*, from the stone, the way he would have said it about a wheel. So Jab put his right hand flat on her shoulder and Dee put the second jar to his left, and Duchess stood with her ears going round and let him. It was the same as a person. The busy thing that was not her was on her legs and her chest and her nose, where she had put her head down to the drifts. He drew it and vented into the stone and drew again.

"Vell did dogs," said Dee, watching. "Nobody ever gave him a horse."

"Half," said Callie, weighing the jar after. "A half, for the horse."

---

They reached the pump house at dusk.

It was a mile. The old line came out of the cut on the far side of the ridge and ran down a long bare slope to the river, and the river was low and dark and fast in a stony bed. On its near bank, the east bank, where the old line came to it, there was the pump house. It was square and stone, with no roof and the stumps of pipes coming out of its river wall and going into the water. There was a flat place in front of it, gravel, where a crew had camped every night for eleven nights twenty years ago and a man had written a count on his wrist with a burnt stick.

Across the river, on the far bank, the fence.

It ran along the far bank as far as Jab could see both ways, a line of posts with wire on them, taller than a man, the wire rusted to the colour of the iron posts on the old line. A footbridge went across the river to it from beside the pump house. It was three planks wide with a rail on one side, grey and old but standing. At the far end of it the fence had a gate in it, a frame of iron with wire across it and a chain on the latch.

The east gate.

And beyond the gate, beyond the fence, the ground went up and up in the dusk to the flat top of the hill. Over the flat top of the hill was the place in the sky, with the light round its edge the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened, and thinned, and thickened.

Pell lay on the cart on his back on the canvas with his splinted foot on a coil of rope. He looked at the pump house.

"She said it had a roof," he said.

"She was fifty-three," said Rook.

---

They said the rules at the river, because Senna had said to, every time, at the gate going out. They had not been through the gate. They had been through the wash, which was worse. Dee said that counted.

Coat off. Shake it. Wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat.

Jab stood on the gravel by the river in the last light and took his coat off.

He did it carefully. He undid the buttons one at a time from the top, down to the one on the left side, over the place under his breastbone. He put his hand in there and took out what was in there before he did anything else.

Little lay in his palm. The small black wooden fish, as long as his thumb, smoked black by a lifetime of hanging near a fire, with one fin broken off before Tonk was born.

Callie had the counter. She did not wait to be asked. She held it out at the end of her arm toward his palm, toward the fish, and watched the needle for ten breaths.

"The room," she said.

Jab looked at her.

"Seven," said Callie. "The room. It was buttoned under. It's on the front. The jar went down your back." She switched the counter off. "It's clean."

He closed his hand on it.

Then he shook the coat out, hard, downwind of everybody. The pale stain down the back of it went off it into the dusk in a thin cloud and went away along the river. He knelt at the edge of the water below the pump house and put his hands in it, with the fish in one of them. It was so cold it was like being hit. He washed his hands and his face and his hair with the river, the way a man had made a crew of eleven do it every night twenty years ago. He kept Little in his fist the whole time and did not let go.

---

Toren counted them in.

He did it at the pump house wall with the paper against the stone, the way Senna had told him to on the morning of the forty-first. *Count them in at the pump house. Every night. Out and in. Write it somewhere better than your wrist.*

"Pell," he said. "Dee. Rook. Jab. Callie." He looked at the mare, tied to the cart wheel with a rope halter, with her ears forward at last, eating. "Six," he said. "And a horse."

He wrote it. Jab watched him write it. *46th. The pump house. In: 6. And Duchess.*

Under it he wrote the rest. He wrote it slowly, and Jab read it over his shoulder in the last of the light, and nobody stopped him reading.

*The wash. The crown went under the cart. T. set on the arch, then walked it onto the post. Pell cut the traces (the right place) and went down the bank: right ankle broken. Red (true, C. tapped once): J., T., C. named, 15 min. J. drew Pell off the road jar and then the frame (J.'s call). A jar broke on the rail. R. hit in the back by the second hound: open again, grey in it, stitched. R. off. C. held the lee off the lip, leaning, J. and P. inside, and took T. out so he could kill two against it. It worked. The crown's gone into the wash since. The road east is cut.*

And under that, in the same hand, after a gap:

*The ash goes uphill. Ten and three. J. says something at the middle of it is lifting it. The same count as the sky.*

---

Callie said the ash at the fire.

They made the fire against the pump house wall on the flat gravel where the crew had made theirs. Pell lay on the cart with his foot up. Rook sat against the stone with his bound chest and his face grey and Dee beside him with two fingers on his wrist. Toren had the paper. Duchess ate. Jab sat by the fire with his hands open on his knees and his palms still a little warm, and Little in his coat on the left side, buttoned.

"Road," said Callie. "None. It all went on Pell in the drift."

She did not look at anybody. She said it the way her mother read the store book, flat, for somebody else.

"Inside. Sixty-four at the gate. One opened on the road for Pell, all spent, on Pell and Toren and me. One broken on the rail, lost. One opened in the cut for Rook, and Miss Wren, and Jab, and the horse, and what's left of it goes on tonight's settling, a Handful each for me and Toren and Miss Wren." She stopped. "That's three out of the frame. Five left. Whole. Forty."

"And the line," said Dee.

"The line's sixteen," said Callie. "It doesn't move. That's twenty-four to work with." She looked at the fire. "We priced forty-eight."

Nobody said anything.

"Half," said Toren.

"Half," said Callie. "The rooms were thirty, on their own. For two hours, five people." She was quiet. "Twenty-four doesn't get five people down the stair and into one room and back up it. Not with two healers. Not deep." She looked across the fire at Rook. "Not with him off."

"Home," said Dee.

"Home, three," said Callie. "Twenty-four. On the cart. Not touched." She looked at Pell. "For a road that was six days."

"It's not six days," said Pell.

He said it from the cart, on his back, without lifting his head.

"The line's cut at the wash," he said. "It'll take people across the bottom on foot, through the drifts, if they're quick and they wash after. It won't take the cart. Nothing takes the cart across that now." He looked at the sky. "North, up the side of the wash, round the head of the drifts, toward the hill, where it's worse. Or south, through the marsh to the river, and down the river on this bank till there's a way back up to the old line. There's no bed for the cart either way. I don't know how far. Two days more, maybe, whichever way. With me on the cart and not walking."

"That's the fifty-fifth at the earliest," said Toren. "Home. Not the fifty-third." He did not look up from the paper. "The cycle's the fifty-fourth."

"The man said guessing isn't stopping," said Rook.

"He said Sowerby could dose her off her blood," said Toren. "For a few cycles. Badly."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

---

"The Director said don't go into it," said Dee at last.

She said it to the fire.

"She said it on the thirty-eighth, and I signed under it, and her word's mine on this road." She had her book on her knee and she did not open it. "*You are not going into it. You are not going looking for whatever's under it. If it comes to you, you do what you have to and you come home.*"

"It's not coming to us," said Rook. "It's taking the ash to it."

"I know what it's doing."

"The boxes are under it," said Toren. "Gran said. Down the yellow stair, in the main building, under the wrong sky. On the left. Eleven rooms." He put his hand on the paper. "And the ash we'd need to get past the first room is going under it. Ten and three. Everything lying where it fell." He looked up. "There isn't a way to the boxes that isn't under it. There isn't a way to get more ash that isn't under it. Callie said it on the thirty-seventh. We just didn't know it'd be moving."

"Then what's the plan?" said Pell, from the cart.

Nobody answered.

Jab looked at the fire.

He looked at them round it. Pell on the cart with his foot on a coil of rope. Rook against the stone with his chest bound, off. Dee with her fingers on Rook's wrist and her book shut. Toren with the paper. Callie with the counter in its bag and her hands open on her knees, her nose stopped. And himself, with his warm palms and a fish.

They were not what they had been at the gate.

He knew that the way he knew his own floor. They were not what they had been at the Sag, or at the long table, or in the pen. Callie had held a lee off a lip over two people in the red and taken one of them out of it on purpose so it would hold. Toren had set a cart on an arch stone and walked it onto a post. He himself had watched a jar go, and said *jar* before it was dry. He had said *open it* and known what it cost. He had said *vent* before anybody told him to, and *no* to a tear. None of them could have done any of it on the forty-first morning.

And that was how they knew what the thing over the hill was doing.

A month ago he would have looked at the ash going uphill and thought it was the wind, or something in the ground, or nothing. He would not have counted it. He would not have known what ten and three was. He knew now. He knew it because it was the thing Dee had spent a fortnight of mornings teaching him at a long table in a shed that leaked. The thing that was going to keep all of them alive inside that fence. It was the thing he was for.

Something up there was doing it too.

"Tomorrow," said Toren.

They looked at him.

"Tomorrow we decide," said Toren. He folded the paper. "With the light, and the counter, and Pell, and the sky where we can see it. Not tonight." He looked round at them. "Tonight we settle. Callie and me and Dee, a Handful each, out of the open jar. And Jab sleeps. And Rook sleeps. And somebody watches the sky." He looked at the hill. "And counts it."

"I'll count it," said Jab.

"You'll sleep," said Dee.

"I'll count it for two armfuls," said Jab. "Then I'll sleep."

Dee looked at him for a long moment across the fire. Then she nodded, once, and turned back to Rook's wrist.

---

He counted it for two armfuls.

He sat with his back against the pump house wall with the fire going down, and the others asleep, or lying down, which was not the same. He could feel which was which without trying. Pell was asleep on the cart. Rook was not, and then was. Dee was not. Callie was settling with her palm flat on a paper on her knee, and then she lay down, and her breathing went slow. Toren was the last. He lay by the fire with his arm in its cuff against his chest and counted them, the way he always did, and Jab felt him do it, Pell and Dee and Rook longest and Jab and Callie, and then he slept.

Across the river, the fence. Beyond it the hill, a black shape against a sky that had gone to stars everywhere except the one place.

Over the flat top of the hill, the place where there were no stars. Round its edge the light the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened, and thinned, and thickened.

Ten, and three.

Down in the wash behind the ridge, a mile off in the dark, he knew without seeing it that the ash was going. The grey of the husks and the hounds, and the pale of his own jar that had been for the stair. It was going up the wash in threads toward the hill, a hand's width at a time, with no wind. It was not hauled. It was lifted. It was going the way it came, at the rate it came.

He took Little out of his coat and held it in his hand in the dark.

*Don't eat inside. Bring Little back. And don't be asleep.*

He was not asleep. He sat against the wall of the pump house with the fish in his fist and his warm palms, and counted the sky over the hill. He was more frightened than he had been on the slab in the fog, or in the pen with the box on the technician's hand. He was more frightened than on the step of the long building with his brother's face to the wall.

He was frightened because he understood it.

At the end of the second armful he put the fish back in his coat on the left side and buttoned it, and lay down by the fire with the others. He did not sleep for a long time. He lay with his eyes open and his hand over the small hard shape under his coat. Over the hill, the thing that was not a person, and was lifting, breathed.
