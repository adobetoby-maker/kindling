# Chapter 33 — Out: Four

Before the light, the six of them were where the night had left them.

Pell was asleep on the cart with his splinted leg out along the boards. Rook lay on his side on the canvas, where Dee had put him the night before so that his back was off the ground. Jab was asleep at last by the dead fire, curled on his side, with one hand flat over the left side of his coat. Callie was asleep with her hand on the hook. Toren lay on the gravel with his arm in its cuff against his chest and his eyes shut, and his lips were moving, because he was counting them. He did it every morning before he opened his eyes.

Dee was not asleep. She had not lain down again after her watch, and there was no gravel on her coat. She was kneeling beside Rook with two fingers on his wrist.

Toren's lips stopped moving when they came to Rook. He opened his eyes.

The sky over the river was grey, going white at the bottom. The pump house wall stood black above them. Across the river the fence ran north along the far bank, and behind it the hill went up into the last of the dark. Over its flat top there was still the place where the sky was wrong, with the light round its edge the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened, and thinned. Nobody counted it. They all knew what it came to.

"He's hot," said Dee, without looking round.

Rook's eyes were open. His face was wet, though the morning was cold enough to put frost on the cart.

"I'm warm," said Rook. "It's a warm morning."

"There's ice on the wheel," said Dee.

"Then I'm warm and the wheel's cold," said Rook. "We're both of us wrong."

Dee did not smile. She took the knot out of the canvas strip round his chest and lifted the pad off his back, and Toren got up and came and knelt on the other side of the canvas, where he could see.

The stitches ran in a short dark line between Rook's shoulders, small and neat and tight, the way Dee had put them in at the cut. The skin round them was red. The red went out a hand's width on each side of the line, swollen and shiny, and when Dee put the backs of her fingers near it, not on it, she took them away again at once.

"It's declared," she said.

"That was quick of it," said Rook.

Jab was standing at the end of the canvas. Nobody had heard him get up. He still had his hand on his coat, and he was looking down at Rook's back.

"He's been hot since the fourth hour," said Jab. "I felt it. I didn't know if I'd dreamed it."

"Put your hand over it," said Dee. "Don't touch."

Jab knelt and held his right palm an inch above the red, and shut his eyes. His face went still, the way it went at the long table. Toren watched it. So did Callie, who was up on one elbow by now with her hand still on the hook.

"There's nothing busy," said Jab after a while. "Not the grey. That's gone. We had all of that out." He did not open his eyes. "It's him. It's him, doing something. Fighting it. It's hot all through, not in bits." He took his hand away. "It's not the thing I can draw."

"No," said Dee. "It's the dog."

She said it to Toren, because Toren was the one who had written the dog down.

"The grey came out yesterday. What's left is what a hound's mouth puts in a man when it bites him. It's the same as a dog bite in a yard, or a nail in a foot." She laid the pad back on. "That's not Sustain. That's Sowerby's. And Sowerby's eighty miles off."

She opened her small bag and took out four folded papers, white, each the size of a thumb, tied together with thread. She untied one.

"Fever powder," she said. "He gave me four for the road. For a hound bite, or a wound gone bad, or a fever in the night." She looked at the paper in her fingers. "I'm to put it in boiled water and see he drinks all of it. I'm to do it once a day. I'm not to do it twice to make myself feel better."

"And if it doesn't break?" said Toren.

"Then by tomorrow night he'll be worse," said Dee. "And then we'll not be waiting for anything."

---

The water came first, then.

Dee's skin was empty. She had put the whole of it into Rook's back at the cut, a little at a time, and nobody had counted the others since. So Toren went to the cart and counted them: two skins hanging from the rail behind the driver's bench, one of them full, and the other a third full, near enough, when he hefted it.

"One and a third," he said.

"That's for drinking," said Dee. "Not for washing. Not for his back." She looked at the river. "The river's for the rest. If it's clean."

Callie was already on her feet with the counter out of its bag. She went down the gravel to the edge of the water above the pump house, where the river came down out of the north fast and dark over the stones, and she held the counter out over it at the length of her arm for ten breaths.

"Seven," she said. "The room." She walked down past the pump house and did it again, below the place where they had washed at dusk. "Nine. Our wash is in it."

"Above the pump house for drinking," said Dee. "Below it for washing. And nothing out of it goes in his back till it's boiled."

Boiling meant a fire, and a fire meant wood, and the pump house had none. It was stone, its roof was gone, and the thorn they had carried on the cart had burned the night before. So Toren and Callie went down the bank below the pump house, where the river had once run high and had left a line of wrack along the gravel a hand above the water. It was sticks and reeds and bleached old branches, dry, jammed among the stones. They picked along it for half a mile and came back with an armful each.

"That's one pot," said Callie. "Maybe two."

"Then it's one now and one tonight," said Dee.

She boiled the pot at the pump house wall. When it had cooled enough, she stirred the powder into a cup of it and held the cup while Rook drank, lying on his side with his hand round hers on the cup. He drank all of it, and he made a face at the end.

"Sowerby's," he said. "It tastes like his office."

"Drink the rest of the pot," said Dee. "Slowly. It's the last boiled water you'll see till dark."

Pell had not waited for anybody.

Duchess was tied to the near wheel by her rope halter, cropping the thin grass along the foot of the pump house wall, and she had not looked at the hill once. Above her, on the boards, Pell had got himself sitting up with his back against the rack behind the driver's bench and his splinted leg out straight in front of him. He had done it alone. His face said what it had cost him, and it also said that he did not want it mentioned, so nobody mentioned it.

Dee knelt on the tailboard beside the foot. She did not undo the splint. She pinched the end of his big toe, where it came out of the canvas strips, and watched it go white and then pink again.

"The blood's going round it," she said. "That's good. It's swelled under the strips. That's not." She loosened one knot a finger's width. "You'll not put it down."

"I've nowhere to put it down," said Pell. "I'll sit." He had the atlas open on his knee in its oilcloth. "A man on his back can't do much. A man sitting up can count."

"Count what?"

"Whatever wants counting." He looked across the river at the hill. "She said to count what comes out of that. The Director. It's on the paper." He did not look at Toren, who had the paper in his coat. "That's a thing a man can do sitting on a cart. I'll do it."

---

Dee measured their floors on the gravel by the cold fire as if the fire were the gate, and the three of them stood in a row for it without being told.

She did Jab first, the way she always did now. "A hand," she said. "Floor's a finger." She turned his palm up and held her own over it, not touching. "Cool. They've come down."

"They came down about the third hour," said Jab.

"I know. I was awake."

She did Callie. "Your stone's what you settled last night. All of it." She kept her fingers on the wrist. "Your own's come back. Not all. Three parts, near enough."

"I slept," said Callie.

"You slept like a stone in a well," said Dee. "I watched you. That's the only reason it's three parts."

She did Toren. He had been the last of them to settle. After Jab lay down, he had sat by the fire with his palm flat on a square of paper that held a Handful of jar C's ash, and he had let it come at the rate it came, which for Toren was most of an hour. It had been full dark by the time the paper was clean.

"Stone's full," said Dee. "Your own—" She waited, with her fingers on his wrist. "Two turns. Not the bit. The bit'll come back by noon, if you don't spend it before."

That was how she measured his own: in turns of the glass, as long as it would hold a strut with no stone under it.

"I'll try not to," said Toren.

"Don't try," said Dee. "Don't spend it."

She did her own last, with two fingers on her own wrist and her eyes on the hill, and she did not say it out loud. She wrote it in her book.

Then there were the coats. Senna's rules said coat off, shake it, wash at the river, and they had done all that the night before; but they had done it at dusk, in the last of the light, and now it was day. Callie went round them with the counter, and they stood still for it one at a time, the way they had stood for Dee.

Jab's coat had the long pale stain down its back where jar B had broken on the rail at the wash. Callie held the counter to it and waited.

"Eight," she said. "It's clean. It's only the jar." She looked at the stain. "It's the stair's ash. It's clean on you."

Dee's coat was twelve. Callie's own was fourteen. Pell's, up on the cart, was twenty, and Dee made him take it off and hand it down to her, and she shook it out downwind herself.

Toren's was thirty.

Everybody knew why. He had been outside the lee at the wash, killing two husks against it, with the grey going up over him in a burst each time. He stood in his shirt on the gravel with his arm in its cuff while Callie held the counter to the coat, and he was cold, and he did not say so.

"Wash it," said Dee. "In the river. Below the pump house. Then beat it on the stones till you're tired, and then some more."

He did it one-handed. He knelt at the edge of the water, pushed the coat under and held it there until his hand stopped feeling, which took ten breaths, and then he pulled it out and beat it on a flat stone and put it under again. After a while Jab came down the gravel, took the coat out of his hand without being asked, and did the beating for him. The grey went off it into the water in a thin cloud and away down the river.

"Sixteen," said Callie, when it had lain on the stones in the sun for an hour. "It's still in the seams."

"Sixteen's a coat," said Dee. "Thirty's a coat I'd not let inside."

Then the hook.

Callie held it up and read it herself. The curve read eighteen, because it had been down the loose bank at the wash twice and on the lip in the wind. Then she held the counter to the grip. The top of the conduit was wrapped in rag where her hands went, and the rag was old and grey and soft, and it had been wet with the wash and had dried on her hands.

"Thirty-four," said Callie. "It's the rag."

She took out her knife and cut it off. She did it carefully, in one long strip, without letting it touch her other hand. Then she stood with the strip hanging off the point of the knife and looked at Dee.

"Not the river," said Dee. "Not the fire."

So Toren dug a hole at the far end of the gravel, away from the water, with the heel of his boot, and Callie dropped the rag into it off the knife. He kicked the gravel back over it and laid a flat stone on top, and Callie chalked a cross on the stone with Pell's chalk. *Don't touch.* It was the second cross they had left on the road.

She washed the curve of the hook in the river with sand, scouring it the way her grandfather had scoured a scale pan, and when she read it again it said eleven. The grip was bare steel now, cold and smooth. Below where the rag had been, a paper tag was still tied round the conduit, where the grey coats had tied it in the pen. *pen, 35th.*

"You could cut that off too," said Toren.

"No," said Callie. "They'll want to write under it. When we get back."

Jab looked up from the wet coat at that. He did not say anything. He nodded, once, as if it were a thing he had been waiting for somebody to say.

---

At the third hour of the morning three of them went to look at the bridge in daylight: Callie with the counter, Dee with the glass, and Toren. Nobody went to look at anything alone any more. Jab stayed at the pump house with Rook and Pell and watched them go.

The bridge was thirty paces across. It was three planks wide, laid side by side on cross-beams, and the cross-beams sat on posts that went down into the river. The planks were grey and split along the grain and furred with old moss at the edges. On the upstream side, the north side, there was a rail, one long pole at the height of a man's hip, lashed to the posts with wire. On the downstream side there was no rail at all.

Below the bridge, downstream, there had been another crossing beside it. Two iron pipes, each as thick as a man's body, had gone over the river side by side on iron legs. The pipes came out of the river wall of the pump house on this bank, and on the far bank they went into the ground. In the middle the legs had gone, and the pipes had broken and dropped into the river, and their ends lay in the water with the current going white round them.

"That was the pump," said Dee. "River water, over to the hill."

"What for?" said Toren.

"I don't know," said Dee. "For something that needed water. Things like that always need water."

Callie walked out onto the bridge first with the counter held out in front of her. She did not look at the water. She looked at the planks.

"Seven," she said, at the first post. "Nine. In the middle." She went on, and at the far end, two planks short of the bank, she stopped and crouched.

"This one's gone," she said.

It was the middle plank. It had cracked across, right through, between two cross-beams, and the two halves sagged a finger's width in the middle. Through the crack, Toren and Dee could see the river.

"It'll take a person," said Callie. "On the outside planks. Not on this one." She put her hand flat on the plank on one side of it, and then on the plank on the other side. "Those are sound. They're old, but they're sound. This one's rotten at the beam."

"And a load?" said Toren.

"What load?"

"The carrier." He looked back across the river at the cart. "It has to come over. It can't come over on a back. It's got to come over on something."

Callie looked at the crack.

"Not on that," she said. She stood up and walked the last two planks to the bank, and the other two came after her, and the three of them stood at the gate.

The gate was a frame of iron as tall as a man and a half and as wide as a cart, with the same rusted wire as the fence stretched across it in squares as big as a hand. It hung from two hinges on an iron post on the right. On the left, where it met the other post, there was a chain. The chain went twice round the frame of the gate and the post, and its two ends had been brought together and joined. They had not been joined with a lock. They were joined with a twist of copper wire gone green, wound round and round on itself in a tight knot the size of a fist, with the ends tucked in.

Toren looked at it for a long time.

He could see the man who had done it. Somebody had stood here twenty years ago, on the last evening, and pulled the gate shut behind eleven people, and twisted a bit of copper through the chain so it would stay. He had not locked it. He had tied it, the way a man ties a gate he means to open again himself.

"His," said Toren. "The man who ran the crew."

"Maybe," said Dee.

"Gran said he wouldn't let anybody sleep inside." Toren did not take his eyes off the knot. "He'd not have left it open."

He did not touch it. Callie held the counter to it instead.

"Twelve," she said. "At the gate. It's fourteen through the wire." She held the counter out through one of the squares, into the fence, at the length of her arm. "Fourteen."

Then all three of them looked through the wire at Caul Hill.

---

Inside the fence the ground was flat for a hundred paces. It was a wide yard of old concrete gone to cracks and weeds, with gravel in the cracks. Beside the gate, on the inside, stood a squat grey hut with a door and one window, both black. Long low sheds ran along the south side of the yard with their roofs fallen in, and near them stood a tall thing on four iron legs, like a water butt on stilts, with its side rusted through in a long red wound.

The pipes came up out of the river bank on the inside and crossed the yard side by side on low concrete blocks to the foot of the hill, and then they went up the hill beside a road. The road climbed the hill in two long slants, back and forth, a great Z cut into its face. At the top of the Z, on the rim of the flat top, there was the building.

It was long. It was the longest building any of them had ever seen that was not the Sag's own berm. It ran along the rim of the hill from north to south, three rows of windows high, grey and flat-faced, and every window was black. It stood on the edge of the flat top like a wall somebody had built to keep the hill in. In the middle of it, at the top of the road, there was a door, and the door was a black square.

Behind the building, a long way up, over the flat top of the hill, there was the sky.

It was bigger this close. At dusk from the rise it had been the size of Callie's thumb at the end of her arm, and from here it was the size of a cart wheel. It did not hang over the building. It hung over something behind the building. Past the north end of the long building there was the top of a wall that was not straight: it curved, a great round wall higher than the building, broken off in jags at the top like a pot with its rim knocked off. The dark patch hung over that, and the light round its edge thickened and thinned over the broken rim.

"That's not the building," said Toren.

"No," said Dee.

"Gran never said anything about a round one."

"She said the main building," said Callie. "She said *inside the main building*. If you've got a main one, you've got others." She did not take her eyes off the yard. "Look at the ground."

They looked.

The yard was not bare. There was grey on the concrete, in the cracks and along the edges of things, but it was not heaped in drifts the way it had been in the wash. It lay in lines, thin, like hair that has been combed. Every line went the same way. The lines came across the yard from the south and the east, from the sheds and the fence, and they met at the foot of the road and went up it. The nearest one lay beside the gatehouse.

It slid a hand's width toward the hill, and stopped.

Nobody said the count out loud. Nobody needed to. Ten, and three.

"It's going up the road," said Callie. "All of it. I can see it on the first bend." She pointed with her chin. "On the top bend. And at the top—" She stopped. "It goes in the door. Some of it. Some goes round the end of the building, over the top. Toward the round thing."

"Some goes in," said Toren.

"Some goes in the door," said Callie. "Where we're going."

Dee turned her back on the fence.

She did it on purpose. She stood with her back to the gate, facing the two of them, and she took out her book and did not open it.

"Say what she said," she said. "The Director. The narrow thing."

Toren had it by heart. He had written it at the long table, and again on the back of the paper, and Dee had quoted it twice at the fire on the forty-sixth. He said it anyway, because she had asked.

"*You're going for the boxes. The live ones, with the wheel on the plate, in lead, on the cart. Two, three if you can. And you're going to look at that sky and count what comes out of it and write it down. You are not going into it. You are not going looking for whatever's under it. If it comes to you, you do what you have to do and you come home.*"

"*Into it,*" said Dee. "What's *it*?"

Toren looked back through the wire, at the round wall with its broken rim and the dark hanging over it.

"That," he said. "The round one. Whatever's in it. Under the sky."

"And the building?"

"The building's in front of it." He said it slowly and in order, the way Pell said a distance. "The door's at the top of the road, on the east side. The stair's inside it. Gran said down the stair, a door, and then a corridor, and the rooms off the corridor on the left. Eleven of them. She didn't say which way the corridor goes." He looked at the long grey face of the building. "If it goes along the building, north or south, it's under the building all the way. If it goes back into the hill, west, it's under the building for as long as the building's deep, and then it goes on under the top. Toward the round one."

"Which?"

"I don't know."

"Then we'll count doors," said Callie.

Dee looked at her.

"The building's about thirty paces deep," said Callie. "I can see its end from here, past the north corner. If the corridor goes west, and the rooms are eight paces apart, or ten, then three rooms are under the building. Four at the most. The rest are under the yard behind it." She did not look up at the sky. "The rest are nearer to that."

Dee opened the book then. She wrote in it standing up, with the book against her forearm.

"Three doors," she said. "If the boxes are in the first three rooms, they're ours. If they're not, they're under that, and they're hers, and she can decide what to do about them with a Sag held and a machine that can see." She wrote. "Nobody goes past the third door. Not to look. Not for a box. Not for anything."

"And the ash that goes in the door?" said Toren.

Dee shut the book.

"The ash that goes in the door is lying where it's lying," she said. "If it's in the first three rooms, we'll be standing in it. We knew that yesterday. It's why we've got two healers and a counter and a line." She looked at him. "It's not a thing. It's ash, going somewhere. If a thing comes to us, then it's come to us. She said what we do then."

They said all of it again out loud at the pump house, because Dee would not have anything decided that had not been said in front of everybody who would have to live with it.

Callie said the ash first. She stood by the cart with her hands open at her sides and said it flat, the way her mother said the store book.

"Inside, forty. Five whole, in the frame. The line's sixteen. That's two whole." She looked at Jab's frame, which stood against the pump house wall on two flat stones. "Twenty-four to work with. Three jars. Home, three. Twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." Dee was still looking at her, so she went on. "And two empty in the frame. A and C. And the road jar in the rack, empty. And five vent jars on the cart. And one socket with glass in it."

"Settled," said Dee.

"A Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in you." Callie stopped. "Jab carries none. Jab's the jar."

"Four going in," said Dee. "Not five. Say it."

"Four going in," said Callie. "Two healers." She did the sum with her lips not quite moving, and the others waited while she did it. "We priced forty-eight for five people. An hour in, two in the rooms, an hour out. The rooms were thirty." She looked at the hill. "It's not five. It's four. But it's four with two healers, not three, and one of them's got to be the jar and the watch in turns, all the time, and nobody's the reserve." She shook her head. "I can't make it come out better than it is. Twenty-four's four people for half the day, with nothing going wrong."

"Then we don't spend twenty-four today," said Toren.

They all looked at him.

"Twelve," he said. "Half. We go in, and we find the stair, and the door, and we find which way the corridor goes. We read the first room. If the boxes are in it, we get them in the carrier and we come out. If they're not, we come out anyway." His voice was steady, and he kept it that way. "Twelve spent is twenty-eight left. We come out at twenty-eight, whatever we've got. Then we've still got a day."

Dee looked at him for a long moment.

"Twenty-eight's today's line," she said. She wrote it. "Sixteen's the line. Both."

"And the rest," said Toren. "Say them."

They said them, and the four who were going in said them together. Red, and nobody trapped: everybody out, at once. Red, and a body trapped: the fewest who can do it stay, named. Two Flasks left inside: everybody out. A healer at their floor twice. A healer still warm after the watch. Jab below his floor once.

"One more," said Dee.

She said it to Jab, but she said it for all of them.

"There's two of us," she said. "You and me. If one of us goes down—hurt, or under, or anything—there's nobody to draw the one that's left. Not after. Not ever." She did not look away from him. "So if either of us is lost, for any reason, it's everybody out. That moment. Not when we've finished what we're doing."

"Everybody out," said Jab.

Toren wrote it on the back of the paper under the rest. *If a healer's lost, everybody out.* Under that he wrote the thing he had written on the step of eleven the night before they left home. *Everybody back.*

Rook had listened to all of it lying on the canvas with his eyes half shut, and nobody had asked him anything. When they had finished, he opened his eyes.

"And the outside," he said.

Dee turned.

"There's two of us out here," said Rook. "One can't walk and one's hot. We can't come in after you. We've both got that. I'd like it said, now, what we're to do if you don't come out."

"If you're not out by the time the hill's shadow's on the gate," said Pell, from the cart, "I'll know. I'll have been counting."

"And then?" said Dee.

Pell looked at Rook. Rook looked at Dee.

"Then I don't come in," said Rook.

"Say it again."

"Then I don't come in." He said it flatly, to the stones of the pump house wall. "I sit at the near end of that bridge where I can see the gate. I'm the only one of us who can get to the near end of the bridge, and I can get there lying down if I have to. If something comes out of that gate that isn't you, I shout. If it's you, I count you." He was quiet a moment. "If it's dark and it's nothing, I don't come in."

Dee knelt down beside him on the gravel.

"It's the Director's word," she said.

"It's yours," said Rook. "I know whose it is." He shut his eyes. "Go on. You've a day."

Then there was the carrier.

It stood in the cart behind the three home jars, on the straw where it had ridden for seven days. It was the size of a small chest, made of the depot's lead in sheets, folded and seamed, grey and dull, with a lid that sat down into a groove round the top and an iron handle at each end. Even empty it was so heavy that it had taken Pell and Toren and Jab together to set it on the cart in the north yard, and Toren had had only one arm to do it with even then.

"It doesn't go on anybody's back," said Toren.

"No," said Pell.

# Chapter 34 — The Yellow Stair

Inside the fence the ground was a floor, and Callie treated it like one from the first step.

"Stop," she said, three paces in.

They stopped. Toren was on the front rope beside her, Jab stood with the frame on the outside of the sledge, and Dee was at the back with the back rope and the glass. Nobody asked her why. She had read the pen on her knees for a quarter of an hour with a counter, and they waited while she read this standing up.

The yard was concrete, cracked into slabs, with weeds in the cracks. The combed lines of grey ran across it from the south and the east toward the foot of the road. Most of them were no wider than a finger, but they were everywhere, and there was no straight way from the gate to the road that did not cross forty of them.

"We'll drag the board over them," said Toren.

"We'll drag the board through them," said Callie. "And then it'll have grey on its bottom all the way up the hill, and down the stair, and into the room. And back." She looked along the yard. "And we'll walk in them. And it'll be on our boots."

"Then where?"

Callie looked for the clean way, one slab at a time, the way she had read the pen.

The pipes ran across the yard on their left, side by side on their concrete blocks, from the river bank to the foot of the hill. Each block was the size of a crate, and there was one every five paces, with the pipes lying rusted in the saddles on top. The combed lines went under the pipes, through the gaps between the blocks. But along the north side of the blocks, in their lee, the concrete was bare. The lines went round the end of each block and under it, and on the north side of every block there was a strip a pace wide where the grey had not settled, because the lines had somewhere else to be.

"Along the pipes," said Callie. "On the north side. Close to the blocks. It's clean there. Mostly." She took a step and held out the counter and waited ten breaths. "Eighteen. At the first block. It was twenty-four in the middle."

"Along the pipes," said Dee.

They went along the pipes.

At the gatehouse she stopped for a breath and looked in at the window.

On the back wall there were marks in white chalk. They were strokes in a row, the kind you make to count days, and Callie counted them without meaning to. Eleven. And a twelfth, begun: a short stroke that went down half as far as the others and stopped.

She knew whose they were. She thought about the two men who had slept inside on the second night because they were drunk, and been sick in the morning, and thought they had got away with it. Over the doorway there was a place where the tray sent a branch across. A smaller trough went from the tray over the corridor along the ceiling, and into the wall above the door through a square hole in the concrete the size of a hand. The rope went with it. A thinner rope, like a branch off a trunk, went across the ceiling in the small trough and in through the hole.

Callie stood in the doorway, and Toren unhooked the lantern from his belt and held it up past her shoulder, and they looked in.

The room was not large. It was six paces deep and five across, and the air in it was colder than the corridor. Along the right-hand wall there were big grey cabinets as tall as a man and twice as wide, with doors on their fronts, standing in a row. Along the left-hand wall there were racks. They were steel frames from the floor to the ceiling, the way Senna had said, but their backs had been torn off. The ends of torn metal stuck out at their edges where somebody had levered the backs away to get at what was behind them, and the wall behind them was bare, with holes in it where things had been fixed.

The boxes were on the floor.

They lay all along the foot of the left-hand wall in a long low heap under the empty racks, where they had been thrown. They were grey steel loaves, dozens of them, more than dozens. They lay on their sides and on their faces and on each other, tumbled, the way stones lie at the foot of a wall that has come down, and some had rolled out into the middle of the room. The dust of twenty years lay on all of them. Here and there one lay with its front up, and they could see the little dark window in it, and the slide across the window, and the plate riveted on beside it.

*So we left them where they fell. On the floor. Hundreds of them, in the end.*

"There," said Toren. His voice was not steady. "There they are."

The rope came down through the hole above the door and down the wall inside the room, just to the left of the doorframe, a line of grey as thick as two fingers, crawling. It reached the floor and went along the foot of the wall to the left, into the heap of boxes. In the heap it split. It went in among the boxes in finer strands, like roots going in among stones, under them and round them and between them, all through the heap. They could see the strands on the grey steel, grey on grey, where one lay over a box and went down the other side.

It was lying where the wire had been. It had gone where the crew had pulled the copper out, behind the racks and down, and through the boxes the crew had thrown on the floor to get at it.

Callie held the counter out at the length of her arm toward the heap and waited.

"Eighty," she said. "At the boxes. From the door."

Then she said the thing she had seen, plainly, the way her grandfather would have said it at the scale house in one line.

"The boxes are in the grey," she said. "And the grey's in the boxes. You can't put a hand on one without putting it in the other."

"Then nobody puts a hand on either," said Dee. "Callie. Read them. From the edge. With the counter. Don't touch."

Callie read them.

She went along the edge of the heap bent over, with the counter at a hand's width, never touching anything. Toren came behind her with the lantern in one hand and the paper in the other, the one he had copied the plate onto on the eleventh day, and he held it to each box that lay face up. Most of them had the wheel. Some did not. Some had a different mark, a square with a line through it, and those were bigger, and the counter at them said what the room said.

"Sixty-eight," said Callie. "It's dead. Seventy. Dead." She moved along. "Sixty-five. That's the room. Dead." She held the counter to a box lying on its side with its window toward her. "A hundred and ten."

She stopped.

"That's warm," she said. "At a hand. That one's alive."

"Wheel," said Toren, holding the paper to it. "Four spokes. And the bar. It's the same."

"Is the slide shut?" said Dee.

Callie looked. The steel slide lay across the little window all the way, in its groove.

"Shut."

"One," said Toren.

She went on. There were a great many dead ones. She read eleven more before she found another that said a hundred and six. It lay face up at the edge of the heap with a strand of grey lying across its top like a hair across a cheek, and its slide was shut.

"Two," said Toren.

There was a third, deeper in, half under two others, that made the counter jump before Callie had it at a hand. A hundred and forty. She held it there for ten breaths, and her face said she did not like it.

"Its slide's not shut," said Toren, bending with the lantern. "It's rusted. It's stuck halfway. The window's half open."

"Then it's not talking only to itself," said Dee, behind them. "It's talking to the room." She did not come nearer. "Leave that one."

"Leave it," said Toren, and wrote it.

They did the halt in the room, because it was twenty turns again.

This time Jab worked. He stood in the middle of the room with the frame on his back and drew them one at a time at the chest, off the open jar in Dee's hand, and vented into the concrete by the door, which was the room's floor and already dirty. Dee watched him and said *vent* before he needed it. When he had done the three of them, she took the leftovers out of his hands, and it took longer than it had on the road.

"Two," said Callie, weighing the jar. "Near enough. For the four of us, and the room." She gave it back. "Four and three quarters."

"We've been in forty turns," said Dee. "That's three and a quarter spent, and the jar says so. We've got twelve for today." She looked at the heap. "Get the two. Then we go."

It was Callie who got the first one into the carrier, and Toren who made the way for it.

He hung the lantern back on his belt and brought the sledge to the edge of the heap, as close as he could get it, and lifted the lid out of its groove one-handed and set it on the board. Then he stood at the carrier's side and put the foot of a strut down on the concrete floor at a slant, and let it come up out of the floor to the lip of the carrier like a plank laid from the ground to the tail of a cart.

"Set," he said. "Walk it up that."

Callie took the tongs. They were heavy, and long. She got the jaws round the box that said a hundred and ten, lying on its side, and closed the rings, and the jaws bit. She did not lift it. She could not have lifted it at the end of a yard and a half of iron, and she did not try. She dragged it out of the heap along the floor to the foot of Toren's strut, and turned it, and set its end on the strut. *Get the weight onto something that is not you.* She pushed it with the tongs, and it slid up the pale hard line of the strut toward the lip of the carrier, grating a little, and the strut took all of it. Toren stood behind the strut, soft, and counted under his breath. The box reached the lip and tipped, and went over into the carrier, and landed on the lead bottom with a sound like a door shutting in another house.

"One," said Toren. He let the strut go. "Four breaths."

The rope did not move.

Callie had been watching it the whole time, out of the side of her eye, the way you watch a dog you do not know. It lay in the heap in its fine strands and crawled west, ten and three, and it did not care about the box or the tongs or the strut or anything. It had been lying across the next box, the one that said a hundred and six, and it was still lying across it.

"The second one's got a strand over it," she said.

"Can you get the tongs under it?" said Toren.

"I can get the tongs round the box," said Callie. "The strand'll be on top of the jaw." She looked at it. "It's only grey."

"It's only grey going somewhere," said Dee. "Do it slowly. If it does anything, let go of the tongs."

Callie was reaching for it when Jab said, "Three."

He said it quietly, behind her, by the door. He was counting.

Callie looked up.

In the far corner of the room, where the racks ended at the back wall, there was a heap of grey. Callie had seen it and not thought about it. It was larger than the others, heaped up in the corner against the back wall at the end of the boxes, and the rope went into it. All the fine strands from the heap of boxes ran along the foot of the racks and into it, the way the roots of a tree go down into the ground.

At Jab's *three*, it moved.

It moved outward. Everything else in that place had moved inward all afternoon, and this moved out. The grey in the corner swelled as if something under it had drawn a breath. It heaved up, and cracked along the top, and something came up out of it the colour of an old wasp nest.

It stood.

"Husk," said Callie.

It had no face. It turned toward her, toward the tongs in her hands and the lantern and the worked things in the room, and it came along the foot of the racks at her.

Toren went past her. He had the spike on his fist before she saw it come, dark and heavy and crooked, and he lit the heel of it, the hand's width along the hook, and put it into the husk as it came, low. He dragged it round against the racks and put the point in.

It came apart. It came apart all at once, the way they did, into grey. There was no wind down here, so the grey did not go up in a burst the way it had at the wash. It fell. It came down on the boxes and the floor in a heap, a husk's worth of loose grey, and lay there.

"A hundred," said Callie, with the counter out. "It's a hundred, near the corner."

"Back," said Dee. "Everybody back from it. To the door."

They went back to the door. Toren let the spike go dark. The four of them stood in the doorway, with the lantern hanging from Jab's frame, and looked at the heap of grey on the floor where the husk had been.

It lay there for ten breaths.

Then it began to go. It went in threads, the way everything went. They came out of the heap and went along the floor toward the foot of the racks, toward the strands of the rope, and into them. It was faster than the threads in the yard, much faster. The whole heap drew out into lines and slid into the rope and was taken along it into the corner, into the big heap, in ten breaths, and the floor where the husk had come apart was bare concrete again.

Three.

The heap in the corner swelled.

None of them could have looked anywhere else. It swelled the way it had before, all at once, as if it had drawn a breath. It heaved up, and cracked along the top, and the thing that came up out of it was the colour of an old wasp nest. It stood, and turned toward the lantern.

"It's the same one," said Callie.

# Chapter 35 — A Hole in It

The husk came at the lantern again.

The lantern hung from Jab's frame, and Jab was standing in the doorway with the frame on his back, so the husk came along the foot of the racks at Jab. It had no face to come with. It came anyway, low, grey on grey, with the dust going up off the boxes where it passed them.

Toren killed it again. He did it the same way, because it had worked: the lit heel, low, hooking, the drag round against the racks, and the point. It came apart, and the grey fell in a heap on the floor a pace from the corner.

"Don't," said Dee.

She said it a breath too late, and the others could hear in her voice that she knew it.

"Don't kill it," she said. "Not again. Every time you kill it, it's on the floor. And then it's in the air." She did not take her eyes off the heap. "Callie."

"A hundred and ten," said Callie. "At the door."

Jab was counting. His lips moved with it the way they moved over Tonk's breathing. Ten breaths, and the heap drew out into threads, all of them at once, and went along the floor into the strands at the foot of the racks, and along the strands into the corner, and the floor was bare. Three breaths, and the corner swelled.

Jab felt it swell.

That was the thing he had not expected. He had felt the pull all afternoon, ever since the stair. It was the same pull he had felt with his palm on the stone at the mouth of the cut, only nearer, and harder: flat, open, patient, pulling at everything loose, and pulling past him. It had nobody in it. It pulled for ten, and stopped.

But at the three it did something else. It pushed.

He felt it the way he felt a vent, because it was the same thing. It was a palm going flat on a floor and something going out of it for three breaths into somewhere that was not the palm. Only this went out into the corner, into the heap. And the heap stood up.

"It's venting," said Jab.

They all looked at him. In the corner the husk was up, and turning.

"At the three," said Jab. "It draws for ten, and then it vents for three. Like we do. Only it doesn't vent into the floor to be rid of it. It vents into the heap. On purpose." He heard his own voice and did not stop it. "It vents where it wants a thing. And the thing stands up."

The husk came.

---

Toren did not kill it the third time.

He stood in front of Jab in the doorway with the spike dark on his fist, and when the husk came along the racks at them he did not light the heel. He put the foot of a strut down on the concrete between himself and the husk, at a slant, leaning away from them, the way you lean a plank against a door you want kept shut. It came up out of the floor thin and pale and hard, as high as his chest.

The husk walked into it. It hit the strut the way a husk hits anything, with all of itself, without looking, and the strut took it and did not move. The husk stood against the pale slant of it, pushing, and the push went down the strut into the floor and did not come up into Toren, because he was soft behind it.

"Holding," said Toren.

"How long?" said Dee.

"I don't know. It's not a wheel. It's pushing. It keeps pushing." His voice was tight. "Every breath it's on it, I pay."

"Then we've got a strut's worth of time," said Dee, "and I'd like to spend it thinking."

Callie had the counter out at the corner.

"The corner's not as big," she said. "The heap. It's smaller than it was before the first one stood up. It's made two husks out of it, and one's walked back in, and one's on the strut." She moved the counter along the racks. "The strands go into it. All of them. From the boxes. From the rope. That's what feeds it."

"Then cut it," said Toren. He said it through his teeth, to the husk. "Cut the strand. Between the corner and the rest. If it can't get to the heap, it can't send anything to the heap."

Dee looked at the strands at the foot of the racks, running into the corner like roots.

"Callie," she said. "Take the strut."

Jab had heard about it. They had done it in the yard with the rack, and again in the pen, and they had the words for it. But he had never seen Callie take a hold off Toren, and now he saw it, in the doorway of a room under a hill, with a husk pushing on the end of it.

"Taking," said Callie.

She put the back of the hook's curve down on the concrete at the foot of the strut, still, and they all saw it go pale.

"Yours," said Toren.

"Mine."

"Letting go," said Toren. The strut went, and the husk leaned into the space where it had been, and the hook was there. It stood across the husk's way, a foot of pale curve on the floor and four feet of conduit up to Callie's hands. The husk hit it and stopped as if it had walked into a wall. Because it had.

Toren went round it.

He went along the foot of the racks past the husk, on the side away from it, to where the strands ran in a bunch along the floor into the corner. There were six or seven of them, as thick as fingers, crawling into the heap. He lit the heel of the spike and nothing more. He put the lit hook down across the strands and dragged it through them, all of them, in one long pull, the way you cut through the cords of a bale.

They came apart where he cut them. There was a gap in them now: a hand's width of bare concrete, with the strands lying on one side of it going into the corner, and the strands lying on the other side of it going into the boxes.

"It's cut," said Toren.

Jab was counting again. It was the ten. The strands on the boxes' side of the cut crawled toward the corner the way they had all afternoon, and came to the gap, and the grey at their ends went on a little way onto the bare concrete and stopped. The strands on the corner's side crawled away from the gap into the heap. The gap stayed where it was.

"It's working," said Toren.

Three.

The strands on both sides of the gap pushed. The corner's side pushed out toward the gap, and the boxes' side pushed out toward the gap, and their cut ends grew. They grew grey across the bare concrete toward each other, a finger, two fingers, like two lines of ants meeting on a floor, and they met, and the gap was gone. The strands lay along the foot of the racks into the corner, whole, as they had been before, and at the end of the three they began to crawl again.

"It mended," said Callie.

"It mended in the three," said Jab.

Toren stood by the corner with the heel still lit on his fist, looking down at the place where the gap had been. There was nothing there now but strands.

"Every time," he said.

"Every time it vents," said Jab. "That's what it's for. The vent. It draws, and it vents where there's a hole, and then the hole's not there." He did not know how he knew it. He knew it the way he had known about the hands in the ditch. "That's all it does."

"Then we stop it getting any more," said Callie.

She was still holding the husk on the hook. It pushed against the pale curve of the steel and did not move it, and she was paying for that off her stone, and her face said what it cost.

"The floor," she said. "It's getting it off the floor. The threads come in at the door, all afternoon, out of the corridor. Off the stair. Off the whole hill." She looked at the doorway where Jab stood. "If I put it across the door, they can't come in. Then all it's got is what's in here."

"And that?" said Dee, with her chin at the husk.

"Toren."

Toren came back round, letting the heel go dark as he came, and set a strut in front of the husk beside the hook, soft. "Taking," he said. "Holding." Callie said "Letting go," and lifted the hook off the floor, and the husk leaned on the strut and not on her.

She went to the doorway. Jab stepped back into the corridor to let her through, and she stood in the doorway facing out, and put the back of the curve down on the concrete of the threshold, still, across the width of the door. The curve went pale.

From the curve, the lee stood up.

It stood up off the outside of the curve into the corridor, on the side the bend faced. It was a pale thin wall, the width of the doorway and the height of Callie's shoulder, standing on the threshold like a board across a gap in a fence. It kept off whatever was on the other side of it. It could not keep off anything that came round the end of it, but there was no end: the door frame was the end, on both sides.

Jab, out in the corridor, looked down at the floor round his own feet.

The threads were coming. They came along the floor of the corridor from the stair and from the steel door, and from the old heap inside the door, in their thin grey lines. They came along the foot of the wall on the room side, the wall with the doors in it, and they turned in toward the doorway the way water turns in toward a drain. At the lee they stopped.

They came up to the pale foot of the wall where it stood on the threshold, and they could not go through it, and they could not go under it, because the curve was on the floor and still. They piled up against it. Ten breaths, and they came on and bunched against the foot of the lee in a little grey ridge. Three breaths, and they stopped. Ten, and they came on, and the ridge grew.

"It's stopping them," said Jab.

"It's stopping the floor," said Callie.

She was looking up, and Jab looked up with her. Over her head, over the doorway, the rope went across the ceiling in its small trough and into the wall through the hole. It had not stopped. It crawled across the ceiling over the top of the lee, and over Callie, and in through the square hole in the concrete, and down the wall inside the room beside the door frame, and along the floor to the boxes and the corner. Ten, and it crawled. Three, and it pushed.

In the corner, the heap swelled a little.

"It's not the floor," said Callie. She said it slowly, looking at the rope over her head. "It's never been the floor. The floor's slow. The rope's fast." She looked at the ridge of grey against her lee. "I can stop what crawls. I can't stop the rope. It goes over the top."

"Then it's the rope," said Toren, from the husk.

"It's the rope."

"How much?" said Dee.

Callie went down to look, with the lee still up. "A quarter," she said. "Of the stone. Near enough. For that." She lifted the curve off the threshold. "Down."

The lee went out. The ridge of grey on the floor of the corridor did not wait. It went in over the threshold in threads, all of it, toward the boxes, and Jab came back into the room behind it.

Toren had been holding the husk for a long time.

They could all see it in his face. The husk leaned on the strut and pushed, and pushed, and did not tire, because there was nothing in it to tire. Toren stood behind the strut with his left arm against his chest and his right fist clenched on nothing, soft, paying by the breath.

"I can hold it," said Toren. "I can't hold it all day."

"Nobody's asking you to," said Dee.

"Then what?"

"Kill it," said Jab.

They looked at him.

"Kill it," said Jab again. "And I'll lift it. Before it goes." He had watched the heap on the floor the second time, lying there for ten breaths before the threads took it, and he said so. "It lies there for ten. Loose. Lying still on a floor."

Dee understood him before the others did. They could see it arrive in her face.

"It's loose," said Jab. "It's lying there. I can lift loose. It's what I did on the east track every morning for a month." He looked at the canvas bag on Toren's shoulder. "And I put it in a vent jar, and I put the stopper in. And then it's in a jar."

"And it can't get it out of a jar," said Dee slowly.

"I don't know," said Jab. "I don't know anything. It can't get the jars on my back. It's been an hour and it hasn't gone near them. It goes for what's lying about."

Dee looked at the husk on the strut, and at the corner, and at the counter on Callie's hip.

"It's dirty," she said. "What you'd be lifting. It's off this floor. It'll have the hill in it. It's not going in a jar, it's going in your hand first."

"I know."

"Clean in the left, then," said Dee. "Off the jar, while you lift. Hold it high. Don't let it into your wrist. Into the vent jar, and stopper, and off. One try." She held out the open jar. "One."

Toren let the strut go. The husk fell forward off it, and as it came up he lit the heel and killed it: heel, drag, point. It came apart at the foot of the racks, and the grey fell in its heap.

Jab was already there. He had come round from the doorway while Toren struck, with the frame on his back, and he crouched by the heap. He did not kneel. Nobody sits down inside the fence, and a knee on that floor was as near to sitting as he wanted to go. Dee crouched beside him with the jar against his left palm. He put his right hand flat on the heap.

It was warm.

It was not like the drift at the wash, warm all the way through. It was loose grey, and most of it was cold, the cold with nobody in it. But there was warm grit all through it, fine, the way sand gets into a loaf. It was the hill. He felt it under his palm, busy, with the cold all round it, and he let it come.

It came up into his palm slow and cold, at the rate it came, and the warm grit came with it. The clean cold from the jar came in at his left hand and went through him and held the warm grit away from his wrist, and he held it high.

And the heap was going. It was going out from under his palm. It was being lifted from underneath, from the far side, from the strands at the foot of the racks. Something else had its hand flat on the same heap and was lifting it too, and it was better at it than he was.

He had never lifted against anybody. There had been nobody on the east track to lift against. He did not haul, because he knew what hauling got you. He held his palm flat and let it come at the rate it came, and the heap went out from under his palm in threads toward the racks faster than it came to him.

Ten breaths.

He took his palm up with a palm's worth of grey in it, a little mound held high and open, cold, with the warm grit in it. Toren had a vent jar out of the bag with the stopper out, and he held it with its mouth up. Jab put the mouth of the jar against his palm and tipped his palm into it. The grey went down out of him into the glass, the way it had gone out of Rook's hand into the jar on the east track, and lay in the bottom on the pale spent ash. Toren put the stopper in with the heel of his hand and pressed it home.

"Three," said Callie.

The corner swelled, and the husk came up out of it.

It came up shorter. They all saw it. It stood up out of the heap in the corner, grey and faceless, and turned toward them and came along the racks, and its head, or the top of it where a head would be, was a hand lower than it had been. It was thinner. It was a husk with a bite out of it.

"It's short," said Callie. "It's short by what's in the jar."

Toren let the heel go dark and set a strut in front of it, and it walked into that.

Jab crouched by the racks with his right palm still held up, open. It was tingling. Dee put her own palm flat on it and drew, and found the warm grit left in his creases, the little of the hill that had come up with the lift, and took it, and turned, and put her palm on the floor.

"Clean," she said.

Callie held the counter to the vent jar in Toren's hand.

"Forty," she said. "At a hand. It's hot. It's the floor in a jar."

"It's the floor in a jar," said Dee. "And the floor's a hand less." She looked at the corner, and at the short husk on the strut. "It can't get it back. It doesn't even try."

"It can't reach into a jar," said Jab.

It was not a question, and Dee did not answer it. She wrote it.

It was not enough, and they could all see that it was not.

A palm's worth, for a whole ten breaths, crouched on a dirty floor with the counter at a hundred and ten, and the corner had given it all back less a hand. And the rope went on coming in over the door and down the wall into the boxes, crawling, filling the corner again.

"It's lifting faster than me," said Jab.

"It's got more hands," said Callie.

Jab stood up. He looked at the rope where it came down the wall beside the door frame, as thick as two fingers, grey, crawling down the concrete, ten and three. It was the thing that fed the corner, and the corner was the heap that made the husk, and everything came down the rope.

He had lifted off a heap, and the heap had been lifted out from under him from the rope's end. But the rope itself was a different thing.

"It's ash," he said. "The rope. It's all ash. It's loose ash going somewhere."

Dee turned and looked at him.

"I lift ash," said Jab. "Flat. Open. At the rate it comes." It sounded simple when he said it. It was the same thought he had had on the east track the first morning, that lifting was the most ordinary work there was. "If I put my hand on the rope, and lift, it's not going to the corner. It's coming to me. And into the jar."

"It's going west," said Dee. "The rope. It's not lying there. It's going. You'll be lifting a river."

"Then I'll lift what goes past."

"What's in it's not a body," said Dee. "You don't know what it is."

"I know what it's doing." Jab looked at her. "It's doing what I do. I felt it vent. I know when it's going to vent." He did not look away. "Let me take one lift off it. Off the rope, by the door, where it comes down. One. And I'll come off when you say."

Dee looked at him for a long moment.

Jab knew what she was doing. She was doing the sum she always did, the one with a price on every line. And she was doing the other one, the one Rook did, where you put your palm over the thing and shut your eyes and see what's there. He had watched her learn to do both on the road. He did not know which one she was doing now.

"One," she said. "Clean in the left. Hold it high. Off when I say, and not a breath after." She held out the jar. "I'll say *off* at the ten."

He put his right palm flat on the rope.

He put it where the rope came down the wall beside the door frame, at the height of his chest, a hand below the hole. Behind him Dee held the open jar against his left palm, and Callie stood by the door with the counter, and Toren stood behind his strut with the short husk on it and watched Jab's face.

The rope was dry and soft under Jab's hand, the way the heap had been, and it was moving. He could feel it crawl west under his palm a hand's width, the whole of it together. Ten breaths. He let it come.

Nothing came.

It was not like the heap. It was not lying there. It was going past his palm, all of it, the way water goes past a hand held flat in a stream. He tried to lift what went past, and it went past. It did not come up. It went on down the wall and into the room and away, ten breaths, a hand's width, and none of it came to him. It had somewhere to be.

"Nothing," he said. "It's going past."

Three.

And then it came.

It came all at once. The rope stopped crawling, the way it stopped at every three, and it pushed. It pushed out the way it had pushed into the corner. It pushed into the corner, and into the cut ends of the strands, and into anywhere there was a hole.

His palm was a hole.

He felt it find his hand. He felt the rope feel the place where something was drawing on it, lifting, taking, the way a hole takes, and it pushed into that place. It pushed into his palm.

It was warm. All of it was warm, the hill and the grit and the loose grey together, and it came up into his palm like hot water up a pump. The clean cold from the jar came into his left hand and met it and held it off his wrist. For a breath. For two.

Three.

Ten.

It did not stop. The ten came, and the rope did not draw back what it had pushed. It was in him now. It was his hand's, and it was not loose any more. His palm was still flat on the rope, and still drawing, because that was what his palm did when it was flat on something. It was lifting. It was taking. The rope felt the hole in it still there, still taking, and it did not draw. It kept coming.

He understood it then, all at once, the way you understand you have stepped off the edge of a bank.

It thought he was a hole in it.

It thought he was a wound, and it was mending him. It was doing to him what it had done to the cut in the strands and the heap in the corner. It had felt a place where something was being taken away, and it was pouring into the place to fill it. Behind the rope there was the corridor, and the tray, and the stair, and the yard, and the whole of the hill: everything lying where it fell.

It would never stop. There was nobody in it to stop.

The warm went into his wrist. He felt it go past the heel of his hand, where the clean cold had been holding it, and up into the wrist and the forearm. It was not hot sand. It was hot water, and it went up his arm the way water goes up a wick.

He could not take his hand off.

He could. He knew he could. His hand was only flat on a rope of grey on a wall, and there was nothing holding it there but him. But his palm was drawing, and the thing under it was pouring, and every part of him that had ever put a hand on Tonk wanted to keep drawing. It was a body that needed everything, and there was more of it, and more, and he could take it. If he kept his hand there he could take all of it. It was what he was for.

It was like Tonk. It was exactly like Tonk. It was the frame on his back on the road, and the fever in the night, and the bad leg, and a small hot body that needed every part of him, every day, for eighteen days, and more. He had given it every part. He had not minded. He had not known there was an end to him until the day they lifted his brother off his back at the gate, and he stood in the yard with his hands empty and did not know what they were for.

He could not remember his own floor.

He looked for it. He went down to look, the way Dee had taught him, and there was warm water all the way down, and he could not find where he stopped.

*Don't be asleep.*

It was Tonk's voice. It was not Tonk. It was a thing Tonk had said to him with a wooden fish in his fist and his face furious. *Don't eat inside. Bring Little back. And don't be asleep.*

He was going to sleep. That was what this was. He was going somewhere warm and wide with no floor in it, and he was going to stay there, drawing, while the whole hill poured into his hand.

"Off," said Dee.

He heard it. He did not move.

"*Off*," said Dee.

She took his wrist.

---

She did not ask him a third time. She put her own hand round his right wrist, the whole of her hand, hard, and pulled, and his palm came off the rope with a small dry sound, like a strip of cloth off a wound.

The warm stopped coming. It stopped at once, the way water stops when you take the jug away. But what had come was in him. It was in his palm and his wrist and his arm to the elbow, hot, all the way through, and it did not hurt. It was worse than hurting. It was full.

"Vent," said Dee. "Into the jar. Now. Toren—"

Toren had the second vent jar open in his good hand, with the strut still up and the husk still leaning on it. Jab put his right palm on the jar's mouth and let it go.

It went. Some of it went. It went out of his palm into the glass, warm and gritty, and he held his hand on the jar mouth for three breaths, and five, and ten, and it kept going. The counter on Callie's hip was ticking faster than it had all afternoon. Jab's arm was still warm all the way up, and the palm was hot.

"Off the jar," said Dee. "Give me the arm."

She did not wait for him to give it. She took his forearm in both her hands and put her palms flat on it, one above the wrist and one below the elbow, and he felt her find it. She drew.

He knelt. He did not mean to. His knees did it, the way Callie's legs had sat her down at the mouth of the cut. He was on his knees on the floor inside the fence, and Dee was crouching over him with her hands flat on his arm, taking the warm out of him a handful at a time, and turning, and venting into the floor, and coming back.

"Callie," said Dee. "Jar."

Callie had it. She held the open jar against the back of Dee's hand so that Dee could draw off it without letting go of him, and the clean cold came through Dee into Jab's arm from a long way off.

"Busy," said Dee, to herself. "Busy. Busy." And after a long time, "Less."

Jab looked at his palm.

It was white. Not all of it. There was a place on the heel of it, where he had pressed it flattest against the rope, the size of a coin. It was white and shiny, like skin that has been scalded. When he put his thumb on it, he could not feel his thumb.

"Busy?" said Dee. She had seen him look. She put her palm over the white place an inch off, and shut her eyes.

He knew before she said it. He could feel it himself, now that the arm was coming down. The heel of his palm was not busy. It was done, the way an old leaning wall is done. It had happened while his hand was on the rope, and it had fixed.

"It's done," said Dee. She did not open her eyes. "That's in the done part. I can't draw it."

"I know," said Jab.

"The rest's coming out." She moved her hand to his wrist. "The rest'll come. That won't."

He knelt on the floor and let her work, and he did not look at the white place again.

"Jar," said Callie.

She said it before the jar was dry. It was Jab's word, and she said it the way he said it, early.

"That's one," said Dee. She did not stop drawing. "Open two."

Callie reached over Jab's shoulder to the frame on his back. Her hand went along the top row, feeling for one of the full ones and not the empties, and lifted one out by the neck, and they heard the stopper come out.

"Jar two," said Callie. "Open. Three whole left."

"Say the one."

"The first's done," said Callie. "It went on the four of us, and the yard, and the room. And Jab's lift. And that." She held the new jar against the back of Dee's hand. "Eight out of the first. We're at thirty-two. Jar two's open."

"Twenty-eight's today," said Dee.

"I know."

"Four more, and we're at today's line."

"I *know*, Miss Wren."

Dee took her hands off Jab's arm and sat back on her heels. His arm was still warm from the elbow down, but it was the warm of a bruise now, not the warm of water, and his palm was white at the heel.

"Your floor," said Dee.

He went down to look, and it was there. He could find it now. A finger, where it had always been. And above it, his own: a hand. Less than a hand.

"It's there," he said.

"I know it's there," said Dee. "I was looking for it the whole time. I'd not have stopped till I found it." Her voice was quite steady. "Stand up, Jab. Nobody sits down inside the fence. That goes for knees."

He stood up.

The short husk was still on Toren's strut. It had never stopped pushing. Toren stood behind the strut with his face grey and wet and his teeth together, soft, paying.

"Toren," said Dee. "How long?"

"Since he put his hand on it," said Toren. "The whole of that."

Jab stood in the middle of the room with his right hand hanging at his side and looked at the rope on the wall.

It had not changed. It came down beside the door frame, as thick as two fingers, and crawled into the room, ten and three. There was nothing on it to show where his hand had been.

He thought about what it had done. It had not wanted him. There was nothing in it to want anything. It had felt a hole and poured into the hole, the way it had poured into the cut in the strands and into the corner. It had done it to him because he was drawing, because he was a place where something was being taken away, and it would have poured into him until there was no more of him to pour into.

It had no floor. That was all it was. It was a thing that drew, and vented wherever there was a hole, and had no floor, and nobody to find the floor for it. It would go on doing it until everything on the hill was in it.

And he had nearly done the same.

He did not know how to think that, so he stood still and let it be there. He had put his hand on the rope because that was what he did, and it had been exactly like Tonk, and he had wanted to keep his hand there. He had wanted to take all of it. He had not been able to find where he stopped, and Dee had had to find it for him.

He put his left hand on his coat, over the left side, and felt the small hard shape of the fish through the cloth. The body. The tail. The place where the fin had been.

*Bring Little back.*

He would. He had been nearly asleep, and he was not asleep now.

"It's got no floor," he said.

Dee looked at him.

"That's what's wrong with it," said Jab. "It draws and it vents, and it doesn't stop, because there's nobody to say *off*." He looked at the white place on his palm. "I'd not have stopped either."

"No," said Dee. "You'd not." She was quiet for a moment. "That's why there's two of us."

"Miss Wren," said Callie.

She was in the doorway with the counter held out into the corridor, and her voice was flat. It was the voice she used for a number she did not like.

"The rope," she said. "In the corridor. It's getting fatter."

Jab went to the door. He went past Toren and the husk and the strut, and stood beside Callie in the doorway, and they looked along the corridor to the west, as far as the lantern light went before it stopped.

The tray ran along the far wall into the dark, and the rope lay in it, grey, crawling. It had been as thick as a wrist. Now it was as thick as a forearm, and it filled the tray, and the tray was bowed down between its iron brackets under the weight of it. It was getting thicker from the west; they could both see it. Further along, at the edge of the lantern light, past the second door, the rope in the tray was as thick as a leg. It bulged out over the edges of the trough, and the bracket under it had bent.

And it was not only crawling west any more.

The grey in it still went west, ten and three, as it had all afternoon. But the rope itself was getting thicker from the west end, and the thickness was coming along it toward them. It came the way a swallowed thing goes down the neck of a snake.

Jab felt it before he saw it. The pull had been flat all afternoon, and far off, and patient. Now it was near. It was very near, and it was big, and it had nobody in it at all. It was like standing next to a mill wheel you cannot see, in the dark, and feeling the air move.

"Something's on it," said Jab. "Coming."

At the very edge of the light, past the third door, the dark moved.

There was a heap of grey on the floor of the corridor there, and it had not been there before. It was higher than a man's knee, higher than his waist, and it lay across the corridor from wall to wall, with the rope in the tray going down into it from above. It was coming toward them. It was not walking, because it had nothing to walk with. It was pulling itself along its own rope, the way a man pulls himself along a rope hand over hand, only there were no hands. The grey went forward, and the heap came after it.

At the three, in the middle of the heap, something opened.

It was a line of light the colour of the edge of the sky, the colour of the bottom of a pond. It opened in the middle of the grey like a mouth that was not a mouth, for three breaths. Then it shut.

Then came the ten, and the heap came on.

"Callie," said Dee, very quietly.

"A hundred and thirty," said Callie. "At the door." And then, "A hundred and forty."

The counter on her hip had changed its sound. It was not ticking any more. It had gone to the thin high sound it had made at the wash, like a wasp in a jar.

"A hundred and fifty," said Callie. "Red. The needle's not stuck. It's red."

"Red, and nobody trapped," said Dee. "Everybody out. Now."

"The box," said Toren.

He was still at the strut with the husk on it. He looked at the carrier on its board by the heap of boxes, with one box in it and the lid lying on the board beside it.

"Lid on it," said Dee. "Leave the box. Out."

Toren let the strut go. The husk fell forward onto the floor where the strut had been, and began to get up. He did not wait for it. He took the lid off the board with his good hand and dropped it into the groove on the carrier, where it went in with a soft heavy sound, and he turned.

"Out," said Dee. "Stair. Toren last."

Jab went.

# Chapter 36 — The Door That Opens Toward You

They came out of the room in the order Dee had said and turned east, toward the stair.

Jab went first, with the frame on his back and the lantern swinging from it, walking fast and holding his right hand away from his side. Dee went behind him with the open jar in the crook of her arm and the glass in her fist, and Callie behind her with the hook. Toren came last. They kept to the room side of the corridor, along the wall with the doors in it, away from the tray, and the steel door stood open twelve paces ahead of them with the stair beyond it.

Nobody ran. Running was how you went down on a floor like that, and Toren had one arm.

Behind them, in the room, the short husk had got up off the floor, and they heard it come out after them, the dry soft sound of it on the concrete.

Toren did not look round for it. He knew where it was the way he knew where the others were.

He looked west instead, once.

The heap was at the second door now. It filled the corridor from wall to wall, grey, higher than his waist, with the rope in the tray going down into it from above like a rope going into a well. It came on. It was slower than a man walking, and faster than he had thought anything that size could be. At the three the line of light opened in the middle of it, the colour of the edge of the sky, and stayed open for three breaths, and shut.

Toren turned his back on it and walked east.

"Door," said Callie, ahead of him.

They had all stopped. Jab was standing a pace short of the steel door with his lantern held up, and he was not going through.

There had been a heap on the corridor side of that door since before any of them was born: twenty years of grey come under the gap in threads. It had lain against the steel a hand deep all afternoon while its threads went west. It was not lying now. It had swelled. It had drawn itself up into a hump against the wall beside the door, between them and the doorway, the way the corner in the room had drawn up. Toren came up behind the others just as the push came. At the three the hump cracked along the top, and something came up out of it.

"Husk," said Jab. "In front."

It stood up in the corridor a pace from the steel door, faceless, between them and the stair, and it turned toward the lantern on Jab's frame.

Toren looked back. The short husk was coming along the wall behind him, and the heap was coming behind the husk, past the second door, toward the first.

He did the sum, and it was not a long one. A husk in front and a husk behind, and a thing he did not have a name for behind that. And every one of them, if it was killed, would lie on the floor for ten breaths and stand up again.

"Callie," he said. "Hold the front one."

She did not ask him how. She put the back of the hook's curve down on the concrete in front of the husk, still, and it went pale, and the husk came at the lantern and walked into the hook and stopped.

"Holding," said Callie.

"Jab. Dee. Round it. Through the door. Up."

There was room. The hook stood across the corridor at a slant, from Callie's hands down to the curve on the floor, with the husk pushing against the curve, and between the curve and the open doorway there was a gap a pace wide along the tray wall. Jab went through it sideways, with the frame scraping the concrete, and the husk turned its blank front toward him as he passed and did not come, because the hook was in its way. "Let it come." He had the spike on his fist, and he did not light it. "I'll hold the door."

Callie let go. She lifted the curve off the floor and went through the gap sideways past the husk, and the husk came after her, to the doorway, to the steel frame she had just gone through.

Toren went past it on the other side.

He did not go past it the way Callie had. He went the way he had gone past the husk on the strut in the room, close, on its blind side, the side away from where it was turning, and he came through the doorway into the flat place a breath after it did. It was in front of him now, going for the lantern on Jab's frame at the foot of the stair.

It turned. It came at the light on his fist, because the light on his fist was nearer than the lantern. He put the lit hook into it low, and dragged it round against the steel of the door, and put the point in. It came apart against the door, and the grey fell at the foot of the steel in a heap.

"Up," he said. "All of you. Don't wait."

"*Up*," said Toren. "I'll shut it."

It did not want to go. It had opened toward them. It had come off its stick with a shriek and swung toward them as wide as a man, and stood there. Now it had to go back the other way, away from him, into the corridor, and it was heavy, and it was stiff, and he had one arm.

The door moved.

It moved against his shoulder. It did not open. It moved a finger's width in its frame, the way a door moves when somebody on the other side of it puts a hand flat on it and leans.

The husk.

He understood it with his shoulder against the steel. He had forgotten it until now. The door had opened *toward* them, which meant it pulled from this side. So from the other side, from the corridor, it pushed. Anybody in the corridor who leaned on it would open it.

It moved again, a finger's width, and came back.

He did not decide anything. There was nothing to decide. He took his shoulder off the door and stepped back from it, and put the foot of a strut down on the concrete of the flat place two paces from the door. He let it come up at its slant from the floor to the steel, at the height of his chest, the way you set a plank against a door you want kept shut.

He had not been able to think about it then. He thought about it now. There was less than half, and it was going.

It did not get stronger all at once. It got heavier, the way the weight had got heavier on the strut at the wash when the crown went. This was not a wheel.

"Callie," said Dee.

Callie weighed the jar.

"Three out of two," she said. "For the four of us at the stair, and here, and Jab's arm again." She set it down. "Five in it. Three whole." She did not wait to be asked for the rest. "Twenty-nine. One over today's line."

"One over," said Dee.

She measured their floors.

"Your stone's gone," she said to Toren. "Your own's a turn. And a bit." She looked at him. "You held that door on your own."

"I held it on the stone," said Toren. "Then I held it on my own. Then I let go."

"When?"

"When they said *up*." He looked at the hill. "Three breaths after."

Dee wrote it.

Callie's stone was a quarter. Her own was where it had been in the morning. Dee's stone was half, where the watch had taken it. Jab's floor was a finger, and his own was less than a hand, and his arm was warm from the elbow down, and the heel of his right palm was white and shiny and would not feel.

Dee held his hand in both of hers, turned up, in the last of the sun.

"It's fixed," she said. "You know it's fixed."

"I know."

"It'll not come back." She did not let go. "It might go red. It might blister. If it blisters, I'll do what Sowerby would, and keep it clean, and it'll close. But it'll not feel, there. Not that bit." She looked at him. "Can you draw with it?"

Jab put his right palm flat on his own left forearm and shut his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "Round it. Not with the bit."

"Then it's a mark," said Dee. "Not a hand." She let him go. "It's the first one the work's put on you that I can't take off. It won't be the last." She wrote it. "Vell had one. On the heel of his hand. The same place."

Pell had been counting since noon.

He was sitting on the cart with his back to the rack and his leg out and the atlas open on his knee. Beside it lay a sheet of Senna's route paper, and on the back of the sheet Pell had made marks: strokes in rows, some of them with a cross through them. Callie looked at the rows of strokes for a moment longer than anybody else did.

"I counted it on my own heart," said Pell. "I've no glass. It's sixty to a turn if you sit still, near enough. I sat still." He held out the sheet. "It never missed. Ten and three. From noon, when you went through the gate, till you came out of that door at the top. Every count."

"And what came out of it?" said Toren.

"Grey." Pell put his finger on the strokes with the cross through them. "Out of the bottom. At the three. Down into the round one. It's like watching rain a long way off, over there, when it isn't raining here." He looked up. "Seven. Seven that I saw, from noon till you came out at the top. That's two hours and more." He tapped the sheet. "I missed some. I'd look down to mark one, and look up, and not know if I'd missed the next. And you can't look at that edge long. It gets in your eyes."

"Callie saw one going in," said Toren. "In twelve. And two coming out."

"Then she saw more than I did," said Pell. "She was looking up, and I was looking down at my marks half the time. It's more toward the end than the start. I'd not swear to it." He folded the sheet. "Write it. I'll count it again tomorrow. Somebody will want to know if it's more."

Toren wrote it. *Pell's count, from the cart. Ten and three, every count. 7 falls of grey seen, gate (noon) to the top of the road coming out, 2 hours and more. He missed some, looking down to mark. More toward the end (Pell's not sure).*

He looked at what he had written. It was the Director's count. It was the thing she had asked for, *count what comes out of it and write it down*, and it was the first thing on the paper that was hers.

---

They told it at the fire.

Dee lit it with the last of the wrack from the morning and put the pot on for Rook's back, and they sat round it in the dusk on the gravel outside the fence, on the right side of the river. Toren said it. He said it the way he had said the evidence at the edge of the water on the forty-third: in order, all of it.

The yard. The lines going up the road. The door at the top, and the stair, and the yellow rail. The door at the bottom that opened toward you, and pulled open in thirty breaths. The corridor going west. The tray, and the rope in it, crawling. The first room, and the boxes on the floor, hundreds of them, the way Gran had said, with the rope gone in among them like roots. Three live ones, and one of those open. One in the carrier.

"One in the carrier," said Pell.

"In the room," said Toren. "With the lid on. Where we left it."

He told the rest. The heap in the corner, and the husk that stood up out of it, that he had killed and that had come back. The same one. The cut in the strands, and how it had mended in the three. Callie's lee across the door, and how it had stopped the floor and not the rope. Jab's palm's worth, into a vent jar, and the husk coming back a hand short.

He told them about the rope. He did not look at Jab while he told it. He told it the way Jab had said it on the floor of the room with his arm in Dee's hands. *It thought I was a hole in it. It thought I was a wound, and it was mending me.* He told them what Dee had done.

Rook, on the canvas by the fire, did not say anything. He had his eyes on Jab's hand.

Then Toren told them about the thing on the rope: the heap as high as a waist, coming along the corridor, pulling itself along its own rope with nothing to pull with. The light that opened in it at the three. The red. The door, and what the door did from the other side.

"It came along the rope," he said. "From the west. From the round one, or from somewhere between. It came to where we were. To where the husk had been killed, and the grey was lying. And to him." He did not look at Jab. "It came to us."

"It came to you," said Rook.

He said it quietly, from the canvas, with his eyes half shut.

"*If it comes to you,*" said Rook, "*you do what you have to do and you come home.*" He opened his eyes. "You came home."

"We came home with one box," said Toren. "In a room, under a hill, behind a door that opens away from it."

"What is it?" said Pell.

For a while nobody answered.

"It's lifting," said Jab.

He was sitting by the fire with his right hand on his knee, palm up, and the white place was catching the light. He had not said anything since the river.

"It lifts like we lift," he said. "Flat. At the rate it comes. It draws for ten, and it vents for three. Only it vents where it wants a thing. Into a heap, and a husk stands up. Into a cut, and the cut's not there." He looked at the hand. "Into a hand, if the hand's drawing on it."

"Then it's a lifter," said Rook.

He said it with the corner of his mouth moving, the way it moved when he was going to laugh and was not sure it would be allowed.

"It's got my job," he said. "Four seasons, on the worst of the north yard. And there it is, doing it for nothing, on its own hill, better than me." He looked at the fire. "A lifter."

Nobody laughed. But Callie's mouth moved, and so did Pell's.

Toren wrote it on the paper. *The lifter.* Jab had said what it did, and Rook had said what that made it, and nobody had a better word.

---

"You've a box," said Pell.

He said it after Dee had changed Rook's pad, in the dark, with the boiled water cooling in the pot and the fire going down. He had said nothing all through the telling. He had sat on the cart and listened, the way he listened to a hub.

"You've one box," he said, "in a lead box of its own, in a room, under a hill. You've twenty-nine inside. Sixteen's the line. That's thirteen to work with. You've a road home that's cut, and a man with a fever, and a man with one leg." He was not looking at anybody. "And in the room with the box, there's a thing that mends what you do to it, every thirteen breaths, and came along a rope to see you, and had its hand in Jab."

"I know," said Toren.

"I know you know," said Pell. "I'm saying it so it's been said by somebody who's not going in." He was quiet a moment. "A box and a half short, and all of you alive, is a thing I'd take back to that woman's office and put on her table. I'd not be ashamed of it."

"The box is in the room," said Callie.

"Then it's in the room."

"Then we go home with nothing," said Callie. "And that thing's got it. And the ash that went in the door today. And everything lying on the whole of that hill, going up it, ten and three." She said it flat. "And Senna's on the narrow bed on the fifty-fourth with a man guessing."

"She is," said Pell. "And she'd say the same as me."

Toren did not know if that was true.

He thought about his grandmother on the pillow with her eyes shut, saying *pull the door*. She had been saving it. She had not said *come back without it*. She had not needed to. She had said it to the whole room, with *I know what every man in that line does with a number once he's got it*.

He looked at the five of them round the dying fire. At Pell on the cart with his leg out, who had said it so it had been said. At Rook, hot, on the canvas, not asleep. At Dee with her book on her knee and her hands still. At Callie. At Jab with his hand palm up on his knee.

*Everybody back.*

He had written it, and he had meant it. He had not known, when he wrote it, that it might one day mean leaving behind a thing that everybody at home needed, so that everybody could come home.

"Tomorrow," he said.

They looked at him.

"Tomorrow we decide," said Toren. "Not tonight. With the light." He had said the same thing on the forty-sixth, and it had been right then, and he did not know if it was right now. He said the rest of it anyway. "Tonight we think about what it did. All of it. Every time it did something, and what we did, and what happened." He looked at Jab. "It mends in the three. You said. It vents where there's a hole. That's the whole of what we know. If there's a way in that, we find it tonight. If there isn't, we go home in the morning with nothing."

"And if there is?" said Dee.

"Then we go back for the box," said Toren. "And I'll say before we go what we'll pay for it. And if it costs more than that, we come out, finished or not."

Dee looked at him across the embers for a long time.

"Write that," she said.

He wrote it, under the day, under the lifter, under Pell's count.

*47th. The pump house. In: 4. One box in the carrier, left in the first room, lid on. J.'s right palm: a white place on the heel, fixed. It mends in the three.*

And under that, because it was true, and because there was nowhere else to put it:

*Gran's door pulls. From the other side it pushes.*

# Chapter 37 — The Flap

The pump was still in the pump house.

Callie had not looked at it the night before. She had been too cold, and too tired, and the wall had been something to put a fire against and nothing else. Now she got up from the fire and took a lantern in through the gap where the door had been, because she could not sit there and think about a rope any more, and everybody at the fire could see that she could not.

The pump stood in the middle of the stone floor under the open sky. It was iron, and it was bigger than the cart. A long iron drum lay on its side on a stone bed, and at one end of the drum there was a wheel as high as Callie, with spokes, and a rod going from the wheel into the drum. A pipe as thick as her body came up out of the floor on the river side and went into the bottom of the drum, and two more came out of the top of it and went through the wall toward the river, toward the broken pipes lying in the water.

It was all rust. It had not moved in seventy years. Callie put her hand on the wheel, and it did not move now either.

"It's a pump," said Pell, from outside.

He was on the cart, which they had drawn up against the outside of the wall so close that its rack stood a pace from the gap. When Callie held the lantern up, he could see in. He was sitting with his back to the rack and his leg out along the boards, the way he had sat all day.

"I know it's a pump," said Callie.

"I know you know," said Pell. "Ask me what you came in to ask me."

She stood by the wheel with the lantern.

"How does it work?" she said.

Pell told her. He told her the way he had told her about the wheel, sitting back with his hands on his knee, not looking at her, looking at the thing.

"It lifts," he said. "And it pushes. That's all a pump does. Two strokes." He nodded at the drum. "There's a plunger in that. The wheel goes round and the rod goes in and out. When it comes out, it lifts: it pulls the water up out of the river through that pipe in the floor, into the drum. When it goes in, it pushes: it pushes the water out of the drum, out through the top, up those pipes, over the river, up the hill. Wherever they wanted it."

"It lifts and it pushes."

"Lift, push. Lift, push." He moved his hand in time with it. "Only there's a thing in between. There's a flap."

Callie waited.

"In the bottom of the drum, where the river pipe comes in," said Pell, "there's a flap. Leather, on a hinge, like a door. When it lifts, the flap opens and the water comes up past it into the drum. When it pushes, the flap shuts, because the water's pushing on it the wrong way. So what it lifted can't go back down the pipe to the river. It's got nowhere to go but out of the top." He looked at the drum. "My father had a little one, on the farm, for the trough. A hand one. The flap went every spring. When the flap goes, you can hear it."

"What does it sound like?"

"Like it's working," said Pell. "That's the thing. It lifts, and it pushes, and it sounds like it's working all day. But it pushes the water straight back down the pipe it came up. It lifts it up and pushes it down again, the same water, all day, and nothing comes out of the top." He shook his head. "You'd not know. You'd pump for an hour and go to the trough and it'd be dry."

Callie stood very still with the lantern.

She was thinking about the heap in the corner of the room under the hill. She was thinking about the ten, and the heap drawn out into threads and gone into the rope, and the three, and the corner swelling. She was thinking about Jab with his hand on the rope by the door, saying *nothing, it's going past*, and then the three, and the push, and his face.

"If you wanted to stop it," she said. "The pump. If you were in the drum."

"I'd not be in the drum."

"If you were. If you wanted it to stop pushing water up the hill."

Pell thought about it. She could see him do it, the way he thought about a distance.

"You'd not do it on the lift," he said slowly. "On the lift it's got the whole river behind it. You'd stand in front of that pipe in the floor and it'd lift the river at you, and you'd not stop the river." He looked at the pipe going down into the stone. "You'd do it on the push. On the push it's only got what's in the drum. What it's just lifted. That's all it's got. A drum's worth."

"And if you took the drum's worth," said Callie. "On the push. Every time. So it pushed and nothing got up the pipe."

"Then it'd lift the river again," said Pell. "And push it at you again. And you'd take that." He was quiet. "Till the river ran out. Or you did."

"And if somebody shut the river?"

Pell looked at her properly then.

"Then it'd have a drum's worth," he said. "And no more. And it'd push it at you, and you'd take it, and it'd lift nothing, because there's nothing to lift. And it'd push again. Less." He was quiet for a long time. "It'd pump itself dry. It doesn't know how to do anything else. A pump doesn't stop because the water's gone. It stops when somebody stops the wheel."

Callie put the lantern down on the stone bed of the pump.

"Nobody stops its wheel," she said.

"Then it'd go on," said Pell. "Pushing nothing. Into whatever was taking it."

---

She went out to the fire.

They were all there. Dee had changed Rook's pad and was sitting with her book. Jab was sitting with his right hand palm up on his knee. Toren had the paper. Rook lay on the canvas with his eyes open. Nobody was talking. Nobody had talked since *tomorrow we decide*.

"Pell," said Callie. "Say it again. The pump."

Pell said it again from the cart, while she stood by the fire with her hands open. Lift, and push, and the flap, and the river. On the lift it's got the whole river. On the push it's only got what it's lifted.

Jab had looked up at *the push*, and he did not look away from Pell until Pell had finished.

"On the push," said Jab.

"That's what I did wrong," he said. He said it to his hand. "I put it on the rope in the ten. On the lift. And it went past, because it had the whole hill behind it and it was going somewhere. And then the three came, and it pushed, and I was a hole." He turned the hand over. "And there was nothing shut. The whole hill was behind the push. So it kept pushing."

"If somebody shut the river," said Callie.

"The lee," said Toren.

He had put the pencil down.

"The lee stopped the floor," he said. "At the door. It stopped everything crawling in. It didn't stop the rope." He was looking at the fire, not at her. "But the rope's a thing. It's a rope. It's lying there. If the rope's cut—"

"It mends in the three," said Jab.

"It mends in the three if it can reach." Toren looked up. "If it's cut at the door, and Callie's lee is in the door, in the cut, it can't reach. It can grow up to the lee. It can't grow through it." He stopped. "Then what's in the room's all there is. That's the drum. The river's on the other side of Callie."

Nobody said anything.

Dee had opened her book, and she was not writing in it. She was looking at Toren.

"Say it in order," she said. "All of it. From the stair."

He said it in order. He said it the way he had said everything since the forty-third, the siding and the wash: not with the answers, because he did not have them, but with the questions in the right order where everybody could see them and see if they stood.

It would be in the room. Everybody agreed with that, and Rook said it from the canvas: "It goes where the grey is." The grey had been lying in the first room all afternoon, and the heap in the corner, and the husks killed and killed again. And the carrier was there. None of them thought it wanted the carrier, but it would be there.

Dee said the rest of it, so that it was said.

"It's in the first room," she said. "The room she sent us to, and the box she sent us for. It came at us there." She did not look at the hill. "So we go to the first door and no further. Not the second, not to look. Nowhere near the round one. We're not going hunting for anything. If it's not in the first room in the morning, we don't go looking for it. We come out."

There were two ways into the room from the corridor for the rope. One was the floor, through the door, where the rope would lie across the threshold. The other was the ceiling, through the hole above the door and down the wall.

"I cut both," said Toren. "The ceiling first, outside, before it goes in the hole. Then the floor at the threshold. The heel. In a ten. Before it pushes."

"And me in the door," said Callie. "On the threshold. In your cut." She held her hand out flat and tipped it back. "The lee off the curve, standing up into the corridor. And leaned back. Up the wall over the door. Over the hole. Like a lean-to, the wrong way up."

"Can you?" said Toren.

"It's a door," said Callie. "And a bit. It's still. It's on concrete." She thought about it with her lips moving, the way she did a sum. "A door's the cheapest thing I know. A door and a bit's more. And if there's husks in the corridor, and there will be, they'll come at it, and that's more again." She looked at Dee. "I want two. Settled. Tonight."

Dee did not answer at once.

"Two's more than you've ever carried," she said.

"I settle well. You said. It's the only thing I do better than Toren."

"It's not the only thing," said Toren.

"It's the only thing tonight," said Callie.

Dee looked at her for a long time. Then she wrote it.

"Two," she said. "You'll settle it slowly, and I'll sit with you, and if it slops I'll stop you at one and a half." She looked up. "And you'll be in the room."

"I'll be in the door."

"You'll be in the room," said Dee. "With your back to it. You'll be facing the corridor with the lee in front of you, and everything that's in that room will be behind you. You'll not see it. You'll not see any of it."

"I know," said Callie.

"Jab," said Toren.

"On the push," said Jab. "Not the lift. When you cut it, it'll mend. That's all it does. It'll push into the cut at the three. I put my hand on the cut, on the room side, at the three, and it pushes into me, and I take a drum's worth. And I put it in a vent jar, and stopper it. And I take my hand off." He looked at Dee. "Before the ten. Every time. Off before the ten."

"I'll say *off*," said Dee. "At the end of every three. Every one. You'll not wait for me to say it twice."

"I'll not wait."

"And what's in the room is all it's got," said Toren. "And every three it pushes some of it into Jab, and Jab puts it in a jar. And every ten it lifts what's lying in the room. And it's less." He stopped. "Pell said. It'll pump itself dry."

"It'll not stand there and let you," said Rook.

He said it from the canvas. He had not moved, but his voice was clear.

"It had a husk on your strut all afternoon," said Rook. "It'll make more. It'll put them in your way. It'll throw what it's got at whatever's worked, and Jab's hand is worked, and so is Callie's back. And so is you." He looked at Toren. "You'll not be cutting. You'll be keeping it off them."

"Then I'll keep it off them," said Toren. "And cut when I can." He looked at the paper. "Every rope it's got in the room is a way it moves. Every one's a way it throws. I cut them, one at a time. Every cut's a hole. Every hole, it pushes, at the three. And Jab's there."

"And when it's got nothing left to push with?" said Dee.

"It opens," said Jab. "At the three. In the middle. The light." He did not look at anybody. "I saw it open. It opens every time it pushes. That's where the push comes from."

"Then that's where I'll put the point," said Toren.

"Say what it costs," said Dee.

Callie said it.

"Twenty-nine," she said. "Inside. One jar open, with five in it. Three whole." She did the sum out loud, because Dee would want it out loud. "Settling tonight. Two in me, that's a Handful and three quarters more. A Handful and a half in Toren. Half in you, Miss Wren, to make you one. That's three and three quarters." She stopped. "Twenty-five and a quarter. The line's sixteen. Nine and a quarter to work with."

"Nine and a quarter," said Toren.

"It's not twelve," said Callie.

"It's not twelve," said Toren. "It's nine and a quarter. That's what I'll pay." He wrote it. "If it's not done at the line, we come out, finished or not."

"And if it's nearly done?" said Dee. "At the line. If it's lying there with nothing left and you've got the point up."

Toren did not answer at once. The others waited for him, and nobody filled the gap.

"Then I'll say it," he said. "Then. Out loud. What we're doing, and what it costs, and how long. And you'll write it. And it'll be mine."

"It'll be mine as well," said Dee. "It's my line." She wrote. "And the rule's still the rule, after. Two Flasks left inside, everybody out. If you say it, it's once. It goes in front of her at home with both our names on it."

"And the home three don't cross that bridge," said Toren. "Whatever happens. Whatever it's lying there with. They're for the road." He looked at the cart, at the rack behind the driver's bench, where the three jars stood grey to the shoulder in the dark. "Say it, somebody who's not going in."

"The home three don't cross the bridge," said Pell. "I'm sitting on them."

Callie settled by the fire with Dee beside her.

It was the most she had ever taken. Dee weighed it out of the open jar onto the square of paper on Callie's knee, a Handful and three quarters, grey and fine, and Callie put her palm flat on it and let it come.

It came. It always came well for her. It came up into her palm slow and cold, and went down, and lay at the bottom of the well on the quarter that was there, and she felt the stone get bigger. She did not haul. She let it come at the rate it came, and she tried to think about nothing, the way Dee had taught them at the long table.

She did not think about nothing. She thought about the door.

She thought about standing in it with the hook on the threshold and the lee up, facing the corridor, with the room behind her: Toren behind her, and Jab, and Dee, and the lifter. She would not see any of them.

She had always held what she could see. That was what Rook had taught her on the forty-fourth. *Watch who's inside. Put your eyes on the people and hold them there.* She had held Jab's shoulders on the old line. At the wash she had held Jab and Pell under the lee, and taken Toren in and out of it with her eyes. The lee went where she looked. It had always gone where she looked.

Tomorrow she would be looking at a corridor.

The lee would not be over anybody. It would not be shaped like where they were. It would be a wall across a door, standing between a corridor and a room and keeping the corridor out. Nobody would be inside it. Everybody would be behind it.

*You can't have all of us,* Dee had said on the forty-fourth. *You'll have to decide who's worth the lee.*

She understood it now, with her palm on the paper. It was not about who was worth it. Tomorrow the lee was not for anybody. It was for the thing that was not them. It was for keeping the rest of the hill off, so that the three people behind her could do what only they could do, where she could not see them do it. She would not be carrying them. She would be holding the door so that they could carry it.

*Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.*

Her mother had said that with her words half gone, on the edge of the bed, with Callie's fingers round her wrist. She had opened Callie's fingers off her wrist, one at a time.

"One and a half," said Dee quietly. "It's lying still. Go on."

Callie went on. The last quarter came up into her palm and went down and lay there.

"Two," said Dee. "Is it still?"

Callie went down to look. The stone lay big at the bottom of the well, bigger than it had ever been, and it was still. It was not slopping. It was not in the hall. It was lying where she had put it.

"It's still," she said.

"Then it's yours," said Dee. She wrote it. "Road, none. Inside, twenty-five and a quarter. One open, with a Handful and a quarter in it. Three whole." She looked up. "Say it back."

Callie said it back.

She had the first watch with Pell, because Pell was awake.

He sat on the cart with his back to the rack and the atlas shut on his knee, and she stood by the cart wheel with the hook and the counter. The fire was down. Toren had settled his Handful and a half at the fire for most of two hours, with his palm flat and his face shut, and then he had lain down by Jab and counted them. Jab had fallen asleep at once. Dee had not.

Across the river the fence stood black against the dark, and the hill went up beyond it. Over its flat top there were no stars, and round the place with no stars there was the light the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened and thinned.

"I'm counting it," said Pell. "In case anybody asks."

"Can you see the falls?"

"No. It's too dark. I can see the edge." He was quiet. "I'd like to see the falls. I'd like to know if it's more at night."

They watched it together.

"I said what I said at the fire," said Pell, after a while. "About a box and a half short. About the woman's table."

"I know."

"I'd say it again."

"I know you would."

"I said it so it had been said," said Pell. "By somebody who wasn't going in. Somebody has to say it, on a road, or you go in without anybody having said it, and then you don't know you chose." He looked across the river at the hill. "It's said now. It's written down. So now you can go in and do it properly."

Callie looked at him.

"That's all," said Pell. "That's what saying it's for."

She stood by the wheel and did not say anything for a long time.

"Pell," she said. "Your father. With the pump."

"What about him?"

"Did he mend the flap? Every spring?"

"Every spring," said Pell. "Cut a new one out of an old boot and put it in. Took him ten minutes." He was quiet. "Took me a year to learn how, and he let me do it wrong for most of that year. He'd stand there and watch me pump water down the pipe for an hour and not say anything. Then he'd say *now go and look at the trough.*"

"That's cruel."

"It's a good way to learn about flaps," said Pell. "You never forget which way they go."

In the morning Rook was cooler.

"*Off*," said Dee.

"I know."

He looked at his palm. Round the white place the rest of it was warm, a little. It was not lodged. It was warm.

He looked at Callie once.

He could not help it. He was crouched by the stump of the first arm-rope, near the door, waiting for a three, and she was a pace and a half from him with her back to him. He had not looked at her properly since they came in. He looked now.

She was standing in the doorway with the curve on the threshold and her hands on the conduit, and the lee stood up out of the curve on the corridor side. She was holding it off her stone, her whole stone, two Handfuls: the husk on the outside, and the heaps, and a door and a bit.

There was blood on her lip.

She turned her head.

She did not turn round. She turned her head a little, over her shoulder, the way you turn your head at a noise behind you, and for a breath her eyes found him: Jab crouched on the floor with his hand out over a stump, and Dee behind him with the jar, and the room behind that.

"Busy," he said.

Callie looked at him a breath longer. Then she turned her head back to the corridor, and she did not turn it again.

It was smaller.

Jab saw it as he went round the hump for the eleventh catch. He had been too near it to see it before, the way you do not see a heap of sacks going down while you are lifting off the top of it. The hump had been as high as Callie's waist. It was as high as her knee now at the high end, and the other end had gone down to nothing, and he could see the carrier's lead lid under it, grey, and the iron handle sticking all the way out.

"It's less," said Toren. "I can feel it on the husk. The husk's less."

It was. The husk on Toren's strut had been as big as a man. It was the size of a boy now, pinned against the cabinet, and it pushed with less.

It opened at the three and did not shut at the end of the three. It shut at the end of four. Then five.

"A hundred and thirty," she said. "Not red."

He knew what it cost. Every Handful that came out of this jar was a Handful that nobody would have. He felt it come in at his left palm and go through him, and wait.

"Callie," said Toren. "Two turns."

"I heard," said Callie, from the door. She did not turn her head. Her voice was flat and thick, the way it was with a nosebleed. "My stone went at the fifteenth three. I'm on my own. I've got two turns. I've not got three."

It pushed into him. It pushed into him because he was drawing, and he was a hole, and it had never known anything else to do with a hole. It pushed what it had, and it had very little.

He did not let the push into his wrist. He held it in his palm, the last of what the lifter had, and felt it go on pushing, five breaths, six, with nothing, into his hand. It was like breath going out of a man who has no more breath.

*Off before the ten.*

He did not take his hand off. Not yet. He knew he had to. He knew the ten was coming a breath behind the three, and that it would draw, and that what he had in his palm was loose and would be drawn back, and him after it. He knew all of that.

But the light was open, a hand from his palm. It was open because he was on it, taking the push, so that there was nothing to shut it with. If he came off now, the ten would come, and it would draw what little was lying in the room, and at the next three it would have something to push, and it would shut.

"Toren," he said.

Toren was there. He was standing over the lifter with his one strut still under it, soft, and the spike on his fist, dark.

"It's open," said Jab. "It's open and I'm on it. It's got nothing to shut it with."

"How long?"

"Till the ten," said Jab. "Then I'm off. Whatever you've done."

Toren did not light the spike. Not with the strut up. He looked past Jab at Callie's back.

"Callie," he said. "I'm giving you the lean. It's coming onto your hook. A pace. Then it's on your steel till I'm done."

"Give it me," said Callie. She did not turn her head.

"Letting go," said Toren.

The strut went out. The lifter slid the last pace along its stump, low, the whole thin grey of it, and came up against the curve of the hook on the threshold, on the room side, and stopped as if it had come up against a wall. Because it had. Jab went with it, crouched, with his palm flat on it and the jar at his other hand, and he did not come off.

In the door Callie made a small sound.

"Mine," she said.

Toren lit the spike.

# Chapter 39 — The Point

The light Toren asked for was at the very end of the spike, a finger's length of it, and nowhere else.

He did not light the heel. He had nothing to light the heel with and the point both, and the heel was for hooking, and there was nothing left to hook. Everything else in the room was where it was.

The lifter lay against the curve of Callie's hook on the threshold, knee-high, grey. Toren was holding nothing now. It had to be one thing at a time, because there was only one of him. Jab crouched beside the lifter with his right palm flat on the edge of the grey, a hand from the light, and Dee crouched at Jab's back with the fourth jar against his left palm, under the line, and the glass running in her other hand. Nobody said anything.

"The wheels roll," said Pell. "You roll them, down one side of the gap and up the other. They're as high as her shoulder." He nodded at Callie. "The axle's two men. The bed's four, or two and a horse with a rope on it. The shafts are one each. The carrier's on its board, and a board slides on a drift. It's flour." He looked at Toren. "And there's a bank on each side of the gap, loose. And you set steps on a loose bank at the wash. On stones."

"I set them on my own," said Toren. "The last of it."

"You'll have a day's worth by tomorrow," said Pell. "Miss Wren says two turns by tonight. And there's a jar of that thing's ash to settle, if it's clean."

"And you?" said Toren.

"I'm a bed and two poles and a bit of canvas," said Pell. "Two of you. I'll not say anything about the camber."

"Price it," said Toren.

He said it to Callie. She stood by the tail of the cart with her arm across her side and the counter in her other hand, and she priced it out loud, because that was the rule, and because saying it was how she found out whether it was true.

"The drifts were eighty, ninety at the foot of the bank," she said. "In the wind. With no wind, lying still, they'll be less. Sixty, seventy. Where the crown went in, where it's turned over, more. A hundred." She looked east, toward the ridge and the cut and the wash behind it, a mile off. "An hour. For all of it. Down, across, up, the pieces, and Pell, and the horse, twice over for the wheels. An hour at eighty. All of us, and the horse."

"How much?" said Dee.

"Ten," said Callie. "Maybe twelve. For the drawing after, in the cut on the far side. And the settling for Toren's steps." She looked at the frame against the wall. "Out of the lifter's."

"Out of the lifter's," said Toren.

"Out of the lifter's," said Callie. "It's got twenty and a half clean. A Handful and a half went on Miss Wren in the night." She did not look at Dee. "It'd have ten and a half left, and the fence's thirteen, and the dirty four. And home, twenty-four, not touched." She stopped. "And we'd be on the old line east of the gap by the fiftieth, with the cart back together. And it's five days from there, on the road we know. With the siding." She did the last sum. "The fifty-fifth. Home."

"Not the fifty-fourth," said Toren.

"Not the fifty-fourth," said Callie. "Nothing's the fifty-fourth. The fifty-fourth was gone before we crossed the bridge."

Toren looked at the paper for a long time.

"It's the fifty-fifth, or the fifty-seventh," he said. "Or later." He looked up. "That's Sowerby dosing her off her blood, badly, a day past the cycle. Or three. And whatever the fifty-fourth does, with a man guessing." He did not say *Gran*. "And ten Handfuls of that thing's ash, and an hour in the drifts, and Callie's ribs, and Dee a finger over her floor."

"Say which," said Dee.

"The wash," said Toren. "In pieces. Tomorrow, first light, if the wind's down. Not today. Nobody carries a wheel through a drift at their floor."

"Not today," said Dee, and wrote it. "And if the wind's up?"

"Then we sit at the cut and wait for it to drop," said Toren. "And I'll write what it costs."

Pell shut the atlas.

"Then today we go to the cut," he said. "It's a mile. The cart'll do that whole. It's this side of the gap." He looked at Duchess, tied to the wheel with her rope halter, eating. "She'll pull it to the cut. And tomorrow she'll walk through a drift, because somebody she knows is walking in front of her. And then she'll hate every one of us for a week."

"She'll be right," said Rook, from the canvas.

"She's always right," said Pell. "She's a horse."

---

They loaded the cart.

The frame went on the cart this time, in the rack behind the driver's bench beside the home jars, because Jab's palm was blistered and Dee said he would not carry a frame of jars a mile on a blister. Rook went on the cart on the canvas, on his side, with his back off the boards. Pell stayed where he was. The carrier sat behind them on its board with the lid in its groove.

Toren went round the cart once, the whole of it, and counted what was on it. Then he counted them.

"Pell," he said. "Dee. Rook." He stopped on Rook the way he always did, longest. "Jab. Callie." He looked at the mare. "Six. And a horse."

He wrote it on the back of the signed paper, standing at the pump house wall.

*49th. The pump house. Out: 6. To the cut. The wash tomorrow, in pieces (Pell's). Home the 55th, if the wind's down.*

He looked at it.

"Dessa left six lines," he said. "At the foot of the page. For *back*."

"I know," said Callie.

"I'll not write in them yet," said Toren. He folded the paper and put it in his coat beside the plate.

Callie went out in front. She did it without being asked, the way she had done it on the first morning on the old north road: twenty paces in front of Duchess, with the hook for a stick and her left arm strapped across her side under her coat. She began to read the road for the wheels.

It was the road they had come on, three days ago, at dusk: the old line going up from the river on its long bare slope to the ridge, and the cut through the ridge, and the wash behind it. She knew it. She read it anyway, for the wheels. She read the loose stones on the bed, and the place where the frost had heaved it, and the mark where the cart had come down it on the forty-sixth with Pell on it and his foot on a coil of rope.

At the top of the slope, at the mouth of the cut, she stopped and turned round.

She looked back once. Everybody does.

The river lay low and dark in its stony bed, with the pump house on its near bank, roofless, and the two broken pipes going into the water. The footbridge went across on its posts, with two grey poles of wrack wood still lying along it over the crack. At the far end stood the gate, with a twist of green wire through its chain, left so it would come.

Beyond the gate there was the yard, and the road going up the hill in its long Z, and the long building standing along the rim with its windows black. There was the drum behind it with its broken top. And over the whole of the top of the hill there was the sky.

The edge of it thickened. She did not count it. It thinned, and thickened at once, and thinned for a long time. It had no count. It was a thing breathing in its sleep, having a bad dream.

Under it, in a room under a hill, there was more ash lying on a floor than she could have lifted in a week. There were hundreds of boxes, and a third live one talking to the room, and three stoppered jars with a chalk cross beside them. She would put the number of every one of them on the Director's table.

In the cart behind Duchess there were two.

It was the forty-ninth day. At home it would be the ninth hour, near enough. Her mother would have walked to the ward for her pills at the eighth hour, with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. Senna would be on the fourth bed on the right, with five days to the narrow bed and the machine that sang flat. Tonk would be in the fifth bed, with a cord round his fist. They were all breathing without her. They had been breathing without her for eight days, and nothing had happened to any of them because she was not there counting.

She did not know what would happen on the fifty-fourth. She did not know if the wind would be down tomorrow.

She knew what was in the cart, and what was in the frame, graded. She knew who was on the cart and who was walking. She knew the road to the cut, and she knew the wash.

She turned round and went on into the cut, twenty paces in front of the horse, reading the road for the wheels. Behind her Duchess came on with the cart, with Pell and Rook riding on it beside the boxes and the home jars, and Jab and Dee walking at the wheel. At the back, where he always walked now, Toren was counting them.
