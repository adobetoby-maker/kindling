## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six to eight connected
chapters per movement (six by default), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background

Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that

# Chapter 24 — Toren: Everybody Back

He had taken his Handful in the night before, the way Dee said he always would have to.

He had done it at the stove in eleven at the ninth hour of the evening, sitting on the floor with the paper twist open on his knee and Dessa on her bed watching him with her bad leg out straight. It had taken him most of an hour, a thousand breaths and more. He had not hauled. He had put his palm flat on the grey heap and waited for it to come at the rate it came, and every part of him had wanted to lean on it, and he had not. At the end there had been a warm stone lying at the bottom of something inside him, a long way down, bigger than any he had settled before.

He woke at the fourth hour with it still there.

The hut was dark. The stove was a red line. He lay on his bed with his coat on and went down once, not to check, only to know, the way Dee had taught him. The stone was there. Above it his own reserve lay where it lay, two turns and a bit of the brass glass, dark. The copied plate was in his coat pocket on one side and his own stone on the other, warm and faint and breathing.

He got his boots on one-handed. The splint on his left arm had come off on the thirty-ninth day. Keel had put the arm in a stiff leather cuff from the wrist to the elbow instead, laced, that he could get on and off himself, and she had told him it was knit and it was not strong, and that if he hauled anything with it on the road she would know when he came back, and he would not enjoy the knowing. He laced the cuff in the dark with his teeth and his right hand.

Rook was already up.

He was sitting on the edge of his bed with his boots on, and his hands lay on his knees. They were not wrapped any more. The red had gone out of the palms to a pink, like skin after a burn has healed over, and they did not shake. He looked at Toren in the red light of the stove.

"You're up," he said.

"So are you."

"I'm always up," said Rook. "Nobody in this station's ever up at the fourth hour. Except everybody."

He stood up. He did it in one movement, without his hands, and stood there with his arms at his sides, and did not sway.

On her bed, Dessa said, "One," without opening her eyes. And then, "I'm writing that down. I'm writing that you got up in one on the forty-first."

"You're not writing anything. You're lying down."

"I'll write it when I'm up," said Dessa. "I've got a very good memory. It's the only thing I've got that doesn't hurt."

---

Wyck was on the step when Toren went out. He was sitting in the dark with the bar across his knees and his strapped arm against his chest and the frost on the concrete round his boots.

"You haven't slept," said Toren.

"I've slept. I slept from the tenth hour to the second. Then I came out here." Wyck turned the bar over in his left hand. "It's the last night I get to say I'm not going because Keel said so. Tomorrow it's just that I'm not going." He looked at the row. "I'd like to have been going."

Toren sat down beside him.

"I know," he said.

"Don't say you know. You don't know. You're going." Wyck did not say it angrily. He said it the way he said everything that was true. "Listen. I'm going to say one thing and then I'm going to go and stand on a lamp-line with two men who stand like they're waiting for a cart, and teach them not to, for twelve days." He looked at Toren in the dark. "Fall like a sack. Up there. On the road, and inside the fence, and on that stair. Whatever you're carrying. You fall like a sack and you roll and you get up, and you don't put your hand out. Not that hand." He nodded at the leather cuff. "Not for anything. Not if your gran's at the bottom of the stair."

"My gran's in a bed."

"Then not if Jab's at the bottom," said Wyck. "Not if Callie is. You'll want to. You'll want to more than anything. Don't." He turned the bar over. "That's all. That's the only thing I know that's any use to anybody going up a hill."

"It's a lot of use."

"It's one thing," said Wyck. He stood up, carefully, the way Keel had made him promise to do everything, and stood on the step with the bar in his left hand. "The fifty-sixth. That's when she takes it off. You'll be back by then, or near it. And when you're back I'm taking your sack at eight paces. You'll sit on the heap."

"I'll sit on the heap," said Toren.

Wyck went in. Toren sat on the step alone with the stone warm in one pocket and the plate in the other, and waited for the light.

---

At first light the yard was full.

He had not expected that. He had thought it would be the six of them, and the cart, and the horse, and perhaps Dessa with the book. But when he came out of Yard C into the north yard, a pace behind Rook and on his left, where he always walked now because of the hole in Rook's back, the yard was full of people who were not going anywhere.

Katori's crew wagon stood by the yard gate. Toren had seen it only once before, on the day it came in, under a canvas net at the south stable with the crates. It had been under the net for six weeks. Now the net was off and the crates were out and Otto was putting things into it where Pell told him to: a rolled tent, water in skins and in a small barrel, food in a sack, a lamp, Pell's atlas in its oilcloth, a coil of rope. And on a bed of straw at the front, behind the driver's bench, a box.

It was lead. It was about the size of a small chest, grey and dull and very heavy, with a lid that sat in a groove and two iron handles, and it had been made in the grey-haired man's shed in two days by the old woman and the young man who did not give their names, out of sheets from the depot. There was nothing in it. It was for bringing things home in.

Beside it, bedded in straw in a wooden rack, four jars stood grey to the shoulder. *P., 4, ON THE CART. ROAD 1. HOME 3.* The weighbridge board said so, in chalk, where everybody in the north yard could read it.

The weighbridge girl had the horse.

She was standing at Duchess's head with her plaster held against her chest and the lead rope in her right hand. Duchess was the crew's grey mare, the one with the bad leg that the girl had been nagging the stable man about since the tenth day. The mare stood square on all four feet in the cold with her breath going out of her in clouds, and the girl was talking to her.

"She's sound," the girl said, before anybody asked. She said it to Pell, and then to Toren, and then to the yard in general. "I walked her myself. Every morning for a week. Twice a day on the poultice, I told him, and he did it once, so I did the other one. She's sound. She'll go all day if you let her stop at water." She put her hand on the mare's neck. "She doesn't like dogs. She'll hate hounds. If she stops and puts her ears back and won't go on, it's not her being difficult. It's something in the ditch."

"Thank you," said Pell.

"I'm only telling you," said the girl. "Nobody at the stable listens to me. I'm the tally." Then she looked at the mare, and her face did a thing that made her look about ten. "She's the best horse inside the berm. There's nine. I know all of them. Bring her *back*."

---

Jab had the frame on.

He was standing by the wagon with the belt high on the bone of his hips and the chest band under the yoke, the way Wyck had told him, and eight jars in the rack on his back, grey to the shoulder, where Tonk's seat had been. The curved ash rail stood up at the top of the frame, with the top row of jars leaning on it. He had not taken it off.

He was looking at the long building.

Toren followed his eyes. From the north yard you could see the side of it, long and low with its turf roof white with frost, and the doors at the front, and the windows of the ward along the side.

"Eighth hour," said Jab. "Pills. Callie's mother." He did not look away from the building. "Visiting's in the afternoons."

"Mrs Lask'll let us in."

"She'll let us in," said Jab. "She'll say it's the afternoons first."

Callie came across the yard from Yard C with the hook over her shoulder and the counter in its canvas bag on her hip. She was not walking beside a barrow. She was walking alone, fast, and her face was the colour of the frost. She stopped by the wagon.

"She wants to walk," she said. "To the long building. For the pills. With the stick. She says she's not going in a barrow on the morning her daughter goes up a hill, because everybody in the station walks past the doors at the eighth hour and she's not having them see her in a barrow." She looked at the long building. "Her leg's come back a bit. Not all of it. Katori's with her. And Ines. They'll be an hour. She says we're to go ahead and she'll be there."

"She'll be there," said Jab.

"She'll be there," said Callie. "She said it the way she says a thing that's decided."

Rook looked at the three of them by the wagon. He looked at the long building.

"Go," he said. "All three. The eighth hour. Go and do it properly." He put his hand on the side of the wagon. "Dee and I've done ours. Pell's done his. We'll be at the gate."

---

Mrs Lask was at her desk inside the doors.

She looked up when the three of them came in out of the frost. She looked at Jab with the frame on his back and the jars in it, and at Callie with the hook, and at Toren with his arm in its leather cuff. She put down her pen.

"Visiting," she said, "is in the afternoons."

"We know," said Jab.

Mrs Lask looked at them for a moment longer. Then she picked up her pen again and looked down at her list.

"I haven't sent for any of you," she said. "I'd like you to know I haven't." She wrote something. "Go on."

The ward was grey with the early light coming in at the windows, and the small lamp at the far end was still burning, turned low. Most of the beds were asleep. In the fourth bed on the right, his grandmother was sitting up against her pillows with her hands folded on the blanket where she could see them.

In the fifth bed, Tonk was sitting up on his bar.

He had both hands on the short smooth rail over his bed and he had pulled himself up on it, all the way, until he was sitting with his back off the pillow. He was not looking at the door. He was looking at the window over his bed, where there was nothing to see but grey. He did not turn his head when they came in. The long cord ran from his wrist across the gap between the beds to the rail of the empty sixth bed, and the knot was on the rail, the one the nurse had tied twice, that nobody was allowed to untie.

The fourth bed on the left, across the ward, was empty and made up tight. It was the one Callie's mother came to at the eighth hour for her pills.

Jab stopped in the middle of the ward with the frame on his back.

Toren went to his grandmother.

---

"You've got your cuff on," said Senna.

"Keel put it on."

"I know she did. She came and told me. She said she'd told you it was knit and not strong and you'd said yes Doctor Keel in a voice she didn't believe." Senna looked at him with her pale eyes. "Sit down. Not on my feet. I can't feel them, but the nurse can, and she'll be rude."

He sat on the chair by the bed.

"Say them," said Senna.

He knew what she meant. He had written them on the thirty-second day in the big book as she said them, not tidied, and he had said them every night since, on the step of eleven, to nobody, until he knew them the way he knew the plate.

"Nothing to eat inside the fence," he said. "Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, every time, coat off, shake it, wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat." He stopped. "And never carry one of the boxes. Not on your back. Not in your arms."

"That's them," said Senna. "Say them at the gate every morning. Out loud. All of you. Like a grace." She moved one of her folded hands, a quarter of an inch. "The man who ran the crew made us. We thought he was an old woman about it. The ones who kept them are the ones who were still alive ten years ago."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so you've heard it from me on the morning, and not only on a page." She looked at the ceiling. "There's one more thing. I didn't put it on the page. I didn't think of it till the night before last, lying here, and then I couldn't think of anything else."

He waited.

"At the bottom of the yellow stair," said Senna, "there's a door. Before the long corridor. A steel door, with the wheel on it. It sticks." She was quiet a moment. "It opens toward you. Not away. Every man on that crew put his shoulder to it on the first morning and it didn't move, and we stood at the bottom of the stair shoving at it for a quarter of an hour, eleven of us, in the dark, with the stuff in the air. The man who ran the crew stood at the top and laughed at us. Then he came down and pulled it." Her mouth moved at the corner. "He laughed at us about it for a week. He said a quarter of an hour at the bottom of that stair was a day off the end of somebody's life, and we'd spent it pushing a door that pulled."

Toren sat very still.

"Pull it," said Senna. "Don't push. Don't stand at the bottom of that stair for a quarter of an hour. You haven't got a quarter of an hour. I've done the sums on it every night since the thirty-second. I know what a quarter of an hour at the bottom of that stair costs a person now. It's on your paper, by the hour." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him. "That's the only thing I've got that isn't on the page. Nobody else alive knows it. I've been saving it for this morning."

"Pull it," said Toren.

"Pull it," said Senna.

---

She was quiet for a while. Toren sat by the bed with his hand on his knee.

Across the ward, the doors opened.

Callie's mother came in on her stick with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. She was walking slowly, the right leg coming a little behind the left, the way it did now, as if somebody else had to tell it when. She had her battered hat on, pulled down. She came down the ward between the beds past the sleepers, and people who were awake turned their heads to watch her come, because she was walking.

She got to the fourth bed on the left and turned round and sat on the edge of it. It took her a while. Nobody hurried her.

Callie was standing at the foot of the bed with the hook on her shoulder.

"There," said her mother. Her voice was slow still, and thick, and the words came one at a time. None of them came wrong. "I walked. Everybody saw. Callie. Callie. That's a hundred and four since the stove. I'm keeping count, for once." She looked at Katori. "Go and find the little man and tell him I'm here and I'd like my pills and I'd like him to not look at my face for once, because my face is busy."

Katori went. Ines stood by the window with her bow.

The nurse came with the pills in a paper cup and a cup of water. Callie's mother took the paper cup in her left hand and the water in her right. The right hand closed on the water cup. It closed slowly, and not all the way, and it shook, and it held. She drank. She put the cup down. She did not say anything about the hand, and nobody else did.

Then she put her left hand out, palm up, on the blanket beside her.

Callie came round the end of the bed and put her own hand in it.

Toren watched them from across the ward. He could not hear what they said. He saw Callie's mother's lips move, and saw Callie's move in answer, and he knew what it was, because he had heard it on the steps a dozen times without meaning to. *Something left?* And Callie's answer, which he could not hear, but which he could see she said all the way through, not short. And her mother's fingers closing on Callie's, and holding.

They held for a long time. Longer than he had ever seen them hold.

Then Callie's mother opened her hand.

She did it first. She did it on purpose, with her eyes on Callie's face, one finger and then the next, the way you let go of a rope when you are sure the man on the other end has it. Her hand lay open on the blanket, palm up, empty. Callie's hand lay in it for a moment longer, not held, and then Callie took it back.

Callie did not grip anything. She stood by the bed with her hand at her side, open, and looked at her mother, and her mother looked back.

"Go on," said her mother. Toren heard that. She said it loud enough for the ward. "Go on, love. I've let go. It's your turn now."

---

In the fifth bed, Tonk had not looked round once.

Jab was standing between the fifth bed and the sixth, with the frame on his back and the eight jars in it and the curved ash rail standing up over his shoulder. He had been standing there since they came in. He had not sat down in the chair, because he could not sit in it with the frame on. He had not taken the frame off, and Toren did not know why, and then he did.

Tonk was sitting up on the bar with his face to the window. His hands were on the bar and his arms were straight and shaking. He had been holding himself up like that since they came in, and he would not let go of the bar and lie down, because lying down would mean turning his head.

"Tonk," said Jab.

Nothing.

"It's the eighth hour," said Jab. "I said I'd come. I've come."

Tonk's arms shook on the bar.

Toren watched Jab stand there. He saw him not reach out. He saw his hands, which had been in his pockets for three days and were not in them now, hanging at his sides, and he saw them want to go forward. He had seen Jab hold them behind his back on the thirty-ninth morning, Senna had told him, for eleven minutes, while his brother did the chair. He was holding them at his sides now the same way, without anything to hold them with.

In the fourth bed, Senna turned her head on the pillow toward the fifth.

"Tonk," she said.

Tonk did not turn round. But his shoulders moved. He was listening. He was always listening.

"I'm never going up that road," said Senna. "The one they're going up. I went up it once, when I was fifty-three, and it took my hands and my feet and twenty years of my life. And I'll never go up it again. I've planned it for them. Every step. I've told them where the gate is and where to camp and what to eat and which way the door opens at the bottom of the stair. And I'll lie in this bed and not walk one yard of it." She said it without any weight at all, the way she said *Milo Faust* to the ceiling every night. "It's a road I can plan and never walk. That's what it is. That's all it is."

Tonk's arms shook on the bar.

"Some of us plan it," said Senna, "and some of us walk it. And some of us are four, and have nails in our legs, and have to sit in a bed next to an old woman with no feet and let the ones who can walk go and walk it." She turned her face back to the ceiling. "It's the worst job there is. I'd know. I've got it too."

---

Tonk let go of the bar.

He let go and fell back on the pillow, not gently, and lay there with his eyes on the ceiling and his face red. Then he turned his head. He turned it all the way round on the pillow, away from the window, and looked at the sixth bed, and at the rail of it, and at the knot.

"Chair," he said.

The nurse was at the end of the ward. She came. She had been waiting, Toren thought, the whole time, the way nurses wait, a long way off, not looking.

"Chair," said Tonk. "I'm doing it. Most way. Don't *help*," he said to Jab, without looking at him. "Don't *you* help."

Jab put his hands behind his back.

It took Tonk nine minutes. Two fewer than the thirty-ninth; Senna said so afterward, from the pillow. He pulled himself up on the bar, and shifted himself to the edge of the bed on his arms a few inches at a time, with the frame on his leg coming with him and his face going white round the mouth each time it moved. He swung his good leg over. He got both hands under his thigh and lifted the bad leg and brought it round, shaking, onto the board the nurse held level. He let himself down into the seat on his arms. The nurse took the last hand's width.

He sat in the chair.

"*Push* me," he said to the nurse. "Not him. You."

The nurse pushed him. Not far. Four feet, round the end of the fifth bed, to the side of the sixth, so that the chair stood beside the rail of the empty bed and the knot was at the height of Tonk's hands.

He looked at the knot.

It was the one he had made a nurse tie twice on the twenty-fifth day, clumsy and huge, pulled tight by somebody who had been told to make it hold and had made it hold. He had said nobody was to untie it, and nobody had. It had been on that rail for sixteen days.

He put his hands on it.

Toren watched him try. His fingers were small and the knot was big and pulled very tight, and he could not get a nail into it. He tried from the top and from the side. He made a noise through his teeth. Jab, behind him, with his hands behind his back, made a small movement forward and stopped it.

"Don't," said Tonk, without turning round.

"I'm not."

"You *moved*."

"I moved," said Jab. "I stopped."

Tonk went back to the knot. He found a loop in it, the loose part where the nurse's second try had gone over the first, and he got his finger into it and pulled, and the loop came. Then another. It took a long time. Nobody in the ward said anything. Across the ward, Callie's mother sat on the edge of her bed with her pills done and her hat on and watched. Callie stood at the foot of her mother's bed and watched. Katori had come back with Sowerby, and they stood in the aisle and watched. Senna lay on her pillow with her eyes turned toward the chair and did not say anything at all.

The knot came undone.

The end of the long cord came off the rail of the sixth bed and lay loose in Tonk's hand. The other end was round his own wrist, where it had been every day since the second, knotted, the twelve-foot cord from the road.

---

Tonk sat in his chair with the loose end in his hand and looked at it.

Then he began to wind it.

He wound it round his own hand, the loose end first, loop after loop, slowly, the whole twelve feet of it from the road, until it was a thick coil round his fist and wrist, and there was no loose end any more. It was all on him. None of it went anywhere.

"I'm keeping my end," he said.

He said it to the coil. He did not look at Jab.

"You don't need yours," he said. "You know where I *am*." And then, because he was four, and it was important, and he had to be sure it was understood: "I'm in the fifth bed. On the *right*."

"I know where you are," said Jab. His voice did not work very well.

Tonk looked at the coil on his fist for a while. Then he put his other hand under the blanket over his knees, where Toren had not seen anything, and brought it out closed.

"Bend down," he said.

Jab bent down. He did it carefully, with eight jars on his back, bending at the knees and not the waist, the way Wyck had taught him to carry a load, until his face was level with his brother's.

Tonk opened his hand.

It was the fish. The small black wooden fish, as long as Jab's thumb, smoked black by a lifetime of hanging near a fire, with one fin broken off before Tonk was born. It had been in his hand through two operations and three pictures and every night of the road. It lay in the middle of his palm.

"Little's minding you," said Tonk.

Jab did not take it.

"*Take* it," said Tonk. "He's minding you. On the hill. That's his job now." He held it out further, at the end of his arm, as far as he could reach from the chair with his leg on the board. "You've to bring him *back*. He's *mine*. He's not yours. You're only *borrowing* him."

Jab took it.

He took it the way you take a thing you have been handed that is worth more than anything else you are carrying. He held it in his palm and looked at it. Then he put it inside his coat, on the left side, over the place under his breastbone where the lean lived, and buttoned the coat over it.

"And don't eat *inside*," said Tonk.

Jab looked at him.

"The fence," said Tonk. "She said." He jerked his head at the fourth bed. "Nothing to eat inside. Nothing to drink. No sitting down. I *heard*." He looked at Jab with enormous seriousness. "Don't eat inside, and bring Little back, and don't be asleep."

"I'll try very hard," said Jab. "Not to be asleep."

"Try *harder*," said Tonk.

Then he held up his fist, with the cord wound round it, and Jab put his own hand round it, round the coil and the small hand inside it, and held it. Only for a moment. Then Tonk took his fist back, himself, and put it in his lap, and looked at the window.

"You can go now," he said.

---

Toren sat by his grandmother's bed with his hand on his knee.

He had watched all of it. He had watched Callie's mother open her hand first and Callie not grip. He had watched a four-year-old untie a knot he had made somebody tie twice, and wind twelve feet of cord round his own fist, and hand his brother a fish. He had watched Jab stand there with his hands behind his back and not help.

He looked down the ward.

Four beds. His grandmother in the fourth on the right. On the forty-third day two orderlies would lift her into a chair, and one of them would push her down the long slope to the yellow line while she complained about the camber the whole way. Then she would lie on a narrow bed while a man with a white hand warmed the machine more times than he wanted to and guessed. Tonk in the fifth, in his chair, with a cord wound round his fist and a frame on his leg until the spring. The sixth, empty, with the rail bare. And across the ward, the fourth on the left, where a woman in a battered hat sat on the edge with her right hand shaking round a water cup, because she had walked in.

He had thought, writing the paper at the stove in eleven, that it was about a hill: a fence, a stair, a box, and the ifs under them. Then it had become a paper about ash, and hours, and who drew and who watched. Sitting by his grandmother's bed, he could see the one if he had never written down, because he had not known it was there.

*If we come back.*

His father had never priced the way home. Behind his father's road there had been nothing to go back to. Behind this one there was a ward.

He had not known that was what leading was. He had thought it was the other thing, the thing his father did, going first.

"Tor," said his grandmother.

He looked at her.

"You've gone quiet," she said. "You're doing a sum. I can see it on you. You look exactly like your father when you do that." She moved one of her hands on the blanket, a quarter of an inch. "What's the answer?"

"Everybody back," said Toren.

Senna looked at him for a long time with her pale eyes.

"Yes," she said. "That's the one." She shut her eyes. "Go on. You'll be late for the gate, and Pell will say a number at you."

He stood up. He bent down and put his forehead against hers, the way she had let him on the cart, once, on the worst night of the road. She could not put her arms round him. She did not need to.

"Pull the door," she said, with her eyes shut.

"Pull the door."

"And count them in at the pump house," said Senna. "Every night. Out and in. The man who ran the crew wrote it on his wrist with a burnt stick. That's why I know how many came out." She did not open her eyes. "Write it somewhere better than your wrist."

---

The home line was at the yard gate.

All of it. Toren had not known it was all going to be there, and it was. Dessa was on a crate by the gate with the big book open on her knees and a pencil in her hand, and her bad leg out straight. Wyck stood beside her with his bar. Katori was there, with her cap pulled down and her hands at her sides, and Ines with her bow, and Otto with his big hands in his pockets, going red about nothing. The weighbridge girl had Duchess's lead rope. The two yard lifters were there, the big quiet men from the lamp-line, and one of them had his arm bandaged where the hound had bitten it on the thirtieth. They were standing with their weight in their heels, and Toren saw Wyck see them doing it and say nothing.

And in a chair by the gate, in a grey yard coat, with a blanket over her knees and Sowerby standing behind the chair with a face like a thundercloud, the day holder.

"She asked," said Sowerby, to nobody. "She asked me nine times since yesterday. I said no nine times. Then she said she'd walk, and I believed her." He looked at the chair. "Twenty minutes. Then she's back in the east corridor."

The day holder did not look at Sowerby. She was looking at the road beyond the gate, the two hundred paces of made road to the south lip of the Sag, where the lamps of the watch stood on their poles along the east track. Her hollow was at the edge of the lip, a saucer-sized dip in the gravel with frost in the bottom of it.

"Morning," she said to Rook.

"Morning," said Rook.

That was all either of them said. Rook stood by the chair a moment longer, the way you stand by a gate you have leaned on for a long time. Then he went on.

---

Dee measured their floors at the gate. She did it the way she had done it on the twenty-fifth, fast, by the light of the gate lamp, two fingers on the inside of each wrist.

"How much?"

"The Handful I settled last night," said Callie. "And a finger and a bit of mine. Above the floor."

"A Handful settled," said Toren. "Most of an hour, last night. And two turns and a bit. I measured it once. I'm not measuring it again."

"That'll do."

"A hand and a bit," said Jab. "Floor's a finger."

Dee held his wrist a moment longer than the others, the way she had before the lift.

"Remember it," she said.

"I know where it is."

"I know you know where it is," said Dee. "I watched you find it with nothing in the jar." She let go. "I'm asking you to remember it anyway. Every morning. At the gate. With the rules."

Then she measured Rook. She put two fingers on the inside of his wrist and looked away over the yard, and counted. Toren watched her face. It did not change. She took her fingers away and wrote something in her own book, and did not say what, and Rook did not ask.

"Ten minutes," was all she said. "Half a turn."

"Half a turn," said Rook.

---

Katori was standing by the gate post.

At the north lip Toren stopped and turned round.

He could see all of it from there. The whole bowl of the Sag below him, three hundred paces from lip to lip, with the lamps of the watch along the east side and the figures at their places. The south lip, with the empty hollow in the gravel and the crate beside it. The two hundred paces of made road going back to the yard gate. The berm, and the north yard beyond it with the whitewash lanes and the shed that leaked. And the long building beyond that, low and white with frost, with the windows of the ward along its side.

And the yard office, with the long window on the second floor running the length of the north wall. There was somebody standing in it. He could not see her face from here. He could see that she was small and square, and that she was not looking at the yard. She was looking out along the road at the north lip of the Sag, where six people and a horse and a cart had stopped.

He counted them. He could not help it. He had been counting other people since the second of the month.

Pell, at Duchess's head. Dee, beside the cart, with her glass in her coat and her book under her arm. Rook, a pace in front of him, with the hole in his back still healing under his coat, standing up without having to be told. Jab, with eight jars on his back and a fish over his heart, looking not at the road ahead but back across the Sag at the long building. Callie, with the hook on her shoulder and the counter on her hip, not holding anything, her hand open at her side.

Six. And a horse.

"Persons in party," he said, out loud, to nobody.

Jab turned his head and looked at him.

"Six," said Toren. And then, because it was not the whole of it, and because Jab had once stood at a window in his socks and said *two* when there was one of him: "And everybody in the beds."

Jab looked back across the Sag at the long building for a moment longer. Then he nodded.

"And everybody in the beds," he said.

---

The road went north from the Sag, past the culvert where a boy had once knelt in the angle of a wall with a child behind him, and on into the grey. It was the old north road for two days. Then, Pell said, there was a place where a line of iron posts came in from the west across the fields, and a road beside them, and that was the old line. It was red in the atlas.

Toren went last.

He walked at the back with the paper in his coat, the signed one, folded on the side with the plate. The stone was in the other pocket, warm and faint and breathing, with a Handful settled at the bottom of him that he had taken in slowly the night before. His left arm was in its leather cuff against his chest. In front of him the cart creaked, and Duchess's breath went out in clouds. Pell said something to Dee about the camber, and Dee said something back that made Rook laugh once, low and careful, the way a man laughs with a hole in his back.

He did the sum, because he could not help it.

Six days north and west on the old line. However long inside. Six days back. Twelve Flasks, lent. Nine committed. Three untouchable, on the cart, for the way home. A fence eleven miles round. A yellow stair. A door at the bottom that opened toward you.

And at the end of it, under all of it, the thing his father had never once written down.

*Everybody back.*

It had a price. He did not know yet what it was. But it was written: in the big book, in Pell's small neat hand under *everybody out*, and on six lines Dessa had left blank at the foot of the departure page. It was in a small boy's fist, wound round twelve feet of cord, and on a woman's open hand on a blanket, and in an old woman's *pull the door*.

It was the first sum he had ever done that had the way home in it.

He walked at the back of the party, up the old north road in the grey morning of the forty-first day, and did not look back again.

## 3. The central outward mission

### One mission: the part and the rip (`OWNER`)

The former Options A and B are now one operation. Senna's treatment shed runs
on a single, pre-Fall machine at least seventy years old (`CANON.md`, Ch. 27–28), and a part in its
calibration train is failing. The only known compatible salvage is at Caul Hill
— the irradiated pre-Fall plant where Senna received her original exposure.
A new and more dangerous rip has opened at or beneath the same complex. It has
made the site harder to enter, but it has also left the one resource the team
cannot make the attempt without: deep stores of ownerless reserve-ash.

The objectives cannot be separated:

1. Carry enough clean, graded ash from Meridian to fund the approach and the
   first radiation-recall cycles. The team cannot count on ash it has not yet
   won.
2. Cross the contaminated approach while Rook, Jab, and Dee Wren
   rotate Sustain duty, recalling active radiological burden before it fixes
   into lasting tissue damage.
3. Reach the calibration assembly and recover the compatible part.
4. Contain or collapse the new rip, kill its new Riftspawn expression through
   a full-team action, and recover the resulting ash so the healers can bring
   everyone home.

The third former option — making the disk mystery the journey's reason — stays
retired. The disk receives one costly clue late in the book, not the book's
engine.

### Why it must leave Meridian

The shed's machine cannot be repaired from spare parts on hand; Meridian's own
hardened depot inventory has nothing compatible (a callback to Annice Fell's
ledger, Ch. 28: things go into that depot, they do not come out). The active
rip also cannot be ignored: if it grows, it changes the Sag from a strained
defense line into an open road for stronger Riftspawn. Caul Hill is roughly a
week's travel past the Sag's worst stretch — close enough to reach inside the
machine's failure clock, far enough to be a true expedition.

### Why adults cannot simply solve it

Two separate, compounding reasons, both institutional rather than a plot
contrivance:

1. **Every ordinary defender who could lead it is already spoken for.** Meridian's
   registered defenders — the people who can actually hold a road shut —
   are stretched thin covering the worsening Sag line itself (this is the
   book's own opening pressure). Pulling one of them off the wall for a
   week to escort a salvage run means a week of the Sag going unheld, which
   the Director's office will not authorize under any argument.
2. **The site selects for a very rare team, not for youth.** Caul Hill's
   radiation cannot be endured by general toughness or fast healing. Sustain
   can recall the *active burden* from tissue, but it cannot undo old damage
   after that damage has fixed. Rook and Jab can do the work; the Homura
   cross-medic understands how to direct it, rotate healers, and increase the
   amount of ash a Kindled soul can safely hold and spend. Callie and Toren
   supply the Ward, Guard, and extraction capability that keep those healers
   alive. The party is selected by complementary doors and training, not by
   the premise that children tolerate radiation better than adults.

### What failure costs

If the part is not recovered, Senna's and (per the same shed, same cycle)
every other patient's course begins to fail within roughly eleven days — the
same interval already established as the treatment's own clock (Ch. 27–28).
Failure is not abstract: it is a countable number of cycles before Senna's
marrow support lapses, stated on the page the way Pell states a distance.
Secondary cost: if the mission is caught leaving without authorization, the
whole clan's residency review is jeopardized, which threatens every household
riding on it (Callie's, Jab's, the Hobb's Wall clan's).

### How it joins the Sag / residency / ash / defender threads

The mission is staffed, provisioned, and made survivable using everything the
book's other threads have taught. The team carries large stores of lifted,
graded ash as fuel, not as trade cargo and not as a lucky find. Toren's Guard
and Callie's widened Ward protect the Sustain rotation. Jab senses changes in
the bodies under his care and learns to recall radiation deliberately. Rook
teaches him the lived technique; Dee teaches the repeatable
capacity discipline: take ash in, settle it without confusing it for personal
reserve, spend it through Sustain, vent the harmful load, and refill before
the soul bottoms out. The defender cohort covers the gap at Meridian while
the expedition is away. The ash secured at the rip then becomes the material
proof that the team can protect Meridian and alter the exploitative residency
economy, rather than simply argue against it.

## 6. Youth-defender cohort design

- **Purpose:** the Sag is worsening faster than Meridian's adult defenders
  can cover, and the station's own liability doctrine (registered Kindled
  residents are sorted into labor by door) already pulls Kindled teenagers
  toward dangerous work informally. The cohort formalizes that pull into
  something with adult oversight, rather than pretending it isn't happening.
- **Membership logic:** not "every Kindled kid at Meridian" — a small,
  specific group built from need and consent: Callie, Jab (if his household's
  situation allows; his instinct to keep working through the Elder's ethic
  makes this an easy yes for him and a harder one for the book to justify
  given Tonk), Toren, plus willing members of the Hobb's Wall clan (Dessa,
  Wyck) and at least one Meridian-native trainee to keep the cohort from
  reading as "the Book One cast plus scenery." Recommend one new named
  Meridian local, invented at drafting, who has no Book One history and
  therefore reacts to everything (the ash economy, the found-family's
  closeness, Rook's method) the way a reader coming in cold might.
- **Adult safeguards:** Dee Wren oversees formal reserve and Sustain drills; Rook
  oversees live Sag-line rotations; no cohort member holds a line alone
  without an adult in immediate support distance; injuries and overdraws are
  logged and reviewed, mirroring the station's own paperwork culture (a
  believable, in-world safeguard rather than an authorial promise the plot
  ignores).
- **Training progression:** shadowing → supervised jar-and-sack work behind
  an adult's held line → controlled short holds under direct supervision →
  full rotations with an adult in support range → the Caul Hill mission as
  the first expedition beyond Meridian's support perimeter. It is not an
  unsupervised youth test: Rook and Dee travel, while Katori and
  Pell divide field/navigation coaching and the defense of the people left
  behind.
- **Moral pressure:** the book should not resolve whether this is right. The
  Director's office is correct that the Sag needs more hands than it has;
  Pell and Senna's mother-thread (Callie's own mother's "you don't get to
  spend yourself on me") are correct that this trades children's bodies for
  a shortfall adults created. Both positions stay true at once. The named
  residency-injustice case (§3, §11) should be a cohort member's family —
  someone who took the ash-lifting-equivalent contract without a found
  family able to absorb the cost, exactly as both prior drafts already
  recommend — so the moral weight lands on a specific person, not a policy
  debate.
- **The first school without calling it one:** no uniforms, no ranked class
  structure, no shared dormitory-and-bell-schedule texture. Training happens
  in the same huts, the same stove-side conversations, and the same north
  yard the rest of the book already lives in. What *does* begin here is
  durable: three marked practice lanes, a shared equipment rack, and Dessa's
  append-only record of drills, injuries, reserve, recovery, and repeatable
  results. Every cohort member keeps a private want unrelated to combat
  (Callie's counting, Jab's caregiving, Toren's leadership anxiety, and
  whatever the new local recruit wants that has nothing to do with the Sag).

## 7. Ash-learning ladder

Grounded directly in the drafted Rook ash-collection scene
(`book-01-tallow-gate/book-02-drafts/movement-01-ash-collection.md`) and the
Ch. 27–28 / Ch. 18 "the jar" material — this ladder should be taught through
exactly that kind of scene, not through explanation.

1. **Recognition and collection.** Reading a drift's Yield by eye and feel
   before touching it (Flask vs. Handful, "a fair one" vs. "a dirty one," per
   the ash-collection draft); the flat-palm technique, working a span at a
   time, holding it low and open rather than closed. Failure mode: hauling —
   trying to pull ash faster than it comes, which yields less, not more.
   Teaching scene: Rook's drafted scene itself, adapted to include a cohort
   member learning alongside Wyck and Dessa rather than only overheard.
2. **Grading.** Handful / Flask / Barrel / Well (`CANON.md`, Yield) as a
   trade vocabulary, learned first as an insult-proof way to size up danger
   ("Handful or Flask?"), then as the literal unit the weighbridge pays in.
   Teaching scene: a weighbridge dispute (Ch. 18's "that's thinner than it
   ought to come" beat is the model) where a cohort member's read is
   challenged and has to be defended with evidence, not confidence.
3. **Cleaning and contamination.** Silt, ditch-water, and "a dirty Handful"
   as concrete hazards — contaminated ash burns unreliably and is priced
   down, or in the worst case damages whoever spends it (a training-safe
   version of an overdraw, introduced here in miniature). Teaching scene: a
   cohort member's first real weighbridge failure, graded down publicly, and
   having to learn why rather than being told the rule.
4. **Storage and carriage.** Jars, sacks, flasks, folded canvas between them
   (all attested in Ch. 18, 27, and the ash-collection draft); why quantity
   and grade both degrade under bad handling, and why a station's economy
   treats a jar of clean Flask as worth defending on the road.
5. **Taking it in — ash held by the soul.** Ash is ownerless, unlike a
   person's expended reserve. Skin-contact absorption is deliberate and
   unhurried: the same flat-palm stillness used to collect it, turned inward.
   Ash does not become a second organ or named storage tank; the soul holds
   it as usable reserve. Rushing intake causes spill, nausea, false fullness,
   or a lock that prevents clean spending.
6. **Increasing usable working depth.** Dee teaches that a
   Kindled soul does not become metaphysically larger in a week; the person
   learns to use more of the depth already present. Intake, settle, spend,
   vent, rest, repeat. Capacity is measured by clean cycles and recovery, not
   by how much ash someone can force in once. This is the training that makes
   the Caul Hill reserve practical instead of merely heavy cargo.
7. **Radiation recall through Sustain.** Rook teaches Jab to feel the active
   harmful burden in a body without confusing it with old injury. Sustain
   draws or recalls that burden while ash pays for the work. The technique
   prevents further damage during exposure; it does not erase damage already
   fixed in tissue and therefore does not retroactively cure Senna. The
   healer must vent the recalled burden and rotate out before it begins to
   lodge in the healer instead.
8. **Spending through a door under pressure.** Absorbed ash can power Edge,
   Stride, Ward, Sustain, or Resilience without first emptying personal
   reserve. Teaching scene: a controlled Sag-line failure where ash-fuel,
   not personal endurance, is the difference between holding and breaking.
9. **Advanced team use and overdraw.** The expedition uses a measured
   rotation rather than a vague shared pool: one person works, one holds the
   reserve, one watches for spill and overdraw. Spending past safe working
   depth causes visible damage even when ash remains available. This is why
   the party needs stores, trained bodies, and discipline rather than one
   miraculous healer.

## 9. Three substantial action sequences

1. **The Sag-line failure (Movement 2).** Tactical problem: a held line under
   direct, close-range pressure, where the danger is a lapse of seconds, not
   raw threat scale — the same problem the ash-collection draft and Ch. 27–28
   already establish. What's different here from Book One's fights: this is
   *training* breaking in real time, not a road ambush; the tension is
   "who's watching whom," and the cost should land on the cohort, making the
   danger of the youth-defender program real rather than theoretical.
2. **The rip-road approach (Movement 4).** Tactical problem: an advancing
   party has to protect its own healers while every pause consumes ash. The
   threat is not only attack; it is being delayed until the Sustain budget
   fails. Callie's moving Ward, Toren's Guard, and Jab's recall first become a
   repeatable team cycle here.
3. **The Caul Hill Sustain-echo (Movement 5).** Tactical problem: attrition
   helps the enemy. The new Riftspawn recalls loose ash and uses it to repair
   itself and nearby spawn. Callie must isolate the current, Toren must make a
   stable physical opening, and Jab must sense and interrupt the renewal
   pulse. The adults keep the radiation lane survivable, but the three
   protagonists solve the fight. Movement 6's Sag hold is consequence and
   public proof, not a second larger climax competing with Caul Hill.

## The Kindling (`CANON`)

The awakening event/ceremony, fixed to age 13. Grants access to power through a
device (an Ember), through what looks like granted magic, or through disciplined
internal cultivation. Which door a person walks through first is accident of
circumstance, culture, and region — not a difference in what is actually happening
inside them.

## The Path — the core truth (`CANON`, load-bearing)

Ember-users believe the power lives in the device. Magic-users believe the power is
granted from outside, by the rift. Both are wrong the same way: **the power is one
force, reached through two doors each side mistakes for the whole house.** It comes
from within — a soul-level connection the Ember or the rift-touched brain merely
interfaces with.

This is not learned by being told. It has to be trained into reflex through real
stakes and real consequence — understanding it and acting on it under pressure are
different skills, and the gap between them is Rook's actual curriculum.

A practitioner who has internalized this reflexively stops being limited to one
interface. Others call this **unbound** — plain language for what such a person can
visibly do: draw on any interface freely.

## Satori — realization and disk (`CANON`, load-bearing)

**Satori** (state) is Rook's word, inherited from Vera Kess herself — her own
clan's word, given to both the thing and the state on purpose, per Ch. 17 — for
the realization itself —
the moment the door disappears and what's behind every door turns out to be the same
light. Mechanism, in Rook's teaching terms: an empty mind (clearing the false model
each tradition installs) and an open soul (no longer guarding against admitting the
power was always internal), connected.

**A/the Satori** (object) is the disk's locked in-world name — deliberately the same
word as the state. Rook's private, unconfirmed belief: the object is the physical
record of someone's completed realization, a soul that filled all the way and no
longer needed the record once true mastery ("god level") was reached. Taught to
Toren as truth, never contradicted on the page, but explicitly this series' own myth
about the object — not a cross-series reveal. The disk's true origin stays
permanently unexplained in-manuscript (cross-series Artifact rule: no series ever
explains it).

## The soul — reservoir and moral weight (`CANON`, load-bearing)

No separate storage term. The soul itself holds source-power. Before Satori it fills
only through a person's Kindling-given door — narrow, slow, easily emptied. After
Satori it fills straight from the source — deep, fast, and this is the actual
mechanism behind rapid recovery, not a separately trained technique. Rook's line:
*"Your soul isn't where the power goes. Your soul is what fills."*

The same connection is what lets a person feel the true cost of a choice rather than
reason around it — part of why Rook's judgment of people is fast and reliable.

**Fast recovery is a mechanism, not a skill.** A student who hasn't reached Satori
heals at ordinary human speed. Rook can top someone else's soul up from outside two
ways — magic-adjacent healing arts and Ember-boosted recovery — and treats neither as
more legitimate; that refusal to pick a side is itself part of the lesson.

**Ash capacity and usable working depth (`CANON`, owner ruling 2026-09-27).**
The soul holds absorbed reserve-ash; there is no second storage organ and no new
"bank" vocabulary. A trained practitioner can learn to use more of the depth already
present: absorb clean ash, let it settle, spend it through a door, vent what the work
cannot safely retain, rest, and refill. This increases safe *working capacity*, not
rank and not the metaphysical size of the soul. Forced intake causes spill, false
fullness, loss of control, or overdraw even while physical ash remains available.
Homura had repeatable cross-medical methods for teaching this; Rook knows pieces by
experience, not the complete protocol.

**A deeper soul/mind connection, reserved for a Book 2 flashback (`POSSIBLE`,
owner-directed).** Book 1 established the mechanism at the level Rook himself
understood it at Homura. Book 2 should go one layer further, through a new
flashback — not Rook's own retelling of the same material again, but a piece
he either withheld the first time because he hadn't finished understanding it
himself, or one that only makes sense to tell once the team is far enough
along to test it. The natural content: what "an empty mind and an open soul,
connected" actually feels like from the inside, in enough concrete, trainable
detail that it can motivate the team's own breakthroughs in this book (the
Guard, above; individual rank movement) rather than staying descriptive. This
mirrors Book 1's own successful pattern exactly — Ch. 17's campfire flashback
directly preceded Ch. 18's rank payoff — and should be built the same way:
revelation first, capability second, not the other way round.

## Heartfire (`CANON`)

The soul-bond phenomenon between two people, indifferent to which door either reaches
power through. Not chosen — recognized. Rook and Vera's bond (see Homura, below).

## The Ember (`CANON`, expanded this pass — growing arsenal, owner-directed)

Personal conduit artifact granted at Kindling. Two configurations, never both at once
below unbound mastery:

- **Edge** — weapon extension; reshapes to need and training (blade, spear-point,
  twin knives); more complex shapes cost more mental effort to hold.
- **Stride** — transport extension; reinforced limb-struts or a light frame for burst
  speed, hard leaps, a short glide.

**Edge has two coupled parts (`CANON`, owner ruling 2026-09-22): the moving
metal form and its edge-light.** The Ember's metal moves and reshapes into the
trained weapon form. A narrow line of light can then kindle along the working
edge; that light is what turns the formed metal from a blunt practice tool into
a truly sharp weapon. A user can form and train with the metal while leaving the
edge-light dark, which permits contact drills without making every mistake
lethal. Igniting the edge-light costs additional concentration and reserve.
Its color, brightness, width, and steadiness are personal expressions and can
range from dull to brilliant; those traits are not, by themselves, rank markers.
Rank is still demonstrated by reliable shape control, repertoire, switching,
and what the user can sustain under pressure. Edge-light is local to the metal's
working edge and is distinct from Blaze-rank full-body visible light.

**Edge's shape-repertoire is rank-gated, not a menu open from day one.** This closes
a gap the manuscript already implied (why Rook keeps telling Toren to "keep the ugly
shape, stop trying to be good at it") without changing a line of locked prose:

| Rank | What Edge can hold |
|---|---|
| Kindled / Ember | One metal shape, usually whatever the user's instinct reaches for under real pressure rather than one they've chosen — the "ugly," unrefined default. Its edge-light may flash or gutter involuntarily. Trying to hold something more deliberate before this is earned is exactly what puts it out. |
| Flame / Fire | One *chosen*, refined metal shape becomes reliable and can be re-summoned with confidence; its edge-light can be deliberately lit or left dark for training — the difference between a shape happening to you and a shape you've picked. |
| Torch | The single-door ceiling of Edge mastery: multiple distinct shapes held and switched between at will (blade, spear-point, twin knives) — full command of one door's weapon repertoire, and as far as that repertoire alone ever goes. |

Unbound mastery (Blaze and up) does not add more Edge shapes on top of Torch's
repertoire — it adds *other doors' domains entirely*, which is a different kind of
growth. See below.

**Stride's second shape — Guard (`POSSIBLE`, owner-directed, Book 2's
central discovery, not Book 1 material).** Stride has always been described as
transport only — burst speed, hard leaps, a glide. Nothing in Book 1
establishes that as a hard ceiling; it was simply never pushed. Book 2's
actual engine is the team discovering that Stride's strut-frame can be held in
a second configuration: locked rigid and angled outward instead of loaded for
motion, taking a hit meant for a person instead of moving them out of its way.
**This is not a third door and it is not Ward** — Ward is a field/barrier
effect projected outward (Marta's wall, Rook's "shut the road"), a magic-
tradition expression. Guard is a *structural* answer using the same
strut hardware Stride already runs on: the device doing something closer to
armor than to a spell, because it never needed the magic door's field-logic to
begin with — the Ember-lineage device answering a problem the old world would
have called engineering, not magic, using the same interface most people
already think of as purely mechanical.

**Why this matters for Edge+Stride together:** a practitioner who can already
blend Edge and Stride (Blaze-adjacent breadth, first proven by Toren in Book 1
under pressure, not yet a repeatable technique) can, once Guard is known
and trained, run Edge for offense and Stride-as-Guard for defense
*simultaneously* — the same blended state, a different pair of expressions
than the speed+weapon combination Book 1 proved. This does not require a
second door. It requires knowing Guard exists, which nobody in this book
has known to look for, matching Homura's own oldest lesson: the truth was
available the whole time; nobody had gone and tried it.

**Discovery, not invention — this should read as found, not built.** The
most satisfying version is an accident under real pressure (someone's Stride
locks wrong in the middle of a fall or a block and holds instead of failing)
that then gets deliberately reproduced and taught, mirroring exactly how Vera
found Satori and how Rook learned it from her — the discovery always comes
from someone under real stakes, not from a workshop.

## Three doors, three native domains (`CANON`, new this pass)

Each of the Kindling's three doors has its own native expression, matching what
the manuscript has already shown on the page rather than inventing new mechanics:

- **The Ember's domain: Edge (offense) and Stride (mobility).** Established above.
- **Magic's domain: Ward (defense) and Sustain (healing, sensing).** This is
  Marta Faust's wall in Ch. 2, and it is what Rook does at the pinch in Ch. 3 when
  Toren recognizes it as "Marta's thing" — a shield/barrier effect, not a weapon
  and not speed. Also covers the healing arts already established (topping up
  another person's soul from outside).
- **Disciplined cultivation's domain: Resilience.** Not a shape and not a shield —
  a body that has decided, over years, not to be moved. This is exactly Wyck's
  third-door manifestation in Ch. 10 ("two of them hit you at full run and you're
  standing where you were standing"), now given its formal name. **Cross-property
  note, bible-only, never on the page (`craft/connections/CONNECTIONS_BIBLE.md`,
  operator-confirmed 2026-09-20):** this door is the same underlying system as
  Valdris's Path in *The Fractured Path*, structural-only. A Kindling-universe
  practitioner has no Architect, no mesh, no lineage teacher — entirely
  self-taught from isolation, which is why it comes slowly. Wyck's own gradual,
  hard-won progression is the confirmed example; nothing about his arc changes,
  it just has a cosmological reason underneath it the reader never sees.

**A single-door practitioner only ever has their own door's domain(s).** This is
why the found-family group needs each other — an Ember-only fighter genuinely
cannot ward, the way Marta could and Toren can't. It is *not* a gap in the Ember;
it is the reason competence is distributed across people rather than concentrated
in one.

**Opening a second door (the graduated Blaze structure, above) means gaining access
to that door's native domain too, blended with what you already had — not a new
tier of your original door's power, a genuinely different kind of ability.** This
is what "more than a stride and a sword" actually means as a person grows: the
arsenal widens by doors opening, not by one door's menu getting longer forever.

### Sustain and radiation recall (`CANON`, owner ruling 2026-09-27)

Sustain can read and recall an *active radiological burden* from a living body.
It does not reverse old tissue damage once that damage has fixed, restore destroyed
marrow, or make exposure harmless. The practitioner senses what is still acting on
the body, draws that burden through contact, spends source-power to separate it from
the person, and vents it before it lodges in the practitioner instead. Physical
contamination controls and ordinary medicine remain necessary.

Radiation recall is expensive. Clean reserve-ash can pay the source-power cost,
which is why a deep-zone expedition needs large measured stores and trained healer
rotation rather than one heroic effort. Running out of ash forces the healer onto
personal reserve; continuing past that point can make the healer the next casualty.
The technique therefore has five visible constraints: exposure time, ash quantity,
safe working depth, venting/rotation, and the difference between active burden and
damage already done.

**Rook:** confirmed Sustain practitioner through his native magic door. He can teach
the embodied act of reading and recall, but not Homura's complete capacity protocol.

**Jab (`CANON` capability; Book Two mastery `PLANNED`):** his Book One "second
thing"—sensing Tonk's decline and supporting his failing body—is Sustain, not a
separate danger power. In Book Two he learns deliberate radiation recall and
ash-funded rotation. Meridian identifies the rarity immediately and targets him as
a primary medical recruit. Recruitment creates an obligation conflict; it does not
transfer ownership of Jab's gift or erase his duty to Tonk.

**Twin-Kindled (`CANON`, owner ruling 2026-09-20).** The rare, anomalous case
of a Kindling that opens two doors from two *different* traditions at once,
at rank one, before any training or earned advancement — not to be confused
with a "two-door Blaze" (below), which is a door opened later, through
progression, within a practitioner's own growth. A twin-kindle has no
framework for it and no community precedent to name it by; there is no
institutional term for this in the quartet era, only the plain, folk one:
**twin-kindled.** This is the same shape of anomaly as the 2680 series'
three-tradition "combined-path" protagonist (`PRD.md` §7) and the suppressed
"Category R" cases Homura later buries — this is one of the earliest known
instances, 530 years before either.

**Callie, worked example (`PLANNED`, side story, not yet drafted):**
twin-kindled Edge (Ember's domain — her hook becomes/gains a blade) and Ward
(magic's domain), together, at her Kindling. Her Ward specifically manifests
as a **personal, mobile shield** — a smaller, body-hugging variant of the
same barrier effect as Marta's wall (above) and Rook's "shut the road": not
a projected field over a group or a room, but a shield that moves with her,
"the wall no one thinks they can move," worn rather than cast. She discovers
it involuntarily — arms thrown up on reflex against a kicking horse — before
she has any idea what either door is.

**Rook, worked example:** born magic-tradition (Lowry Bend, a hand-mending village
whose trade is exactly the Ward domain — see Homura below), and learned Ember at
the station through Satori. He is therefore at minimum a confirmed **two-door Blaze**: Edge and
Stride natively from the Ember, Ward natively from the door he was born to — which
is exactly why Ch. 3's "shut the road" reads as the same kind of thing Marta's
wall was, and why he can blend Edge, Stride, *and* Ward in the same fight without
it being unbound's outer ceiling. Whether he has ever opened the third door
(Resilience) is not yet established and does not need to be — it stays open for a
later book.

## Riftspawn — what they are, and why their ash refills a soul (`CANON`, deepened
this pass, owner-directed)

Creatures that arrive through unstable wormholes; dissolve into a reserve-ash on
defeat (the loot mechanic). Specific species behavior/weakness/cost is established
per-book and held as canon — see `BOOK_MAP.md` → Opposition and off-page motion for
Book 1's locked roster.

**Working theory — Homura's own, measured but never proven beyond doubt, the
thread cut short when the station fell. Rook teaches it because it's the best
account anyone ever gave him, not because he can prove it, and this is the correct
epistemic register for any character repeating it on the page.**

Riftspawn are not creatures that came through the wormholes so much as *matter*
that did — raw, unfiltered source-power snagged loose by an unstable tear and given
monstrous shape by nothing more than the hostility of a world it doesn't belong in.
It never passed through a door. No Kindling shaped it, no soul organizes it, no
Ember or magic-tradition gave it a discipline to hold itself together with. It is
power with no one home to hold it — which is exactly why it comes apart into ash
the instant something interrupts whatever crude shape it was wearing, and exactly
why that ash is such clean fuel. A person's own expended reserve has to be borrowed
from another *person* and repaid, with interest, because it already belongs to
someone (see "The soul," above). Riftspawn ash belongs to no one. That is the whole
difference, and it is why hunting is the frontier's actual economy, not just its
danger — and, in the oldest tellings that touch the Mother and the Father below,
why a wormhole is described less as a door and more as a wound: something the
world's own making leaking back out through a tear that was never supposed to open.

**Variation — the different Riftspawn are different raw expressions of the same
force (owner-directed, the "fun twist"):** if a Riftspawn is raw power wearing a
shape with nobody home, then different Riftspawn are echoes of different worked-
power expressions gone feral — the same underlying force the Ember, magic, and the
soul itself all reach for, showing up unshaped and undirected. Husks are the
plainest echo: mass without a door at all, aggression with nothing behind it.
Verge-hounds echo something closer to sensing — pack timing that reads, to a
trained eye, uncannily like Dessa's own thirty-foot awareness, just feral and
untaught. Further types, invented as needed past Book 1, should each echo one
recognizable expression of worked power gone feral — an Edge-echo that grows
something like a blade out of raw aggression, a Stride-echo that moves faster than
its body should carry it, a ward-echo that shrugs off blows the way Marta's wall
does, or a sustain-echo that patches other Riftspawn the way healing patches a
person, with no mercy behind it because there is no one home to mean it. This is a
rule new types get checked against, not a bestiary to fill in wholesale ahead of
need. Book 1's own first use of it is **a Breach** — see `BOOK_MAP.md`.

**Worked-power response is species-specific (`CANON`, Book 1 Ch. 53).** Worked
power generally attracts Riftspawn attention, but that is not a universal rule
that overrides observed species behavior. Verge-hounds treat the visible light
of Jab's lit Edge as a boundary: they turn away from the light and drive at the
dark gap. Jab can therefore steer them by placing light where he does not want
them to go. The light is a fence, not a lure or a killing effect. Preserve this
exception when Book 2 records Jab's capability; do not generalize it to every
Riftspawn species.

**Book Two sustain-echo (`PLANNED`, owner ruling 2026-09-27).** The new,
more-dangerous Caul Hill rip produces a Riftspawn that recalls loose ash toward
itself and uses that current to reconstitute damaged spawn. It does not create ash
from nothing. Its renewal can be broken only by a team solution: Ward isolates the
ash-current, Guard/Edge opens and holds a physical route, and Sustain senses and
interrupts the renewal pulse. The creature's final name, anatomy, and exact visual
design remain drafting decisions; the mechanic and team requirement are locked.

## The Sag line and ash-lifting — the settled economy's version of hunting
(`POSSIBLE`, Book 2 development, extends the Ch. 28 plant)

**Confirmed Hobb's-Wall-specific, not shared.** The other edition's Ch. 27-28
stages the arrangement differently — the whole group registered as a liable
"working company" under conditional residence with Embers held under receipt,
rather than an individual bought-labor debt — and has no equivalent of a
Sag-line-style contract at all. Same pattern as the Homura-second-copy
divergence: developed here, does not travel.

If hunting is the frontier's economy (above), ash-lifting is the same economy
run inside a station's walls, bureaucratized. Reserve-ash is still ownerless,
clean fuel — but where the frontier lets whoever kills a Riftspawn simply take
what's left of it, a settlement with something to protect turns the same
resource into a controlled, contracted extraction job, and that job becomes a
lever on the people who have nothing else to trade.

**The Sag line** is a stretch of berm-edge road at Meridian's north yard that
has never fully settled since 2130 — a known local weak point in whatever
usually keeps wormhole activity rare this close to the station (consistent
with the failing-filter theory: a trend shows first at its weakest points,
long before anyone agrees to call it a trend). Riftspawn come through there
more often than anywhere else near Meridian, which means reserve-ash actually
accumulates there in usable quantity — real fuel, sitting exposed — provided
something holds the source down long enough to gather it before it disperses
or is scavenged.

**The technique.** Ash-lifting is not labor a strong back can perform alone.
It requires holding the road shut for the whole shift — the same Ward-family
barrier Marta used at the cut in Book 1, sustained rather than thrown in a
single crisis — while other laborers move behind the line and sack the ash. A
lapse of even a few seconds can mean a fresh Riftspawn body between the
line-holder and the crew. This is a narrow, specific qualification: it needs
someone with a working defensive door, or (like Rook) enough Ward-adjacent
instinct to fake it under real strain, and almost nobody at Meridian without
formal magic-tradition training can do it at all. That scarcity is the whole
reason the job exists as a contract rather than an open trade.

**The trade mechanism.** Meridian keeps two gates deliberately separate:
medicine, and residency. A course of treatment can be — as Senna's is —
unconditional, cleared at the gate, owed to no one, and the station is telling
the truth when it says so. Residency inside the berm is a different gate
entirely, *reviewed*, and a household's standing is assessed on what the
station reckons it contributes, not on what it needs. A household with
nothing to trade does not get to live near medicine that was never being
withheld from it. Ash-lifting is the specific contract Meridian will accept
from a Kindled resident whose only asset is a combat-capable door: four
seasons on the worst-paid, most dangerous work on the books, in exchange for a
line item on a residency review rather than a wage that reflects the risk.
The low pay is not an oversight — the wage is a rounding error beside the real
price, which is measured in years of a specific person's risk that the
station extracts because there is no one else positioned to refuse it.

**Why this is the book's structural antagonist, not a villain.** This is the
mechanism Director Corliss's broader Kindled registration and labor system
runs on, generalized past any one contract: unaffiliated Kindled residents are
sorted into whatever labor category their door qualifies them for, paid in
scraps of currency and reviewed access rather than fair value, and told —
correctly, by the numbers — that the alternative (no system, no station, no
protection) would cost more. It is defensible on its own terms and still
prices desperation. Rook's debt is the sympathetic, personal-stakes version
the reader already has reason to feel. Movement 5's named injustice should be
someone who took the equivalent contract without a found family able to
absorb the cost when it went wrong.

## The split world (`CANON`)

- **Tech-zones** — regions that retained functioning hardware; Meridian is one.
- **Magic-saturated zones** — regions where wormhole-borne power dominates; cultures
  adapted quickly.
- **"Alien tech"** — technology that functions specifically inside magic-saturated
  zones, apparently a conduit for magic infusion. Central unsolved-science thread for
  the series; not resolved in Book 1.

Rook moves easily between tech-, magic-, and Ember-dominant regions — a demonstrated
expression of the Path: not loyal to one interface, so no region reads him as an
outsider on that basis.

**A failing filter (`POSSIBLE`, owner-directed, adopted from the parallel run
— unconfirmed in-world theory, not authorial fact).** Homura-era theorists
suspected the 2080 detonations did not only cause the EMP and tech collapse
already established — they may have damaged something underneath the world
that other series in the shared cosmology would call a filter on the Source.
On this theory, the Mars disk's activation and the wormholes were not an
independent, coincidental trigger — they were a *consequence* of that damage,
the door opening because something holding it shut had already cracked.
**This is one-directional and ongoing, not a single event: the filter keeps
failing, magic-saturated territory keeps expanding at tech-zones' expense, and
Well-tier collapses (see Yield, above) are its leading edge, not independent
capstone disasters — expect them to recur across the series at escalating
severity.** This reframes any future "unite the fractured world" arc as a race
against a real deadline, not a one-time political achievement. It also gives
an in-world reason (never stated as confirmed) for two things that would
otherwise read as unearned creep across a long series: later generations of
Kindled sometimes reaching further, faster, than earlier ones under
comparable training; and Riftspawn types diversifying and growing larger
across decades rather than holding to a fixed bestiary. Both are the same
door sitting more open over time. Held at the same epistemic register as the
Riftspawn-echo theory above — a working suspicion, not a fact the text ever
confirms.

## Homura and Satori — released at Movement 4's campfire (Ch. 17), `CANON`, now
locked in full detail

**Rook's home:** Lowry Bend, ~400 miles south-east of the Hobb's Wall region — a
hand-tradition (magic) village with no stone-tradition (Ember) history at all.
His mother's hand-mending trade was the village's whole model of power; Rook
himself was doorless for eleven months after his own Kindling at thirteen before
anything worked.

**Homura:** a research valley built deliberately at a fracture (a place of
constant tearing/wormhole activity), ~200 miles north of Lowry Bend. Run by
**Orrin Vell** (five foot nothing, universally feared, the station's lead
researcher). About sixty people, forty of them subject-children pulled from nine
different traditions on purpose, paired cross-tradition for every test so no one
could hide inside their own door's assumptions. Paid subjects' families in kind
(a doctor twice a year, medicine, a standing debt) rather than coin — this is how
Rook was recruited at fourteen, to buy his mother medical care she never actually
lived to use much of.

**Vera Kess**, fifteen, stone-tradition (Ember), eight generations of a clan that
had kept more of its original vocabulary than most — including the word
"Satori." She and Rook despised each other for roughly a year (his hand-
tradition vs. her stone-tradition, the oldest argument in the world, re-fought
in miniature). It broke when she was badly hurt defending two younger children
from a Riftspawn incursion into the valley and there was no healer among the
station's own children young enough to help her — Rook sat outside her sickroom
for nine hours and stopped being able to locate the argument at all. She gave
him the word "Satori" in that same conversation — her own realization had come
a year earlier, alone, unbelieved.

**The findings:** four years of matched instrument readings across nine
traditions, converging on identical patterns regardless of door — the actual,
provable end of the tradition/door argument, two years from being published
Compact-wide when the station was destroyed.

**The attack:** Cinder Company came over the wire on the night of the eleventh
of the ninth month (a fixed in-world date, usable for future continuity),
organized and deliberate, not a Riftspawn-style rush — took the gate first to
prevent escape, not entry. They came for the records specifically, not simply to
kill; Rook has never found the second copy or learned who commissioned the
destruction (deliberately open, a Book 2+ question). Vera held a doorway shape
for eleven children for as long as she could and died there, spending her last
minutes making Rook promise to save the papers — a promise he could not keep.
Eleven of sixty escaped over the west wire; nine were children, including a girl
named **Dee Wren**, whom Rook personally walked to safety over two months. A boy
named **Ander Croft**, eleven, is the only other confirmed Satori case before
Dessa — died the same night as everyone else.

**The Ember Rook carries is Vera's** — recovered off the floor of the room where
she died, carried every day since, "cut for a fifteen-year-old girl with small
hands." He has never told anyone this in eleven years.

**The lane:** two years of grief-numbed withdrawal at Lowry Bend after Homura,
ended when the same or an affiliated Cinder Company crew, hired as village
defense against a fracture incursion, killed Rook's mother and both brothers
through friendly fire in the dark. The company paid compensation and claimed the
responsible man was "put off the books." Rook did not connect this to Homura for
six years — the two griefs were "two entirely separate holes" until a bootmaker
selling salvaged company pay-chits turned one over in front of him five years
ago and they became one hole. He has spent six years since hunting the company
(eleven chits bought as tracking/trade currency, four remaining), finding their
camp cold twice before the current pursuit.

**Why this changes the revenge arc:** killing Cinder Company avenges the people.
Teaching the Path — finishing, in a living student, what Homura's paperwork never
got to say — avenges what they actually destroyed. Rook names this explicitly at
the Ch. 17 campfire as the reason he began teaching the clan on purpose, the
morning after Dessa's Movement 2 Satori spark: a second, slower way of answering
for Homura that doesn't require the killing to matter.

**Rook's real name stays reserved** — promised to Wyck specifically, first,
when Cinder Company is settled (Movement 5).

## Rank — the flame ladder (`CANON`, owner-directed — supersedes the earlier
generic "Kindled → trained → unbound" placeholder)

**No floating HUD/status window.** This world's power system is explicitly internal
and felt (soul-filling), not software. Rank is spoken, not displayed — per
`craft/modules/litrpg.md`'s diegetic rule, it surfaces when it changes choice,
stakes, interpretation, or emotion, never on a timer or as an overlay.

**The ladder, in order:**

| Rank | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Kindled** | The door's open, nothing shaped yet. Everyone's starting point the moment the Kindling happens. Dessa's exact case — "Kindled to nothing" — is a Kindled practitioner whose door never opened onto a working interface; she is Kindled and stays there until Movement 2's Satori touch. |
| **Ember** | Glows, warms, stays contained. Where most of the cast sits through early Movement 1 — the deliberate pun on the device's own name is intentional: an Ember-rank person and an Ember the object share a name because early power *is* small, held, private. |
| **Flame** | Catches and holds on its own for the first time — the threshold state, the first time a person's fire sustains itself without them white-knuckling it. |
| **Fire** | Real output, drives real work — the fire a camp actually gathers around, not just a candle a person carries. |
| **Torch** | Fire made portable and chosen, not merely endured. **The ceiling of single-door mastery.** Garrick is Torch: thirty-one years, genuinely skilled, and never higher, because Torch is as far as one door goes. Rank (breadth: how much of you is lit) and skill (how well you control what's already lit) are different axes — a highly skilled Torch is still a Torch. |
| **Blaze** | Where it stops staying inside one door — unbound's threshold. Heat becomes visible light for the first time, which generally draws Riftspawn attention like other worked power. Species-specific responses still govern: Verge-hounds treat Jab's lit Edge as a boundary and attack the dark gap instead (Book 1 Ch. 53). This is Rook's rank. |
| **Glory** | The apex — "god level," already on the series HORIZON in `SERIES_MAP.md`, where the Satori a person carries stops being necessary and dissolves. So bright it cannot be looked at directly. The transition into it cannot be self-sustained: it has to be *lent* — sustained by someone already carrying it, or by a bonded pair through Heartfire — the same borrow-and-repay economy the soul already runs on elsewhere in this document. A person at Glory has to consciously bank their own light to stand in a room with anyone below it — not a courtesy, a survival requirement for whoever they're standing near. |

**What "immortality" actually means, mechanistically — the shedding (`CANON`,
owner-directed).** Every rank transition above happens in a change, not a slope —
real and embodied, not gradual drift. What the change costs and does scales with
the rank, and only the last one completes it. Ordinary mortality, underneath
everything else already established about the soul, runs on the same clock every
human body has always run on: the oxygen free radicals ordinary blood carries as a
byproduct of being alive, doing slow accumulating damage to the body's own DNA
across a lifetime until the body fails. This is not a new mechanism — it's the same
"soul fills narrow and slow before Satori, deep and fast after" scaling already
established above, named down to its actual biological floor. Homura's instruments
measured the correlation, indirectly: practitioners who'd taken a genuine jump in
rank tested lower afterward for exactly that kind of cellular damage — a finding
nobody at the station lived long enough to carry further before Cinder Company
ended the work along with everything else it took. Every rank up pushes back
against that clock a little; this is the mechanism behind the faster healing
already established as recovery scales with rank. **At Glory, for the first time,
the clock does not slow — it stops.** The ordinary blood of mortality is shed, and
what fills a person in its place carries none of that damage forward. This is why
Glory alone is spoken of as immortality, and why the transition can't be survived
unassisted: shedding the only blood a body has ever run on, all at once, is not
something anyone has done alone. It is lent — the same borrowed-and-repaid economy
that sustains the light itself, above.

**Blaze has its own room to grow (`CANON`, owner-directed expansion).** The three
known doors this world actually has — the Ember, the rift-granted magic tradition,
and disciplined internal cultivation (all three named in "The Kindling," above) —
give "more than one door" real graduated texture instead of a single leap from
Torch straight into full unbound. A two-door Blaze draws on two of the three at
will; a three-door Blaze has genuinely mastered all three known traditions; and
full unbound — "any door needed" — is the further step past even that: no longer
limited to reaching only through the three traditions anyone's named, able to reach
for whatever a moment actually requires. This carries the same duration/control-
vs-breadth split already established for the single-door rungs one level higher,
and it leaves real, visible room for a Blaze-rank character to keep growing on the
page rather than the rank reading as a ceiling. See "Three doors, three native
domains," above, for what a door actually grants once it's open — Rook is now
confirmed (Ch. 3, Ch. 17) as at minimum a two-door Blaze (Ember + his native
magic-tradition), with the third door and full unbound both still open questions
for a later book.

**The Reading** stays as the formal, rare Homura-tradition diagnostic — a
deliberate, felt self-assessment used for precision or dispute, expressed as a
plain fraction or band a character would actually say aloud ("maybe a fifth of the
room's lit"), never mid-combat, never as page furniture. The ladder words above are
what people actually say to size each other up in ordinary dialogue; a Reading is
for when the word alone isn't enough.

Nothing here is written into prose yet — none of these words appear in the locked
Movement 1 chapters, so this is forward-compatible with what already exists on the
page.

## Meridian training-yard lineage (`HORIZON`, owner-directed 2026-09-27)

**Meridian is the geographic and institutional home of the planned 2680-era
story.** The present series is in 2150, so Book Two plants a lineage capable of
surviving roughly 530 years; it does not pretend the eventual academies already
exist.

The seed is the north yard in Book Two: marked practice lanes, shared equipment,
field rotations, and an append-only record of exercises, observed failures,
injuries, reserve use, recovery time, and repeatable results. Three approaches
begin beside one another rather than as rival institutions:

1. **Rook's mixed-door field method** — solve one real problem through the door
   and personal expression a practitioner actually has; compare outcomes rather
   than forcing identical shapes. Toren becomes an early recorder and teacher of
   this method, not a pre-declared legendary founder.
2. **Wyck's Resilience/body discipline** — stance, contact, load, safe failure,
   and the hard distinction between tolerating pain and avoiding damage.
3. **The Homura medical line** — Ward, Sustain, ash working-depth, rotation,
   radiation recall, and the rule that a healer who ignores the abort line becomes
   the next patient.

Rift-response fighters remain the field expression connecting all three. Dessa's
written ledger and Senna's oral histories are the mechanism by which knowledge can
outlive the first cohort. A future Touren lineage, Wyck's school, and an independent
rift-fighter tradition may grow from these roots. Their later names, charters,
rivalries, curricula, and founder histories remain **provisional horizon
material** until the 2680 proposal is reconciled against the completed Kindled
books. Book Two may plant records and habits; it must not import future claims as
present-day fact.

## The Mother and the Father — the Makers (`CANON`, owner-directed, deep-`RESERVED`)

**Clean-room note first:** this is a structural idea, not a borrowed one. Where the
touchstone genre convention runs to a large, populated pantheon of higher beings
(many named figures, a Greco-Roman-style divine cast), the owner's explicit
direction narrows this series to two: a Mother and a Father, and nothing between
them and the Kindled. No tier names, deity names, or specific invented terminology
are imported from any published work — the ladder above (Kindled through Glory) is
this series' own, and the two Makers are this series' own.

**The myth, as it's actually held in-world:** somewhere behind everything — the
Fall, the wormholes, the Kindling itself — the oldest half-remembered tellings hold
that there were, and are, two: a Mother and a Father, the Makers of the universe.
Not a court, not a pantheon, not a bureaucracy of lesser gods — two. Every Kindled
person is, in the oldest tellings, an unfinished child of theirs, and the whole
climb from Kindled up through Glory is understood — where it's believed at all — as
the Path leading back toward what the Makers already are. This is **myth, held with
varying literalness by different people and regions**, not an authorially confirmed
fact — consistent with how Rook's private Satori theory is handled elsewhere in
this document: a belief a character can hold and teach as true without the text
itself confirming it.

**Working default for how it touches the world-building already in place:** the
Kindling wakes "a residual, mostly-unused portion of the human genome/brain" — the
myth's answer to *why that door exists in anyone at all* is that the Makers put it
there, or left it there, when they first drew off enough of what other series' own
mythologies call the Source to make mortals who could someday walk the same road
they did. Whether that's literally true, a later civilization's story about
something stranger and colder, or pure comfort invented after the Fall the way
Senna describes the Kindling ceremony itself being invented — **stays open, on
purpose, indefinitely.** Tech-zones like Meridian default to treating it as folk
religion; magic-saturated regions are more likely to hold it as living belief. This
gives later books room to texture different settlements differently without any of
it being decided yet.

**Naming default:** "the Mother" and "the Father" — plain functional titles, in
keeping with this series' own naming convention (the Path, the Kindling, the
Ember, the Source are all titled the same way). No personal names are assigned.
Open for the owner to override if proper names are wanted instead of or alongside
the titles.

**Reserved status — this is deep HORIZON, not Book 1 material.** It does not
appear on the page as doctrine, explanation, or confirmed fact anywhere in Book 1.
The only permitted surface in Book 1 is incidental, unexplained folk texture if a
scene calls for it — an oath, a curse, a phrase a background character uses without
comment ("Mother's mercy," "by the Father's word") — never spoken by Rook, never
glossed, never connected on the page to the Kindling, the Path, or Glory. Anything
beyond incidental color is a Book 2+ decision.

**Relationship to the shared cross-series Source:** this stays this series' own
myth for now, exactly like the Satori-object theory above — it is *not* written
into `craft/connections/CONNECTIONS_BIBLE.md` and makes no claim on what any other
series' cosmology is. If the owner later wants to propose the Mother and Father as
this series' own name for the shared Source, that proposal goes through the same
discrete approval pass already required for any other `CONNECTIONS_BIBLE.md` edit —
not folded in silently here.

## Audience and tone (`POSSIBLE` — new)

**Gap closed:** the parallel run relied only on the generic Light-author default
(clean for a thirteen-year-old reader: no profanity, sexual content, glamorized
cruelty, or lingering gore). Given this book's explicit promise of real, felt wounds
and at least two named-character deaths (Garrick, and Wyck or an equivalent), that
default is affirmed rather than overridden: violence has real consequence and cost,
staged through impact and aftermath rather than graphic detail; deaths land
emotionally on the page, not off-page as a report; profanity light-to-none; romance,
if present at all in Book 1, stays secondary. This matches the tonal register the
other Monroe Jackson SF/fantasy seats already use and keeps this book consistent
with the house standard without needing a harder-edged carve-out.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

## Learning discipline

- One edit is an example, not a universal law.
- Promote a broad instinct only when several accepted edits support the same
  reader-facing preference, or when the owner states the preference directly.
- Do not learn voice from continuity fixes, factual corrections, typographical
  repairs, rejected suggestions, reverted edits, or untouched AI prose.
- Do not carry names, setting facts, or plot events from an example into a new
  movement. Learn the difference between WAS and NOW, not the example's subject.
