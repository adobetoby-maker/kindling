# Chapter 1 — Callie: Persons in Party

Three hundred and forty paces. Callie had said it, so Callie counted it.

She walked at the front with the lamp held low on her left side, over the verge. She did not need the lamp to find the verge. She had come up this shoulder at dusk, going the other way, fast, with ditch water over her boots and five sets of hound sign in the mud in front of her. She knew where the good ground was. The lamp was for the people behind her.

*Twelve. Thirteen.*

Otto had the barrow. He had the strap over his good shoulder and the far handle braced against his hip, and he was pulling it the way he did everything, carefully, with his tongue between his teeth. The boy lay on the folded blanket on the boards. Katori walked at the barrow's head with one hand on the rim. She was not holding it. She was only there.

The brother walked beside it with the boy's hand in his.

Callie had told him to do that. She had not expected him to do it.

*Thirty.*

"Verge ends at the second lamp," she said, without turning round. "Nine feet of hard ground after, then up."

"I heard you the first time," said the brother.

"I know. I'm saying it again because you're tired."

He did not answer. She heard the barrow wheel find a stone and ride over it, and the boy made a small sound, and the brother said something to him too low to catch. It was the voice people used on horses in a bad stall. Callie knew that voice. She had used it on her mother on the plain.

*Fifty-one.*

---

Behind the barrow came everybody else, and they came badly.

The big boy from hut eleven walked with his one good arm hanging and his strapped arm tucked in his coat. Wyck, his name was. He had stood in the gap by the bank and let two hounds run into him, one after the other, and he had not gone back a step. Now he was walking as if every step were a separate decision.

Dessa limped beside him. She had her hand on the back of Toren's coat.

Toren was the one Callie kept looking back at.

He was the boy from the step. Last night he had sat two doors down from her in the dark with his arm in a sling and told her the lamps had a written reason. Tonight he had come past the big boy faster than a person could run, with struts of light up his legs and something crooked and grey lit in his good fist. He had gone through two hounds. Then his feet had gone out from under him on the slabs and he had landed on the strapped arm.

He was grey now. He held the arm against his chest with his other hand. His right hand was wrapped in white and there was blood coming through the white.

Every so often his wrapped hand went to his coat pocket. It stopped there. Then it came away again.

Callie knew what that was. She had done it with her hook on a hill.

*Eighty.*

At the very back came the man with the wrong shoulders. Rook, the others called him. He walked about as fast as a very old man, with one hand out to nothing, as if he expected a wall to be there. He had held the road shut. Callie had not seen it happen, only what it did: a hound going at the empty air beside him and not arriving. Now he looked like somebody had wrung him out and hung him up wet.

Nobody offered him an arm. Callie noticed that. Dessa had looked back at him twice and not offered, and she thought that probably meant he had refused it, and they had had that argument before.

*A hundred and six.*

---

The lamps in the Sag were behind them now.

The station's night crew had been down there in the dip when all this started. They were still there, with their lamps on poles and a barrow and a scale, walking the road bed where the hounds had come apart. Somebody was calling out a number. Somebody else was calling it back.

"They'll have that," said Katori quietly, not to anyone. "All of it. Every grain off that road."

"Does it matter?" said Otto.

"It matters to somebody," said Katori. "It always does."

Callie looked back once. There was ash in the lamplight, lying in long grey ropes down the joints of the slabs. Five hounds. A Flask each, if they were ordinary hounds, and they had not seemed ordinary. That was a winter for somebody. She did not know whose.

She turned round again and kept counting.

*A hundred and fifty.*

At two hundred the verge ran out, exactly where she had said it would. She stopped at the second lamp and held her own up so the others could see the nine feet of hard ground, and then the place where the ditch shallowed, and the made road beyond it.

"Up here," she said.

They came up onto the made road one at a time. The barrow stopped jolting. The boy on the boards let his breath out in a long shudder, the way a person does when a pain stops for the first time in hours.

"Jab," he said.

"Mm."

"Is it the place?"

"It's the place."

"It's got a *light* on it."

Callie looked ahead. It did. The north gate stood in the dark bank a hundred and forty paces off, a slab of plate steel with a lamp over it. The lamp was small and flat and white. It did not move at all.

"I told you," said the brother. "Thousands of them."

The boy considered this.

"That's one," he said.

Jab laughed. It was not a big laugh. It came out of him as if something had knocked it loose. Callie heard Otto laugh too, surprised, and Katori did not laugh, but her hand moved on the barrow rim.

*Two hundred and twenty.*

---

At three hundred and twenty the gate began to open.

The brother said a word before the barrow was level with it. He said it loudly and he said it first.

"*Clinical.*"

Four grey coats came out at a fast walk, hooded, with the flat clear panels in front of their faces. Behind them came a bin on two wheels. Callie knew the bin. She had watched one like it swallow four hundred miles of ash yesterday morning at the south gate. The grey coats went past the brother. They went past the barrow. They went to the ash on Katori's coat and the hound ash on Callie's sleeves and the ash crusted in the cracks of Otto's boots.

"Ma'am, I need you to step away from—"

"*Clinical*," said the brother again. Louder. "There's a boy on this barrow and he's four years old and it's clinical. Now. Not after the boots."

The nearest grey coat turned his flat panel round. Callie could see the brother's face in it, small and grey and streaked.

There was a pause. It lasted about two seconds.

Callie watched it happen. She watched the grey coat decide. She thought: *Somebody told him that word. Somebody told him it on the road, and told him to say it first.* She did not know who. She wished she could thank them.

"Right," said the grey coat, and turned and shouted one word through the gate.

---

The woman came out without a bin.

Callie knew her at once. She was about sixty, with short white hair and a lined brown face and small quick hands. Yesterday morning she had stood in front of the tailboard at the south gate and said *All right, my love, what's happened to you?* Then she had put her hand under Callie's mother's elbow, not holding, and walked her forty paces in.

Now she went straight to the barrow. She looked at the boy for about four seconds without touching him. Then she put two fingers on his neck and looked away over the dark fields while she counted.

"Hello, my love," she said. "What's your name?"

"Tonk."

"That's a very good name. I'm going to look at your leg and I'm not going to move it."

"It's *broken*," said Tonk.

"I know it is," said the woman, unwrapping. "Everybody's told me."

Nobody had told her anything. She had been out of the gate for about eleven seconds.

She got the wrapping off and looked at the leg in the lamplight. Callie looked too. She did not mean to. She looked because she always looked. She saw it for about as long as it takes to breathe in, and then she looked at the gate lamp instead and held her breath until the picture went.

The woman's face did nothing at all.

"Stretcher," she said over her shoulder, in a level voice. "And go and wake Sowerby. Tell him it's a fracture at seven weeks with a sinus, and he'll want Keel."

"Wake him?"

"Wake him."

Then she turned and looked at the brother properly.

"And you?"

"His brother."

"Have you been carrying him?"

"Eighteen days."

Something went across the woman's face. Callie saw it and could not read it, and she was good at faces.

"Then you'll want this from me and not from a door," the woman said. "He goes in now. You don't. You're carrying road dirt and ash, and you've got an open wound on that arm, and he's going into a room where people will open him up. Everything that comes through this gate gets washed and written down. That takes twenty minutes. He hasn't got twenty minutes. You have."

"I promised him," said Jab.

"I know."

"You don't. I promised him. He asked if I'd be there, and I said they'd have to carry me out."

The woman put her hand on his arm, above the bandage, and left it there. She did not agree with him.

"Then you'll be there for the next one," she said. "There'll be more than one. Say goodnight to him properly. Don't say it fast, and don't lie. Then let go of his hand, because if I have to take it off you, that's the thing he'll remember."

---

Callie turned away. It was not hers to watch.

She could still hear it. She heard the boy's voice go up, and the brother's voice stay level. She heard *twenty minutes* and *I'll come after*, and *that's not a maybe*. She heard the boy say *You lied*, with a four-year-old's terrible aim, and the brother say *That one I did. I'm sorry.*

Katori was standing beside her. Katori was not watching either. She was looking at the berm.

"He'll be all right," said Callie. She did not know which of them she meant.

"One of them will," said Katori. "Tonight."

Then two people went past carrying a stretcher, and the gate took the boy in, and the brother stood in the middle of the made road with his hands at his sides. The light over the gate seemed to go out of his eyes for a moment. Then it came back.

He did not fall down. Callie watched for it, and it did not happen.

---

Then they took everything else.

They took the boots. They took Otto's barrow to be hosed and the two lamps and the coats off five backs. They took the brother's pack and his hide cover and the two halves of the broken carrying frame. Callie had not seen who carried the frame down the road. Somebody had. It went into a bin in two pieces, and the curved rail of it stuck up over the rim.

A grey coat held out a hand to Callie.

"The hook, miss."

She had had it back since the eighth hour this morning. They had given it to her at the hatch, washed, with a paper tag on the handle. The black husk smear down the inside of the curve had been gone. She had stood at the window holding clean steel and not known what to do with it.

Now there was hound ash in the curve again, grey and fine.

"Twice in two days," she said.

"Yes, miss," said the grey coat. "Some people make a habit of it."

She gave it to him. He took it by the conduit, not the curve. She noticed that, the same as she had yesterday. Somebody here taught them how to hold things.

Katori put her Ember in a glove without a word. It was the flat grey slab she turned over in her fingers when she was thinking. Callie had seen her hand it over yesterday at the south gate too. Katori's face had not changed then either.

Toren took his out of his coat pocket.

He looked at it before he handed it over. It was a stone. Just a stone, dark, a little bigger than an egg. He held it for a second in his wrapped hand and his thumb moved on it once, the way you touch a stove to see if it has gone cold. Then he put it in the glove. Callie saw his mouth go flat.

*Cold*, she thought. *It's cold again.*

Rook came last. He took something small out of an inside pocket and held it out on his palm. It was a flat grey stone, much smaller than Katori's, rounded at the corners. His hand was too big for it. His fingers did not close over it. They went round it, as if they had learned to.

The grey coat took it. Rook's hand stayed where it was for a moment, still shaped round nothing. Then he put it in his pocket.

And the brother—Jab—put his hand up under his collar.

---

Callie watched this one. She could not help it.

There was a cord round his neck, under the shirt. He lifted it over his head. On the end of it was a thumb-length of dull metal, river-worn. It did not look like anything. He held it out, and the grey coat's glove took it, and Jab's hand stayed up at his throat where it had been.

"It's not ash," he said.

"No, sir. It's a conduit and it's got ash on it, and it's been in something tonight. It'll be washed and tagged. You'll have it back at the hatch."

"You don't understand what it is."

"I've took eleven hundred of them, sir," said the grey coat. He was not unkind. He said it the way you say the road goes left.

The metal went into a small bin. Somebody tied a paper tag to the handle and wrote on it.

Jab stood in the road in his socks with his collar open and one hand still at his throat. He looked, Callie thought, like somebody who had set a thing down at the edge of a river and turned round to find the river gone.

She had stood exactly like that yesterday with her hands empty.

---

Then they hosed them down.

The water came out of a long canvas hose, and it was warm. It had been warm yesterday too. Callie still had not got used to it. Otto shut his eyes and turned his face up into it and said nothing at all. Wyck stood in it like a post. Dessa said, "Oh, that's *disgusting*," about the ash coming off her, and meant the ash and not the water.

Jab stood in it with his face up and his eyes shut. He did not cry. Callie looked away before she could find out whether he was going to.

Rook sat down in the road to be hosed. He did it in three movements. A grey coat said *sir* and Rook said, "I'll get up again in a minute. I'd like to be wet sitting down first."

The gate nurse came back out while the water was still running. She looked at Toren's wrapped hand and unwrapped it and wrapped it again. She put her fingers along the splint on his arm, slowly, from the wrist to the elbow, while he looked at the sky.

"Did you land on it?"

"Yes."

"Hard?"

"Yes."

"Well, it hasn't come through," she said. "If you've shifted it, you'll know by morning. You'll know by the noise you make." She looked up at him. "You're one of Sowerby's. From the south gate. Two days ago."

"Yes."

"He told you to lie down."

"He did," said Toren.

"And you went out through the north yard in the dark instead, and did something with your legs." She sighed. "I'll tell him. He'll enjoy it."

---

The reception pen at the north gate was the same as the one at the south. Callie had not known they would build two the same. It was poured concrete, forty feet by twenty, with a drain in the middle and a bench along one wall. There were hot pipes behind the bench and a hatch at the far end with a window in it.

The window was dark.

"Oh," said Otto, when his back found the pipes.

"Yes," said Callie. She had said *oh* herself yesterday. Everybody said it.

Ines was on the other side of the pen gate. Callie heard her before she saw her. Ines and Pell had stayed at the north yard gate with a lamp, because somebody had had to stand there and argue with the gate crew about keeping it open. Pell had done the arguing. Ines had done the standing.

"Is that all of you?" Ines called through the bars. Flat, the way she said everything.

"It's all of us," said Katori.

"How many?"

Dessa put her head back against the hot pipes and shut her eyes.

"Five," she said. "Five hounds. Four in the road and one in the pipe. And the last one didn't get to be the last one." She paused. "I was told to tell you."

There was a silence on the other side of the gate.

"Good," said Ines, at last. "Good. I'll write that down."

"You can't write," said Pell's voice.

"Then you'll write it down," said Ines, "and I'll watch you."

---

Jab sat on the bench two places down from Callie. He sat with his back against the pipes and his hands on his knees, and he looked up.

There was a light in the ceiling. It was in a fitting behind a cage of wire. It was flat and white and small, and it did not flicker.

"Don't look at it long," said Callie. She said it without opening her eyes. "It makes the rest of the room go dark. It took me a whole day to work that out, and nobody told me."

He looked down again.

"How long have you been here?"

"Since yesterday morning."

"That's all?"

"That's all." Callie opened her eyes and looked at the drain. "Four hundred miles on a wagon, with my mother on the back of it. She's in the long building. At the north end." She thought about saying more and did not. "Callie."

"Jab."

"I know," she said. "Your brother said it about ninety times."

He made a sound that was nearly the laugh from the road.

"He says everything about ninety times," he said.

"He counted the lamps in the dip. Out loud. I heard him from the shoulder." Callie turned her head. "He got five. There were seven."

"He'll want to know that," said Jab. "He'll want to argue about it."

"Tell him I said seven."

"He'll say you're wrong."

"I'm not wrong," said Callie. "Tell him that too."

---

Ivor Hallet came through the door behind the hatch at about the twelfth hour, and he was not pleased.

He had a grey cardigan on under his oilskin, buttoned wrong. His pen hung on its string round his neck. A young man with a cough came in behind him carrying a lamp and a ledger, and Hallet said several things to the young man about the time of night and the distance from Yard A to the north gate. He said them quietly. He said them at length. Then he sat down at the desk behind the window, and pulled the ledger toward him, and saw who was sitting on the bench.

He looked along the row of them for a long time.

"Right," he said. "I was in my bed. I'd like that noted somewhere, and there's no column for it, so I'm saying it. I'm going to do this in the order it's got to be done in. I'd ask you all to let me, because if I do it out of order I have to start again, and it's past midnight."

Nobody said anything.

"Two things," said Hallet. "The first is a party, and I'll come to it. The second is the rest of you." He did not look at any one of them. "There are eight persons in this pen who went out through the north yard tonight, past a gate crew who told them not to, onto a public road outside the line. And I'm told by three different people that some of you did things on that road which a person who isn't Kindled would find remarkable."

"Yes," said Katori.

"You didn't notify this window."

"There was a child on the road," said Katori.

"I know," said Hallet. "That's going on the sheet as well. I'm going to put it first." He picked up the pen. "The sheet goes upstairs by the sixth hour, and I'd like it to be a true one. So I'm going to go along that bench by your bands. You'll give me one line each. What you did. Not what you'd like me to write. What you did."

---

He went along the bench from the far end.

Katori held up her wrist with the red thread in the paper band.

"I cut them," she said. "Four. Maybe five. I didn't count, and I'd rather not say five if it was four."

Hallet wrote.

Otto held up a band with no thread. "I pulled a barrow," he said, and went red.

"That's not remarkable, sir."

"No," said Otto. "It's the only thing I did."

"Then I'll write it down anyway," said Hallet, "because I'd like one line on this sheet that nobody upstairs can argue with."

Callie held up hers. The red thread was dark with wet.

"I read the ground," she said. "That's all. I'm dry. I couldn't have done the rest if I'd wanted to."

Hallet looked at her through the glass for a moment. Something moved at the corner of his mouth.

"That's your first line," he said.

"It's the true one."

"I know it is, miss. I've still got the card on my desk." He wrote. "Next."

Dessa said, "I told them where things were. I didn't move. I can't really move." She held up her band. "I'd like *I can't really move* put down, since you're writing."

Wyck said, "I stood in a gap."

"Did anything move you?"

"No."

"Did anything try?"

"Two," said Wyck. "One after the other." He looked at the drain. "They went into the wall."

Hallet wrote that down without any expression.

---

Then he came to Toren.

"Mr Voss," he said. "Two days ago you sat on that bench at the other gate and wrote me a card. It said your Ember was out at present, and you didn't know when it was coming back."

"It was out," said Toren.

"And tonight?"

Toren was quiet. Callie watched his wrapped hand move toward his pocket, and stop, because there was nothing in the pocket now. They had taken it at the gate.

"Stride," he said. "And the spike, lit. At the same time."

The pen was very quiet.

"For how long?"

"About a minute," said Toren. "Then I fell over. Then it went."

"Went where?"

"I don't know," said Toren Voss. "That's the trouble with it."

Hallet held the pen above the paper. Then he wrote. It took a while.

"I'll put *about a minute*," he said. "It's a true card, Mr Voss. It's just got a new line on it. They do that."

---

He looked at the last band.

He did not ask for a name. He looked down at the ledger instead, and ran the pen along a line, and then looked up through the glass.

"Mr Marsh," he said.

Callie did not know who he meant.

Then Rook said, "Present, Mr Hallet," from the far end of the bench, in a voice that had not changed at all.

"Elias Marsh," said Hallet. He said it the way you read a thing back to make sure you have it right. "Out of Lowry Bend. Admitted two days ago, south gate, as a clinical outpatient, with a puncture wound through the back." He turned a page. "Doctor Sowerby's note says you're not to stand for more than a few minutes at a time for a fortnight. It's underlined twice."

"I didn't stand," said Rook. "I was held up. It's different."

"What held you up?"

"The road," said Rook, "mostly."

Callie had heard everybody call him Rook. The girl with the limp. The big boy. Katori, on the road, once, sharply. Callie turned and looked at Toren, because she could not help it.

Toren was not surprised. He knew the name. Callie could see that at once. But he was looking at Rook the way you look at somebody while a stranger takes a coat off them. As if a thing that belonged to Rook was being taken out and hung on a hook in front of everybody, and Toren did not like it, and could not stop it.

Rook's eyes were shut. His back was against the pipes.

"One line, Mr Marsh," said Hallet. His voice had gone a little quieter.

"I shut the road," said Rook. "Either side of me. For about as long as it took. Then I sat down on a wall." He opened his eyes. "Write that it was a good road, Mr Hallet. It held."

Hallet wrote it. He wrote the whole of it, as far as Callie could tell. Then he blotted the page and closed the ledger on it and put his hand flat on the cover.

"That's the second thing," he said. "Now the first."

---

He looked at Jab.

"You, sir," he said, "are the reason all of this is on my desk at midnight. I'll do yours properly. Persons in party."

"Two," said Jab.

Hallet looked past him at the bench, at eight wet people with their backs against the pipes.

"There's rather more than two in this pen, sir."

"They're not mine," said Jab. "They came out to the road and got me." He stood up. He went to the window and put his hands flat on the ledge. "Two. Me and my brother. He went in through the gate about twenty minutes ago. He's four and he's called Tonk. He's in the party."

The pen stopped for about a second and a half.

Callie knew what came next. She had stood at the other window yesterday morning and said *six* when there were five of them, and made the man write down her mother. She watched Hallet's face now. She watched it do the thing it had done for her.

"Two," said Ivor Hallet. "One clinical, admitted at the north gate tonight." He wrote it. "That's correct. I'd rather have it that way than the other. Name as you'd want it written."

Jab told him.

"Age?"

"Seventeen."

"Household?"

"Him and me."

"Head of household?"

There was a silence that went on slightly too long.

"Me," said Jab.

"Yes," said Hallet, writing. "I'm sorry, sir. It's only a column."

Callie shut her eyes. She had said *I'm thirteen* to that, yesterday. She had thought it was the worst thing anybody had ever written down about her. She thought now that she had been lucky. At least there had been somebody in her column besides herself.

---

"Declaration of capability," said Hallet.

He laid a buff card on the ledge inside the window. He did not push it through.

He said the whole thing. Callie had heard it yesterday and she heard it again, and she found she could say it along with him in her head. Meridian kept a register of Kindled persons inside the line. About eleven hundred names. Not secret. Your name, your rank if you had a word for it, and what you could do. Not what you would like to do. What you would do at four in the morning if a wall came down. A band with a red thread. The north yard, where the work was. And you told this window things.

"And if I don't?"

"Transient. Fourteen days, a grey band, no north yard, and a review. I don't sit on the review." Hallet looked at him. "I'll save you the next question. Your brother's admission is clinical. It went through at the gate. It isn't conditional on this card, or on your band, or on one single thing you do or don't do in this station. If anybody here ever tells you different, you come down to this window and say so to me. I'll make it the worst fortnight of that person's life."

He said it more quietly than the rest.

"That's not procedure," he said. "That's mine."

Jab stood at the hatch with his hands on the ledge. Callie watched his back. It was very straight.

"Then what's the card for?" he said.

"Where you sleep," said Ivor Hallet.

---

He pushed the card through, and a pencil after it.

Jab brought them back to the bench. He sat down in the place he had been, two along from Callie, with the card on his knee. He looked at it for a long time. Callie knew what it said at the top. *NAME. RANK (if known). CAPABILITY.* And then a great deal of empty space.

He wrote his name. His hand was not steady. He went over one of the letters again to make it neat.

Then he stopped, with the pencil above the next line.

His other hand went up to his collar. It did it by itself. It went to the hollow of his throat where the cord had been for however many years, and it found nothing there, and it stayed.

Callie looked at the drain.

"They give it back," she said. "At the hatch. In the morning. Washed."

He turned his head.

"Mine came back this morning," said Callie. "Clean. Somebody had got four hundred miles off it." She thought about the hound ash in the curve, and the grey coat's glove. "Then I took it out on a road tonight and they took it off me again."

Jab looked at her for a moment.

"The good thing. We can take out what's still in her that's still doing harm. That's a course. It takes about twenty weeks. It's unpleasant, and it works. We've run it two hundred and eleven times. I've got the numbers in this folder and you can read them. It starts this afternoon.

"And we can hold the marrow up from outside. Not replace it. Hold it up. That's a transfusion and a preparation we make here, measured on the machine in the shed, on a cycle. She had the first of it the morning after she came in. The cycle is eleven days. It doesn't stop.

"With those two things, this woman is not going to die this winter. Without them, she'd have died within about five weeks. I'd have given you six."

Nobody said anything.

"And then the bill," said Doctor Sowerby. "I'll let her ask for it. It's hers."

"Say it," said Senna Voss.

He said it. It took about ninety seconds, and he did not soften one word of it. Toren heard every word go into him and could not have repeated any of them afterward. When Sowerby had finished, he asked Senna whether she wanted him to stay in the room. She said no, thank you. He went. He did not look back.

The four of them sat in four counted chairs.

---

"Right," said Senna. "I'm going to say the whole of it once, properly, with all of you here. That's the rule in this family. I didn't make it, and I'm not going to be the first one to get out of it.

"Then I should like nobody ever to say it for me again. Not in any room, to anybody, for the rest of my life. If somebody asks, you send them to me. I have a great deal of time now and very little to do with it."

She looked past Toren then, at the next bed. At the boy in the chair beside it.

"You too, brother," she said. "You're in it now. I told you."

The boy said, "Yes, ma'am," very quietly. Toren saw him put both hands flat on his knees.

"First. I'm going to live." Senna let it sit. "That's the biggest one, and it's first, and nobody's going to take it off me by being sad in front of me before I've finished. A week ago I was five weeks from dying in a cart. Now I'm not. Three children and a man carried me three hundred miles to make that true. If any one of you looks at your boots while I'm saying it, I shall be extremely rude.

"Second. I'm going to live *here*.

"Not for the winter. Not until I'm stronger. Here. There's a machine in a shed at the north end of this station, and it's the only one of its kind anybody in this room has ever heard of. I have to be inside a mile of it every eleven days for the rest of my life. Eleven. Not a fortnight. Not if I'm careful. Eleven.

"So I won't see Sallow Creek again. I won't see Hobb's Wall, or that dreadful weighmaster I was so fond of, or the Level. None of you are to build me a chair on wheels and get clever about it. It isn't the distance. It's the eleven days. I've done the sums four times and so will you, and we'll all get the same answer."

Wyck said something under his breath. Senna did not stop.

"And I've been in a cart since the second of the month. I've crossed three hundred miles of the worst road in this country on my back, being no use to anybody. It's the only thing I was ever actually good at. I've done it for the last time. I'd like that noticed by somebody. It won't be in any of that man's folders."

"It's noticed," said Dessa.

"Thank you."

---

"Third," said Senna. "This is the one I'd rather not, so I'll do it quickly."

She put her right hand out from under the tray and laid it on the blanket, palm up.

"The course takes the nerves. Not all at once and not everywhere. It takes the hands and the feet, and it doesn't give them back. It's not a small number of people. It's nearly everybody. He showed me the number, and then he made me say it back to him.

"It's started already. It started before the course did, if I'm honest, which I'm trying to be." She looked at the hand. "The ends of the fingers. Like I've had them in snow. It'll get worse. By the spring I'll be able to move these and pick things up if I look at them while I do it. I won't know I'm holding them.

"And I won't walk. I can walk now, poorly, as far as that door. By the spring I shan't. That's the feet. That one's simple, and there's nothing to discuss."

"Gran—" said Toren.

"Don't do that face. Just say it."

"Is it worth it?"

"Yes," said Senna Voss. She said it immediately, without any weighing at all. "Yes, it is. I want you to hear how fast I said that. You'll want it one day, when somebody you love is in a bed and you're doing the same sum.

"I'm seventy-three. I've had twenty years of something that was always going to finish me. A man in a corridor has just offered me eight more. He told me to my face that he's seen twelve, and he's seen three, which he didn't have to do. Eight years. I'll watch that one—" her hand moved a little toward Toren "—stop being thirteen. I'll watch that one—" toward Wyck "—be told what to do by somebody and not do it. I'll watch Dessa Cole run a room. That's not a consolation prize. That's the whole thing I've wanted since the morning we left Sallow Creek. They're going to sell it to me for my hands and my feet and the rest of the road. I'd have paid more. I told him so. He said everybody says that in the first week."

She stopped and got her breath.

"I've finished," she said. "Somebody go and find out about the tea in this place. It isn't good, and I refuse to believe it's the best they can do."

---

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Wyck got up and went to find out about the tea, and took his bar with him. Dessa put her head back and shut her eyes. Rook sat in his chair with his hands on his knees and looked at nothing.

Toren looked at his grandmother's hand on the blanket.

He thought about the road. He had carried numbers down it for sixty-four days. Nineteen days, his father's paper had said, with three ifs. The ifs had killed his father. Then the number had broken and been rebuilt and broken again, and at the end of it they had come in under a lamp in the rain with the number at zero.

He had thought zero was the end of the sum.

It was not. It was the start of another one. Eleven days, and eleven, and eleven. There was no zero in it anywhere.

In the next chair, the boy from the culvert had his head down.

Senna looked across at him.

"Brother," she said. "Yours is at the ninth hour tomorrow?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then you'll want the tea as well," said Senna. "Wyck's gone for it. He won't bring enough. He never does."

---

Visiting was in the afternoons. The family meeting was allowed. Staying after it was not. At the half hour a nurse came and moved them out, kindly and without any possibility of argument.

There was a bench outside the doors, against the long wall of the building, in a strip of pale sun.

Somebody was already sitting on it.

It was the girl from the step. Callie. She had the hook across her knees again. It had come back from the hatch this morning a second time, washed a second time, and she was holding it by the conduit with both hands. She had her left arm tucked in against her ribs. There was the line of bald pink skin over her left ear, with the stitches in it, where no hair would grow.

She looked up as he came out.

"They told you it's afternoons," she said.

"They told me that the first night. I sat out here till the first hour." Toren looked at the bench. "Can I—"

She moved the hook. He sat down on the other end, slowly, in two movements, because the stitches at the back of his leg pulled if he did it in one. That was one fewer than Rook needed. He had started counting other people's movements lately and could not stop.

They sat in the sun and looked at the drain in the yard in front of them.

"Your mother's in there," said Toren.

"Fourth bed on the left. Opposite yours." Callie did not look away from the drain. "They're taking a picture of the inside of her head this afternoon. Then the doctor talks to the family tomorrow. At the ninth hour." She turned the hook over on her knees. "Your gran's got a loud voice. I heard some of it through the door."

"How much?"

"Eleven days," said Callie. "And the tea."

---

They were quiet for a while.

"You read that off the grass," said Toren. "The night before last. The mule. You knew it was a mule from the—"

"From the size of the mound." Callie kept her eyes on the drain. "And I read the other thing. Before that. On the big road."

He went very still.

She had not meant to say it. He could see that. She said it anyway, the way you set down something you have been carrying so you can use both hands.

"Something came across the pasture from the east," she said. "Heavy. Three legs to a stride, not four. Wide, the way a thing stands that's made for pushing. And a fourth one that wasn't a leg. It cut the grass off level. It cut a line in the turf a man long, clean as a knife through a loaf." She was not looking at him. "It hit the rail on the east side and bent it. Somebody was in the ditch under it. There's a hollow at the edge the size of somebody lying down. And it's dead, and somebody lifted it with bins."

Toren did not say anything for a long time.

"You got all of that," he said at last, "off *grass*."

"It's what I do. It's the only thing I'm good at." She turned the hook over again. "I'm not asking who. I just read it. And you were sitting here. And I didn't want to sit next to you knowing it, and you not knowing I knew."

He looked down at his wrapped hand.

"Yes," he said.

That was all. She did not ask anything else. He felt her not asking. It was the most restful thing that had happened to him in Meridian.

---

His hand went into his coat pocket.

It did it by itself. It had done it every forty steps for sixty days. It found the stone and stopped there, and then, as if it remembered something, it came out again and lay on his knee.

Callie was watching. He knew she was.

"I did that," she said. "With my hook. On a hill. I kept my hand on it and kept looking at it, to see if it was still there. Every time I looked, it went."

Toren turned his head.

"Somebody told me that," he said slowly. "On a towpath. That checking on it is what puts it out."

"Somebody told me something like it. After." Callie looked at her hands. "She said you can stop holding a thing and it goes on being held. She said she didn't know that herself for a long time."

He was quiet. Then it came out of him, low and flat, the way a thing comes out that has sat in a person with nobody to tell it to.

"It's out," he said. "Again."

She waited.

"It went out after the Breach. Two days. I couldn't find it at all. Then last night it came back, and I didn't ask it, it just—came. For about a minute. And now it's out again." He looked at the drain. "I don't know if it's going to do that every time. Go and come back. Or if last night was the last of it, and I spent it on a road."

Callie thought about it. He could see her thinking, the way you can see somebody read a track.

"It's not gone," she said. "It's empty. Those are different."

"How do you know?"

"I'm empty now. Nearly. I can feel it at the bottom. You can't use it for anything, but you know it's there." She stopped. "And you know more than me. Yours came back in two days. You've done it once already. I only know it about mine."

Toren had not thought of it that way.

"Katori's took nine days, once," said Callie. "The second time she emptied herself. She said she was sure it was gone. Absolutely sure."

"Nine days," said Toren.

"That was her. Yours was two." Callie stood up, slowly, one hand on her ribs. "I don't know anything about yours. I'm only telling you what somebody told me. Because it helped."

She did not look back as she went. He noticed that. He noticed that he was grateful for it.

He sat on the bench in the sun with his hand on his knee, and he did not put it in his pocket. It was harder than it should have been.

---

He came back at the start of the afternoon, when the woman at the desk let visitors through.

His grandmother was lying against her pillows with a new grey tiredness under her eyes. They were taking her down to the shed later, for the first of the course. She did not mention it. She was talking.

She was talking across the ward.

In the bed opposite, a woman was sitting up. She had brown hair, loose on the pillow, and a lean weathered face, and her right hand lay on the blanket like a glove. Callie was in the chair beside her. Callie had her hook across her knees again and was holding it very tightly.

"You're the grandson," said the woman in the bed, before Senna could say anything. "She's been telling me about you."

"Gran—"

"I've been telling her about Wyck," said Senna. "Mostly. You came into it."

"Your grandmother came in on a board," said the woman. "She'd like that understood. She'd have walked if she could. She's told me four times."

"I walked in," Senna told Toren. "This woman walked in. Through the south gate. On her own feet. They were talking about it at the desk yesterday, which they never do." She looked across the ward. "Tilda. This is my grandson. Tor, this is Tilda. She's the best company I've had since the spring, and she's been here two days."

Tilda looked at Senna for a long moment. Toren saw something go across her face. It was the look people got at a market when they met somebody worth talking to.

"I said that," said Tilda. "On the first morning. To a stranger, by a fire. *Tilda.* Nothing else. It was the whole of it."

"Did you."

"Almost exactly."

"Then we've got that settled," said Senna, "and we can get on to the interesting part."

---

The interesting part was a piece of bread.

A nurse had brought Senna a plate with bread on it. Senna picked it up. She did it slowly, looking at her own hand the whole time. She watched her fingers go round the bread the way you watch somebody else's fingers do a thing you have told them to do. When she had it, she lifted it, still looking, and took a bite, and put it down, and let go of it. Looking.

Tilda watched her do it.

"Your hands," said Tilda.

"They've started," said Senna. "The ends. Like snow. It's the treatment, and the old business under it. It takes the hands and feet and doesn't give them back. I can feel the weight of a thing. I can't always feel the thing." She looked across at Tilda's right hand on the blanket. "And yours?"

"It's stopped taking instruction. Since the winter."

"Can you feel it?"

"I can feel it. I just can't tell it anything." Tilda looked at her own hand. "It's like shouting at somebody in the next field."

Senna was quiet for a moment. Then her long weathered face did something that made her look about thirty years younger.

"Well," she said. "Which of us is going to cut the other's bread?"

"Neither," said Tilda at once. "We'll look at it."

Senna laughed. It was a short dry sound like a stick breaking.

"I've been saying that to three children and a man for a week," she said. "Not one of them has laughed."

"They're frightened of you," said Tilda.

"They are. I've worked very hard at it."

"I'll need to know how you do it," said Tilda. "I've got five of my own, and none of them are frightened of me at all. The horse is. The horse is the only one."

Toren looked at Callie. Callie was looking at her mother. Her hands had come open on the hook. She did not seem to know it.

"I heard you this morning," Tilda said to Senna, more quietly. "Through the ward. Saying the whole of it once."

"Everybody heard me. I meant them to."

"It's a good rule," said Tilda.

"It's the best rule anybody ever gave me," said Senna. "A boy on a wall made it up. I recommend it."

Tilda looked at the ceiling. Toren saw her put it somewhere, carefully, the way a person puts away a tool she means to use.

"Tilda has a daughter who reads ground," Senna told Toren. "That's what the gate nurse said. I thought you'd want to know before she read yours."

"She already did," said Toren.

Senna looked at him. Then she looked at Callie for a long time with her pale eyes.

"Did she," she said.

---

Rook was lying on the floor of hut eleven when Toren got back.

He was lying on his side on a blanket by the stove, because the bed was too soft and the floor was not. His eyes were shut. Dessa was sitting on her bed with her bad leg out, watching him with a face like a locked door.

"He's fine," she said, before Toren could ask. "He's not fine. He's lying down. It's the same thing today."

"He sat in a chair this morning."

"He sat in a chair. Then he walked to the yard office and stood at a counter for most of an hour, and he won't say why. Then he walked back and lay down on a floor. That's the whole of his day." Dessa did not look away from Rook. "He held the road last night. On both sides of him. For as long as it took. With a hole in his back that Sowerby says is a fortnight from standing." Her voice did not change. "I felt him do it. I was up on the bank with my eyes shut and I felt him go thin. That's the only word I've got. Thin. Like a rope with half the strands cut."

"I can hear you," said Rook, with his eyes shut.

"I know you can. I'm saying it so you can."

Wyck was on the step outside. He had the iron bar in his left hand. He had got it from a scrap bin at the yard office on the first day, eighteen inches of square-section steel that a man had cut down for him for nothing. He turned it over. He dropped it on the step. He picked it up.

Toren went out and sat down beside him.

"Say it," said Toren, after a while.

"Say what."

"You've been about to say something since the ward."

Wyck turned the bar over. He dropped it. He picked it up.

"He said he'd go," he said.

Toren did not answer.

"At the fire on the lane. He said *I'll walk you to Meridian, and when you're through the gate and she's in front of somebody with proper instruments, I'll go.* He said it to all of us. I wrote it down in my head, because I write down everything that man says, and so does everybody in this hut." Wyck looked at the bar. "We're through the gate. She's in front of instruments. She's had the whole of it said in front of her this morning. And he's lying on a floor and he hasn't said a word about it."

"Then ask him."

"I'm not asking him," said Wyck Tanner. "If I ask him, he answers. Then I'll never know if he'd have said it on his own."

---

Toren thought about the window at the south gate.

It had been two days ago, and it felt like a year. The pen with the hot pipes. The man with the pen on a string. *Persons in party.* And Toren saying *five*, and hearing his own voice do something, and Dessa putting her hand flat on his back.

Then Rook, asked for a name.

Rook had said *Rook*. Hallet had said that was not a name he could write. And there had been a silence that went on too long, and then Rook had said, "Elias Marsh."

Toren had known the name since the cairn. Rook had given it to all of them there, in a road, standing up. But he had given it to them. It had been a present. At the window it had been taken, the way the grey coats took boots.

And Hallet had turned back a great many pages in a very large book, and found it.

*You were at this gate eleven years ago. You gave a statement over two days concerning a research station and a company of men. The file says* referred.

*I found out at the time*, Rook had said. *A very polite young woman told me the word means it's gone to the right place. And it had. That's the part nobody ever believes. It had gone to precisely the right place, and it's still there.*

Last night, in the other pen, Hallet had read the name back again without being asked. *Mr Marsh.* And Rook had said *Present* as if it were nothing.

Toren had watched Callie hear it. He had watched her turn and look at him, and he did not know what his own face had done.

---

He said it on his own that night.

He said it at the eleventh hour, in the hut, with the stove going and all of them there. That meant Dessa had gone to the long building and got Senna in the chair with the wheels, over four hundred yards of wet path, with Wyck pushing and complaining. She had done it on the strength of a message that said *hut eleven, tonight, all of us*, and nothing else.

Rook stood with his back to the stove. He could not sit down and get up again twice in one day.

"I'm going to do this poorly," he said, "and I'm going to do it in one go. There's a rule in this room about all of us at once and nobody finding out second. It was set by somebody on a wall, and it's the best rule anybody's ever given me.

"On the lane I said I'd walk you to Meridian. I said that when you were through the gate and she was in front of instruments, I'd go. I meant it entirely. I had it worked out. There was a version of this week where I stood at that gate the day she went in and shook four hands and went east. I've carried that version about with me since before any of you were hurt. It's been a great comfort to me. I'd like to put it down now."

He stopped.

"I'm not going," he said.

Nobody moved.

"That's all of it. There's no second part. I'm not going, and there isn't a reason I can give you that would stand up in front of the man I was in that lane. I've stopped needing one." He looked at the stove rather than at any of them. "I went to the yard office this afternoon. I signed. It's ash-lifting on the Sag line, and it's the worst money in this station. Nobody else will take it, because you have to be able to hold a road shut. I've signed for four seasons, which in this place is a thing you sign."

Dessa made a sound. Rook went on.

"I'll say the other half out loud now, so nobody has to work it out on their own at four in the morning. Her course is clinical. It went through at the gate. It isn't conditional on one single thing any of the rest of us does. The man at that window told me so to my face, and made a promise about it he didn't have to make, and I believe him.

"But she has to be inside a mile of that shed every eleven days for the rest of her life. That means inside this berm. Being inside this berm is residency. Residency here is *reviewed*. A household gets reviewed on what the station reckons it's worth.

"So that's what I've signed for. Not for her medicine. For her address."

He looked at them then.

"If anybody here wants to say *you said you'd go*, now's when. I'll hear the whole of it standing up."

---

There was a long silence.

Toren looked at Wyck. Wyck was looking at the bar in his hand. He had it in his left fist, and he was holding it very still.

"Right," said Wyck.

That was the whole of it. The stove ticked. Dessa put her head back against the wall and shut her eyes.

And Senna said, "Well," in a voice that had a great deal in it. "I shall need somebody to push this chair on Tuesdays."

Toren thought: *I will.*

He did not say it. It did not need saying. He only thought it, and then he found he had decided it, and that it was the first thing he had decided in Meridian that nobody had asked him to.

He looked at Rook standing with his back to the stove. At the way he held his shoulders, which was not a way anybody held their shoulders on purpose. At his hands, which were not steady, and which he did not put away.

Four seasons. The worst work in the station. With half the strands cut.

Toren did the sum. He could not help it. He had been doing sums since the second of the month. It came out the way all of them came out now, without a zero anywhere in it.

Then he looked at his grandmother, who was looking at Rook. She was not doing any sum at all. She was only looking at him, with a face Toren had never seen her wear on the road.

He looked away. It was not his.

# Chapter 4 — Callie: The Last Sum

The line was yellow, and it was as wide as her hand.

It was painted across the floor at the bottom of a long slope under the building, in front of a door wider than the gate at the south pen. The air down here was cool. The walls were thick and grey, and there were no windows at all. On the wall beside the door there was a sign.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

"It's the machine," said the man in white pushing her mother's bed. "It doesn't like metal. It pulls. Anything iron that goes in there, it'll have it off you and across the floor, and it won't ask." He looked at her mother. "Anything iron on you, ma'am? Pins? Rings?"

"Nothing," said her mother. "I sold the rings at the Reach."

He looked at Callie, and at the hook.

"You'll have to wait here, miss."

"I know."

Her mother's left hand tightened on hers. It could still tighten.

"Forty minutes," said her mother. "I've lain still on a wagon for a month. I can lie still in a room for forty minutes."

"I know."

"You'll be here."

"I'll be here."

They took her through the heavy door. It closed behind her with a sound like a lid going down on a box.

Callie sat on the bench against the wall with her grandfather's hook across her knees. There was nothing on the other side of that door she could see or hear or reach.

Then the knocking started.

---

It came through the wall in long runs, like somebody hammering a pipe in another building. Fast and steady and mechanical. It stopped, and started again faster, and stopped. It did not sound like a thing that could look at the inside of anybody.

Callie counted the runs. She could not help it. Eleven knocks, a gap, eleven. Then a long one she lost count of. Then eleven again.

She held the hook. She held it harder. Her fingers closed round the conduit the way they had closed round it on the hill, with the whole wagon on it, when she had not been able to open them.

She heard wheels on the slope.

Another rolling bed was coming down, with a man in white at one end and Jab walking at the other. There was a small boy on it. He was flat on his back with a tube in his hand and a padded trough under his leg, and his hair stuck up again at the back. He had a wooden fish in his fist.

Jab had a paper tray in his other hand. There was a thumb-length of dull river metal in it, on a cord.

He saw her on the bench and his face did something small.

"They're taking my mother first," said Callie.

"I know. They told us." He looked at the door. "We're next."

The boy on the bed turned his head and looked at Callie. He looked at her for a long time with great seriousness.

"You're the girl who *whistled*," he said.

"I am."

"It was really loud."

"It was meant to be."

"Jab said you said seven lamps," said Tonk. "There were *five*."

"There were seven," said Callie. "Two were behind a cart. You were lying down. You couldn't see behind the cart."

Tonk thought about this.

"You're wrong," he said.

"I'm not wrong."

"He said you'd say that," said Tonk, with great satisfaction, and Jab put his free hand over his eyes.

---

Jab sat down on the other end of the bench with the tray on his knees.

The knocking went on. Tonk lay on his bed by the yellow line and talked to the man in white about the machine, and asked whether it had teeth, and was told it did not, and did not believe it.

Callie watched Jab instead.

He was sitting with his hands open on his knees. He was looking at the door. Then he put his right hand flat on his own chest, over the place where the metal was not. He shut his eyes.

He did not do anything else. He did not move. His face went very quiet, as if he had put a question down on a table and taken his hand off it.

Callie knew the look. It was the look she had when she went down into herself to see what was in the well.

After a while he opened his eyes and took his hand away. Nothing had happened. She could see that nothing had happened. She could see what it cost him that nothing had.

He saw her watching.

"I was asking something," he said. "Through the wall. Whether she's all right in there. Your mother." He looked at the tray in his lap. "It's no good. It doesn't reach past my own arm. And it's dry."

"Mine's dry too," said Callie.

"What's yours?"

"A ward. And an edge. Off the hook." She turned the hook over. "Two doors. I'm told it's rare. It doesn't feel rare. It feels like being empty twice."

He almost smiled.

---

"There's somebody in there by herself," said a voice from the top of the slope. "She's lying very still."

It was the girl with the bad leg. Dessa. She was pushing a chair with wheels down the slope, and there was an old woman in it with a blanket over her knees. It was Senna.

Dessa came down carefully. Callie could see the bad leg taking the weight of the slope and not liking it. She stopped the chair by the bench. Then she sat down on the floor with her back to the wall, because the bench was full, and stretched the leg straight out. She looked at the heavy door with her head a little on one side.

"She's about twenty feet in," she said. "On her back. She hasn't moved. There's two of them at the far side, and one's sitting down."

Callie looked at her.

"How do you—"

"I know where people are. About thirty feet, all round, without looking, in the dark, all the time." Dessa said it flatly, like a thing she had said a great many times and was tired of saying. "It's not a trick. It doesn't cost me anything. It's just there."

"All the time?" said Jab.

"All the time. It doesn't go off." Dessa did not look away from the door. "That's the part people never ask about."

"We were on our way back from the shed," said Senna, from the chair, with her eyes shut. "They start the course today and they did the first of it. Dessa came to push me back, and she stopped at the top of that slope and said *there's somebody down there by herself.* And I said, Dessa, it's a hospital, everybody's somewhere by themselves. And she came down anyway." She opened one eye. "Dessa Cole, sit up. You're making the corridor nervous."

"I'm sitting how I sit."

---

The knocking stopped. Started again, faster.

"She still hasn't moved," said Dessa. "She's very good at it."

"She's been practising," said Callie. "On a wagon. For a month."

"It shows."

Callie found her fingers had come open on the conduit. She had not told them to.

"Would you tell me," she said. "If she moves."

"I'll tell you everything," said Dessa. "The dull bits as well. If I only say something when something happens, you'll go mad between them." She shifted the leg. "I count things. I can't help it."

"I count," said Callie.

Dessa turned her head.

"Everything," said Callie. "Husks. Seconds. Days to Meridian. The ash in the jar. My mother's breathing." She stopped. "I put it on my card. At the bottom. *I count.* I don't know why."

Dessa looked at her for a long time with that hard straight look.

"Good," she said at last. "Somebody in this place should."

"So do I," said Jab.

Dessa did not even turn her head.

"I know," she said. "You counted the paces the night before last. Out loud. All three hundred and forty."

"I told him the number," said Callie.

"Then you're both as bad as each other," said Dessa, "and I'm the only one in this corridor who counts what's actually there." She looked at the door. "Somebody's stood up. Two of them are going to her. She's still not moving."

---

Jab was looking at Dessa.

"You can do that through a wall," he said.

"Through a wall, through a road, through the ground. It doesn't care what's in the way."

"Mine won't go past my own arm." He said it low. "It comes when it likes. I've had it since the road, and I don't know what it is. I've been sitting on this bench asking it about a stranger through a foot of concrete like an absolute fool."

Dessa looked at the door for a while.

"I was Kindled at thirteen and nothing happened," she said. "Nothing at all, for two years. Where I come from there's a phrase for it. *Kindled to nothing.* People say it in front of you." She moved her hand on her knee. "Then one night a man told me something about an empty mind and an open soul. And something came on in me, and it's been on ever since. And if you asked me to make it bigger, I couldn't. I couldn't tell you how.

"So no. I don't think you're a fool. I think you've got one that only comes when it's needed. Everybody would tell you that's worse." She paused. "I'd point out it came twice this week, and both times it was needed. I'd like to know what that adds up to."

Jab did not answer. Callie saw his hand go to his collar and find nothing, and stay there.

"She's still," said Dessa. "The knocking's stopped. They're bringing her."

The heavy door opened.

---

Her mother came out on the rolling bed with her eyes shut and her hair every which way. She opened her eyes as they crossed the yellow line, and found Callie.

"It knocked," she said. Her voice was very tired. "Like a man mending a roof inside my head. I didn't care for it."

"You stayed still."

"I'm very good at staying still." Her mother turned her head and saw the chair, and Senna in it, and Dessa on the floor, and a boy on a bed with a wooden fish. "Oh. We've got company."

"You didn't move once," said Dessa. "The whole forty minutes. I was counting."

Callie's mother looked at her.

"I know," she said. "I could tell there was somebody counting." She shut her eyes. "There usually is."

Then it was Tonk's turn. The man in white asked him if there was anything on him, and Tonk said no, and asked Jab if there was anything on *him*, and Jab held up the paper tray.

"It's a rule," said Jab. "No metal past the line. So I stop here."

Tonk looked at the yellow line on the floor.

"Do *they* keep it?" he said.

"Everybody keeps it. That's the only reason it's any good."

Tonk looked at the line a moment longer. Then he let go of his brother's hand.

Nobody had asked him to. Callie saw it happen. She saw Jab's hand stay in the air for a second after, open, and then come down to the tray.

Tonk went in with the fish. The door shut like a lid.

The knocking started again. Jab sat very still.

Callie put the hook down across her knees and did not grip it. She sat on the bench beside him, and did not say anything, and let the knocking count itself.

---

The man in the long coat came to the door of hut fourteen at dusk.

Callie knew him. He had stood on top of the bank at the south gate at fourteen minutes and looked down at all of them, and at her longest. He was tall and thin, about fifty, with a close grey beard and a quiet face. He had a folded paper in one hand and did not offer it.

"I'm from the yard office," he said. He did not give his name. "I've read your card. Starting with the first line. Mr Hallet said you'd asked."

"It's the true one."

"I think they're all true. That's why it came to me." He did not come in. "We'd like you to come to the north yard. Not now. When you're not dry. We'd like to see what it says on the card. The hook, and the thing you can put in it. Nobody here's seen that. Nobody I've ever met has."

Callie stood in the doorway. She felt Katori behind her, not moving.

"My mother's got a doctor at the ninth hour tomorrow," she said. "I'm not doing anything till after that."

"No. Of course not." He put the paper back in his coat. "There's no hurry. It's only a request. You can say no to it." He looked at her a moment longer. "Most people don't."

He nodded to Katori and went away down the row.

"Most people don't," said Pell, behind her.

"I heard."

"That," said Pell, "is the sort of thing I'd want a column for."

Somebody had written *all of them* at the desk. Somebody else had read it and fetched five chairs and set them in a half circle and gone away. Callie looked at them and did not like them, and knew exactly why this time. She had seen Senna's four.

Her mother was sitting up against three pillows with her hair brushed.

"There you all are," she said. "Sit down. They've counted you."

They sat. Katori and Pell and Ines and Otto, and Callie in the chair nearest the bed. Across the ward Senna sat up with a cup in both hands, watching her fingers round it. A nurse came to pull the curtain.

"Leave it," said her mother. "She'll hear anyway. I'd rather she heard it straight."

Across the ward, Senna did not look up. But the corner of her mouth moved.

Doctor Sowerby came in at the ninth hour exactly, small and bald and in a hurry. He stopped at the foot of the bed and looked at each of them the way Ines looked at a range.

"Right," he said. "The bad thing first. Then the good thing. Then the bill. Stop me."

Nobody stopped him.

"There's a growth in her head." He put two fingers against the side of his own head, on the left, above and behind the ear. "Here. Deep. It's in the part that makes words, and the part that tells the right hand and the right leg what to do. It's been growing since before the winter. Probably well before. We can't take it out. There's no way in that wouldn't take more of her than it took of it. And there's nothing we have that makes it smaller. I've looked. I looked again this morning, because I didn't want to be wrong."

He looked at her mother and nobody else.

"We can't treat it," he said. "I want that said with you sitting up. In a fortnight somebody in a corridor will say *treated* to your daughter, and she'll hear something it isn't."

"Thank you," said her mother.

Callie heard the next part and did not hear it.

The words went into her the way the cold had gone into her on the causeway, a long way down and all at once. She was in a chair by a bed. She was also in a hayfield in the spring with her grandfather in the grass. She was also on the tailboard at three in the morning, watching her mother try to braid her own hair with a hand that would not take instruction.

And she was counting. She did not know what.

"—the good thing," Sowerby was saying. She made herself hear it.

"There's swelling round it. A lot of what you've been living with is the swelling, not the growth. The headaches, mostly. Some of the hand, maybe. Some of the words. That we can bring down. There's a medicine. Pills, morning and night. Some people get a bit of the hand back. Some get words back. Nearly everybody gets their mornings back without the head.

"The fits. There's a second medicine for those. They'll come less. Maybe not at all, for a while." He put his hand down. "It'll make your days better. I can promise that. I've got the numbers in this folder. I can't promise it makes more of them. I won't say it does."

"No," said her mother. "Don't."

"And then there's the part everybody asks me for. So I'll—"

"Stop," said her mother.

He stopped.

"I know what the next part is," said her mother. "You're going to tell me how long."

"If you want it."

"I don't."

Nobody in the ward moved.

"That's your right," said Sowerby at once. He said it without any weight, the way you say the door is on the left. "It's in the folder. It'll stay in the folder. Whoever you say can have it, whenever you say. Including you. You can change your mind any day you like, and I won't ask you why."

"I want it," said Callie.

It came out of her too loud for the ward. Across the room Senna looked up.

Her mother turned her head on the pillow.

"I want the number," said Callie. Her voice was doing something. She made it stop. "I'm not asking to be frightened. I'm asking to know. I'm the household. The man at the window said so. For the window, it's me."

"Then the window can have it," said her mother. "You can't."

Callie stared at her.

"It isn't yours," said her mother. She said it very gently and very clearly, and every word was in the right place. "It's mine. It's in my head. It's the one thing in there that's still mine to decide about."

"I have to know how many—"

"I know you do. That's why." Her mother's left hand came across the blanket and found Callie's wrist. "I've watched you do a sum every morning since Gaunt's Reach. Fifteen days. Nineteen. Nine pounds of flour. Seven matches. A hundred and fifty-eight seconds. You do it before you've got your boots on. You'd do it every morning with this, and you'd get it right, you always do. I'd rather you hated me for that than did it."

Callie could not say anything at all.

"And I'm not having it myself either," said her mother, to the room now. "I've spent a month on the back of a wagon counting miles. I'm not going to spend whatever I've got counting days." She let go. "That's all. You can go on to the bill."

Doctor Sowerby looked at her for a long moment. Then he took the folder from under his arm and put it on the end of the bed, closed, with his hand flat on it.

"The bill," he said.

The bill was short. Her care was clinical. It had gone through at the gate. It was owed to nobody. If anybody said different, they could come and hear him explain it slowly.

"And where she sleeps," said Katori quietly.

"Where she sleeps is a different window," said Sowerby. "I don't sit at it."

"I'm not sleeping in a ward," said Callie's mother.

He looked back at her.

"I've nothing against it. It's very clean. The woman opposite is the best company I've had since the spring. "You can. Most people don't want to. You'll need somebody with you who knows the fits. Who knows what to do when one comes."

"I know the fits," said Callie. It came out flat and small.

"She counts them," said her mother.

Sowerby looked at Callie, and back.

"Then I'll see you in the mornings," he said. "Eighth hour. With the girl. You can go tomorrow."

He went away down the ward as fast as he had come.

"Well," said Senna, into the silence.

Everybody looked at her. She was not looking at any of them. She was looking at the cup in her hands, watching her fingers on it.

"You heard me yesterday," she said. "I know you did. I'll only say it's still a good rule." She drank, looking at the cup, and put it down. "That's all. I'll go back to my tea."

Callie's mother lay against her pillows. She looked at the ceiling. Then she looked at the five of them in their five counted chairs.

"All right," she said. "Once. And then nobody does it for me.

"I've got a thing in my head. They can't get it out and they can't make it smaller. They can make it easier. They're going to, and I'm going to let them, and I'll take their pills like a good woman.

"I'm not having a number. There's one in that little man's folder and it can stay there. Nobody in this room goes and asks him for it behind my back. If I find out anybody has, I'll be extremely rude, and I've been practising.

"And I'm not living in a ward. I'm going to live in a hut with a stove, with my daughter, and with whichever of you will have me. And I'm going to walk in here every morning for pills, like somebody going to buy bread."

"That's it," she said. "I'd like nobody to say it for me again."

Otto was crying. He did it without any sound, sitting very straight with his stitched arm in its sling, and he did not wipe his face.

Pell had the atlas on his knee. He did not open it. After a while he put his hand flat on the cover and left it there.

"Not everything goes in the book," he said, to nobody.

"No," said Callie's mother. "Not everything."

---

Callie went out.

She did not decide to. She got up and went down the ward and past the desk and out through the doors. She did not stop until she was round the back of the long building, where there was nobody. There was a strip of wet grass between the wall and a drain, and a stack of old crates, and grey sky.

She sat on the crates with her arms round her ribs and her head down on her knees.

She did not cry. It was too far down to reach, the way the well had been on the plain when it was dry to the stone. And in the dark behind her eyes she was counting. She did not know what. She could not stop.

Katori found her there.

She did not say anything. She sat down on the next crate, a little apart, with her cap pulled down. She waited.

"She won't let me have it," said Callie, into her knees.

"No."

"It's the only thing I can do. I count. I put it on my card. It's the one thing I know how to do for her."

"What would you do with it?" said Katori.

Callie lifted her head.

"The number," said Katori. "Say he'd told you. Say it was whatever it was. What would you do with it?"

Callie opened her mouth. She shut it.

"Count," she said.

"Yes," said Katori. "That's why."

---

They sat a long time.

"She's not asking you to stop holding her," said Katori at last. "She's asking you to stop looking at the number. That's not the same thing. You can hold on to her with both hands for as long as there is. You just can't count it."

"I don't know how."

"No. You'll find out. Badly and slowly. You'll hate it." Katori stood up. Then she did not go. She stood looking at the long wall of the building. "There's a boy on a corridor floor in there. His brother went in at the ninth hour, the same as your mother's doctor. I said I'd be there when it came out."

Callie looked up at her.

"Go," she said.

"You'll be all right?"

"No," said Callie. "Go anyway. He hasn't got anybody. He said so at the window. *Two.*"

Katori looked at her a moment longer. Something moved behind her face, the way wind goes over a field.

Then she went.

---

In the middle of the day Callie found herself at the south pen, in front of the hatch.

She had not planned to go. Ivor Hallet looked up from his book, and saw her face, and put down his pen.

"Miss."

"My mother's care is clinical," said Callie. "She's going to live in the hut. Fourteen. What does it take to stay in the hut? After fourteen days."

Hallet told her. He told it plainly and in order, the way he told everything. Two gates, kept apart on purpose. Medicine, which was not withheld from anybody and never had been. And residence, which was reviewed. A household was reviewed on what the station reckoned it contributed.

"Not on what it needs," said Callie.

"Not on what it needs. I won't dress that up for you."

"What do we contribute?"

Hallet turned back some pages.

"On paper? Three Handfuls, lifted, less station. A sponsor line in a name that came in heavy. And you, miss. A red thread. A card that's gone upstairs." He looked up. "There's work in the north yard for red threads. Most of it's ordinary and counts ordinary. There's one kind that counts heavier than the rest." He picked up the pen and did not write. "I'd rather you heard about it from somebody who's doing it. You're in Yard C. You've got a neighbour who signed for it yesterday. He'll tell you the truth, because he doesn't seem able to tell anybody anything else."

---

Her mother already knew. Of course she did.

"Senna told me," she said, when Callie came back into the ward. "While you were at the window. Don't look like that. There was nobody else to tell me, and she wanted to, and I wanted to know."

Across the ward Senna sat with a cup in both hands.

"Four seasons," said Senna, not looking up. "The worst work there is. Holding a road shut while people behind you sack what comes through. He signed yesterday for my address. Not my medicine. My address."

"And you let him," said Callie's mother.

"I let him. Because he'd decided, and it was his to decide. If I'd said no, he'd have done it anyway, without me saying yes." Senna drank, watching the cup. "Yours is thirteen. Mine was a grown man who'd already decided. It's a different sum."

Callie stood at the foot of her mother's bed. She could not sit.

"You don't get to spend yourself on me," said her mother.

"I haven't—"

"Not seasons. Not for an address. Not for a window." Her mother's voice was very steady. "I told you at the bottom of the scarp you don't get to spend me. It goes both ways. I didn't say that half then, because I didn't know I'd need to."

"Then where do you live?" Callie heard her own voice crack and did not care. "After fourteen days. If there's nothing on the paper—"

"I don't know," said her mother. "I've got thirteen days before the fourteenth. I'm not going to spend them on the fourteenth. And neither are you." She looked up at Callie from the pillow. "You promised me. On the plain. You don't sign one thing for me without me. This is me, saying don't."

Callie stood at the foot of the bed with her hands empty.

She did not say *I promise*. She had promised already. She only nodded, once, and her mother saw it and shut her eyes.

---

At the fourth hour a boy of about ten came down the row with three buff cards in his hand.

Callie watched him from the step of fourteen. He stopped at eleven and knocked and waited. He stopped at twelve, where nobody answered, and slid a card under the door. Then he came to fourteen.

"Miss Callie?" he said. He said the rest of her name, the one off the scale-house door, carefully, as if he had practised it.

He held out the card and waited to be told there was no reply.

She took it. It was the size of her hand.

*Requested at the Director's office, second floor, yard office, concerning the north road incident. Attendance is not compulsory. Please bring your band.*

The date under it was eleven days away.

Callie read it twice. Then she looked down the row at the door of eleven, and the door of twelve with a card under it, and the step of fourteen where she was sitting. Three doors. One boy with three cards.

"There's no reply," she said.

The boy nodded and went away up the row.

# Chapter 5 — Jab: Four Pins

He sat on the corridor floor with his back against the wall. There was no bench on that side, and he was not going to go and find one.

The doors were eleven feet away. They were double doors with a round window in each and a strip of rubber down the join. Every so often somebody went through them and the air moved. Every time, his whole body came up off the wall. Then it went back down again.

Dessa Cole arrived at about the quarter hour with a wooden chair. She dragged it the last twenty feet, because carrying it was not available to her. She set it down against the opposite wall. Then she did not sit in it. She lowered herself onto the floor beside him with the bad leg out straight, put her back against the concrete, and looked at the doors.

"You're going to want to know," she said. "So I'm going to tell you. I'll tell you the dull bits as well. If I only say something when something happens, you'll go mad between them."

Jab did not trust his voice. He nodded.

"Six people in there," said Dessa. "He's on the far side, about twenty-two feet. He's the only one lying down. Four of them are standing round him. Two of them are close in. One's moving about. That'll be somebody fetching. Nobody's fast."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know what's good," said Dessa. "I know what's fast. I've been in a room where people got fast." She shifted the leg. "Nobody's fast."

---

They were there for two hours and ten minutes. Dessa talked for most of it, in a flat voice, about very small things.

Nobody moved for a long time. Then somebody stepped back from the table and stood still for about forty seconds and stepped in again. Then two people swapped places. Dessa said that was the sort of thing people did when they were being careful about where their hands went. Jab decided to believe her.

At the hour and a quarter, somebody went out through a door on the far side and came back with a fifth person. Jab's stomach turned over.

"Don't," said Dessa. "Everything in a place like this is somebody fetching somebody. If it were bad, they'd fetch fast. And they'd fetch from this side, because this side's where the rest of the building is."

"How do you know that?"

"My grandmother's been in this building five days," said Dessa, "and I've spent all five of them sitting in corridors. I count everything. I'm the only person here who knows which way the bad news walks."

She said *my grandmother*. Jab had thought, in the ward, that the old woman belonged to the boy with the sling. Perhaps she belonged to all four of them. He did not ask. He thought Dessa had decided the word a long time ago and was not going to discuss it.

At the hour and a half, Wyck came and sat down on his other side without saying anything at all. He turned a piece of squared steel bar over and over in his left hand. He dropped it twice on the concrete. He picked it up.

At the hour and three quarters, Otto came with a paper cup of tea. He gave it to Jab and went away again, going red.

At two hours, Katori came.

She did not sit down. She stood against the far wall with her cap in her hands and did not speak. Jab looked at her once. Her face was the same flat careful face it always was. But she was standing the way you stand when you have come from somewhere else that was bad, and have put it down outside the door, and can still feel the weight in your arms.

He did not ask. He was learning.

At two hours and ten minutes, the doors opened.

---

Doctor Keel came out with her sleeves down and her arms scrubbed pink to the elbow. There was something folded over her forearm. Jab was on his feet with no memory of standing up.

"He's alive and he's asleep and he's got both legs," she said.

Then she waited about four seconds while that went in. Jab understood, distantly, that she said those words first on purpose. That she did it every time. That somebody had once taught her to.

"Now the rest of it. It's long, and there's a chair behind you. Sit down or don't, but don't stand there swaying at me.

"It was worse inside than it looked in the picture. That's usual. The dead piece was longer than I'd hoped. About as long as my thumb. But it was loose, which is the one bit of luck in the whole business. Loose means it came out whole, and I didn't have to go chasing bits. I took it out. I took out a good deal of the muck round it. I've washed that leg out with more fluid than you'd think a leg could hold.

"I've taken the bad join apart and set it straight. It's the straightest that leg's been since the day it broke. It's held by four pins through the bone and a bar on the outside. It's the ugliest object you'll ever see on a child, and it stays on him until the spring.

"That's the good half. Here's the rest, in order.

"There's a gap in the bone where the dead piece was. That's expected. There's a way of dealing with it. It's slow, and it involves him being in that frame a long time, and it involves me.

"The infection. I've taken out what it was living in, and he's on the medicine. In two weeks we'll know whether it's gone out of him or whether I'm going in again. I told you four in five. It's still four in five. It doesn't become five in five because I've had a good morning."

"And the growing," said Jab.

Keel looked at him.

"You've got a memory on you," she said. "Yes. The growing. I could see it. The place that leg grows from has been out in the weather for two months, and it's damaged. Not gone. Damaged. So it'll grow, and it won't grow as fast as the other one. I can't tell you today by how much. Anybody who does tell you is guessing.

"So he'll walk with a limp. He'll have one boot built up. I'll measure him every year until he stops growing. And somewhere in the middle of that, I'll very likely have him back on a table.

"That's the whole of the bill. He'll be asleep another two hours. Then he'll wake up and he'll be sick and he'll be extremely angry. The anger's the best sign in the world, and I want you to enjoy it."

She took the folded thing off her arm. It was a cloth with something in it.

"He asked me for this at the door," she said. "It went in with him. I put it in his hand myself, like I said I would. It's been on his chest for two hours. I've had to wash it. The nurse says if I don't hand it to you personally, there'll be a row."

The wooden fish was as long as Jab's thumb. It had been smoked black by a lifetime of hanging near a fire. One fin had broken off before Tonk was born.

Jab took it.

He turned round and put his forehead against the corridor wall. He stood there with his back to all of them for about a minute.

Nobody in that corridor said one word, to him or to each other, until he had finished.

Tonk woke at the middle of the afternoon, and was sick, and was extremely angry.

He was angry about being sick. He was angry about the bar on his leg, which he could see, and which he described in detail to everybody who came near the bed. He was angry about the tube in his hand. He was angry that Jab had not been there when he woke. Jab had been there. Tonk had been asleep for it. It did not matter in the slightest.

"You *weren't*."

"I was."

"You *weren't*, and it was ages, and nobody *told* me—"

"All right," said Jab. "I wasn't."

Tonk lay back on the pillow, satisfied and exhausted.

"It's got a *bar*," he said.

"I saw."

"It's got a bar, and it's got *nails in my leg*."

"Four of them."

"*Four?*"

"Four," said Jab. "I counted them."

His brother looked at the ceiling of a long white room and thought about this for a while. Jab sat in the chair and watched the whole of it turning behind a four-year-old's face.

"It's the rail," Tonk said at last.

"What is?"

"The bar." Tonk moved one hand, vaguely, at the leg in its cradle. "It's the rail. Like on the frame. So it doesn't *hang*."

Jab looked at the thing bolted through his brother's shin. He did not say anything for a moment, because his throat had shut.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly what it is."

"They did it *inside*," said Tonk, with enormous scorn, and went to sleep.

"He won't need that in a week," said the young woman in white, going past with a tray. It was the one who had stitched his arm on the bench. "There's a bell on a string."

"I know," said Jab.

She stopped.

"I'm not telling you to take it off," she said.

"I know," said Jab.

Senna, in the next bed, did not open her eyes. But Jab heard her let her breath out, slowly, the way a person does when a thing has gone the right way.

Otto moved into hut twelve on the third morning.

He arrived at the door with his blanket rolled under his good arm and a bowl of something hot in the other hand, and stood on the step radiating anxiety until Jab opened his eyes.

"Katori says," said Otto. "They're bringing Callie's mum home today. To fourteen. "How properly?"

"She said I'd know," said Otto, and went red.

Jab ate it. It was porridge, and it was good. Otto watched him eat the whole of it, the way a man watches a fire he has lit to make sure it catches.

There was a buff card on the table under the bowl. It had come under the door yesterday afternoon while Jab was in the corridor. He had read it last night by the stove and then turned it face down.

*Requested at the Director's office, concerning the north road incident. Attendance is not compulsory.*

"Callie got one," said Otto, seeing him look. "And the boy in eleven. And the man with the back. All for the same hour." He looked at the card and then away from it. "Pell says they've joined you all up. He says he'd want a column for that."

---

The man in the long coat came at the middle of the morning.

He was tall and thin, about fifty, with a close grey beard and a quiet face. He asked for Jab by name, which he had off a card. He did not give his own.

"I'm from the yard office," he said. "I've read your declaration."

"All of it?"

"Both sides." He had a folded paper in one hand and did not offer it. "I'll tell you what I came to say, and then I'll say the other things. There's a Flask of ash on the board in your name."

Jab stood in the doorway.

"The hound at the culvert head," said the man. "The one you killed. The Sag crew lifted it the same night, with the rest. There was a disagreement at the yard office about whose name it went against. I'm told it lasted some time. It was settled by a small woman in a cap who doesn't raise her voice." He almost smiled. "It's yours. Less station, as everything is. It isn't a great deal. It's more than nothing, and it'll be on the paper at your review. I'd rather you heard about it from a person than found it on a wall."

"I didn't kill it for money."

"No," said the man. "Nobody's ever killed one for money in the whole history of this station. They kill it and then there's money. That's a different sentence. It took me about nine years to learn to say it the right way round." He put his hands behind his back. "Now the rest.

"Your household's reviewed in eleven days. You and a child in a bed. On the paper there's a Flask of ash and a red thread and nothing else. I won't pretend that's a strong sheet.

"Your brother's care doesn't depend on it. You've been told that. It's true. What depends on it is whether you go on sleeping inside this berm, four hundred yards from him. Or whether you're outside, coming in on a pass on visiting days."

"And what puts something on the sheet?"

"Work," said the man in the long coat. "There's all kinds. Most of it's ordinary and pays ordinary and counts ordinary." He paused for exactly as long as it takes to change the subject without seeming to. "And there's the north yard."

---

He explained it standing on the step, without any dressing on it at all.

There was a stretch of road at the edge of the north yard that had never settled. The Sag. Things came through there more than anywhere else near Meridian. The station kept a line on it, and somebody to hold that line shut, and a crew behind to lift what was left of whatever came through. What was left was reserve-ash. Reserve-ash was fuel, and money, and the reason anybody went near the place.

"You've seen the Sag," said the man. "You walked through it the night before last with lamps in it."

"I saw the lamps."

"Holding the line is a narrow trade. Almost nobody here can do it. You can't. Your card says so, and I believe it.

"What you can do is the other job. When the line goes down—and it goes down; it went down four times last winter and twice this autumn—the people sacking the ash are on their knees with their backs to whatever's just arrived. Somebody has to be standing over them with something sharp until the line comes back. At the moment there isn't a somebody. There's the holder, and he has to try to do both. The holder who's just signed on is the man in eleven, with a hole in his back."

Jab said nothing.

"It's the worst work in this station," said the man. "It pays like a joke. Everyone involved knows it pays like a joke. It pays like that because the people who take it have got nothing else to put on a review." He held up the folded paper, briefly, and put it back in his coat. "Four seasons. It's a thing you sign.

"And it would count. I'll be honest about that, because if I'm vague you'll find out in eleven days and think I was clever. It would count heavier than anything else you could put on that sheet. It would very likely settle where you sleep for the next four years. By my arithmetic, that's about a third of the time your brother has to keep coming through those doors."

"You've done that arithmetic," said Jab.

"I do it every time. I'd be no use here if I didn't." He looked at Jab levelly. "And I'll tell you the thing I'm supposed to tell you. This is a request. You can say no."

"Do people say no?"

"Most people don't," said the man in the long coat.

---

He turned round at the end of the row.

"There's one more thing. The second thing on your card. The one you haven't got a word for."

Jab said nothing.

"There's a column for it," said the man. "It's a wide column and there's very little in it. Nobody's going to take you anywhere. Nobody's going to ask you to do it in a yard for an audience. If anybody does, you may tell them I said they weren't to." He paused. "But you wrote it down, and you didn't have to. I'd like you to know a person read it. We'd like to understand what's on the register. That's the whole sentence. There's no second half."

"It doesn't come when I call it," said Jab.

"No," said the man. "It says that on the card. In three separate places, which I noticed."

Then he went away down the row, past the door of eleven. A man was sitting on that step with his coat over his knees, turning something small and flat over in his fingers. He did not look up.

---

Rook was still there an hour later.

Jab had not meant to go and stand in front of him. He had walked the length of the row twice, and then a third time. On the third time the man on the step said, without raising his voice:

"You've been up and down four times. Sit down or go in. Don't wear the path out. It's the only one I've got."

Jab sat down on the step of twelve, which was his own, one door along.

They did not say anything for a while. Down at the end of the row the lamp on its pole was not lit yet.

"He came to you as well, then," said Rook.

"This morning."

"Long coat. Grey beard. Doesn't give a name. It isn't a trick. He genuinely doesn't think it's relevant." Rook turned the flat grey stone over in his fingers. "He's not lying to you. I want to start there, because a lot of people would rather he was. Everything he told you is true. He told it in the right order. He didn't leave the worst of it out."

"He said most people don't say no."

"He says that to everybody. He said it to me on this step a few days ago, in the rain. And then I signed, standing up, at a counter. I haven't once decided it was the wrong thing. I haven't once felt clean about it either."

"Why did you?"

"Because there's a woman in that long building who has to be within a mile of a machine in a shed every eleven days for the rest of her life." Rook looked at the unlit lamp. "Her medicine was never the station's to sell. They never tried to sell it. They didn't have to. They own the ground the shed stands on, and a bed is a different window. It's an extremely good arrangement. It's the best I've ever seen. Nobody in it is even doing anything wrong."

"That's the part I can't get round," said Jab.

"No," Rook agreed. "Nor me."

---

"How old are you?"

"Seventeen."

"And the boy's four. And there's nobody else."

"There's nobody else," said Jab.

Rook nodded slowly. He did not say any of the eleven things people had been saying to Jab since the gate.

"Then I'll tell you one thing, and it's the only thing I've got that's worth anything. Somebody had to say it to me twice before it went in. You can say no.

"That's all. I'm not telling you to. I'm not telling you not to. If you came to me in a week and said you'd signed, I'd not say one word about it. I'd have no standing to, and I'd understand exactly why." He put the stone away in his coat. "I'm telling you it's allowed. People muddle that with being told what to do. They're different things. Nobody says the first one out loud in this place, because everybody's busy being reasonable at each other."

"What happens if I say no?"

"Then you're reviewed on a Flask of ash and a red thread. I don't sit on the review, and neither does he. And I won't lie to you about which way it goes."

"Then it isn't a choice."

"It's a choice with a price on it," said Rook. "That's what a choice is. The other kind's a menu." He got his breath. "And here's the last of it. Don't decide tonight. There's eleven days. Everything in this station is shaped so you decide it at three in the morning on the second night, when you've just come off a road and you're frightened and you'd sign anything to stop the feeling. That isn't anybody's plan. It's what the shape of the place does to people. The only defence is to know it's happening."

---

Jab sat on his step and looked at his hands.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask."

"At the culvert." He kept his eyes on his hands. "After. When he'd gone off, and I put my hand on him. You were there. You'd sat down on the wall."

Rook did not say anything.

"You saw it," said Jab.

"I saw it."

"Do you know what it is?"

Rook was quiet for a long time. The lamp at the end of the row came on, flat and white.

"I know what it looks like from the outside," he said at last. "I've done a thing that rhymes with it. Not the same thing. It rhymes." He looked at the lamp. "And I'm not going to put a word on yours tonight. Not because I'm being careful with you. Because a word's a thing you carry, and you've got enough on your back. And because I'd want to be sure, and I'm not."

"It doesn't come when I call it."

"No. I read that in your face at the culvert. You didn't call it. You stopped wanting it to do anything, and it went." Rook looked at him sideways. "That's not nothing. That's the hardest part of anything I know how to do. Most people spend years on it. You did it in a ditch with a child going grey under your hand."

Jab did not answer. He could not.

"Ask me again in a while," said Rook, and got himself up off the step in three movements with his hand on the door frame. "Not tonight. I've a girl of fifteen who counts how long I sit out here in the cold, and she's going to come out in about a minute and say a number at me."

The door of eleven opened. Dessa stood in it.

"Forty minutes," she said.

"There," said Rook. "What did I tell you."

---

Wyck came to the ward on the fourth afternoon.

He came in at the start of visiting with his right arm strapped across his chest and the iron bar in his left hand. The nurse told him to leave the bar at the desk. He did not leave it. He stood at the foot of Tonk's bed and looked at him without any expression.

"You're the one that shouted," he said.

"You're the one that didn't *move*," said Tonk.

Wyck considered this.

"No," he agreed.

"It ran at you. It ran at you and you went like *this*—" Tonk did a thing with his shoulders that cost him and that he did anyway, four inches of shoulder and then nothing "—and it went in the *wall*."

"That's about what happened."

"*Why* didn't you move?"

"I don't fall over," said Wyck.

Jab watched his brother take this in. He watched him file it, permanently, wherever a four-year-old keeps the dozen facts that are going to build him.

"Wyck," said Wyck, to Jab, as an afterthought. He pulled the chair round with his foot and sat on it backward with the bar across his knees, and stayed an hour and a half.

---

He was not good with children. Jab watched him and did not entirely understand it. He did not soften his voice. He did not ask bright questions. Twice he said things that would have made an aunt at home put her hands on her hips.

"That's going to hurt worse tomorrow than it does now."

"*Wyck*," said Jab.

"He knows," said Wyck, without turning round. "He's had it seven weeks. He knows what it does. He doesn't know what tomorrow's going to do. If I tell him it'll be fine, he'll find out at the second hour in the dark that I'm a liar. Then he won't have anybody."

And Tonk, in the bed, said: "Does it *stop*?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Ages. And it doesn't stop all at once. It stops a bit at a time. One day you notice you've gone a whole morning and not thought about it. That's the day it starts being ordinary." Wyck held up his right hand, out of the strapping. He moved three of the fingers. The last two did not move. "These don't work. They haven't since the spring. I dropped everything I picked up for two months, and I was extremely unpleasant about it."

Tonk's eyes went very round.

"Can I *touch* them?"

"If you like."

Tonk pinched the two dead fingers, hard, with great concentration, watching Wyck's face. Wyck looked back at him with an entirely level expression.

"Nothing," he said.

"*Nothing?*"

"Nothing at all."

"Do it *again*," said Tonk, delighted and appalled, and Jab put his head down on the edge of the bed and laughed until his stitches pulled.

---

Tonk asked about the squirrel on the fifth day.

He asked it out of nowhere, in the afternoon, lying flat with the bar on his leg and the ceiling to look at. They had been talking about something else entirely.

"When we go back," he said, "I'm putting the thing on the heap."

Jab was folding a blanket. He went on folding it.

"You said," Tonk went on, with the confidence of a man quoting a signed agreement. "You said when my leg's mended I walk up there with a pocket full of something, and I do it *myself*, and you'd show me the tree. You *said*."

"I did say that."

"So when's my leg mended?"

Jab put the blanket down. He sat on the edge of the chair and looked at his brother. He made himself do the thing he had made a rule about seven weeks ago, and had kept, every single time, even when it cost him a night.

"Your leg's going to mend," he said. "It'll take until the spring before you stand on it. A while after that before you walk properly. And it's never going to be quite the same as the other one. That's all true, and you'd have got it out of somebody here by the end of the week anyway.

"And we're not going back."

Tonk's face did not do anything. That was what it did.

"Not ever?"

"Not to live. It's eighteen days of road, and you've got to come to this building every year till you're grown. I'm not going to tell you a story about it. That's what it is."

"But the *heap*."

"I know."

"You *said*." His voice went up and broke. "You said I could *put it back*—"

"I know what I said. I meant it when I said it. I can't do it, and I'm not going to pretend I can." Jab took his brother's hand, the one without the needle. "So here's what I've got instead. You can tell me it's not as good. It isn't.

"That squirrel got robbed by me. Not you. You never took anything off anybody in your life. So it was always me who owed him. And what I owe him I can't pay, because he's four hundred miles north. He's either got through his winter or he hasn't."

"Then it doesn't *count*," said Tonk, furiously.

"It counts," said Jab. "That's the whole trouble with it. It counts, and I can't pay it where I took it." He looked out of the window over the bed, at a turf roof and a grey sky. "So I'll have to put it back here."

"*Here?*"

"Here. Somewhere in this place. To somebody. I don't know who yet, or what with." He heard himself say it. He heard what it meant about four minutes after his mouth had finished. "That's what it is when you can't get back to the heap. You don't get out of owing. You just have to find a different heap."

Tonk lay and thought about this a long time, with his brows down. He was doing the arithmetic of a four-year-old. It was not the same arithmetic. It was not nothing either.

"All right," he said at last. "But *I'm* doing it. Not you."

"You're doing it."

"*Me.*"

"You," said Jab.

---

The light through the window had the thin quality that made everybody in the ward look underwater. In the next bed, Senna was asleep, or pretending. Across the ward, the fourth bed on the left was empty now, and made up tight. Callie's mother had gone home two days ago, in a barrow.

"Jab."

"Mm."

"You know when we were on the hill."

"Which hill?"

"The *last* one." Tonk's hand moved on the blanket. "With the smoke. You showed me, and I said what's it called, and you said Meridian. And I said no, *it*."

Jab put the cup down.

"I remember."

"And you said I had to see it up close first."

"I did."

"I've seen it," said Tonk.

He had. He had seen a gate come open in an earth bank at night with a lamp burning over it. He had seen a room with a light in the ceiling that did not move. He had been inside a machine that knocked like a man mending a roof. He had had a piece of his own bone taken out of him by a woman with her sleeves rolled up. He had had a rail bolted on the outside of his leg, so it did not hang, and a wooden fish put in his hand by somebody who had not had to.

"Well, then," said Jab. "Has it got a name?"

Tonk looked at the ceiling for a long moment.

"Not yet," he said.

# Chapter 6 — Toren: The Shed

The card came at the fourth hour. A boy of about ten brought it to the door of hut eleven and stood waiting to be told there was no reply.

It was buff, and the size of a hand. There were two lines on it.

*E. MARSH and T. VOSS are requested at the Director's office, second floor, yard office, concerning (i) the south approach incident and (ii) the north road incident. Attendance is not compulsory. Please bring your bands.*

The date under it was eleven days away.

Dessa read it over Toren's shoulder. Then she took it out of his hand and read it again.

"They've put them on the same card," she said.

"It's a card, Dessa. They've probably only got the one kind—"

"They have got more than one kind of card," said Dessa Cole. "I've been in this station five days and I've counted eleven kinds of paper. There's a new green one on the ward door." She held it up. "They put those two things on one card because somebody sat at a desk and joined them. The south approach is *you killed something nobody can kill*. The north road is *you did it again, with half the station watching, two days later*. Somebody's drawn a line between them."

Toren went to the door.

The boy had gone on down the row. He stopped at twelve and knocked, and waited, and nobody came. He slid a card under the door. Then he went on to fourteen.

Callie was sitting on the step there. Toren watched her take the card and read it. He watched her read it twice. Then she looked up the row, at the door of twelve, and at him.

"She's got one too," said Toren.

"Of course she has," said Dessa, behind him. "And the boy in twelve. That's three doors and one hour." She gave him the card back. "They haven't joined two things, Tor. They've joined three."

Wyck was on the step with his bar.

"*Attendance is not compulsory*," he said, without looking round.

"Yes."

"They wrote that down." He turned the bar over in his left hand. He did not drop it. "That's the bit I'd want a column for."

---

They did the bread that night, because Dessa said the day had to have a halt in it.

Senna came in the chair with the wheels. Dessa had fetched her again, four hundred yards over the wet path, with Wyck pushing and complaining about the camber. She had to be back in the ward by the tenth hour. Dessa had told the nurse the ninth. That was Dessa's idea of a margin.

It was Meridian bread. It was grey and even and came in a paper wrapper with a number printed on the side. Dessa cut it poorly, in slabs, on the top of the stove-box, with a great deal of concentration.

"I know the order," she said.

"I know you do," said Senna.

Dessa picked up the end crust.

"Senna."

She put it in Senna's hand. Then, without being asked, she folded the fingers over it.

Toren saw her do it. He saw Senna look down at her own hand as if it belonged to somebody sitting next to her.

"It's there," said Dessa.

"I know it's there. I can feel the *weight* of it. I can't feel the bread." Senna turned the hand over slowly, watching it. The crust stayed where it was. "It's like being handed things through a glove. A thick one. That's the whole of it. It isn't dramatic, and I'd be obliged if nobody made a face."

It had been the ends of her fingers yesterday. Like snow, she had said. Now it was the whole of both hands.

The course had started yesterday. Sowerby had drawn a line on a page for them. The hands would go over months, he had said. By the spring. And then he had said, in the same voice he used for everything, that sometimes it went early. That the old damage under it could make it go faster than the line. That he'd tell them if it did.

He had not needed to tell them. They could see.

"Do you want—" said Dessa.

"I do not," said Senna Voss, "want anybody in this room to hold my bread for me. Not tonight. Not in the spring. Not in eight years. I'd like that minuted." She lifted the crust the way you lift a thing with a tool. "I can see it perfectly well. I'll simply have to look at it. I've looked at worse."

She ate the crust, looking at it.

---

"Me," said Dessa. She put a slab down beside her own hand and did not eat it.

"Wyck."

Wyck took it, left-handed.

"Rook."

Rook took it. He was sitting on the floor with his back against his bed, because the floor was easier to get up from than a chair. He held the bread and did not eat it either.

"Tor."

Toren took it.

"Milo," said Dessa.

She cut the last slab. She put it on the cloth at the end of the row, and folded the cloth over it.

Then she did the thing she had not done on the bank at the Level, or on any night since. Toren could see she had decided it on the path over from the ward, with Wyck complaining behind her.

"He'd have hated this place," she said.

Wyck made a noise.

"He would. He'd have hated it for about four days. Then he'd have known everybody's name in the whole north yard. He'd have been telling that Hallet man his business. He'd have got a lamp. He'd have got one of those lamps off the pole somehow. He'd have found out who owns them." She stopped. She put the knife down. She went on anyway, the way you walk over a bad patch of road without slowing down. "He'd have stood in the middle of that street with three lights on it and said something completely idiotic about how it wasn't as good as his."

"It wasn't as good as his," said Wyck.

"It wasn't," Rook agreed.

"It was *terrible*," said Dessa. "It was a jar of fireflies. It was never once enough to see by, the whole road. And it's the only thing that ever got any of us anywhere."

Nobody said anything.

Senna put her right hand flat on the top of the stove-box, which was warm. She could only just tell it was warm. By the spring she would not be able to tell at all. She left it there anyway.

"Milo Faust," she said to the room, in a voice like a dry stick breaking. "There. It's been said tonight. It'll be said tomorrow. I'll say it every single night in this station until they carry me out of it, and none of you have to."

---

Toren sat on the step at about the twelfth hour. Wyck had pushed the chair back across to the long building and come back. The stove was banked. Everybody had stopped talking.

He put his hand in his coat pocket, the way he had done every forty steps for two months.

It was warm.

He did not take it out.

It was not hot. It was not much, and it was not steady. It came and went under his fingers like something breathing. It was the least impressive thing anybody had ever felt. It was the way it had felt on a towpath in the spring, with a sack of corn across his knees.

He had not checked since the bench. That was the truth of it, and he had told nobody. A girl who read ground had watched his hand go to his pocket and told him that checking was what put it out. She had said *you know more than me*. And he had sat on the bench after she left with his hand on his knee, and made it stay there.

So he had not checked for a day and a half. And at some point in that day and a half, while he was not looking, it had come back.

*It's not gone. It's empty. Those are different.*

He sat on a concrete step in a place with a written reason for everything. His arm was broken. His grandmother was four hundred yards away with a pair of hands that were already going. There was a summons on the table behind him that had joined him to two people he had known for less than a week.

He held a warm stone in his pocket, and did not look at it, and did not ask it for anything.

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour.

---

They brought Callie's mother home in the morning, in a barrow.

Toren was on the step with Wyck when they came along the row. The big boy from fourteen had the barrow—Otto. He had the strap crossed over his chest and one handle in his good hand and the other braced against his hip. The woman sat on a folded blanket on the boards with her back against the front of the barrow and her legs out. She had a battered wide-brimmed hat pulled down against the light.

Callie walked beside it with one hand on the rim. Not holding. Only there.

Wyck watched the whole length of it come. He watched the strap and the one-handed grip and the handle against the hip.

"You've got it on your hip," he said, when Otto was level with the step. "The other handle. You'll have a bruise the size of a plate by tonight."

Otto stopped.

"I've got one arm," he said.

"So've I," said Wyck. His collarbone sat wrong under his coat, and the strapped arm did not move at all. He held up the bar, as if that settled it, and then dropped it on the step and did not pick it up. "Put the strap under the handle, not over. It'll take the weight off your hip and put it on your chest. Your chest doesn't mind."

Otto looked down at the strap. He moved it. He took the handle again and walked two steps and stopped.

"That's better," he said, surprised.

"I know," said Wyck. "I've been carrying things with one arm longer than I'd like. I've had a lot of time to think about hips."

"Thank you."

"Wyck."

"Otto," said Otto, and went red, and walked on.

The woman on the boards had watched all of it from under her hat.

"He's cleverer than he looks," she said to Callie, not quietly.

"*Mum*."

"I'm only saying. Most people are."

---

Callie looked at Toren as the barrow went past.

She looked at his face first. Then her eyes went down to his hand. It was in his coat pocket. It was staying there. It did not come out and go back.

He did not know what she saw. He knew she saw something. He saw her see it.

He nodded.

She nodded back. She did not say anything, and neither did he. Then she went on down the row beside the barrow, with her hand on the rim, and did not look round.

"What was that," said Wyck.

"Nothing."

"It wasn't nothing. I'm sitting right here." Wyck picked up the bar. "It's all right. You don't have to tell me. I'll work it out. I'm cleverer than I look."

---

Senna's course was in the shed on Tuesdays and Fridays and some other days besides. That afternoon was one of the others. Toren pushed the chair.

Rook had said *Tuesdays*. It had not been a joke, exactly. Toren had decided to take it as a job.

The shed was not a shed. It was the long slope under the north end of the long building, going down, where the air got cool and the walls went thick and grey and there were no windows. At the bottom there was the wide door and the yellow line and the sign.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

"There's a tray," said the man who came out to meet them.

He was about forty, with rolled sleeves and grey hair cut very short, and ink on the side of one hand. He did not give a name. He looked at Toren's sling, and his wrapped hand, and at Senna in the chair.

"I know," said Toren.

He took the stone out of his pocket. It was still warm. He did not look at it. He put it in the paper tray and set the tray on the bench against the wall, and his hand did not want to let go of it, and he let go.

"It's not iron," said the man.

"No."

"Then it's the other thing." He did not seem to mind. "Same rule. Nobody's ever been able to tell me what the other thing does to the room, so the other thing stays out as well."

"What does the room do?"

The man looked at him. He seemed to decide something.

"Come to the line," he said. "You can look. You can't come in."

---

Toren stood at the yellow line and looked through the wide door while they took Senna in.

It was a long room with a low ceiling and a great deal of cable. In the middle of it was the machine. It was not beautiful. It was a thick pale ring standing on its edge, taller than a man, with a narrow bed running through the hole in the middle of it. Everything about it was old. The paint had worn off the corners where hands had gone for decades. There were patches on the casing where somebody had cut a panel out and bolted a different one in.

Behind it, along the back wall, there was a rack.

It ran the whole width of the room, floor to ceiling, in a steel frame. It was full of grey boxes, row on row. Each had a little window in the front, and a light behind the window. Most of the lights were a steady pale green. There were cables running from box to box along the bottom of the rack like the roots of a hedge.

"It reads," said the man, beside him. "It looks through her. That's the picture. The same thing looked at your friend's head, and the little lad's leg. And then, for her, it doses. It sets how much of the course goes in, and it measures the preparation for her marrow. It tells us how much is enough and how much would kill her."

"What's the rack?"

"The train." The man said it the way you name a horse you have worked for a long time. "Calibration train. Each of those is a reference. It's got a thing inside it that we know the true value of. The machine checks itself against them, one after another, before it does anything. If they agree, it's telling the truth. If they don't, it isn't."

"Do they agree?"

"Mostly," said the man.

---

They got Senna onto the narrow bed. She lay down on it with the blanket over her and her hands flat at her sides, looking at the ceiling.

"It sings at me," she said, to nobody. "I'd like that understood. It isn't knocking. It sings."

The man went to a desk by the wall inside the door. There was a screen on it that glowed a flat green. He did something with his hands, and the rack began to hum.

Toren felt it in his teeth before he heard it. The green lights along the rack went pale, one after another, left to right, like somebody walking along a row of windows with a candle. When the walk reached the end, the man looked at his screen.

He did not say anything. He did something else with his hands. The walk went along the rack again, left to right.

He looked at the screen a second time.

Toren watched his face. He had spent two months learning weather off people. This man's weather did not change much. But it changed.

"Is that right?" said Toren.

"It's right now," said the man. He did not look round. "It does that. The third row down, the fourth box along, wakes up slow. It did it twice last week. It did it in the summer, and it was fine for a month after."

"Is that bad?"

"It's old," said the man. "It's older than your grandmother. It was built before the Fall, in a place that knew what the Fall was going to do to things and built it anyway. Everything in it wakes up slow now and then. So do I." He did the thing with his hands again, and the machine began to sing. "Go and sit down, lad. Forty minutes. I'll call you."

Toren sat on the bench beside the paper tray.

He did not touch the stone. He listened to the machine sing through the wall. It was a high sound with a lower one under it, like wind in a wire.

He thought about his grandmother at the south gate, in the rain, on the tailgate with a stranger kneeling beside her in a grey coat. *What year did it happen?*

*2130.*

*Where were you?*

*Inside the fence line. Eleven days.*

*Which fence line?*

*The old plant at Caul Hill. I was fifty-three and I went in for the copper, and I knew what was in there, and so did every soul on that crew, and we went in anyway because it was copper.*

He had heard her say it and not thought about it since. There had been too much else to think about. Now he sat outside a room built by people who knew what the Fall would do, and listened to a machine older than his grandmother sing through a wall. He thought about her going into a place like that for copper at fifty-three, and coming out, and paying for it every day for twenty years.

He thought: *There's a room in this station she can't live without, and I've seen the inside of it now.* He did not know why that mattered. It seemed to.

He looked at the rack in his head. Third row down. Fourth box along. He did not know why he wanted to remember that either. He remembered it anyway.

At dusk, Rook sat on the step of eleven.

He had got himself down in three movements with his hand on the door frame, and he sat with his coat over his knees and his back very straight. Toren was on his bed inside with the door open, because the stove was hot and the evening was not. He could see Rook's back and the row beyond it.

Rook took something out of his coat and began to turn it over in his fingers.

Toren knew what it was. He had known since a campfire, weeks back up the road. It was a flat grey stone, small, rounded at the corners. It was the stone Vera Kess had carried at Homura, and died holding across a doorway, and Rook had taken up off the floor after. Rook turned it between his thumb and his first finger the way a man turns a coin he has carried so long he has forgotten it. His hand was too big for it. It went round.

Dessa had held that stone once. In a field, the morning her door came on.

Two doors down, past the dark door of twelve, somebody else was sitting on a step. It was the small woman from fourteen. Katori. Toren could just see her at the edge of the lamplight, with her back to her door frame and her cap pulled down. She was turning something over in her fingers too.

A slab of grey stone. Bigger than Rook's. The same way. Without looking.

Toren sat up.

---

Katori's hand stopped.

He saw it stop. He saw her head come up, very slowly, and turn. She looked down the row at the step of eleven. At the man on it.

Rook had the stone flat on his palm now, the way you hold a thing up to see it. The lamp at the end of the row was behind him. Toren could see what Katori could see. The stone was water-grey. On its two long edges there were two places worn smooth and a little darker than the rest. A thumb and a first finger had gone there, for years and years.

Katori stood up.

She put her own slab away inside her coat and buttoned the coat over it. She walked down the row. She went past the dark door of twelve. She stopped on the wet concrete a pace in front of the step of eleven, in the edge of the lamplight, with her hands at her sides.

Rook looked up.

Toren did not move. He did not breathe. He was in a doorway behind a man's back and it was not his, and he could not have looked away.

Katori was looking at the stone on Rook's palm.

"Where did you get that," she said.

---

Rook did not close his hand. Toren saw that. He did not put it away. He sat on his step with the stone flat on his palm and looked up at the small woman in front of him, a head shorter than most people, standing the way a fence post stands.

Something went across his face. Toren could only see the side of it. It was not surprise. It was the look of a man who has heard a voice in the next room that he had stopped expecting to hear anywhere.

"Off a floor," said Rook at last. "A long time ago."

Katori said a name.

It was one word. She said it quietly and without any weight, the way you say a thing you have not said out loud in a very long time, and have kept in the same place all that time so as to know where it was.

"Vera," said Katori.

Rook shut his eyes.

"Yes," he said.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Down the row, a stove ticked. The lamp stood at the end of the row.

"She had small hands," said Katori.

"She did." Rook opened his eyes. "I've had to hold it wrong."

"May I."

He did not answer. He only held his hand out a little further, flat, with the stone on it. The way you hold something out to a horse you are not sure will take it.

Katori took it.

She took it between her thumb and her first finger. Her thumb went to one of the two worn places on the long edge. Her finger went to the other. They went there by themselves, the way a foot goes into its own print in the mud. The stone lay in her small hand as if it had been cut for it.

Rook watched it sit there.

Toren could see his face now, a little. He saw it do something he would not have known how to describe. He did not try. He did not ever tell anybody he had seen it, and when Dessa asked him years afterward he said he had been asleep.

Katori held it for about three seconds. She did not look at it. Three seconds was about as long as either of them could stand.

"She was my sister," said Katori.

"I'm Rook," said Rook.

---

That was all he said for a while. Then:

"She talked about her people. She said they'd kept words. Their own words, for things, that nobody else had." He was looking at the stone in Katori's hand. "She never said she had a sister."

"She wouldn't have," said Katori. "She was the best of us. She'd not have wasted a word on the rest." Her voice did not change. "She was unbearable about it."

Rook made a sound. It was not a laugh. It was the place where a laugh would have been.

"She was," he said. "She was completely unbearable."

They were quiet. Then Rook said, very carefully, like a man putting something heavy down on a floor that might not take it:

"Her people gave the stone and the thing it's for the same name. The same word, on purpose. She told me so. In eleven years I've never met anybody who could tell me why."

Katori looked at him a long moment.

"I can tell you why," she said.

He waited.

"Not tonight," said Katori.

"No." He let out a long breath. "No. Not tonight."

---

She gave it back. She did it slowly. She held it out flat on her own palm the way he had held it out to her. He took it, and his hand closed round it the way it had learned to. Too big. Going round.

"You carried it," said Katori.

"Every day."

"Keep carrying it."

He looked up at her.

"I'm going to want to talk to you," said Katori. "I don't know when. Not soon. I've been holding this very hard for a long time, and I don't know how to put it down yet. I'm not going to learn it tonight on a step." She stood with her hands at her sides. "But I'm going to want to."

"I'm here four seasons," said Rook. "I signed."

Katori made a small sound. It was nearly a laugh too.

"So I've heard," she said.

She turned and walked away. She did not walk back to fourteen. She walked past it, past the doorway where Toren could see Callie sitting with her knees up, and round the end of the hut into the dark between the rows, where the lamp did not reach.

---

Rook sat on his step with the stone shut in his hand.

After a while he said, without turning round, "You're awake."

"Yes," said Toren.

"You heard."

"Yes."

Rook was quiet.

"She'll want somebody," he said. "Not me. Not tonight. Somebody who doesn't need anything from her." He was looking at the dark where Katori had gone. "It's not you either, Tor. You're mine. She'd see me on you."

Toren looked down the row. Callie had got up from her doorway. She stood there a moment, looking at the dark between the rows. Then she went round the end of the hut after Katori, slowly, one hand on her ribs.

"There," said Rook. "That's the one."

He did not get up. He sat on the step with Vera's stone in his fist and his back very straight, and Toren sat on his bed behind him in the doorway and did not say anything at all. There was nothing he could have said that would not have been his own, and none of it was his.

After a long time, Rook opened his hand and looked at the stone.

"Her sister," he said to it, very quietly. "Eleven years. Two doors down."

Then he put it away in his coat, and sat there until the lamp went out.

# Chapter 7 — Callie: Hand Before Lamp

Katori was sitting on the ground behind hut fourteen with her back against the concrete wall. Her knees were up and her cap was pulled down over her eyes.

Callie sat down beside her, a little apart. She did it slowly, the way she did everything now, with one hand on her ribs. She put her back against the wall and did not say anything.

They sat there a long time.

"You saw me," said Katori at last. She did not lift her cap. "At the tailboard. On the ninth day. Before dawn."

"Yes."

"I thought you had. You were against the wheel." Katori was quiet. "You never asked."

"You didn't want me to."

"No," said Katori. "I didn't."

On the far side of the wall the stove ticked. Callie could hear her mother breathing through the concrete, very faintly. In and out.

"She went away when she was young," said Katori. "To a place that paid for things we needed. A doctor twice a year, and medicine, and a debt that stood. She didn't come back. Nobody ever came to tell us how." She said it the way Pell said a distance. Flat. So true it was almost dull. "Eleven years. That's all. That's the whole of what I'm going to say tonight."

"All right."

"I've never said even that much to anybody. Not Pell. Not Ines." Katori's hand moved on her knee. "I've been carrying it very hard."

"I know," said Callie. "You said. On the hill."

---

"You said something on the hill too," said Katori. "You said you can stop holding a thing and it goes on being held."

"I said it about a hook."

"You did." Katori lifted her cap at last and looked at the dark. "He's been holding it eleven years. Every day. Wrong, because his hand's too big." Her voice did not change. "It fitted, Callie. It went into my hand like it was cut for it."

"I saw."

"I know you saw. I'm glad it was you."

They sat a while longer.

"I'm not leaving this station while that man's in it," said Katori. "And I can't say one more sentence to him. Not yet. Maybe not for a long time." She let out a breath. "That's a stupid place to be."

"Then stay till you can."

"That's the winter."

"It was the winter anyway," said Callie. "You said so this afternoon. The crates stay under the net. Pell says nobody goes out on the high road after the frost. He says it like a law."

Katori laughed. It was a small tired sound in the dark. It was the first time Callie had ever heard her laugh all the way through.

---

"He needs lifters," said Katori, after a while. She said it slowly, the way she said things she was working out as she went. "Behind his line. When he can stand up straight. Somebody holds the road shut, and people behind him lift what comes through before it scatters. People who know how. People who don't put grit in it."

Callie said nothing.

"I've got a crew that lifts," said Katori. "Ines can count anything. Pell can write it down so nobody at any window can say it isn't so. Otto learned from the best there is, on the high plain, by starlight." She was looking at the dark. "And the best lifter I've ever seen is thirteen. And it's her call."

"My mother says I don't sign anything."

"Lifting's not signing. It's wages. A day's work and a day's pay, and you go home to a hut with a stove. It's what you've done every day since you were eleven." Katori turned her head. "I'm not asking you to hold any road. I'm not asking you to put anything in that hook for anybody. That's yours. The man in the long coat can ask for it, and you can tell him what you like."

"He says most people don't say no."

"He does."

"I haven't decided if I'm most people," said Callie.

Katori looked at her a long moment.

"No," she said. "You haven't. That's the best thing I've heard said in this station."

Callie thought about it. She thought about a stretch of road where things came through, and a man with a hole in his back holding it shut. She thought about people on their knees behind him in the ash. She thought about her mother, asleep on the other side of this wall, who had said *don't*.

"I'll look," she said. "When he starts. I'll come with you and look at his road. And I'll read it." She stopped. "That's all. I'll read the ground. That's the first line."

"That's the first line," Katori agreed.

---

When Callie went back in, her mother was awake.

She was lying in the bed by the stove with her eyes open. The blanket was over the window, the way Hallet had said. Stove light was on her face.

"Katori?" she said.

"She's all right. She's sitting outside."

"Something's happened."

"Yes."

"Is it yours to tell?"

Callie thought about it.

"No," she said.

"Then don't," said her mother, and shut her eyes. "Come and sit down. My feet are cold, and there's a girl in this hut with warm hands and nothing to do with them."

Callie sat on the floor with her back against the bed frame. She put her hand under the blanket and found her mother's right foot, the one that would not take instruction, and held it. It was cold. She held it until it was not.

The others came in one by one. Pell to the floor by the door. Ines to the bed nearest it, with her bow. Katori last, very quietly. She took her cap off and lay down on her bed with her face to the wall and her coat still buttoned.

The stove ticked down. The room went dark except for the red line through the grate.

---

Her mother's breathing changed as she went to sleep. It went slower and deeper, the way it always had. On the barrow in the road. On the wagon bed on the plain. In the white bed in the long room with the bag on the hook.

Callie heard it.

And she began to count. She did not decide to. It began the way it always had, under everything, the way her heart began. *One.* In and out. *Two.*

She stopped.

She did not count the third one. She let it go past. And the fourth.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done. It was harder than the causeway, or the span, or taking her hand off a hook with a wagon on it. Every part of her wanted to count. It was the only thing she knew how to do.

She did not count.

Her mother went on breathing. In and out, slow and deep, in a real bed under a real ceiling. She had been breathing before Callie started counting. She would go on without her. It did not need Callie to count it. It never once had.

Callie held on to her foot with both hands, and listened, and let it be held.

Then she went down into herself, the way she always did, to see.

There were two fingers of water in the bottom of the well. Cold and clear. She had not put them there. They had come back on their own, slowly, while she was busy with other things.

She did not use them. There was nothing here to use them on.

The first morning in the hut, her mother woke without a headache.

She lay very still a long time, as if she did not trust it.

"It's gone," she said, when Callie sat up. "The head. Behind the eye. I woke up and waited for it, the way you wait for somebody who's always late. And it didn't come."

"It's the pills."

"I know it's the pills. The little man said it would be. I'm not stupid." Her mother looked at the blanket over the window. "I just didn't believe him. I thought he was being kind." She was quiet. "He isn't kind. He's something better. He's right."

Her right hand still lay on the blanket like a glove. Her right leg still would not hold her. Going in for the pills at the eighth hour took Callie and Otto and the barrow, and her mother said a word on the step that Callie had not known she knew. But she said it clearly, and it was the right word, and she laughed after.

The third night, she had a fit.

It was not a big one. That was the first thing Callie thought, afterward, and she hated herself a little for thinking it.

Her mother was sitting up by the stove with a cup of tea in her left hand. Pell was telling her about the stable man and Duchess's leg. In the middle of a sentence her mother stopped listening. Callie saw it happen. Her eyes went somewhere else. The cup tipped.

Callie was across the hut before the tea hit the floor.

She took the cup. She got her mother's head. Katori was there on the other side, and Ines, and nobody said anything, because everybody in fourteen had done this on the plain.

And Callie counted.

She counted out loud, the way she had on the wagon. She counted because the little man had asked her to. *You'll need somebody with you who knows the fits.* He needed the number. The number was medicine. It was the one kind of counting that was for somebody else.

*Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty. Forty-one.*

It stopped at forty-one.

Her mother came back slowly, the way she always came back. She looked at the ceiling. She looked at Callie.

"How long," she said. Her voice was thick.

"Forty-one."

"That's short."

"It's short."

"Good." Her mother shut her eyes. "Put the—" She stopped. Her mouth moved. Callie watched her go looking for the word, the way you go looking for a tool in the dark. "The thing. For water. That you boil."

"The kettle."

"The kettle." Her mother opened her eyes. "Put the kettle on, then. I've spilled my tea."

"One fit," said Callie. "Forty-one seconds. At about the eighth hour of the evening. She lost a word after. Kettle. She got it back."

Sowerby wrote it down.

"Anything else?"

Callie looked at her mother, sitting on the edge of the bed in the long room with her sleeve rolled up.

She had done another sum in the night. She had lain on the floor by the stove and done it without meaning to. One fit in three days. Last month, three in a fortnight. The pills were doing what he said. The swelling was going down. And the thing under the swelling was still there, doing whatever it did, at whatever speed it did it.

She had started to work out what that meant.

She had stopped herself at the second step. She had lain on the floor with her eyes open until she was sure she had stopped.

"No," said Callie. "That's all."

Her mother looked at her from the edge of the bed. She did not say anything. After a moment she put out her left hand and took hold of Callie's wrist, and held it for about three seconds, and let go.

Rook started on the ninth day.

Callie went with Katori's crew at first light, the way she had said she would. She had the hook on her shoulder. She had not decided anything else.

The Sag was where the north road went down into its dip before the culvert. Callie had come through it in the dark on the night of the hounds, going out fast and coming back slow. She had not looked at it properly. Now she did.

It was a long shallow bowl of road, three hundred paces from lip to lip. The made surface broke up into slabs at the bottom, tilted, with black water in the joints. "It's never settled," said Pell, beside her. He had the atlas under his arm. "Not since 2130, the yard office says. Things come through here more than anywhere for forty miles. Nobody knows why."

Callie did not say anything. She was reading.

Something had come through in the night. Husks. A Handful's worth, maybe two. The yard's night crew had put them down at dawn, and the ash lay in the dip where they had come apart. It was in long grey drifts along the east track and in a smear down the black water of the west ditch.

That was not what Callie was reading.

She was reading the slabs.

Three of the joints at the bottom of the dip were open wider than the others. The edges were sharp. The black water in them had silt on top that had not settled. "The yard office says it's been worse since the summer," he said. "They say it like a weather report."

"It isn't weather," said Callie. "It's ground."

There was nothing to see. There was nothing to hear. The road went on past him, the same slabs and the same slumped shoulder, and the wind moved the dead grass on the bank. But there was a place across the whole width of the dip, about twenty feet north of Rook, where the ground stopped being *ground* to Callie. It was not that she could not read it. It was that there was nothing there for her to read. As if somebody had laid a clean sheet of paper across the road.

"That's a ward," she said.

"It's his," said Katori.

Callie looked at it a long time. It was the same thing as hers. It came off the same door. She knew that the way you know two tracks were made by the same animal. But hers hugged her body. It came off when she lifted her foot. His lay across a road as wide as a house.

She did not want it. She was not jealous. She only looked, and learned the shape of it, and put the shape away.

Jab was on the bank.

He had come on his own, a little after the crew. He had not signed anything. Otto said the man in the long coat had told him to come and look, and he had said he would look. That was all.

He stood above the west ditch with the fish-knife shape on his right fist. It was dark. No light on the edge. Just the metal, heavy in the belly, hooked at the end. He was standing very still.

Callie looked at where he had put himself.

The west shoulder had slumped worst of all. There was one place, halfway down the dip, where the bank came down to the ditch in a long easy slope, and anything that came up out of the ditch could walk straight onto the track. Everywhere else the bank was steep. That was the one door.

Jab was standing in it.

He was not standing over the lifters. He was standing where the lifters' backs would be, if something came. Where he could see all of them and the road north and the ditch at once.

The day holder came back after an hour and took the line.

Rook let his hands down. The clean sheet of paper went out of the road. He stood very still for a moment. Toren moved half a step closer to his left side and did not touch him.

He came down onto the dry stone of the east track and looked at the drift in the middle of it. "Flask," he said. "A fair one. And a Handful in the ditch, and it's a dirty Handful, and we'll be lucky."

He set the sack down. He took two jars out and worked their bottoms into the grit until they stood on their own. He drew both stoppers and laid them in the mouth of the sack where they would not roll.

"Who's first?"

"Her," said Katori.

Rook looked at Callie.

"Kneel down," he said. "Here. Beside me."

"Watch the hand," said Rook. He put his own hand flat on the track in the middle of the drift. The whole palm, heel down first, then the fingers laid after it, the way a man sets a plank. He pressed until the ash packed up under it and there was nothing between his skin and it.

"Flat," he said. "All of it flat. Fingers too. There."

Under his hand the grey dulled. It went on dulling, out from the heel in a circle that widened and slowed and stopped. He lifted his palm and moved it a span and set it down again, and the circle started over.

"Now you."

Callie put the hook down on the stone beside her. She put her hand flat on the drift.

She knew this. She had done it on the plain every day since she was eleven, off husk lines in the silt, two fingers and then the flat of the hand, like cream off milk. But she did it the way her grandfather had shown her. Quick. Pulling.

"Don't haul," said Rook. "It isn't rope. It comes at the rate it comes. Pulling at it doesn't get you more. It gets you less." He watched. "No. No. There. *There.* That's it."

She felt it come.

It was not like the plain. It was slow and heavy and cold, like water finding its way into a boot. It came up into the palm of her hand and lay there. It did not want anything. There was nothing in it to want.

"Hold it high," said Rook. "It'll go down through you if you let it. Then you've got the ache tomorrow, and the road's got the money." He moved his own hand a span further. "Nothing in it to want anything. That's the whole of why you can carry it at all. Nobody home. And you're the better hole."

---

They worked along the drift that way on their knees, a span at a time. What they lifted they held low and open, fingers apart, not closed.

Behind them Otto was working the other end of the track, carefully, with his tongue between his teeth. Ines was counting spans under her breath. Pell stood with the atlas and wrote.

When the track was done, Rook took his palm off the stone. Where they had worked, the drift had gone colourless at the edges, like a puddle drying. The wind came up the dip and took it.

He tipped the first jar's mouth against his palm and held it there. The ash went down out of him into the glass. It was grey again by the time it lay in the bottom.

"Now you," he said.

Callie tipped the second jar against her palm. It went out of her the same way. She watched it lie in the bottom of the glass. She had put a great deal of ash in a great many tins. She had never once watched it go.

Rook put a finger in his jar. He took it out and looked at what was on the finger, and rubbed it off between finger and thumb, slowly.

"Hand before lamp," he said. "Old saying and a true one. Nobody standing at a scale has ever once believed it." He turned the jar a quarter. "Clean. That's clean, and that's a fair Flask, and I'll say so in front of whoever's weighing."

He worked the stopper in with the heel of his palm. Then he stopped with his hand still on it.

"Thinner, though," he said. To the jar.

---

"It's thinner," said Callie.

Rook turned his head and looked at her.

"I lifted husks on the plain for two years," said Callie. "A Handful off a husk line comes up like wet sand. This came up like flour." She looked at her own jar. "It's the same weight in the glass. It isn't the same in the hand."

Rook looked at her a long time.

"I thought it was me," he said. "I thought I was old and my hand was going. It was thinner at the weighbridge last week and I said nothing then either." He set the jar down against the sack. "I'd sooner it was me."

"It isn't you," said Callie.

"No," said Rook. "I don't suppose it is."

He looked up the dip at the slab by marker post six. Callie had not told him about the slab. He looked at it anyway, for a long moment, and then he looked away.

---

The ditch had had most of the rest.

What was left lay along the black shallow in a smear, with the light still crawling through it. The smear was short now, and going.

Rook went down the bank into the water anyway. He put his knee in it and his hand on the bottom.

"This is the other lesson," he said. "The bad one."

His hand dragged.

"There it is," he said. "That's silt. That's four feet of ditch bottom coming up with it. It'll weigh dirty, and whoever's on the scale will write it dirty, and they'll be right." He moved the hand two inches. It dragged again. "Come on. Come *on*."

Up on the bank somebody moved.

It was only the day holder, shifting her feet at the south lip. Nothing else. But Rook's head turned toward it, fast, the way a head turns when it has spent eleven years turning toward things on banks.

He was a moment late with it. What he had not got yet went out from under his palm and down into the mud.

"No," he said.

He looked at the place where it had gone in.

"Thirty years," he said, "and you turned your head."

---

He came up the bank and put what he had into a third jar. It was not much. He stoppered it and set it beside the others. Then he sat down on the dry stone with his forearms across his knees and his hands hanging off the ends of them.

His hands were not steady.

He did not put them away. Callie saw that. He sat there with his hands shaking in front of everybody, and did not hide them, and did not say anything about them.

Toren came and sat on the stone beside him. He did not say anything either. He sat on Rook's left, on the side where the hole in his back was, and looked at the road.

Callie looked at her own hands. They were steady. There was ash in the creases of them, fine and grey.

She looked up at the bank. Jab was still standing in the one door, with his dark knife. He had not moved the whole time. He was not looking at Rook's hands. He was looking at the ditch, at the place the Handful had gone into the mud, the way you look at a thing you are going to remember.

"Flask and a fair one," said Rook, to nobody at all. "And a dirty Handful, less what the ditch got."

Pell wrote it down.

---

They walked back up through the north yard at the middle of the morning, the crew with the jars, and Rook very slowly with Toren beside him.

Callie walked at the back with the hook on her shoulder.

At the yard gate, the man in the long coat was standing with his hands behind his back. Callie did not know how long he had been there. He looked at the jars as they went past, and at Rook, and at Jab coming down off the bank with the dark knife gone back into the metal on his cord.

Then he looked at Callie.

He did not say anything. He only nodded, once, as if he had seen something he had come to see.

Callie did not nod back.

"I read the ground," she said, as she went past him. "That's all I did."

"I know," said the man in the long coat. "That's what I wrote down."

# Chapter 8 — Jab: Calibration

It woke him at the second hour.

He did not know at first what had woken him. Hut twelve was dark. The stove had gone down to a red line. Otto was asleep in the bed by the door, on his back, breathing like a big dog.

Jab lay still and listened. There was nothing to hear.

Then he felt it again. It was not a sound. It was under his breastbone, where it lived. It was not up and not down. It was a lean, the way a floor leans in an old house. Everything in him was sliding one way, very slightly, toward the north end of the station.

*Worse*, it said. That was all it ever said.

He knew what it was worth. Four times in five it was right. When it was wrong, it was wrong in the direction he wanted. So he had made a rule. He did not ask it when he wanted an answer.

He had not asked. It had come on its own.

He got up. He got his boots on in the dark. He did not wake Otto. He went out of the door and up the row, past the dark steps of eleven, and turned onto the main street.

The lamps were off. They had gone off at the first hour. He walked in the dark down the middle of the street, counting paces, and stepped wide of the loose grating between the second pole and the third without thinking about it.

---

Mrs Lask was at the desk. She looked up.

"I haven't sent for you," she said.

"I know," said Jab. "I'm going in anyway."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she looked down at her list.

"Fifth on the right," she said, as if he did not know. "The nurse is already with him. She came on at the first hour and went straight there. I'd like you to know I didn't send for you, and neither did she, and I'd like to know who did."

"Nobody," said Jab.

Mrs Lask wrote something on her list. He did not wait to see what.

---

The ward was dark except for a small lamp at the far end and another by the fifth bed on the right. The young nurse was there, the one who had stitched his arm on the bench. She had the blanket off Tonk's leg.

Tonk was awake. His face was red and wet. His eyes were too bright.

"Jab," he said. "The nails are *hot*."

Jab sat down in the chair.

"I know," he said. He did not know. He looked at the nurse.

"Fever," she said, low. "Came up about the twelfth hour. It's high. And this one's angry." She pointed without touching. The pin nearest the knee went in through a red place in the skin. The red had spread. There was a wet shine round the pin where there should not have been. "It might be only the pin. That happens. The skin gets in a temper round the metal and it'll clean up with medicine. Or it might be deeper."

"How do you tell?"

"Keel tells," said the nurse. "I've sent for her."

---

Tonk's hand found the cord. It was still tied to his wrist, the other end on Jab's. It had been there every night for eight nights now. Nobody had taken it off. The bell on its string hung from the bed rail, and Tonk had never once rung it.

He pulled the cord. It was a small pull. Jab was already there.

"I *pulled* it," said Tonk.

"I know. I'm here."

"You were *already* here."

"I came early," said Jab. "I had a feeling."

Tonk looked at him with his too-bright eyes.

"Is it the *thing*?" he said. "Your thing?"

Jab did not answer for a moment. Tonk had never asked about it before. He had not known Tonk knew there was a thing to ask about.

"Yes," he said. "It told me you were worse."

"I *am* worse," said Tonk, with the air of a man confirming a report. "It's right."

"It's right this time."

"Is it going to *fix* it?"

---

Jab looked at his brother's leg in its padded trough, with the four pins through it and the bar along the outside, and the red place at the top pin.

He had written it on the card. He had pressed hard with the pencil. *It does not touch his leg. It has never gone within a hand's breadth of the bone and it never will.*

He had written it because he was afraid. He knew that now. He had watched a woman with her sleeves rolled up take a dead piece of bone out of his brother, and set the leg straight, and bolt it. He had watched her do it with instruments and pictures and nine children's worth of knowing. He did not know anything. He had a thing that came when it liked and went out like a bucket going dry. If he put it into that leg and it did the wrong thing, he would not know it had done the wrong thing until it was too late to undo.

"No," he said. "It's not going to fix it. That's Keel's."

"Why?"

"Because I don't know what it does," said Jab. "And she does."

Tonk thought about that. His face was very hot.

"Can it do the *other*?" he said. "The warm one. From the wall."

Jab went still.

He had not known Tonk remembered the wall. Tonk had been going grey. He had not been looking at anything.

"I can try," said Jab. "It might not come. It doesn't come when I call it."

"Try *anyway*."

---

So he put his hand flat on his brother's chest.

Not the leg. Nowhere near the leg. The chest, the way he had at the culvert, over the small fast heart.

He did not push. He had learned that much. He did not reach, or lean on it, or ask it to run uphill. He sat there in the lamplight with his hand on his brother and let go of wanting it to do anything.

It was very hard. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything since the wall.

Nothing came.

Not nothing. He could feel the bottom of the bucket, and there was something in it. A little. More than there had been on the bench on the first night. It had come back while he was busy with other things, the way Callie said hers did. But it did not move. It lay at the bottom and did not come up, and he did not make it.

After a while he took his hand away.

"It didn't come," he said.

"No," said Tonk. He did not sound surprised. "It was warm, though. Your *hand* was."

"That's just a hand."

"It was warm," said Tonk, and shut his eyes. "That's allowed."

---

Doctor Keel came at the third hour with her sleeves already rolled and her hair flat on one side from a pillow.

She looked at the pin for eleven seconds. She put two fingers on Tonk's foot and watched the toes. She put the back of her hand on his forehead. Then she turned round.

"It might be the pin," she said. "It might be deeper. I'm not guessing, and I'm not opening him again on a guess." She said it fast and flat. "I want a picture at first light. Before I decide anything. If it's the pin, I clean it and change his medicine and we carry on. If it's the bone, I'm going in again, and it'll be today."

"And the two weeks?" said Jab.

"The two weeks still stand. This isn't the answer to that. This is a bad night." She looked at him. "Everybody gets one. I told you he'd hate us all at some point in the middle. It's started early. That's all."

"Are you going to do it *inside* again," said Tonk, with his eyes shut.

"Maybe," said Doctor Keel. "I'll tell you before I do. I won't sneak up on you."

"You *did* last time."

"I told you last time," said Keel. "You weren't listening. You were talking about a fish."

---

At first light they took him down the slope on the rolling bed.

Jab walked beside it with the cord still between them and the paper tray in his free hand. The metal lay in the tray on its cord. It was pale and clean from its second washing. He did not look at it.

At the bottom of the slope was the wide door and the yellow line.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

He untied the cord from his own wrist at the line. It was the first time. Every other time, he had untied it from the rail, or from Tonk's hand while Tonk slept. He untied it now from himself, and held the loose end out.

Tonk looked at it.

"You keep it," said Jab. "It's not metal. It can go in."

Tonk took the loose end and closed his hot fist on it. He did not let go of Jab's hand straight away. Last time he had. This time he held on first, hard, for about three seconds.

Then he let go.

The door shut like a lid.

---

Jab sat on the bench against the wall with the tray on his knees.

He heard wheels on the slope. He knew the sound of these wheels. It was the chair.

Toren was pushing it. His arm was still in the sling, and he was pushing one-handed, leaning his weight on the handle to steer. Senna was in it with a blanket over her knees and her hands in her lap. She was watching her hands.

"It's the eleventh day," said Senna, before anybody could say anything. "They told me the first hour. I'd like to know why it's always the first hour. Nothing good has ever happened to anybody at the first hour."

"The little lad's in first," said Jab. "They brought him down early. He had a bad night."

Senna looked at him. Then she looked at the door.

"Then I'll wait," she said. "I've a great deal of time, and nothing to be rude about till they call me."

Toren stopped the chair by the bench and sat down on the other end of it. He did not ask anything. He looked at the door, and at the tray on Jab's knees, and at Jab's empty wrist where the cord had been.

"The pin," said Jab. "Nearest the knee. It might be only the pin."

Toren nodded. He put his hand in his coat pocket and left it there.

---

Callie came down the slope a quarter of an hour after, with the hook on her shoulder.

"Otto came and banged on fourteen," she said. "He said you'd gone out at the second hour and not come back, and he'd gone up to the long building and Mrs Lask said *fifth on the right, and the picture at first light*." She looked at the door. "Mum's pills aren't till the eighth hour. Katori's got her."

She sat down on the floor with her back to the wall, because the bench was full, and laid the hook across her knees. She did not hold it hard.

Behind the wall, the knocking started.

It came in its long runs, fast and steady and mechanical. Jab had heard it twice before through this wall. Once for Tilda. Once for Tonk. He did not count it. He did not have to. He could see Callie's lips moving on the floor beside him.

*Eleven. Gap. Eleven.*

Then her lips stopped.

"That's ten," she said.

---

Nobody said anything.

The knocking went on. Callie's lips moved again.

"Eleven," she said quietly. "Eleven. Ten. Eleven." She looked up. "It's dropping one. Not every time. Every third run. Maybe every fourth."

"When your mother was in," said Jab, "was it—"

"Eleven every time," said Callie. "I counted all of it. I couldn't help it." She looked at the door. "It's not the same."

The knocking stopped.

It stopped in the middle of a run. There was no gap after it and no next run. There was only the wall, and the cool grey air, and nothing.

Toren had taken his hand out of his pocket. He was sitting forward with his elbow on his knee.

"Third row down," he said, very low, to nobody. "Fourth box along."

Jab turned his head.

"He told me," said Toren. "The man who runs it. A week ago. He ran the rack twice before he'd let it look at her. He said that box wakes up slow. He said it did it in the summer, and it was fine for a month after."

The knocking started again.

It stopped again.

---

Somebody came down the slope fast.

It was Doctor Sowerby. He came down it at something that was very nearly a run, with no folder under his arm, and he went past them to the heavy door without looking at any of them, and knocked on it with his fist.

"Nobody runs in here," said Senna, from the chair, to nobody. "I'd like that understood."

The door opened a hand's width. Jab saw the grey-haired man with the ink on his hand. The two of them spoke through the gap, too low to hear. Then Sowerby went in and the door shut.

Jab sat with the tray on his knees.

His brother was on the other side of that door, lying very still on a narrow bed through the middle of a pale ring, with a cord in his fist. The machine that was supposed to be looking through him had stopped looking. He could not see him. He could not hear him. He could not get to him.

He put his hand on his chest, over the place where the metal was not.

He did not ask. He only put it there.

"He's still," said Callie, from the floor.

Jab looked at her.

"I can't do what Dessa does," she said. "I don't know where he is. But he's four and he's frightened and he's in a loud room with nobody he knows. And nobody's run out. And nobody's shouted." She kept her eyes on the door. "If he'd moved, they'd have come out to tell you. They'd need you to make him lie still. They haven't come."

"You read that off the door."

"I read it off the not-coming," said Callie. "It's the same as grass. It's what didn't happen."

---

The knocking came back.

It came in long runs, and Callie's lips moved, and she did not say *ten*. It went on for a long time. Then it stopped at the end of a run, the way it was meant to.

The heavy door opened.

Tonk came out on the rolling bed with his eyes screwed shut and his face red and furious, and the cord in his fist. He opened his eyes when he crossed the yellow line and found Jab.

"It *broke*," he said.

"I heard."

"It stopped knocking and a man *swore* and then a different man came in and *they* swore." Tonk's voice was high with fever and outrage. "And then it went again. And then it stopped again. And they did it *three times*."

"You stayed still."

"I stayed *very* still," said Tonk. "I stayed still for *three* of them."

Doctor Keel was at the top of the slope. She must have been in the room at the back, behind the desk, where the picture came out. She came down to the line and looked at Tonk and then at Jab.

"It's the pin," she said. "Not the bone. I think."

"You think."

"*I think* is not a phrase I like," said Doctor Keel. "It's what I've got. The first two pictures were no use to anybody. The third one's a picture of a leg if you squint and you know what you're looking for, and I do." She put her hands in her pockets. "There's no collection in the bone that I can see. There's a temper round the top pin. I'll clean it this morning and change his medicine. He'll be hot for two days and cross for three."

"And the two weeks?"

"Six days from now," said Keel. "I'll need a picture for that. A proper one." She looked at the heavy door, and did not say anything else.

---

They could not take Senna in straight away.

The grey-haired man came out and stood in the doorway and looked at the old woman in the chair. His face had the look of somebody who has been awake since the first hour doing arithmetic.

"Give me a little while," he said to her. "I need to talk to the doctor. And then I'll need to warm it up twice. Maybe three times."

"I've had eleven days," said Senna. "I can have a little while."

He went back in. Sowerby came out after him and stood just inside the door with him, on the far side of the yellow line. Neither of them seemed to remember that the corridor had people in it. Or they remembered, and had decided it did not matter, because there was no curtain in the building worth the name.

Jab heard all of it.

"How long," said Sowerby.

"It's been drifting a month. It's been worse this week. Today I had to walk the rack four times to get one clean read." The grey-haired man rubbed the ink on his hand with his thumb. "I can correct it by hand. I've been doing that since Tuesday. Every run takes longer. Every run I'm less sure."

"How long can you correct it by hand?"

"I don't know. Weeks. Maybe less. It's not one box. It's one box that's worst. When one goes, the ones either side of it start working harder to agree with it."

"Is there a spare?"

The grey-haired man laughed. It was not a laugh.

"There's never been a spare," he said. "There's never been a second one of anything in that room. I've been through the depot list twice this month. Things go into that depot. Nothing comes out that fits."

Sowerby was quiet.

"Then we ration it," he said at last. "Cycles first. Every cycle, on the day. Pictures after. Only the pictures we can't do without."

"And the lad's two-week?"

"Is a picture we can't do without," said Sowerby. "So is the woman with the growth, if she fits badly. So is the next child who comes through that gate." He put his hand flat on the door frame. "Warm it up. She's waited long enough."

---

Toren had heard it too. Jab could see it in his face. *Cycles first.* That was Senna. *Pictures after.* That was Tonk, and Callie's mother, and whoever came next.

Toren was not looking at any of them. He was looking through the wide door at the back of the long room, where Jab could just see the edge of a steel rack with little green lights in it. One of them was not green.

Senna was looking at it too.

She had turned her chair a little, with her useless hands, by leaning. She was looking through the door at the rack with a face Jab had not seen on her. It was not frightened. It was the face of somebody recognising an old road.

"The last time I stood this close to one of those racks," she said, "I was fifty-three. And I'd come for the copper."

Toren's head came round.

"Gran?"

"It's nothing," said Senna. "It's an old woman in a chair saying things." She looked down at her hands in her lap, watching them. "There were rooms full of them. All in rows. With the little windows. We pulled the backs off and took the wire." She was quiet. "We didn't know what they were for. We only knew what they were made of."

Nobody said anything.

Then the grey-haired man came to the door and said, "Mrs Voss," and Toren got up and pushed her to the yellow line, and stopped there, because that was the rule.

---

She was in there two hours.

The three of them sat outside. Nobody sent them away. Tonk had gone up the slope with Keel and the nurse and his cord, to have his pin cleaned. He had told Jab to stay and *mind the old lady*, because she had been rude to the machine and it might be rude back.

So Jab stayed.

They did not talk much. Callie sat on the floor with the hook on her knees and did not count out loud. Jab could see her not counting. He could see it in her mouth, the way it stayed shut when the knocking came in its runs. He could see how much it cost her. He thought she was counting anyway, somewhere inside. He thought she was not letting it out, because out loud it would be for Toren to hear, and it was not hers to give him.

Toren sat with his hand in his pocket. It stayed there. He did not check. Jab had watched him do the checking at the culvert, on the road, in the ward. He had watched him stop. He did not know what had happened to make him stop, and he did not ask.

And Jab did the arithmetic, because his head would not do anything else.

Eleven days to Senna's next cycle. Six days to Tonk's picture. Three days to Callie's review, and to the card on the table in hut twelve that asked the three of them to a room on the second floor of the yard office. Four days to his own review. Four seasons of Rook's debt, starting now. Thirteen years of Tonk coming back through those doors to be measured.

And one machine.

He had known that. Sowerby had told him on the first morning, in a corridor. *There's one machine in a shed at the north end of this station that takes the picture. I don't know of another within four hundred miles.* He had heard it as a fact about Meridian. Like the lamps. Like the two gates.

He had not heard it as a thing that could stop.

---

Senna came out at the end of the second hour on the rolling bed, grey and small, with her eyes open.

"It sang wrong," she said. "I'd like that written down somewhere. It sang flat."

"Did it work?" said Toren.

"He says so." She looked at the ceiling of the corridor. "He looked at his screen for a very long time before he said so. And then he said *yes* to me, and *I think* to the doctor, when he thought I couldn't hear." She shut her eyes. "I've been lied to by better men. He's not good at it. I like him for that."

Toren took the chair handles. He did not push yet.

"Eleven days," he said.

"Eleven days," said Senna. "And I'll be here, and so will it." She did not open her eyes. "I've no intention of going anywhere, Tor. I've told everybody."

---

Tonk's fever came down a little by the evening.

Not all the way. Keel had said two days. But the red round the top pin had been cleaned and dressed, and the new medicine was going into him through the tube. He lay flat with the cord in his fist and the fish on his pillow and looked at the ceiling.

"Did the machine get cross?" he said.

"It got tired," said Jab.

"Machines don't get *tired*."

"This one does. It's very old."

Tonk thought about this.

"Like Rook," he said.

Jab opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"Yes," he said. "A bit like Rook."

"Rook's *got* a hole in him," said Tonk. "Wyck told me. Has the machine got a hole?"

"Sort of," said Jab. "There's a bit in the back that's gone wrong. And they haven't got another bit."

"Then they should *get* one," said Tonk, and went to sleep before Jab could tell him that there was nowhere to get one from.

---

He sat on the step of twelve that night.

He did not decide to. He came back from the ward at the tenth hour when they turned the visitors out, and he could not go in. Otto was inside, asleep, breathing like a dog. The stove was banked.

Two doors down, on the step of eleven, Toren was sitting with his back against the door frame.

On the step of fourteen, on the other side, Callie was sitting with her knees up.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The lamp at the end of the row put its flat white circle down on the wet concrete, the colour of bone.

"Third row down," said Toren at last. "Fourth box along. I looked at it through the door. The light in it wasn't green."

"What colour?" said Callie.

"Yellow. Like the line on the floor."

Callie was quiet.

"It dropped one every third run," she said. "At the end, when it was working. Before that, every other." She did not count anything else out loud. Jab heard her not do it.

"He can fix it by hand," said Jab. "For weeks, he said. Maybe less."

"Which," said Toren.

"He didn't know."

---

They sat on their three steps.

Jab thought about the card in hut twelve. *Requested at the Director's office.* All three of them, the same hour. He had thought it was about the north road. About the hounds, and the light, and people going out through a gate they had been told not to. Maybe it was.

He thought about the man in the long coat. *There's a column for it. It's a wide column and there's very little in it.* He thought about Rook on this same row, in the dusk, saying *I've done a thing that rhymes with it*.

He thought about Callie on the floor of the corridor, reading a door by what did not come out of it. About Toren sitting outside a room he could not go into, learning the rack by heart. About himself on a bench with a hand on his chest, not asking.

None of it could make a box in a rack turn green.

"There's no other one," said Callie. "Is there. Anywhere."

"Four hundred miles," said Jab. "Sowerby said. He's looked."

"Four hundred miles is only as far as he's looked," said Toren.

Neither of the others answered that. Jab did not think Toren had meant it to be answered. He thought Toren had said it the way you say a thing to find out whether it is true, and then heard it out loud, and did not know yet.

---

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour.

It did not flicker. It went, all at once, the way it did every night, for its written reason. The dark came down over the three steps and the row and the whole of Yard C.

Jab sat in it.

He could not see Toren now, or Callie. He knew they were there. He could hear the stove tick through the door of eleven. He could hear Callie shift on her step and settle again.

He thought about a squirrel, four hundred miles north, and a heap he could not get back to.

He had told Tonk he would find a different one. Somewhere in this place. To somebody. He had thought it would be a person when he found it. He had thought he would know it when he saw it.

He had not thought it might be a room.

He sat in the dark with his hand on his knee. Somewhere at the north end of the station, under a long low building with a turf roof, there was a pale ring standing on its edge in the dark, and a rack of little windows behind it. One of them was yellow. All three of them were held up by it, one way or another. Senna's marrow. Tonk's picture. Whatever Callie's mother would need, the next time a fit ran long.

It was older than anybody who ran it. It was the only one.

And it was starting to wake up slow.
