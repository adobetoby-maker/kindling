## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six to eight connected
chapters per movement (six by default), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |

# Chapter 8 — Jab: Calibration

It woke him at the second hour.

At first he could not tell what had woken him. Hut twelve was dark, the stove had gone down to a red line, and Otto was asleep in the bed by the door, on his back, breathing like a big dog.

Jab lay still and listened. There was nothing to hear.

Then he felt it again. It was not a sound but a lean under his breastbone, where it lived, neither up nor down, the way a floor leans in an old house. Everything in him was sliding one way, very slightly, toward the north end of the station.

*Worse*, it said. That was all it ever said.

He knew what it was worth. Four times in five it was right. When it was wrong, it was wrong in the direction he wanted. So he had made a rule. He did not ask it when he wanted an answer.

He had not asked. It had come on its own.

He got up and got his boots on in the dark without waking Otto, and went out of the door and up the row, past the dark step of eleven, and turned onto the main street.

The lamps were off. They had gone off at the first hour. He walked in the dark down the middle of the street, counting paces, and stepped wide of the loose grating between the second pole and the third without thinking about it.

---

Mrs Lask was at the desk. She looked up.

"I haven't sent for you," she said.

"I know," said Jab. "I'm going in anyway."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she looked down at her list.

"Fifth on the right," she said, as if he did not know. "The nurse is already with him. She came on at the first hour and went straight there. I didn't send for you, and neither did she. So who did?"

"Nobody," said Jab.

Mrs Lask wrote something on her list. He did not wait to see what.

---

The ward was dark except for a small lamp at the far end and another by the fifth bed on the right. The young nurse was there, the one who had stitched his arm on the bench. She had the blanket off Tonk's leg.

Tonk was awake. His face was red and wet. His eyes were too bright.

"Jab," he said. "The nails are *hot*."

Jab sat down in the chair.

"I know," he said. He did not know. He looked at the nurse.

"Fever," she said, low. "Came up about the twelfth hour. It's high. And this one's angry." She pointed without touching. The pin nearest the knee went in through a red place in the skin. The red had spread. There was a wet shine round the pin where there should not have been. "It might be only the pin. That happens. The skin gets in a temper round the metal and it'll clean up with medicine. Or it might be deeper."

"How do you tell?"

"Keel tells," said the nurse. "I've sent for her."

---

Tonk's hand found the long cord. It was still knotted round his wrist, and the other end was tied to the bed rail, the way Jab left it every night when the ward turned him out. Eight nights now. The bell on its string hung from the same rail, and Tonk had never once rung it.

Jab untied the rail end and knotted it round his own left wrist.

Tonk pulled the cord. It was a small pull. Jab was already there.

"I *pulled* it," said Tonk.

"I know. I'm here."

"You were *already* here."

"I came early," said Jab. "I had a feeling."

Tonk looked at him with his too-bright eyes.

"Is it the *thing*?" he said. "Your thing?"

Jab did not answer for a moment. Tonk had never asked about it before. He had not known Tonk knew there was a thing to ask about.

"Yes," he said. "It told me you were worse."

"I *am* worse," said Tonk, with the air of a man confirming a report. "It's right."

"It's right this time."

"Is it going to *fix* it?"

---

Jab looked at his brother's leg in its padded trough, with the four pins through it and the bar along the outside, and the red place at the top pin.

He had written it on the card. He had pressed hard with the pencil. *It does not touch his leg. It has never gone within a hand's breadth of the bone and it never will.*

He had written it because he was afraid. He knew that now. He had watched a woman with her sleeves rolled up take a dead piece of bone out of his brother, and set the leg straight, and bolt it. He had watched her do it with instruments and pictures and nine children's worth of knowing. He did not know anything. He had a thing that came when it liked and went out like a bucket going dry. If he put it into that leg and it did the wrong thing, he would not know it had done the wrong thing until it was too late to undo.

"No," he said. "It's not going to fix it. That's Keel's."

"Why?"

"Because I don't know what it does," said Jab. "And she does."

Tonk thought about that. His face was very hot.

"Can it do the *other*?" he said. "The warm one. From the wall."

Jab went still.

He had not known Tonk remembered the wall. Tonk had been going grey. He had not been looking at anything.

"I can try," said Jab. "It might not come. It doesn't come when I call it."

"Try *anyway*."

---

So he put his hand flat on his brother's chest.

Not the leg. Nowhere near the leg. The chest, the way he had at the culvert, over the small fast heart.

He did not push; he had learned that much. He did not reach, or lean on it, or ask it to run uphill. He sat there in the lamplight with his hand on his brother and let go of wanting it to do anything.

It was very hard. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything since the wall.

Nothing came.

Not nothing. He could feel the bottom of the bucket, and there was something in it. A little. More than there had been on the bench on the first night. It had come back while he was busy with other things. But it lay at the bottom without moving, and it would not come up, and he did not make it.

After a while he took his hand away.

"It didn't come," he said.

"No," said Tonk. He was not surprised. "It was warm, though. Your *hand* was."

"That's just a hand."

"It was warm," said Tonk, and shut his eyes. "That's allowed."

---

Doctor Keel came at the third hour with her sleeves already rolled and her hair flat on one side from a pillow.

She looked at the pin for eleven seconds. She put two fingers on Tonk's foot and watched the toes. She put the back of her hand on his forehead. Then she turned round.

"It might be the pin," she said. "It might be deeper. I'm not guessing, and I'm not opening him again on a guess." She said it fast and flat. "I want a picture at first light. Before I decide anything. If it's the pin, I clean it and change his medicine and we carry on. If it's the bone, I'm going in again, and it'll be today."

"And the two weeks?" said Jab.

"The two weeks still stand. This isn't the answer to that. This is a bad night." She looked at him. "Everybody gets one. I told you he'd hate us all at some point in the middle. It's started early. That's all."

"Are you going to do it *inside* again," said Tonk, with his eyes shut.

"Maybe," said Doctor Keel. "I'll tell you before I do. I won't sneak up on you."

"You *did* last time."

"I told you last time," said Keel. "You weren't listening. You were talking about a fish."

---

At first light they took him down the slope on the rolling bed.

Jab walked beside it with the long cord still running from his wrist to Tonk's, and the paper tray in his free hand. The metal lay in the tray on its short neck cord, pale and clean from its second washing. He kept his eyes off it.

At the bottom of the slope was the wide door and the yellow line.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

At the line he untied the long cord from his own wrist. Every night before this he had moved that end to the bed rail when they turned him out, so that Tonk was never tied to nothing. This time he held the loose end out.

Tonk looked at it.

"You keep it," said Jab. "It's not metal. It can go in."

Tonk took the loose end and closed his hot fist on it. He did not let go of Jab's hand straight away. Last time he had. This time he held on first, hard, for about three seconds.

Then he let go.

The door shut like a lid.

---

Jab sat on the bench against the wall with the tray on his knees.

He heard wheels on the slope. He knew the sound of these wheels. It was the chair.

Toren was pushing it. His arm was still in the sling, and he was pushing one-handed, leaning his weight on the handle to steer. Senna was in it with a blanket over her knees and her hands in her lap. She was watching her hands.

"It's the eleventh day," said Senna, before anybody could say anything. "They told me first light. Why is it always first light? Nothing good has ever happened to anybody at first light."

"The little lad's in first," said Jab. "They brought him down early. He had a bad night."

Senna looked at him. Then she looked at the door.

"Then I'll wait," she said. "I've a great deal of time, and nothing to be rude about till they call me."

Toren stopped the chair by the bench and sat down on the other end of it. He asked nothing. He looked at the door, and at the tray on Jab's knees, and at Jab's empty wrist where the cord had been.

"The pin," said Jab. "Nearest the knee. It might be only the pin."

Toren nodded. He put his hand in his coat pocket and left it there.

---

Callie came down the slope a quarter of an hour after, with the hook on her shoulder.

"Otto came and banged on fourteen," she said. "He said you'd gone out at the second hour and not come back, and he'd gone up to the long building and Mrs Lask said *fifth on the right, and the picture at first light*." She looked at the door. "Mum's pills aren't till the eighth hour. Katori's got her."

She sat down on the floor with her back to the wall, because the bench was full, and laid the hook across her knees. Her hands were loose on it.

Behind the wall, the knocking started.

It came in its long runs, fast and steady and mechanical. Jab had heard it twice before through this wall, once for Tilda and once for Tonk. He had no need to count it; he could see Callie's lips moving on the floor beside him.

*Eleven. Gap. Eleven.*

Then her lips stopped.

"That's ten," she said.

---

Nobody said anything.

The knocking went on. Callie's lips moved again.

"Eleven," she said quietly. "Eleven. Ten. Eleven." She looked up. "It's dropping one. Not every time. Every third run. Maybe every fourth."

"When your mother was in," said Jab, "was it—"

"Eleven every time," said Callie. "I counted all of it. I couldn't help it." She looked at the door. "It's not the same."

The knocking stopped.

It stopped in the middle of a run. There was no gap after it and no next run. There was only the wall, and the cool grey air, and nothing.

Toren had taken his hand out of his pocket. He was sitting forward with his elbow on his knee.

"Third row down," he said, very low, to nobody. "Fourth box along."

Jab turned his head.

"He told me," said Toren. "The man who runs it. A week ago. He ran the rack twice before he'd let it look at her. He said that box wakes up slow. He said it did it in the summer, and it was fine for a month after."

The knocking started again.

It stopped again.

---

Somebody came down the slope fast.

It was Doctor Sowerby. He came down it at something that was very nearly a run, with no folder under his arm, and he went past them to the heavy door without looking at any of them, and knocked on it with his fist.

"Nobody runs in here," said Senna, from the chair, to nobody. "I'd like that understood."

The door opened a hand's width. Jab saw the grey-haired man with the ink on his hand. The two of them spoke through the gap, too low to hear. Then Sowerby went in and the door shut.

Jab sat with the tray on his knees.

His brother was on the other side of that door, lying very still on a narrow bed through the middle of a pale ring, with a cord in his fist. The machine that was supposed to be looking through him had stopped looking. He could not see him. He could not hear him. He could not get to him.

He put his hand on his chest, over the place where the metal was not.

He did not ask. He only put it there.

"He's still," said Callie, from the floor.

Jab looked at her.

"I can't do what Dessa does," she said. "I don't know where he is. But he's four and he's frightened and he's in a loud room with nobody he knows. And nobody's run out. And nobody's shouted." She kept her eyes on the door. "If he'd moved, they'd have come out to tell you. They'd need you to make him lie still. They haven't come."

"You read that off the door."

"I read it off the not-coming," said Callie. "It's the same as grass. It's what didn't happen."

---

The knocking came back.

It came in long runs, and Callie's lips moved, and she did not say *ten*. It went on for a long time. Then it stopped at the end of a run, the way it was meant to.

The heavy door opened.

Tonk came out on the rolling bed with his eyes screwed shut and his face red and furious, and the cord in his fist. He opened his eyes when he crossed the yellow line and found Jab.

"It *broke*," he said.

"I heard."

"It stopped knocking and a man *swore* and then a different man came in and *they* swore." Tonk's voice was high with fever and outrage. "And then it went again. And then it stopped again. And they did it *three times*."

"You stayed still."

"I stayed *very* still," said Tonk. "I stayed still for *three* of them."

Doctor Keel was at the top of the slope. She must have been in the room at the back, behind the desk, where the picture came out. She came down to the line and looked at Tonk and then at Jab.

"It's the pin," she said. "Not the bone. I think."

"You think."

"*I think* is not a phrase I like," said Doctor Keel. "It's what I've got. The first two pictures were no use to anybody. The third one's a picture of a leg if you squint and you know what you're looking for, and I do." She put her hands in her pockets. "There's no collection in the bone that I can see. There's a temper round the top pin. I'll clean it this morning and change his medicine. He'll be hot for two days and cross for three."

"And the two weeks?"

"Six days from now," said Keel. "I'll need a picture for that. A proper one." She looked at the heavy door, and said nothing else.

---

They could not take Senna in straight away.

The grey-haired man came out and stood in the doorway and looked at the old woman in the chair. His face had the look of somebody who has been awake since the first hour doing arithmetic.

"Give me a little while," he said to her. "I need to talk to the doctor. And then I'll need to warm it up twice. Maybe three times."

"I've had eleven days," said Senna. "I can have a little while."

He went back in. Sowerby came out after him and stood just inside the door with him, on the far side of the yellow line. Neither of them seemed to remember that the corridor had people in it. Or they remembered, and had decided it did not matter, because there was no curtain in the building worth the name.

Jab heard all of it.

"How long," said Sowerby.

"It's been drifting a month. It's been worse this week. Today I had to walk the rack four times to get one clean read." The grey-haired man rubbed the ink on his hand with his thumb. "I can correct it by hand. I've been doing that since Tuesday. Every run takes longer. Every run I'm less sure."

"How long can you correct it by hand?"

"I don't know. Weeks. Maybe less. It's not one box. It's one box that's worst. When one goes, the ones either side of it start working harder to agree with it."

"Is there a spare?"

The grey-haired man laughed. It was not a laugh.

"There's never been a spare," he said. "There's never been a second one of anything in that room. I've been through the depot list twice this month. Things go into that depot. Nothing comes out that fits."

Sowerby was quiet.

"Then we ration it," he said at last. "Cycles first. Every cycle, on the day. Pictures after. Only the pictures we can't do without."

"And the lad's two-week?"

"Is a picture we can't do without," said Sowerby. "So is the woman with the growth, if she fits badly. So is the next child who comes through that gate." He put his hand flat on the door frame. "Warm it up. She's waited long enough."

---

Toren had heard it too. Jab could see it in his face. *Cycles first.* That was Senna. *Pictures after.* That was Tonk, and Callie's mother, and whoever came next.

Toren was not looking at any of them. He was looking through the wide door at the back of the long room, where Jab could just see the edge of a steel rack with little green lights in it. One of them was not green.

Senna was looking at it too.

She had turned her chair a little, with her useless hands, by leaning. She was looking through the door at the rack with a face Jab had not seen on her. It was not frightened. It was the face of somebody recognising an old road.

"The last time I stood this close to one of those racks," she said, "I was fifty-three. And I'd come for the copper."

Toren's head came round.

"Gran?"

"It's nothing," said Senna. "It's an old woman in a chair saying things." She looked down at her hands in her lap, watching them. "There were rooms full of them. All in rows. With the little windows. We pulled the backs off and took the wire." She was quiet. "We didn't know what they were for. We only knew what they were made of."

Nobody said anything.

Then the grey-haired man came to the door and said, "Mrs Voss," and Toren got up and pushed her to the yellow line, and stopped there, because that was the rule.

---

She was in there two hours.

The three of them sat outside. Nobody sent them away. Tonk had gone up the slope with Keel and the nurse and his cord, to have his pin cleaned. He had told Jab to stay and *mind the old lady*, because she had been rude to the machine and it might be rude back.

So Jab stayed.

They did not talk much. Callie sat on the floor with the hook on her knees and did not count out loud. Jab could see her not counting. He could see it in her mouth, the way it stayed shut when the knocking came in its runs. He could see how much it cost her. He thought she was counting anyway, somewhere inside. He thought she was not letting it out, because out loud it would be for Toren to hear, and it was not hers to give him.

Toren sat with his hand in his pocket, and it stayed there; he never checked. Jab had watched him do the checking at the culvert, on the road, in the ward. He had watched him stop. He did not know what had happened to make him stop, and he did not ask.

And Jab did the arithmetic, because his head would not do anything else.

Eleven days to Senna's next cycle, six to Tonk's picture, three to Callie's review and the Director's room on the second floor of the yard office, and four to his own review. Four seasons of Rook's debt, starting now. Thirteen years of Tonk coming back through those doors to be measured.

And one machine.

He had known that. Sowerby had told him on the first morning, in a corridor. *There's one machine in a shed at the north end of this station that takes the picture. I don't know of another within four hundred miles.* He had heard it as a fact about Meridian. Like the lamps. Like the two gates.

He had not heard it as a thing that could stop.

---

Senna came out at the end of the second hour on the rolling bed, grey and small, with her eyes open.

"It sang wrong," she said. "It sang flat. Somebody ought to know that."

"Did it work?" said Toren.

"He says so." She looked at the ceiling of the corridor. "He looked at his screen for a very long time before he said so. And then he said *yes* to me, and *I think* to the doctor, when he thought I couldn't hear." She shut her eyes. "I've been lied to by better men. He's not good at it. I like him for that."

Toren took the chair handles. He did not push yet.

"Eleven days," he said.

"Eleven days," said Senna. "And I'll be here, and so will it." She did not open her eyes. "I've no intention of going anywhere, Tor. I've told everybody."

---

Tonk's fever came down a little by the evening.

Not all the way. Keel had said two days. But the red round the top pin had been cleaned and dressed, and the new medicine was going into him through the tube. He lay flat with the cord in his fist and the fish on his pillow and looked at the ceiling.

"Did the machine get cross?" he said.

"It got tired," said Jab.

"Machines don't get *tired*."

"This one does. It's very old."

Tonk thought about this.

"Like Rook," he said.

Jab opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"Yes," he said. "A bit like Rook."

"Rook's *got* a hole in him," said Tonk. "Wyck told me. Has the machine got a hole?"

"Sort of," said Jab. "There's a bit in the back that's gone wrong. And they haven't got another bit."

"Then they should *get* one," said Tonk, and went to sleep before Jab could tell him that there was nowhere to get one from.

---

He sat on the step of twelve that night.

He had not decided to. He had come back from the ward at the tenth hour, when they turned the visitors out and he had tied the long cord's end to the bed rail, and he could not go in. Otto was inside, asleep, breathing like a dog. The stove was banked.

One door down, on the step of eleven, Toren was sitting with his back against the door frame.

On the step of fourteen, on the other side, Callie was sitting with her knees up.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The lamp at the end of the row put its flat white circle down on the wet concrete, the colour of bone.

"Third row down," said Toren at last. "Fourth box along. I looked at it through the door. The light in it wasn't green."

"What colour?" said Callie.

"Yellow. Like the line on the floor."

Callie was quiet.

"It dropped one every third run," she said. "At the end, when it was working. Before that, every other." She did not count anything else out loud. Jab heard her not do it.

"He can fix it by hand," said Jab. "For weeks, he said. Maybe less."

"Which," said Toren.

"He didn't know."

---

They sat on their three steps.

Jab thought about the card in hut twelve. *Requested at the Director's office.* All three of them, the same hour. He had thought it was about the north road. About the hounds, and the light, and people going out through a gate they had been told not to. Maybe it was.

He thought about the man in the long coat. *There's a line on the register for it. It's a long line with very little written on it.* He thought about Rook on this same row, in the dusk, saying *I've done a thing that rhymes with it*.

He thought about Callie on the floor of the corridor, reading a door by what did not come out of it. About Toren sitting outside a room he could not go into, learning the rack by heart. About himself on a bench with a hand on his chest, not asking.

None of it could make a box in a rack turn green.

"There's no other one," said Callie. "Is there. Anywhere."

"Four hundred miles," said Jab. "Sowerby said. He's looked."

"Four hundred miles is only as far as he's looked," said Toren.

Neither of the others answered that. Jab did not think Toren had meant it to be answered. He thought Toren had said it the way you say a thing to find out whether it is true, and then heard it out loud, and did not know yet.

---

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour.

It did not flicker. It went, all at once, the way it did every night, for its written reason. The dark came down over the three steps and the row and the whole of Yard C.

Jab sat in it.

He could not see Toren now, or Callie, but he knew they were there; he could hear the stove tick through the door of eleven, and Callie shift on her step and settle again.

He thought about a squirrel, four hundred miles north, and a heap he could not get back to.

He had told Tonk he would find a different one, somewhere in this place, for somebody, and he had thought it would be a person when he found it, and that he would know it when he saw it.

He had not thought it might be a room.

He sat in the dark with his hand on his knee. Somewhere at the north end of the station, under a long low building with a turf roof, there was a pale ring standing on its edge in the dark, and a rack of little windows behind it. One of them was yellow. All three of them were held up by it, one way or another. Senna's marrow. Tonk's picture. Whatever Callie's mother would need, the next time a fit ran long.

It was older than anybody who ran it. It was the only one.

And it was starting to wake up slow.

## 1. Verdict: the strongest Book Two engine

**The team survived the road. Meridian is where they find out that surviving
it wasn't the same as being safe — and that the only place any of them
actually gets better is back outside the wall, doing the work Meridian cannot
spare enough qualified adults to do.** Concretely: Meridian's Sag line is
worsening faster than its registered defenders can cover; a new and more
dangerous rip has opened near Caul Hill; and the one machine that keeps Senna's
marrow-support course calibrated—and gives Meridian its only advanced imaging
for cases such as Tilda's and Tonk's—needs a part from the irradiated plant
beside it. Reaching the part and securing the rip are one mission. The team must carry large stores of
graded ash because Sustain can recall active radiation from a living body only
while someone keeps paying for the work. That necessity turns three separated
arrivals into one company: Callie, Jab, and Toren go with Rook and Dee Wren,
while their coaches and cohort make the expedition possible.
Training, combat, ash economics, and found-family bonding all happen because
the mission must be prepared for, survived, and paid for. Institutional
politics supplies pressure and consequence. It does not replace the adventure.

- **Emotional promise:** each protagonist discovers that the cost of the road

## 3. The central outward mission

### One mission: the part and the rip (`OWNER`)

The former Options A and B are now one operation. Senna's treatment shed runs
on a single, pre-Fall machine at least seventy years old (`CANON.md`, Ch. 27–28), and a part in its
calibration train is failing. The only known compatible salvage is at Caul Hill
— the irradiated pre-Fall plant where Senna received her original exposure.
A new and more dangerous rip has opened at or beneath the same complex. It has
made the site harder to enter, but it has also left the one resource the team
cannot make the attempt without: deep stores of ownerless reserve-ash.

The objectives cannot be separated:

1. Carry enough clean, graded ash from Meridian to fund the approach and the
   first radiation-recall cycles. The team cannot count on ash it has not yet
   won.
2. Cross the contaminated approach while Rook, Jab, and Dee Wren
   rotate Sustain duty, recalling active radiological burden before it fixes
   into lasting tissue damage.
3. Reach the calibration assembly and recover the compatible part.
4. Contain or collapse the new rip, kill its new Riftspawn expression through
   a full-team action, and recover the resulting ash so the healers can bring
   everyone home.

The third former option — making the disk mystery the journey's reason — stays
retired. The disk receives one costly clue late in the book, not the book's
engine.

### Why it must leave Meridian

The shed's machine cannot be repaired from spare parts on hand; Meridian's own
hardened depot inventory has nothing compatible (a callback to Annice Fell's
ledger, Ch. 28: things go into that depot, they do not come out). The active
rip also cannot be ignored: if it grows, it changes the Sag from a strained
defense line into an open road for stronger Riftspawn. Caul Hill is roughly a
week's travel past the Sag's worst stretch — close enough to reach inside the
machine's failure clock, far enough to be a true expedition.

### Why adults cannot simply solve it

Two separate, compounding reasons, both institutional rather than a plot
contrivance:

1. **Every ordinary defender who could lead it is already spoken for.** Meridian's
   registered defenders — the people who can actually hold a road shut —
   are stretched thin covering the worsening Sag line itself (this is the
   book's own opening pressure). Pulling one of them off the wall for a
   week to escort a salvage run means a week of the Sag going unheld, which
   the Director's office will not authorize under any argument.
2. **The site selects for a very rare team, not for youth.** Caul Hill's
   radiation cannot be endured by general toughness or fast healing. Sustain
   can recall the *active burden* from tissue, but it cannot undo old damage
   after that damage has fixed. Rook and Jab can do the work; the Homura
   cross-medic understands how to direct it, rotate healers, and increase the
   amount of ash a Kindled soul can safely hold and spend. Callie and Toren
   supply the Ward, Guard, and extraction capability that keep those healers
   alive. The party is selected by complementary doors and training, not by
   the premise that children tolerate radiation better than adults.

### What failure costs

If the part is not recovered, Senna's and (per the same shed, same cycle)
every other patient's course begins to fail within roughly eleven days — the
same interval already established as the treatment's own clock (Ch. 27–28).
Failure is not abstract: it is a countable number of cycles before Senna's
marrow support lapses, stated on the page the way Pell states a distance.
Secondary cost: if the mission is caught leaving without authorization, the
whole clan's residency review is jeopardized, which threatens every household
riding on it (Callie's, Jab's, the Hobb's Wall clan's).

### How it joins the Sag / residency / ash / defender threads

The mission is staffed, provisioned, and made survivable using everything the
book's other threads have taught. The team carries large stores of lifted,
graded ash as fuel, not as trade cargo and not as a lucky find. Toren's Guard
and Callie's widened Ward protect the Sustain rotation. Jab senses changes in
the bodies under his care and learns to recall radiation deliberately. Rook
teaches him the lived technique; Dee teaches the repeatable
capacity discipline: take ash in, settle it without confusing it for personal
reserve, spend it through Sustain, vent the harmful load, and refill before
the soul bottoms out. The defender cohort covers the gap at Meridian while
the expedition is away. The ash secured at the rip then becomes the material
proof that the team can protect Meridian and alter the exploitative residency
economy, rather than simply argue against it.

## 6. Youth-defender cohort design

- **Purpose:** the Sag is worsening faster than Meridian's adult defenders
  can cover, and the station's own liability doctrine (registered Kindled
  residents are sorted into labor by door) already pulls Kindled teenagers
  toward dangerous work informally. The cohort formalizes that pull into
  something with adult oversight, rather than pretending it isn't happening.
- **Membership logic:** not "every Kindled kid at Meridian" — a small,
  specific group built from need and consent: Callie, Jab (if his household's
  situation allows; his instinct to keep working through the Elder's ethic
  makes this an easy yes for him and a harder one for the book to justify
  given Tonk), Toren, plus willing members of the Hobb's Wall clan (Dessa,
  Wyck) and at least one Meridian-native trainee to keep the cohort from
  reading as "the Book One cast plus scenery." Recommend one new named
  Meridian local, invented at drafting, who has no Book One history and
  therefore reacts to everything (the ash economy, the found-family's
  closeness, Rook's method) the way a reader coming in cold might.
- **Adult safeguards:** Dee Wren oversees formal reserve and Sustain drills; Rook
  oversees live Sag-line rotations; no cohort member holds a line alone
  without an adult in immediate support distance; injuries and overdraws are
  logged and reviewed, mirroring the station's own paperwork culture (a
  believable, in-world safeguard rather than an authorial promise the plot
  ignores).
- **Training progression:** shadowing → supervised jar-and-sack work behind
  an adult's held line → controlled short holds under direct supervision →
  full rotations with an adult in support range → the Caul Hill mission as
  the first expedition beyond Meridian's support perimeter. It is not an
  unsupervised youth test: Rook and Dee travel, while Katori and
  Pell divide field/navigation coaching and the defense of the people left
  behind.
- **Moral pressure:** the book should not resolve whether this is right. The
  Director's office is correct that the Sag needs more hands than it has;
  Pell and Senna's mother-thread (Callie's own mother's "you don't get to
  spend yourself on me") are correct that this trades children's bodies for
  a shortfall adults created. Both positions stay true at once. The named
  residency-injustice case (§3, §11) should be a cohort member's family —
  someone who took the ash-lifting-equivalent contract without a found
  family able to absorb the cost, exactly as both prior drafts already
  recommend — so the moral weight lands on a specific person, not a policy
  debate.
- **The first school without calling it one:** no uniforms, no ranked class
  structure, no shared dormitory-and-bell-schedule texture. Training happens
  in the same huts, the same stove-side conversations, and the same north
  yard the rest of the book already lives in. What *does* begin here is
  durable: three marked practice lanes, a shared equipment rack, and Dessa's
  append-only record of drills, injuries, reserve, recovery, and repeatable
  results. Every cohort member keeps a private want unrelated to combat
  (Callie's counting, Jab's caregiving, Toren's leadership anxiety, and
  whatever the new local recruit wants that has nothing to do with the Sag).

## 7. Ash-learning ladder

Grounded directly in the drafted Rook ash-collection scene
(`book-01-tallow-gate/book-02-drafts/movement-01-ash-collection.md`) and the
Ch. 27–28 / Ch. 18 "the jar" material — this ladder should be taught through
exactly that kind of scene, not through explanation.

1. **Recognition and collection.** Reading a drift's Yield by eye and feel
   before touching it (Flask vs. Handful, "a fair one" vs. "a dirty one," per
   the ash-collection draft); the flat-palm technique, working a span at a
   time, holding it low and open rather than closed. Failure mode: hauling —
   trying to pull ash faster than it comes, which yields less, not more.
   Teaching scene: Rook's drafted scene itself, adapted to include a cohort
   member learning alongside Wyck and Dessa rather than only overheard.
2. **Grading.** Handful / Flask / Barrel / Well (`CANON.md`, Yield) as a
   trade vocabulary, learned first as an insult-proof way to size up danger
   ("Handful or Flask?"), then as the literal unit the weighbridge pays in.
   Teaching scene: a weighbridge dispute (Ch. 18's "that's thinner than it
   ought to come" beat is the model) where a cohort member's read is
   challenged and has to be defended with evidence, not confidence.
3. **Cleaning and contamination.** Silt, ditch-water, and "a dirty Handful"
   as concrete hazards — contaminated ash burns unreliably and is priced
   down, or in the worst case damages whoever spends it (a training-safe
   version of an overdraw, introduced here in miniature). Teaching scene: a
   cohort member's first real weighbridge failure, graded down publicly, and
   having to learn why rather than being told the rule.
4. **Storage and carriage.** Jars, sacks, flasks, folded canvas between them
   (all attested in Ch. 18, 27, and the ash-collection draft); why quantity
   and grade both degrade under bad handling, and why a station's economy
   treats a jar of clean Flask as worth defending on the road.
5. **Taking it in — ash held by the soul.** Ash is ownerless, unlike a
   person's expended reserve. Skin-contact absorption is deliberate and
   unhurried: the same flat-palm stillness used to collect it, turned inward.
   Ash does not become a second organ or named storage tank; the soul holds
   it as usable reserve. Rushing intake causes spill, nausea, false fullness,
   or a lock that prevents clean spending.
6. **Increasing usable working depth.** Dee teaches that a
   Kindled soul does not become metaphysically larger in a week; the person
   learns to use more of the depth already present. Intake, settle, spend,
   vent, rest, repeat. Capacity is measured by clean cycles and recovery, not
   by how much ash someone can force in once. This is the training that makes
   the Caul Hill reserve practical instead of merely heavy cargo.
7. **Radiation recall through Sustain.** Rook teaches Jab to feel the active
   harmful burden in a body without confusing it with old injury. Sustain
   draws or recalls that burden while ash pays for the work. The technique
   prevents further damage during exposure; it does not erase damage already
   fixed in tissue and therefore does not retroactively cure Senna. The
   healer must vent the recalled burden and rotate out before it begins to
   lodge in the healer instead.
8. **Spending through a door under pressure.** Absorbed ash can power Edge,
   Stride, Ward, Sustain, or Resilience without first emptying personal
   reserve. Teaching scene: a controlled Sag-line failure where ash-fuel,
   not personal endurance, is the difference between holding and breaking.
9. **Advanced team use and overdraw.** The expedition uses a measured
   rotation rather than a vague shared pool: one person works, one holds the
   reserve, one watches for spill and overdraw. Spending past safe working
   depth causes visible damage even when ash remains available. This is why
   the party needs stores, trained bodies, and discipline rather than one
   miraculous healer.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

## Science fiction

## Learning discipline

- One edit is an example, not a universal law.
- Promote a broad instinct only when several accepted edits support the same
  reader-facing preference, or when the owner states the preference directly.
- Do not learn voice from continuity fixes, factual corrections, typographical
  repairs, rejected suggestions, reverted edits, or untouched AI prose.
