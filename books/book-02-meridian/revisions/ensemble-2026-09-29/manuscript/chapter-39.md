# Chapter 39 — The Point

The light Toren asked for was at the very end of the spike, a finger's length of it, and nowhere else.

He did not light the heel. He had nothing to light the heel with and the point both, and the heel was for hooking, and there was nothing left to hook. He asked it of his own, because the stone was gone, and he felt the draw behind his breastbone, thin, like the last of the water going out of a barrel through a pinhole.

It lit: a finger of white-grey at the end of the ugly crooked thing on his fist.

Everything else in the room was where it was.

The lifter lay against the curve of Callie's hook on the threshold, knee-high, grey. Toren was holding nothing now. It had to be one thing at a time, because there was only one of him. Jab crouched beside the lifter with his right palm flat on the edge of the grey, a hand from the light, and Dee crouched at Jab's back with the fourth jar against his left palm, under the line, and the glass running in her other hand. The husk Jab had killed was a heap on the floor by the cabinets, and it had not stood up again. In the doorway Callie stood with her back to the room, with the lee standing out into the corridor off the curve, and the lifter leaning on the room side of the same steel a foot from her heels.

The light was open. It was a line along the top of the lifter, the colour of the bottom of a pond, as wide as two fingers now. It had opened at the three and had not shut, because it had nothing to shut with, and Jab was taking what came out of it.

Toren could see all of them. He could not see the lee from where he stood, but he could see Callie's shoulders, and they had taken the lean when it came and had not moved since. He could see Jab's palm, held flat, and his face, which was very still. It was the face Toren had seen on the step of eleven, when Jab put his hand on a drift in his sleep. He had been able to see all of them since they came in. It was the only thing he had been able to do the whole time that was not holding.

"Six," said Dee, very low. "Seven."

The ten was coming. Jab would take his hand off at the ten. He had said so.

Toren put the point in.

He did not stab. He put it in the way you put a knife into a loaf you are cutting at the table, point first, down, into the line of light, a hand from Jab's palm. He put it in slowly, because there was nothing to be quick for. There was no hide and no bone. There was only grey, and light.

The point went into the light, and the light went out.

It went out the way a lamp goes out when you pinch the wick, all at once, with nothing after it. There was a sound. Afterward Toren did not know whether it had been a sound. It was more like the air in the room changing, the way the air in a house changes when somebody who was in it has gone out and shut the door.

Then the lifter came apart.

It came apart the way husks came apart, all at once, into grey. But it was not a husk. It was all of it. The whole long low hump went, from the end that lay against Callie's curve back to where it had lain over the carrier, and it did not burst, because there was no wind. It fell. It fell in on itself like a heap of flour when you take away the board that was holding it up, and it spread out across the floor, over the carrier and the boxes and the concrete, and over Jab's boots and Toren's. It lay there in a long low slope of grey, knee-deep in the middle, loose.

Callie's curve had nothing against it now.

Toren let the point go dark. At the end. Not after.

"Off," said Jab. He had taken his hand away. He held it up, open, with a palm's worth of grey in it, and turned, and Dee had the last vent jar out, and he tipped the grey into it. "It's off."

"Ten," said Dee.

They counted. For ten breaths nothing moved. The grey lay on the floor where it had fallen and did not draw out into threads. It did not go anywhere. It lay there, loose, and was only grey.

Three.

Nothing.

---

Toren looked at the ropes.

The stumps of the arm-ropes had been sticking out of the hump, and they were gone; they had fallen with it. The long end of the first arm-rope lay by the door where he had cut it, and it was not a rope now. It was a line of grey on the concrete in the shape of a rope, lying there the way a snake's skin lies. The strands at the foot of the racks were lines of grey. The rope that came down the wall from the hole was a streak of grey down the concrete.

"The corridor," he said. "Callie. The corridor."

Callie did not turn round. She did not need to. She was looking at it.

"It's gone," she said. Her voice was thick and flat. "The big one. It's down. It's not a rope. It's a bank of grey along the wall." She was quiet. "The tray's full of grey. It's not going anywhere. The ridge against the lee isn't going anywhere." She breathed. "The husk's come apart. The one on the outside. It just came apart. Nobody touched it."

Jab had gone down onto his knees when the lifter fell, and he did not seem to know it.

"They were its hands," he said.

Nobody answered him.

Toren looked at the floor of the room: at the long slope of grey that had been the lifter, and at the carrier's iron handle sticking up out of it.

"It's dead," he said. He did not know if that was the word. There had been nobody in it to die. "It's stopped."

"It's stopped," said Dee.

"I'm at my floor," said Callie.

She said it from the door, flat, with her back to them. Her voice had the thick sound of blood in it, and her hands on the conduit were white to the wrists.

"I'm at my floor. I'm stopping."

Toren turned.

The lee went out first, beyond her in the corridor. The ridge of grey that had been heaped against its outside slid in over the threshold in a slow soft spill, round the curve and over it, and lay there with the rest. But the curve itself was still pale. The last of her was still in it. She had not got that far yet.

Then her legs went.

They went the way they had gone at the mouth of the cut at the wash, all at once, without asking her. But she was not standing at the mouth of a cut with a wall behind her. She was standing in a doorway with the hook in her hands and the conduit going down in front of her to the curve on the threshold, and the Ward was still in the steel when her knees went. It was still fixed.

She went forward onto it.

She went down onto the conduit of her own hook, with the steel still fixed in the air in front of her like an iron bar set in a wall. It took her across the ribs on the left side, the side she had walked into it on the forty-fourth. She made a sound. It was not loud.

Then the Ward went out of the steel, because she had nothing left to keep it there, and the hook went down onto the threshold with a clang, and she went down on her knees beside it with her arms across her side.

"Callie," said Toren.

"Don't," said Callie. She was on her knees. Nobody sits down inside the fence, and she had not sat. "Don't touch it." She breathed out through her teeth, carefully, the way you let a rope out. "That's cracked."

"Same place?" said Dee. She had not moved from Jab's back. She could not.

"Same place," said Callie. "It's cracked this time. I heard it."

---

Dee measured them where they were.

She did it standing, because she would not kneel, with two fingers on each wrist in turn. She did Jab first. She did Callie on her knees in the doorway, with Callie's arms still across her side. She did Toren.

"Your own's at your floor," she said to him. "Near enough. A hair over. You've nothing to light with."

"I know."

"You held two struts, and then that thing on one," said Dee. "On your own, the last of it. And lit the point after. I watched you do it."

She did not say that it had been right. She did not need to, and Toren did not need her to.

"Callie's at her floor," said Dee. "Not under. Jab's a hand. His floor's where it was." She stopped. She had not done her own.

She did it now. She put two fingers on the inside of her own left wrist, the crooked one, and looked at nothing.

They watched her face. They had watched it over the jar and the glass and Jab's back the whole fight, and it had been white and still and absolutely steady. It was still steady.

"I'm at my floor," said Dee.

Nobody said anything.

"I've been the watch," said Dee. "The whole time. Every time he came off, I took his hands. Nineteen times. There's nobody to take mine." She took her fingers off her wrist. "My stone went at the tenth, near enough. I've been on my own since then."

"A healer at their floor," said Jab.

"Once," said Dee. "That's once. Twice, and it's everybody out." She looked at the glass in her fist. "And a healer lost, and it's everybody out that moment. I'm not lost. I'm at my floor." She looked at Jab. "But I'll not be drawing anybody. Not here. Not today. You're the only healer in this room now. And nobody can draw you." She looked at the open jar in her hand. "Jar four's at five. Three under the line, inside the two turns. That's what it cost. That's what gets written."

"Then we go," said Toren.

"We go," said Dee. "Twenty turns of the glass, from now." She turned the glass. "That's the price of the box, and whatever's on this floor. Twenty. And then everybody out, finished or not."

"The box," said Toren.

He went to the carrier. He did not go through the grey. He went round it, along the foot of the cabinets, to where the iron handle stuck up out of the slope, and crouched there. He could see the lead lid under the grey, in its groove, and he could see where the grey lay over it a hand deep.

"Jab," he said.

"I know," said Jab.

He came along the cabinets on his knees with his right hand held out. He had not got up since the lifter came apart, and Toren did not think he had noticed.

"It's cold," said Jab.

He had put his palm an inch off the grey on the carrier's lid, the way he put it over a patient, and he had his eyes shut.

"It's cold," he said again. "Most of it. It's the cold with nobody in it." He moved his hand along, an inch off. "There's warm in it. Grit. The hill. Here, and here. Not much." He opened his eyes. "Most of it's clean. It pushed the hill out at us the whole fight. What it had left was what it was made of."

"How much?" said Dee.

Jab looked along the slope: from the carrier to the threshold, and from the boxes to the middle of the floor, knee-deep in the middle and thinning out.

"More than I've ever seen," he said. "In one place."

"More than we can lift," said Dee.

"More than we can lift in twenty," said Jab.

"Then it's what gets us back over the line," said Dee. "The fence is at thirteen. That's under. What we lift goes in the empties, and what's in a jar counts. Three turns of lifting puts us back over sixteen. If it doesn't, we go at the third, with what's in the jars." She did not look at Toren. "It doesn't make the crossing right. It's the same once, and it goes on the paper with the rest."

---

They lifted.

There was no other word for it, and Toren would not have used one. It was the east track, and it was Rook's drift, and it was the old north road on Callie's first morning with her grandfather's flat palm. It was the most ordinary work any of them knew, done on the floor of a room under a hill, beside a thing that had tried to fill Jab's hand with the whole of the world.

The empties were in the frame on the cabinet top: the three fence jars that had been opened that morning and the day before, and the two that had been empty since the cut at the wash. Five. Dee lifted them out one at a time and pulled the stoppers and stood them in a row on the cabinet top.

"Nobody kneels," said Dee. "Jab. Up off your knees. You crouch. You don't put a knee down."

Jab looked down at himself as if the knees were somebody else's, and got up onto his heels.

"I'm on my knees," said Callie, from the door.

"You're hurt," said Dee. "You're allowed your knees. You're not allowed to lift."

"I can lift," said Callie.

She came across the floor on her knees with one arm across her side, to the edge of the slope of grey by the door, where the ridge had spilled in over the threshold. She put her right hand flat on it.

"I've been lifting since I was eleven," she said. "Off husk lines, in the silt. Lifting's what I'm for." She did not look at Dee. "It doesn't come out of me. It comes up into my hand. I don't need a stone for it."

Dee looked at her for as long as it took to breathe in.

"Slowly," she said. "And off it when I say. And you'll not bend."

"I'll not bend."

So Callie lifted on her knees by the door, with her back straight and her arm across her side and her right palm flat on the grey. Jab lifted by the carrier, crouched, with his palm an inch off first every time, feeling for the cold, and then down on it; he took only where it was cold, and moved his hand wherever it was warm. Toren lifted one-handed at the edge of the slope by the boxes, slowly, at his own rate, which was the slowest in the yard. And Dee stood by the cabinets with the glass and the counter on her shoulder, and called the turns.

It came up cold.

It came up into Toren's right palm slow and heavy and cold, the way it had on the east track when Rook knelt beside him and said *don't haul*. There was nothing in it. There was nobody in it to want anything, and it did not want to go anywhere now. It lay in his palm and was ash. He held it high and took it to the cabinet top one-handed, and tipped a jar against his palm, and it went down out of him into the glass and lay there, grey.

He went back. He could not do anything fast. He had never been able to. He crouched at the edge of the slope with his left arm against his chest and his right palm flat, and let it come at the rate it came, and the rate it came for him was slow. Across the room Jab filled a jar while Toren filled a quarter of one. By the door Callie filled half a jar on her knees with her face grey and set, and did not bend.

"Three," said Dee. She looked along the jars on the cabinet top. "That's us back over the line."

"Five," said Dee. "The counter's a hundred and ten. It's coming down. It's settling."

When the lifter fell, a little of the grey had gone into the air, and the counter had been at a hundred and forty. There was no wind under the hill. It came down out of the air onto the floor and lay there with the rest.

"Ten," said Dee.

Jab stoppered a jar and started another. "One," he said. "Cold. All of it."

"Twelve."

Callie's hand stopped.

"This one's got grit in it," she said, looking at her own palm. "I can see it. It's not like the plain. It came up with the rest."

"Put it in your second," said Dee. "Apart. We'll grade it at the river."

"Fifteen."

Jab stoppered his second. Callie stoppered her first and set her second going. Toren had half a jar, near enough, and his palm ached the way Callie had said it ached on the east track, from the inside.

"Sixteen," said Dee. "The box."

---

None of them had forgotten the box. They had been lifting round it.

Jab had lifted the grey off the lid. Toren put his good hand on the lid and lifted it out of its groove, and the first box was there in the lead bottom, where it had landed the day before with a sound like a door in another house.

The second one was on the floor at the edge of the heap of boxes. It had been lying there with a strand of grey across its top like a hair across a cheek. The strand was only a line of grey on the steel now, and it was not going anywhere.

Callie could not do the tongs. Toren saw her know it before he said it. She looked at them where they lay by the boxes, where she had dropped them the day before, a yard and a half of iron gone grey to the rings. Then she looked at her own arms across her side.

"You," she said to Jab.

Jab took the tongs. He had never used them. He got the jaws round the box that had read a hundred and six, and closed the rings, and the jaws bit. He did not lift it. He dragged it along the floor through the grey to the side of the carrier.

Toren set a strut. He did not have much to set it with; he had a hair above his floor. He put the foot of it on the concrete at a slant up to the lip of the carrier, and it came up out of the floor thin and pale, and he was soft behind it.

"Set," he said. "Walk it up."

Jab walked it up. The box slid up the pale line of the strut to the lip and tipped and went over, and landed on the lead beside the first one.

"Two," said Toren.

He let the strut go. He went down to look at what he had left, and he did not like what he saw, and he did not say it.

"And the third?" said Dee.

Toren looked at the heap of boxes. The third one was there. He could see it, half under two others, deep in, where Callie had read it the day before at a hundred and forty, with its slide rusted halfway across its window. It was talking to the room. It had been talking to the room for twenty years.

There might be others. There were hundreds of boxes on the floor. Callie had read a dozen the day before and found three live ones, and there might be another with a shut slide in the next dozen, or the next. But it would take her a quarter of an hour on her knees with the counter at a hand, and she could not do it now, and nobody else could read them the way she did.

"Seventeen," said Dee.

"Two," said Toren. "Not three. The third's open. We'd be carrying what the lead's meant to stop." He put the lid in its groove, and it went in with a soft heavy sound. "Two."

"Two," said Dee. "Written."

The vent jars stayed. There were three of them in the canvas bag on the cabinet, stoppered and heavy, full of what Jab had taken off the lifter at every three. Dee held the counter to the bag and did not say the number out loud. She took the chalk out of her pocket, Pell's chalk, and set the bag on the floor at the foot of the racks, and drew a cross on the concrete beside it.

"It stays under the hill," she said. "Where it came from."

Then she looked at the glass. "Nineteen," she said. "Twenty. Out."

---

They went out.

The sledge went out through the door over the threshold, with the carrier on it and two live boxes in the carrier and the lid in its groove. Toren had the front rope. Jab had it behind him, with the frame on his back and five jars of the lifter's in it, three full and two not, beside the fence's last whole jar and the fourth jar, under the line, with the rest of what was in it. Callie walked beside the sledge with the hook for a stick and her arm across her side, and did not pull. Dee came last with the glass, and as she came out of the room she picked the lantern up off the corridor floor.

The corridor was grey. The big rope was a bank of grey along the foot of the room-side wall, in the shape of a rope, lying there. The tray on the far wall was full of grey and not moving. They went along the corridor, and nothing moved in it.

At the foot of the stair Toren stopped and looked at the ramp.

It went up: twenty steps' worth of smooth concrete to the landing, and thirty more. The iron ring was set in the wall at the landing.

"On a slope, up or down," he said, "there's a rope round something that doesn't move."

"And nobody below it," said Callie.

They took it up the ramp a flight at a time. Toren and Jab went up to the landing with the front rope and pulled from there, above the load. The back rope went from the tail of the board up through the iron ring at the landing and twice round the foot of the yellow rail's post, so that if the front rope went, the ring and the post would hold the sledge and not anybody. Dee took in the slack round the post as the board came up. Callie stood on the landing beside her and called it, *pull* and *hold*, with one hand on the last of the tail where it came off the post, and nothing else.

The board came up the ramp a pace at a time.

At the landing it had to stop against the turn of the wall while they moved both ropes up to the ring at the top, and Toren set a strut under the back of the board where it stood on the slope, so that it would not slide while the rope was off. It held for four breaths. He felt it take the last of him that was above his floor.

"I'm at my floor," he said. "I'm stopping."

He let it go. The rope was on the top ring by then, and the board did not move.

"Pull," said Jab.

They pulled it up the second flight, and it came over the top step into the hall of the long building, into the light from the door.

---

It was the middle of the afternoon, and the sun was going down over the drum.

At the top of the road Callie stopped. She could hardly stand straight. She turned round anyway and looked up, because she had said she would, every time, going in and coming out.

Toren took the paper out.

She counted, and he watched her lips. Ten. Three.

Ten. Four.

She stopped.

"It's not," she said.

He looked up. The edge of the dark over the drum thickened, and he counted without meaning to. Eight. And it thinned. And it thickened again at once, before it had thinned for three. It thinned. It thickened for a long time, twelve, fourteen, and it thinned for five.

"It's lost the count," said Callie.

She watched it for a long time, and nothing came out of the bottom of it while she watched. Then something did: a long grey veil, at no count at all, in the middle of a thickening, and it went down behind the broken rim of the drum.

"One," she said. "But not at the three. There isn't a three."

Toren wrote it. *Coming out, after. The sky's not ten and three. It's lost the count. 1 fall, not at a three.* He looked at the words for a while.

"It's still there," said Jab, beside him. He was looking up at the dark over the drum. "The sky. That's still there."

"It was never the lifter," said Dee.

"No," said Jab. "The lifter was keeping time to it. Or it was keeping time to the lifter." He looked at the white place on his palm. "I don't know which."

The bridge had poles on it.

They saw them from the gate: two long poles of grey wrack wood, the two they had used to slide the carrier off the cart onto the tailboard. They lay along the far end of the bridge, one on the outside plank on each side, over the crack in the middle one. They lay lengthwise, reaching from the cross-beam before the crack to the cross-beam after it, so that anything on them would sit on the beams and not on the rotten plank.

Rook was lying at the foot of the first post on the near side. He was not sitting. He was lying on his side on the gravel with his coat half off and his face grey and running wet, and his eyes were open, watching the gate.

Toren brought the gate round behind them and twisted the green wire back through the chain. He did it one-handed. He left the ends so they would come.

They brought the sledge across the bridge on the poles. The board slid along them over the crack, and the middle plank took nothing.

At the near end Dee knelt on the gravel beside Rook.

"You were sitting," she said.

"I was sitting," said Rook. "I've been looking at that plank all day. I knew what he'd have left for it, coming out." He did not look at Toren. "I didn't light anything. I dragged two poles along a bridge on my knees. That's not working. That's furniture."

"You're off," said Dee.

"I'm off," said Rook. "I'm very off." He shut his eyes. "Count them."

---

Pell had them tip the cart.

He told them from the boards, sitting with his back to the rack and his leg out. Lift the shafts, all of you that can, till the tail comes down. It's a cart, not a house. When the shafts go up, the tail goes down, and the tailboard's gone anyway, so the bed's a ramp to the ground.

Jab and Toren and Dee lifted the shafts, and the tail of the cart came down onto the gravel. They pulled the sledge up the bed of the cart on the front rope, with Callie holding the back rope and not pulling. When it was up behind the home jars, they let the shafts down slowly, and the cart came level, and the carrier sat on its board on the bed of the cart with two live boxes in it.

"There," said Pell. "Lead's not for backs."

Coat off. Shake it. Wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat.

They did it. Callie did it on her knees at the edge of the water, with one hand, and Dee washed her hair for her, slowly, and Callie let her.

Then Toren stood at the pump house wall with the signed paper against the stone.

"Dee," he said. "Jab. Callie."

He looked at Rook, on the canvas where they had carried him, with his eyes open. He looked at Pell on the cart, with his leg out and his pencil, and at the mare, tied to the wheel with her rope halter, eating.

"Four in," he said. "Six. And a horse."

He wrote it.

*48th. The pump house. In: 4. The lifter's stopped. T. put the point in, at the open, with J. on the push and C. in the door, holding the lean at the last. Under the line on purpose, inside 2 turns: 3 Handfuls (jar 4). T. and D. W.'s call. Once. For the Director. Still under after the kill; back over by the third turn of lifting. 2 boxes in the carrier, in lead, on the cart. The third left (open). C.'s ribs cracked (left, the same). D. W. at her floor (once). T. at his. The sky's lost the count.*

He looked at it for a while. Under it, in the same hand, he wrote: *Everybody back.*
