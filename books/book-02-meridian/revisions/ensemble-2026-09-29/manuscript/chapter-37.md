# Chapter 37 — The Flap

The pump was still in the pump house.

Callie had not looked at it the night before. She had been too cold, and too tired, and the wall had been something to put a fire against and nothing else. Now she got up from the fire and took a lantern in through the gap where the door had been, because she could not sit there and think about a rope any more, and everybody at the fire could see that she could not.

The pump stood in the middle of the stone floor under the open sky. It was iron, and it was bigger than the cart. A long iron drum lay on its side on a stone bed, and at one end of the drum there was a wheel as high as Callie, with spokes, and a rod going from the wheel into the drum. A pipe as thick as her body came up out of the floor on the river side and went into the bottom of the drum, and two more came out of the top of it and went through the wall toward the river, toward the broken pipes lying in the water.

It was all rust. It had not moved in a long time, and when Callie put her hand on the wheel, it did not move now either.

"It's a pump," said Pell, from outside.

He was on the cart, which they had drawn up against the outside of the wall so close that its rack stood a pace from the gap. When Callie held the lantern up, he could see in. He was sitting with his back to the rack and his leg out along the boards, the way he had sat all day.

"I know it's a pump," said Callie.

"I know you know," said Pell. "Ask me what you came in to ask me."

She stood by the wheel with the lantern.

"How does it work?" she said.

Pell told her. He told her the way he had told her about the wheel, sitting back with his hands on his knee, not looking at her, looking at the thing.

"It lifts," he said. "And it pushes. That's all a pump does. Two strokes." He nodded at the drum. "There's a plunger in that. The wheel goes round and the rod goes in and out. When it comes out, it lifts: it pulls the water up out of the river through that pipe in the floor, into the drum. When it goes in, it pushes: it pushes the water out of the drum, out through the top, up those pipes, over the river, up the hill. Wherever they wanted it."

"It lifts and it pushes."

"Lift, push. Lift, push." He moved his hand in time with it. "Only there's a thing in between. There's a flap."

Callie waited.

"In the bottom of the drum, where the river pipe comes in," said Pell, "there's a flap. Leather, on a hinge, like a door. When it lifts, the flap opens and the water comes up past it into the drum. When it pushes, the flap shuts, because the water's pushing on it the wrong way. So what it lifted can't go back down the pipe to the river. It's got nowhere to go but out of the top." He looked at the drum. "My father had a little one, on the farm, for the trough. A hand one. The flap went every spring. When the flap goes, you can hear it."

"What does it sound like?"

"Like it's working," said Pell. "That's the thing. It lifts, and it pushes, and it sounds like it's working all day. But it pushes the water straight back down the pipe it came up. It lifts it up and pushes it down again, the same water, all day, and nothing comes out of the top." He shook his head. "You'd not know. You'd pump for an hour and go to the trough and it'd be dry."

Callie stood very still with the lantern.

She was thinking about the heap in the corner of the room under the hill. She was thinking about the ten, and the heap drawn out into threads and gone into the rope, and the three, and the corner swelling. She was thinking about Jab with his hand on the rope by the door, saying *nothing, it's going past*, and then the three, and the push, and his face.

"If you wanted to stop it," she said. "The pump. If you were in the drum."

"I'd not be in the drum."

"If you were. If you wanted it to stop pushing water up the hill."

Pell thought about it. She could see him do it, the way he thought about a distance.

"You'd not do it on the lift," he said slowly. "On the lift it's got the whole river behind it. You'd stand in front of that pipe in the floor and it'd lift the river at you, and you'd not stop the river." He looked at the pipe going down into the stone. "You'd do it on the push. On the push it's only got what's in the drum. What it's just lifted. That's all it's got. A drum's worth."

"And if you took the drum's worth," said Callie. "On the push. Every time. So it pushed and nothing got up the pipe."

"Then it'd lift the river again," said Pell. "And push it at you again. And you'd take that." He was quiet. "Till the river ran out. Or you did."

"And if somebody shut the river?"

Pell looked at her properly then.

"Then it'd have a drum's worth," he said. "And no more. And it'd push it at you, and you'd take it, and it'd lift nothing, because there's nothing to lift. And it'd push again. Less." He was quiet for a long time. "It'd pump itself dry. It doesn't know how to do anything else. A pump doesn't stop because the water's gone. It stops when somebody stops the wheel."

Callie put the lantern down on the stone bed of the pump.

"Nobody stops its wheel," she said.

"Then it'd go on," said Pell. "Pushing nothing. Into whatever was taking it."

---

She went out to the fire.

They were all there. Dee had changed Rook's pad and was sitting with her book. Jab was sitting with his right hand palm up on his knee. Toren had the paper. Rook lay on the canvas with his eyes open. Nobody was talking. Nobody had talked since *tomorrow we decide*.

"Pell," said Callie. "Say it again. The pump."

Pell said it again from the cart, while she stood by the fire with her hands open. Lift, and push, and the flap, and the river. On the lift it's got the whole river. On the push it's only got what it's lifted.

Jab had looked up at *the push*, and he did not look away from Pell until Pell had finished.

"On the push," said Jab.

"That's what I did wrong," he said. He said it to his hand. "I put it on the rope in the ten. On the lift. And it went past, because it had the whole hill behind it and it was going somewhere. And then the three came, and it pushed, and I was a hole." He turned the hand over. "And there was nothing shut. The whole hill was behind the push. So it kept pushing."

"If somebody shut the river," said Callie.

"The lee," said Toren.

He had put the pencil down.

"The lee stopped the floor," he said. "At the door. It stopped everything crawling in. It didn't stop the rope." He was looking at the fire, not at her. "But the rope's a thing. It's a rope. It's lying there. If the rope's cut—"

"It mends in the three," said Jab.

"It mends in the three if it can reach." Toren looked up. "If it's cut at the door, and Callie's lee is in the door, in the cut, it can't reach. It can grow up to the lee. It can't grow through it." He stopped. "Then what's in the room's all there is. That's the drum. The river's on the other side of Callie."

Nobody said anything.

Dee had opened her book, and she was not writing in it. She was looking at Toren.

"Say it in order," she said. "All of it. From the stair."

He said it in order. He said it the way he had said everything since the forty-third, the siding and the wash: not with the answers, because he did not have them, but with the questions in the right order where everybody could see them and see if they stood.

It would be in the room. Everybody agreed with that, and Rook said it from the canvas: "It goes where the grey is." The grey had been lying in the first room all afternoon, and the heap in the corner, and the husks killed and killed again. And the carrier was there. None of them thought it wanted the carrier, but it would be there.

Dee said the rest of it, so that it was said.

"It's in the first room," she said. "The room she sent us to, and the box she sent us for. It came at us there." She did not look at the hill. "So we go to the first door and no further. Not the second, not to look. Nowhere near the round one. We're not going hunting for anything. If it's not in the first room in the morning, we don't go looking for it. We come out."

There were two ways into the room from the corridor for the rope. One was the floor, through the door, where the rope would lie across the threshold. The other was the ceiling, through the hole above the door and down the wall.

"I cut both," said Toren. "The ceiling first, outside, before it goes in the hole. Then the floor at the threshold. The heel. In a ten. Before it pushes."

"And me in the door," said Callie. "On the threshold. In your cut." She held her hand out flat and tipped it back. "The lee off the curve, standing up into the corridor. And leaned back. Up the wall over the door. Over the hole. Like a lean-to, the wrong way up."

"Can you?" said Toren.

"It's a door," said Callie. "And a bit. It's still. It's on concrete." She thought about it with her lips moving, the way she did a sum. "A door's the cheapest thing I know. A door and a bit's more. And if there's husks in the corridor, and there will be, they'll come at it, and that's more again." She looked at Dee. "I want two. Settled. Tonight."

Dee did not answer at once.

"Two's more than you've ever carried," she said.

"I settle well. You said. It's the only thing I do better than Toren."

"It's not the only thing," said Toren.

"It's the only thing tonight," said Callie.

Dee looked at her for a long time. Then she wrote it.

"Two," she said. "You'll settle it slowly, and I'll sit with you, and if it slops I'll stop you at one and a half." She looked up. "And you'll be in the room."

"I'll be in the door."

"You'll be in the room," said Dee. "With your back to it. You'll be facing the corridor with the lee in front of you, and everything that's in that room will be behind you. You'll not see it. You'll not see any of it."

"I know," said Callie.

"Jab," said Toren.

"On the push," said Jab. "Not the lift. When you cut it, it'll mend. That's all it does. It'll push into the cut at the three. I put my hand on the cut, on the room side, at the three, and it pushes into me, and I take a drum's worth. And I put it in a vent jar, and stopper it. And I take my hand off." He looked at Dee. "Before the ten. Every time. Off before the ten."

"I'll say *off*," said Dee. "At the end of every three. Every one. You'll not wait for me to say it twice."

"I'll not wait."

"And what's in the room is all it's got," said Toren. "And every three it pushes some of it into Jab, and Jab puts it in a jar. And every ten it lifts what's lying in the room. And it's less." He stopped. "Pell said. It'll pump itself dry."

"It'll not stand there and let you," said Rook.

He said it from the canvas. He had not moved, but his voice was clear.

"It had a husk on your strut all afternoon," said Rook. "It'll make more. It'll put them in your way. It'll throw what it's got at whatever's worked, and Jab's hand is worked, and so is Callie's back. And so is you." He looked at Toren. "You'll not be cutting. You'll be keeping it off them."

"Then I'll keep it off them," said Toren. "And cut when I can." He looked at the paper. "Every rope it's got in the room is a way it moves. Every one's a way it throws. I cut them, one at a time. Every cut's a hole. Every hole, it pushes, at the three. And Jab's there."

"And when it's got nothing left to push with?" said Dee.

"It opens," said Jab. "At the three. In the middle. The light." He did not look at anybody. "I saw it open. It opens every time it pushes. That's where the push comes from."

"Then that's where I'll put the point," said Toren.

---

"Say what it costs," said Dee.

Callie said it.

"Twenty-nine," she said. "Inside. One jar open, with five in it. Three whole." She did the sum out loud, because Dee would want it out loud. "Settling tonight. Two in me, that's a Handful and three quarters more. A Handful and a half in Toren, and that's more than he's carried too. Half in you, Miss Wren, to make you one. That's three and three quarters." She stopped. "Twenty-five and a quarter. The line's sixteen. Nine and a quarter to work with."

"Nine and a quarter," said Toren.

"It's not twelve," said Callie. "It's less than today cost us."

"It's not twelve," said Toren. "It's nine and a quarter. That's what I'll pay." He wrote it. "If it's not done at the line, we come out, finished or not."

"And if it's nearly done?" said Dee. "At the line. If it's lying there with nothing left and you've got the point up."

Toren did not answer at once. The others waited for him, and nobody filled the gap.

"Then I'll say it," he said. "Then. Out loud. What we're doing, and what it costs, and how long. And you'll write it. And it'll be mine."

"It'll be mine as well," said Dee. "It's my line." She wrote. "And the rule's still the rule, after. Two Flasks left inside, everybody out. If you say it, it's once. It goes in front of her at home with both our names on it."

"And the home three don't cross that bridge," said Toren. "Whatever happens. Whatever it's lying there with. They're for the road." He looked at the cart, at the rack behind the driver's bench, where the three jars stood grey to the shoulder in the dark. "Say it, somebody who's not going in."

"The home three don't cross the bridge," said Pell. "I'm sitting on them."

Callie settled by the fire with Dee beside her.

It was the most she had ever taken. Dee weighed it out of the open jar onto the square of paper on Callie's knee, a Handful and three quarters, grey and fine, and Callie put her palm flat on it and let it come.

It came. It always came well for her. It came up into her palm slow and cold, and went down, and lay at the bottom of the well on the quarter that was there, and she felt the stone get bigger. She did not haul. She let it come at the rate it came, and she tried to think about nothing, the way Dee had taught them at the long table.

She did not think about nothing. She thought about the door.

She thought about standing in it with the hook on the threshold and the lee up, facing the corridor, with the room behind her: Toren behind her, and Jab, and Dee, and the lifter. She would not see any of them.

She had always held what she could see. That was what Rook had taught her on the forty-fourth. *Watch who's inside. Put your eyes on the people and hold them there.* She had held Jab's shoulders on the old line. At the wash she had held Jab and Pell under the lee, and taken Toren in and out of it with her eyes. The lee went where she looked. It had always gone where she looked.

Tomorrow she would be looking at a corridor.

The lee would not be over anybody. It would not be shaped like where they were. It would be a wall across a door, keeping a corridor out of a room. Nobody would be inside it. Everybody would be behind it, doing things she could not see, and she would not know how they were until one of them said so.

*You can't have all of us,* Dee had said on the forty-fourth. *You'll have to decide who's worth the lee.*

She did not know who that was, tomorrow. She did not know what a lee was for, if it was not over anybody. She had never held anything she could not see.

*Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.*

Her mother had said that with her words half gone, on the edge of the bed, with Callie's fingers round her wrist. She had opened Callie's fingers off her wrist, one at a time. Callie had not known then what she meant, and she did not know now.

"One and a half," said Dee quietly. "It's lying still. Go on."

Callie went on. The last quarter came up into her palm and went down and lay there.

"Two," said Dee. "Is it still?"

Callie went down to look. The stone lay big at the bottom of the well, bigger than it had ever been, and it was still. It was not slopping. It was not in the hall. It was lying where she had put it.

"It's still," she said.

"Then it's yours," said Dee. She wrote it. "Road, none. Inside, twenty-five and a quarter. One open, with a Handful and a quarter in it. Three whole." She looked up. "Say it back."

Callie said it back. Everything above the line was what tomorrow could cost. The line itself was for getting out.

---

She had the first watch with Pell, because Pell was awake.

He sat on the cart with his back to the rack and the atlas shut on his knee, and she stood by the cart wheel with the hook and the counter. The fire was down. Toren had settled his Handful and a half at the fire for most of two hours, with his palm flat and his face shut. It was half as much again as he had ever carried. Dee had sat with him through the last of it, the way she had sat with Callie, and at the end she had put two fingers on his wrist and said it was lying still. Then he had lain down by Jab and counted them. Jab had fallen asleep at once. Dee had not.

Across the river the fence stood black against the dark, and the hill went up beyond it. Over its flat top there were no stars, and round the place with no stars there was the light the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened and thinned.

"I'm counting it," said Pell. "In case anybody asks."

"Can you see the falls?"

"No. It's too dark. I can see the edge." He was quiet. "I'd like to see the falls. I'd like to know if it's more at night."

They watched it together.

"I said what I said at the fire," said Pell, after a while. "About a box and a half short. About the woman's table."

"I know."

"I'd say it again."

"I know you would."

"I said it so it had been said," said Pell. "By somebody who wasn't going in. Somebody has to say it, on a road, or you go in without anybody having said it, and then you don't know you chose." He looked across the river at the hill. "It's said now. It's written down. So now you can go in and do it properly."

Callie looked at him.

"That's all," said Pell. "That's what saying it's for."

She stood by the wheel and did not say anything for a long time.

"Pell," she said. "Your father. With the pump."

"What about him?"

"Did he mend the flap? Every spring?"

"Every spring," said Pell. "Cut a new one out of an old boot and put it in. Took him ten minutes." He was quiet. "Took me a year to learn how, and he let me do it wrong for most of that year. He'd stand there and watch me pump water down the pipe for an hour and not say anything. Then he'd say *now go and look at the trough.*"

"That's cruel."

"It's a good way to learn about flaps," said Pell. "You never forget which way they go."

---

In the morning Rook was cooler.

He was not cool. Dee had her fingers on his wrist at first light, and her face said so. But the sweat had stopped, and his eyes were clear, and when she lifted the pad the red round the stitches had not gone any further out in the night. It had not come in either.

"It's holding," she said.

"Like the post," said Rook.

"The post was in lead," said Dee. She gave him the second of the four papers in boiled water, and he drank it and made the same face.

Then Rook got up. He did it in three movements, with a hand on the cart wheel and a hand on Pell's good knee, and Pell said "Mind the leg," and Rook said "I'm minding it." He stood with his coat over his shoulders and walked slowly down the gravel to the near end of the bridge, and sat down at the foot of the first post with his back against it. He had not asked anybody.

"Sitting's allowed," he said, when Dee looked at him.

She measured the rest of them at the cold fire. Toren's own had come back in the night to two turns, near enough, under the stone he had settled. Callie's own was three parts. Dee did her own last and wrote it without saying it. Then she took the last three vent jars out of the straw on the cart and put them in the canvas bag.

They ate. They drank the boiled water, and a little of the full skin. They said the rules at the bridge end, and *pull the door* at the end of them.

Rook said the rules with them from the post. At the end he looked up at Callie.

"You'll have your back to them," he said.

"I know."

"When I shut the road at the Sag," said Rook, "I never once looked round at the people lifting behind me. Not once, in six weeks. I looked at what was coming up out of the bottom." He was looking at the hill. "If I'd looked round, I'd have dropped it. You can't hold a thing and look behind it. You hold what's in front. They'll hold what's behind." He looked back at her. "You'll want to look. Don't."

"I'll not look," said Callie.

"You will," said Rook. "Once. Everybody does. Then you'll not do it again."

---

They went in faster the second time, at the end of the morning.

There was nothing to drag, because the sledge was in the first room under the hill. They went along the pipes on the north side of the blocks, and up the Z, and round the heap at the first bend, and at the top of the road, at the twentieth turn, Dee drew them off the open jar. Callie weighed it. There had been a Handful and a quarter in it at the gate.

"A half," she said. "Three quarters left in it."

At the top of the road she counted the sky, ten and three, and at the ninth count a fall came out of the bottom of it and went down into the drum.

"One in twelve," she said. "The same as yesterday going in."

Toren wrote it.

The lanterns in the hall. The yellow rail. The stair. The long grey slide of the husk's ash lay down the steps from the top, where Toren had killed it against the hook, in a streak going down the stair in threads, and they went down beside it. At the landing the counter said fifty. At the bottom it said sixty.

The door was open.

It stood against the concrete wall of the flat place where it had swung the day before, wide, as far as it would go. Toren looked at it as he came off the last step, and then he looked at the place on the floor two paces from it where his strut had stood.

"Open," he said.

"Good," said Dee. "That's thirty breaths we don't have to spend."

The corridor had changed.

The tray was bowed all along the tray wall. The rope in it was as thick as a leg, grey and heavy, crawling, and it had bent two of the brackets so that the tray hung down at a slant between them. Where the small trough crossed the ceiling toward the first door, the rope in it was as thick as an arm.

And on the floor, along the foot of the wall on the room side, there was another rope.

It had not been there yesterday. It came out of the dark from the west, along the floor, as thick as Toren's thigh. It lay along the foot of the wall with the doors in it, heavy and grey, and it turned in at the first door and went over the threshold into the room.

"It laid that," said Jab quietly. "Coming along. Behind it."

Callie read the floor. The threads came along it from the stair the way they had all day yesterday, and they went into the big rope on the floor and into the one in the tray, and everything in the ropes went west. But the big rope on the floor went east into the first room.

"It's going in," she said. "It's feeding the room." She held the counter out. "A hundred and ten. At the first door. From here."

They went along the corridor to it on the tray side this time, under the tray, because the rope on the floor was on the room side. Nobody touched either one. Two paces short of the doorway Callie stopped, and Toren unhooked the lantern from his belt and held it out past her shoulder, and they looked in.

---

It was lying on the carrier.

They had thought it would be a heap, and it was a heap. It lay three paces in from the door, against the boxes heaped under the racks on the left, grey, as high as Callie's waist and as long as two of her, with its back up in a long hump. It lay over the sledge and the carrier. The lead lid of the carrier was under it, and one iron handle stuck out of its side. It lay there the way a dog lies over a bone in a yard, not eating it, lying on it. It was lying on what they had come for.

The big rope came in over the threshold and went across the floor into it. The rope from the hole came down the wall beside the door and went across the floor into it. And out of the hump three more ropes came, each as thick as an arm, and lay across the heap of boxes and the floor like arms flung out in sleep.

It was not asleep. It had nothing in it to sleep. They could see the grey on it crawl toward the middle, all of it, for ten breaths, and then stop.

At the three, the line of light opened along the top of the hump, the colour of the bottom of a pond, for three breaths. Then it shut.

"Two heaps," said Toren. He had the lantern in one hand and the spike dark on the fist of the other. "The corner. And by the cabinets."

The corner heap was where it had been, in the far corner where the racks met the back wall. The other lay at the far end of the row of cabinets along the right-hand wall.

"And one out here," said Dee, behind them. "By the stair door. Where you killed the one on the ground yesterday."

"They'll stand up when we start," said Jab.

"They'll stand up when we start," said Dee.

She put her back to the tray wall, with the glass in her hand and the open jar in the crook of her arm and the canvas bag with the three vent jars on her shoulder. She took the lantern out of Toren's hand and set it on the corridor floor against the tray wall, where it lit the doorway and the corridor both. Then she lifted the strap of the counter off Callie's hip and put it over her own shoulder.

"You'll not see it," she said. "I will."

Callie let her take it.

Dee looked at the three of them, one at a time: at Toren with the spike; at Jab with the frame and the lantern hanging from it and his right hand held away from his side; at Callie with the hook.

"On a ten," she said. "Mine. I'll count it."

She counted it, and they all counted it with her, and Toren did it under his breath. The line of light shut on the top of the hump.

"Ten," said Dee. "Go."

---

Toren lit the heel.

He did not go in over the threshold. He stopped in the corridor in front of the door and reached up first, over his head, to the small trough where it went into the wall above the doorframe. The rope in it was as thick as an arm. He put the heel into it a hand from the hole and dragged it through, and it came apart, and half of it hung out of the trough over the door, spilling.

Then he put the heel down across the big rope where it lay on the threshold, low, and dragged it through. The rope was as thick as his thigh, and it did not come apart like a strand. It came apart like a sack of flour opened with a hook, all along the cut, and the grey spilled out of both ends onto the concrete. He went through it into the room, and turned, and let the heel go dark.

"Cut," he said. "Both."

Dee went in over the threshold after him, and Jab went in with the frame, close, the way they had said at the fire. Callie went last.

She stepped over the spilled grey at the threshold into the room and turned round, so that she stood with her back to the room and her face to the corridor. She put the back of the hook's curve down on the threshold, on the concrete, in the cut, and held it still. Then she reached for the stone at the bottom of the well. It was big, and it was still, and she let it go into the curve where the curve touched the floor.

The lee stood up.

It stood up off the outside of the curve into the corridor, the width of the door frame, pale. Callie did not let it stand straight. She leaned it back from the threshold, up and up along the corridor side of the wall over the door, over the top of the doorframe and on up the wall to the ceiling, over the square hole with the cut rope in it, like a lean-to roof leaned the wrong way. It was a door and a bit, and she felt the stone go into it harder than it went into a door.

The cut end of the big rope lay on the corridor side of the curve, a foot from her boot, spilling. The cut end of the ceiling rope hung out of its trough above her on the corridor side of the lee, a hand from the hole it could not get to.

"Holding," said Callie.

Behind her, the others were already moving.

Jab backed up to the nearest of the cabinets along the right-hand wall, the way he backed up to the tailboard at every halt. They were big grey steel cabinets, as high as his shoulder, with flat tops. He let the rack come down on the top of the nearest one, slid his thumbs out of the chest band, and stepped out from under it. He had never taken the frame off inside a fence before.

"It's a shelf," said Dee, beside him. "Not a floor."

"It's a shelf," said Jab.

His back came up straight, and for a breath he stood as if he had forgotten what that felt like. The frame stood on the cabinet top with three full jars in it and three empties and a broken socket, and the lantern hanging off the side of it, lighting the room yellow from above. Dee put the canvas bag down on the next cabinet, and it rang like a bell.

"Seven," said Dee, counting. "Eight."

The room was as they had left it, with the lifter lying in the middle of it. The cabinets stood along the right-hand wall with the frame and the bag on the two nearest. The racks stood along the left-hand wall, with the boxes heaped at their foot, and the lifter lay three paces in against the boxes, over the carrier, with its hump turned toward the corner heap. The other heap lay at the far end of the cabinets. The stump of the big rope ran across the floor from the threshold to the hump, and the three arm-ropes lay out from the hump across the boxes and the floor. Toren stood a pace behind Callie's back, between the lifter and the rest of them.

Jab crouched by the stump.

It was the room-side end of the big rope, the end Toren had cut on the threshold. It lay across the floor from the threshold toward the lifter, three paces, as thick as Toren's thigh, and at the far end it went into the grey hump that lay over the carrier. At the near end, a pace from Jab's knee, it stopped. There was the spill, and then bare concrete, and then the curve of Callie's hook lying on the threshold, and Callie standing behind the curve with her back to him.

He could not see the lee from where he crouched. It stood on the other side of the curve, in the corridor. But he could see Callie's back, and her hands open on the conduit, and her shoulders, and her shoulders were very still.

"Nine," said Dee.

Jab put his right hand out over the cut end of the rope. He did not put it down yet. He held it an inch off, the way he held it over a patient before he knew what was there, and the white place on the heel of it caught the lantern light. Dee put the mouth of the jar to his left palm.

"Clean in," she said. "Three."

---

It pushed.

Jab felt it before he put his hand down. He felt the whole grey hump behind the rope do what it did at every three, the thing he had felt through his palm on the rope by the door. It drew breath. It vented. It pushed out into every place in the room where there was a hole, and the cut was a hole.

He put his palm flat on the end of the rope, round the white place, on the fingers and the side of the hand.

It came. It came up the rope at him from the hump like something shoved down a pipe, all at once, warm and gritty and loose, and into his palm. The clean cold came in at his left hand from the jar, and went through him, and held it off his wrist, and he held it high. It was not the hill. It was not the whole of everything lying where it fell, the way it had been on the rope by the door. It was one push. It was a palm's worth, and a little more, all at once.

Then it stopped. It stopped because there was no more behind it. That was the thing he had not known until he felt it. On the rope by the door the push had come and come, and this came once, and it was all there was, and it stopped.

In the same three, the rest of the room moved.

There was a long heavy dry sound from the boxes, like a wet rope lifted off a floor. "It's up," said Toren, and Jab, with his palm on the stump, said "Busy," in the same breath. One of the arm-ropes had come up off the boxes, heavy and slow, the way a wet rope comes up when somebody lifts the end of it, and it swung across the room at the door. It swung at the worked thing standing in the doorway with its back turned.

Toren's strut was already there. It went up from the concrete beside his boot at a slant over Callie's shoulders, the way you lean a plank over a door, and the arm-rope hit it at the height of her head with a sound like a sack hitting a wall. It stopped there, draped over the pale line of the strut, and began to slide off it slowly, the way a wet rope slides off a rail.

"Holding," said Toren, through his teeth.

Callie felt it through the floor under her boots, and she felt her whole body start to turn round. It started the way Toren's arm had started to go out when he fell in the stream, without asking her. She stopped it. She stood in the doorway with the curve on the threshold and her hands open on the conduit, and she kept her eyes on the corridor.

The corridor was pushing too. At her boot, the cut end of the big rope pushed out from its cut toward the threshold, to mend, the way the strands had pushed toward each other on the floor of the room the day before. It came up against the pale foot of the lee where it stood on the curve, and could not go through, and heaped. She felt it heap. It was like somebody leaning a sack against the outside of a door she was holding shut. Over her head, the cut end of the ceiling rope pushed out of its trough toward the hole, and met the lean of the lee, and stopped, and heaped against it there.

At the far edge of the lantern light, by the stair door, the heap swelled, and cracked, and something stood up out of it the colour of an old wasp nest, and turned toward her.

She did not turn round.

"That's the three," said Dee, behind her. "Jab. *Off.*"

Jab took his hand off. He took it off with the grey held high and open in it, and turned, and Dee had a vent jar out of the bag with the stopper out. He put its mouth to his palm and tipped his palm, and the grey went down out of him into the glass, and Dee put the stopper in with her thumb.

"One caught," she said.

The arm-rope slid the rest of the way off the strut, onto the floor behind Callie, and lay there.

"Letting go," said Toren, and the strut went.

Jab looked at the cut.

The end of the rope lay on the floor a pace from Callie's heels. It had not grown. It had not reached for the threshold. It lay where Toren had left it, with its spill, and the spill was going away from the threshold in threads, back along the rope toward the hump, because the ten had begun.

"It's where it was," said Jab.

"It's where it was," said Dee. She wrote it with the vent jar under her arm. "Jab. It's where it was."

In the door, with her back to all of it, Callie did not look round. She held the door.
