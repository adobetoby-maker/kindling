# Chapter 45 — Everybody Back

The top half was quiet, the way Dessa had said.

From the made road they could see all of it. The Sag lay below them like a bowl of smoke, three hundred paces from lip to lip. On the far side the lamps stood along the upper edge of the east track, with the watch between them and a second line below, two of its lamps dark. Ines was a small shape on the west rim. At the top of the dark lane a grey blade lay lit and flat, with Katori behind it. On the south lip Dessa sat on her crate with her eyes shut.

They went down toward all of it with the cart.

Toren walked beside the walking wheel with his good hand an inch off the rim. The made road went down the north slope in a long straight line, cracked and tilted, with grass in the cracks. Dee had Duchess's head. Pell sat on the high side of the cart, leaning, with his leg out and his hands on the rail. Rook lay on the canvas behind him on his side, not asleep, looking at the fog. Jab walked at the tailboard with the frame on his back and his left hand flat on his coat.

Callie walked behind the cart, not in front of it. Dessa had said from the corner, and the corner was at the bottom. She read the road over the tailboard with the counter held out, and said the numbers.

"Thirty," she said. "Forty. Fifty, at the edge of it."

The fog began a third of the way down.

It did not have an edge the way a wall does. It had an edge the way water has one on sand, thin at first and then not thin. Toren walked into it beside the wheel, and it came up round his boots, grey, and then round his shins. It was not wet. It was not anything. It was dust that had not decided to lie down.

"Sixty," said Callie. "Seventy."

"Jab," said Toren.

"Nothing," said Jab. "It's lying there."

The walking wheel knocked at every turn. In the bottom of the Sag it sounded very loud, like a man tapping on a door.

"Now," said Jab. "Pushing."

Toren put his hand up without looking. Dee stopped Duchess, and the cart stopped with her.

"*Pushing,*" said Toren. He did not shout it. It went across the fog.

"*Everybody back!*" said Dessa, from the far lip.

On the far slope the lamp-line moved. From down here the lamps were a row of yellow blurs, and the dark shapes between them stepped back from the blurs, all together, and stood.

The bottom heaved.

It came up under the fog all round them, not a sound, a movement, and they all felt it through their boots. The grey round their shins rose to their knees and sank back. And off to the left, east, beyond the road, where the slabs dropped away toward the ditch and the joints, something stood up.

It stood up out of the fog twenty paces off, grey on grey, and turned.

Nobody moved. That was the rule, and all six of them had heard it said across the bowl. *You stand still where you are.* Toren kept his good hand an inch off the rim. He had a full stone at the bottom of him, and he did not reach for it. Jab's knife stayed off his fist. Nothing on the cart was worked. Nothing on the cart was lit.

The husk turned toward the lamps.

It went up the east slope at the yellow blurs, away from them, through the fog, into the light. Something came down off the west rim, thin and fast, across the whole width of the Sag. The husk stopped. It came apart.

"*Down,*" said Dessa.

"Go," said Toren.

They went on.

The bottom was slabs.

Two of them knew it already. Toren had crossed it on the forty-first, with the watch on the lip and nothing coming up. He had knelt in it on the twenty-fifth with a woman bleeding in the fog. Jab had knelt in its black water that same day, with his hand on the day holder's chest. They went across it now with the fog to their knees, and it was like walking across a floor you know in the dark.

The slabs were tilted. Some had dropped at one edge into the water, and some had risen. The road ran over them in a line of concrete, cracked into blocks, with black water in every crack. The cart went over it a slab at a time, rocking, with the walking wheel singing and knocking and Pell leaning on the high side saying *easy*.

"Eighty," said Callie. "Eighty-five."

"There's the corner," said Dee.

---

The white post was not standing where it had stood. It lay flat in the water on the east side of the road, under the fog, with the *6* on it facing up, and they could see the white of it through the grey. Beside it the slab under the road's east edge had dropped. It had dropped on the twenty-fifth, a hand, and folded the day holder's sheet. It had dropped again since. Now the whole east half of the road sloped down at that slab into the black water, two hands at its low corner, and the water was over the lip of it.

The walking wheel was on the east side.

"Callie," said Toren. "Ground."

She came up past the tail of the cart on the east side. She did not go in front of it. She crouched at the edge of the dropped slab with the hook's end held out flat, and read.

"Soft," she said. "The dropped one's rocking. It's sitting on water." She moved the hook. "The next one's stone. There. Past the corner. It's not moved. It's sitting on the old bed." She looked up at Toren. "Under where the axle'll be when the wheel's in the middle of the drop."

"Set there," said Toren.

"Set there."

"And the ditch side?"

Callie turned her head and looked east. Past the dropped slab and the post in the water, the ground went down in a long shallow slope to the ditch. The fog lay thicker there, heaped over the joints, and it moved.

"That's where they come up," she said. "All of them. Look at it."

"Then that's yours," said Toren.

Callie did not stand.

She knelt on the stone slab past the corner, on the ditch side of the road, on both knees. She put the back of the hook's curve down on the edge of the slab, still, with the bend facing east. Then she did not take the conduit in both hands. She took it in one, her right, by the tail, where it ended. Her left arm stayed across her ribs. She knelt beside the hook, not behind it.

"On the hill it stayed in the steel," she said. She said it to Toren, or to herself. "When my knees went. It held a breath with nothing of me behind it, and I went down onto it. I thought that was the falling." She settled her hand on the tail. "It wasn't the falling. It was the steel. This time I'm putting it there."

"How long?" said Toren.

"The half," said Callie. "And my own. And the ribs." She did not look at him. "Say when the wheel's past, and I'll let it down. Slow. When I say."

Her face went still.

The lee stood up.

It stood up off the outside of the curve, east, toward the ditch and the joints, pale. She did not let it stand straight. She leaned it back over the road, over the dropped slab, like a lean-to. It went back and back: over the place where the cart would be when the wheel was in the drop, over the place where Toren would stand, over Duchess's head.

It was longer than a door. They could all see that. It stood along the edge of the road from the corner past the stone slab, the length of the cart and the horse together, a pale wall with the fog heaped against its outside. It was over all of them at once, cart and horse and people, not a door with her behind it.

"It's a line," said Callie. Her voice was flat. "It's not a door. It's a line. Along the road." She breathed out through her teeth. "It's dear. Holding."

"Bring her on," said Toren.

Dee brought Duchess on. The mare walked into the shelter of the lee with her ears flat and her eyes white, and she did not stop, because Dee was walking in front of her. The cart came on behind. The good wheel rolled along the west side of the road on sound slabs. The walking wheel came to the edge of the dropped slab and went down onto it.

"Set," said Toren.

The strut came up out of the stone slab past the corner, at its slant, under the axle beside the walking wheel. The wheel went down into the drop, down the slope of the slab toward the water, and the weight came onto the strut.

"Holding," said Toren.

It was a wheel. It was not the cart on the arch. It was one wheel on a drop, with Pell on the high side and a load of lead in the middle, and Toren stood soft behind it and paid by the breath.

"Rolling," said Pell.

The wheel went on across the drop.

"Now—" said Jab. He had it late. "Pushing."

Toren could not call it across the Sag. He had a wheel on a strut in a drop, and his stone going under it.

"*Pushing!*" That was Callie. She said it loud and flat from beside the hook, in the voice she used for a number.

"*Everybody back!*" said Dessa, from the far lip.

The bottom came up.

It came up all round the dropped slab. The fog on the ditch side heaved against the outside of the lee, and the joints in the ground below the ditch opened in the grey. Things stood up out of them. One. Two. Three. Grey on grey, through the pale of the lee.

They did not turn toward the lamps.

The lamps were far off, up the slope. And here, two paces from them, there was a pale wall standing along the edge of the road, worked. Under the road there was a strut, worked. There was a girl kneeling with her hand on the tail of a hook, and a boy holding a wheel.

They came at the lee.

The first one hit it, and they all felt it. It came through the stone and the strut and up through Toren's boots, with the sound of a sack thrown against a wall. The lee did not move. The husk stood against its outside, pushing, with no face.

"*Holding,*" said Callie.

She did not get up. She knelt with her right hand on the tail of the conduit and her left arm across her ribs, and her eyes were not on the husk. They were on the cart: on Duchess, and Dee at her head, and Pell leaning, and Rook on the canvas, and Jab at the tailboard, and Toren at his strut. She could see every one of them from where she knelt.

The second husk hit the lee beside the first.

"The wheel," said Toren. "It's in the middle. Three more breaths."

"*Three,*" said Callie.

The third husk did not hit the lee. It went along the outside of it, toward the end, where the lee stopped at the tail of the cart.

"*End,*" said Callie. "It's going round the end."

Toren could not move. He had the wheel.

"Mine," said Jab.

He went past the tailboard to the end of the lee with the frame on his back, and the knife came on his left fist, dark. He lit the hook of it and nothing else. The husk came round the end of the pale wall at the worked thing, and met the lit hook, and came apart. Its grey fell on the slab at the end of the lee and slid off into the water.

Something came down across the Sag from the west rim. It came thin and fast, at a long slant over the road, and struck the first husk where it stood against the lee's outside. The husk came apart, and its grey slid down the outside of the pale wall into the fog.

"*Ines,*" said Callie. She had not moved her eyes.

"Past," said Pell. "The wheel's past."

Toren let the strut go. At the end. Not after.

The walking wheel came up off the dropped slab onto sound stone beyond the corner, singing, and the cart came level.

The last husk stood against the lee's outside, pushing. Ines's second arrow came down across the bottom and took it.

"*Down,*" said Dessa, from the far lip.

"Down," said Callie.

She let the lee down. She did not drop it. She brought it in off the road the way you pay a rope back through your hands, the lean-to coming upright, and shorter, and then out. Then she knelt on the slab with the hook lying flat and her right hand still on the tail of it, and breathed out through her teeth, slowly.

"How much?" said Toren.

"All the half," said Callie. "And some of mine." She did not get up yet. "It held on the steel. I put it there, and it held, and I let it down when I said." She looked at her hand on the tail of the conduit. "I didn't have to be the wall."

"No," said Toren.

"On the hill my knees chose it," said Callie. "At the washout I forgot it. I stood behind it, the way I always stand to a door." She got up, one knee and then the other, with the hook for a stick. "I don't have to."

At the tail of the cart Jab nodded, once, as he had nodded at the pump house when she kept the tag.

---

They came up the south slope in the next quiet.

The lamps were there. The lamp-line stood along the upper edge of the east track, and the made road came up past the end of it to the lip. As the cart came up the last of the slope, the line opened for it.

Otto came down from his place with his big hands out. He took Duchess's rope from Dee without a word, and one of the yard lifters took the other side of her head. They walked the mare up the last of the slope, over the lip and onto the gravel, and the cart came up behind her with the walking wheel knocking. Pell leaned on the high side with his face grey. Rook lay on the canvas with his eyes open. Dee walked beside the mare with her hand still out, as if she had forgotten it was empty.

"Behind the lamps," said Dessa.

They were behind the lamps.

Otto and the yard lifter went back down to their places, and the line closed again below them. Every head on it had turned to the six, except Katori's.

Toren stood on the gravel at the south lip and turned round, and looked back across the Sag. The fog lay in the bottom. The dropped corner lay down there, with the post in the water. The north lip was three hundred paces off, and empty.

He counted them. He could not help it.

"Six," he said. "And a horse."

"I know," said Dessa.

She was on the crate. She had not moved. She sat with her bad leg out straight and the big book in her lap and her eyes shut.

Toren went and stood in front of her.

She opened her eyes.

None of the six had seen her face since the forty-first. It was thinner. It was white, with marks under the eyes, and set, the way Dee's had been set at Caul Hill over the jar. She looked at Toren. She looked at his arm in its cuff, and at his face, and at his coat, which was still grey in the seams.

"You're late," she said.

"Two days," said Toren.

"I know how many days. I've been counting." She shut her mouth hard. "I've got six lines. I've not written in them."

"Not yet," said Toren.

"Not yet," said Dessa.

Dessa looked past him at the cart: at Pell, and at Rook on the canvas, at Jab with the frame and Callie with the hook, and at Dee. Then she looked back at Toren, and shut her eyes again.

"Draw them," she said. "Now."

The table was two boards on trestles behind the lamp-line, with jars on it. They were the watch's jars, Otto's dirty ones, the sweepings from the lip. On a canvas beside it a yard lifter lay on his side, grey in the face, with a basin by his head. One of the gate crew knelt beside him with a wet cloth.

"He's the worst," said Dessa, with her eyes shut. "He was on the lamp-line below the others since first light. He was sick inside an hour of it coming up. Then Otto. Then Katori, and she'll not say. Then the other one." She did not open her eyes. "The girl's in her arms. Ines is the least. Wyck's been on the lip." She stopped. "I've not been anywhere. I've sat."

"You're not busy," said Jab. He had come to the table with the frame. "I felt it go round you, from the road. You're the only one it's not on."

"It came up to thirty paces," said Dessa. "It's not come nearer than that all day. I'd have known."

Jab swung the frame off his back onto the table. He took out the lifter's first jar, with its quarter, and Callie's clean one, with seven and a half.

"Dee," he said.

"I'm the watch," said Dee. She stood at his left shoulder with the glass. "Work."

He knelt by the yard lifter on the canvas. He could kneel here. It was the lip. He put his right palm flat on the man's belly, round the white place, with the jar against his left, and shut his eyes.

"Busy," he said. "It's in the belly. He's swallowed it. It's all through the belly and the chest."

He drew. They all watched the yard lifter's face. For a long time it did not change. Then it did.

"Vent," said Dee.

Jab held it high and turned, and put his palm flat on the gravel at the edge of the lip, where the grey from the watch's kills had lain all day.

"Not into my floor," said Callie. She had come to the table and was standing with the hook for a stick. "Into the road's."

"It's the road's," said Jab.

Dee took his leftovers. Jab went back on.

---

Toren stood by the table and looked down at the lamp-line.

Otto was in his place in it, grey in the face, with sick down the front of his coat, and there was room beside him for a man with a strut. Toren took a step down the gravel toward it before he had decided anything, the way his hands built a fire.

He stopped. He turned round to the crate.

"Dessa," he said. "Who comes off, and who takes their place?"

Dessa did not open her eyes.

"Otto next," she said. "Wyck takes Otto's place. Wyck." Her voice went up, flat, across the lip. "*Otto's coming off. Take his bar.*"

Wyck came down the lamp-line from his place on the lip. He had the bar in his left hand. His right arm was strapped across him, and his face was set the way it went on the road when he was doing a thing Keel had told him not to.

"I'm cleared tomorrow," he said, to nobody. "It's one day."

"*Taking,*" said Wyck, at Otto's shoulder.

"Yours," said Otto.

"Mine."

"*Letting go,*" said Otto. He stepped back, and Wyck stepped into his place with the bar planted and his weight back in his heels.

The six of them heard it. Callie turned her head. Jab, kneeling at the canvas with his palm on the yard lifter, looked up.

Otto came up the slope to the table with his big hands hanging.

"Otto," said Jab, from the canvas.

"I'm all right."

"You're busy," said Jab. "Stand there."

Otto stood there. Jab drew him, and Otto looked down at Jab's hand on his chest the whole time, and went red about nothing.

*Taking. Yours. Mine. Letting go.* Callie and Toren had learned it in the north yard on the thirty-fourth, with a rack and a sack. None of the six had known that the whole watch was saying it now.

"Who taught them that?" said Toren.

"You did," said Dessa. "It was in the book."

Jab took his hand off Otto's chest. "Clean," he said.

"Katori," said Dessa. "Next."

Katori did not come.

She was at the top of the dark lane between the third lamp and the fourth, with her grey blade lit and lying flat along the edge like a stove across a room. She had not moved since the six came over the north lip. None of them thought she had moved since the fourth hour.

"*Katori,*" said Dessa. "*Off.*"

"I don't come off," said Katori. She did not turn her head. "Nobody's got the lane."

They all looked at the lane.

It ran down from the gap between the third lamp and the fourth, dark, toward the ditch. The hounds came up it. That was what it was for. Hounds went for the dark, and the dark was the lane, and at the top of the lane there was Katori with a blade, and nobody else.

"I'll take the lane," said Toren.

Katori turned her head then.

She looked at him: at his arm in its cuff, and his face, and his right fist, which had nothing on it.

Toren knew that look. She had given it to him on the step of eleven, weeks ago, and he had thought then that she was deciding whether he was worth telling something to, and had decided not yet.

"With what?" said Katori.

"A strut," said Toren. "And the spike. Not together."

"How much have you got?"

"A stone," said Toren. "Full. And two turns of my own and a bit. I've held a wheel in the bottom. That was four breaths." He did not look away. "I can hold a hound off a strut. I've held a husk on one at Caul Hill for the whole of a thing I'll tell you about. I can't hold it and kill it. It's one or the other."

Katori looked at him.

"Don't block it," she said.

"What?"

"The lane. Don't put the strut across it." She turned back to the dark. "You block it, they go round. Round the lamp. Into the light, where Otto is. Otto's got a bar and no blade." She was quiet. "Narrow it. Put the strut in at a slant, from this side. Make the lane a pace wide at the top. Then they come one at a time. Or they don't come."

"One at a time," said Toren.

"And when one comes, you don't kill it," said Katori. "You hold it. On the strut. And you say so." She looked at him again. "And somebody else kills it."

"Who?"

"Me," said Katori. "When I've been drawn."

Dessa's voice came across the lip, flat.

"*Katori. That's mine to say. You're off.*"

Katori looked at Toren a moment longer. Then she lifted the blade off the lane, still lit.

"*Taking,*" said Toren.

He stepped into the top of the lane beside her, with the third lamp at his left shoulder and the fourth two paces off at his right.

"Yours," said Katori.

"Mine."

"*Letting go,*" said Katori. She let the blade go dark and stepped back, and walked up the slope to the table with her cap pulled down, and did not look back once.

At the top of the dark lane Toren stood with the fog in the bottom of the Sag below him and nothing on his fist, and waited.

---

Toren waited a long time.

Behind him, at the table, the work went on. Jab said *busy*, and after a while *clean*. Dee said *vent*, and there was the soft sound of a palm going flat on gravel. Callie said the jar.

"The lifter's first's done," she said. "The quarter. My first's open. Seven and a half."

"It's in my chest," said Katori.

"It's all through you," said Jab. "You've been in it since the fourth hour."

Katori did not answer him.

The fog lay in the bottom. It crept up the lower slope and slid back. It heaved a little, and lay down.

"*Pushing,*" said Jab, from the table. He had caught the gathering that time.

"*Everybody back!*" said Dessa.

The lamp-line stepped back behind the lamps. Toren did not. He was at the top of the lane, and the lane was the dark, and the dark was where they came.

The bottom heaved. The grey came up the slope toward the lamps, and things stood up in it and went up at the light. Two. Three. The bars met them. The girl came down the lip path with her blade and killed one.

And something came up the dark lane.

It came low and fast, the colour of the dark, with two green points in it where the lamplight caught. It came straight up the lane at the top of it, where Toren was standing.

"Set," said Toren.

He put the foot of the strut down at the top of the lane on the third-lamp side, at a slant, leaning out over the lane toward the fourth. It came up out of the gravel pale and hard, as high as his waist. The lane was a pace wide at the top now: a pace of dark between the end of the strut and the fourth lamp's pole.

The hound hit the strut.

It did not hit it the way a husk hits. It hit it the way a dog hits a gate, all teeth and weight and scrabbling claws, and it fell back and came again. It could not get past the strut. It went along the end of it, into the pace of dark that was left, and came through the pace at him.

"*Holding,*" said Toren. "*It's on the strut. It's coming through the end.*"

There was nobody beside him. He could not kill it. He had the strut.

He let the strut go.

It went out, and the hound came through the whole width of the lane at him, low. He lit the heel and nothing more. He put the lit hook of the spike down across its path, low, and it went into the light, because it was going for him and the light was in the way. He dragged the hook round against the third lamp's pole and put the point in.

It came apart. The grey fell on the gravel at the top of the lane.

He let the heel go dark.

"Set," he said, and put the strut back in.

His breath was going. He could feel it. The stone had paid for the hound on the strut for as long as it was on it, and then for the heel, and it was less than half now.

"That's one," he said.

"*Down,*" said Dessa.

Katori came back.

She came down from the table without being told, with her cap pulled down and her blade dark on her fist. She was not grey in the face any more. She was white. She stood at Toren's right shoulder on the fourth-lamp side of the lane, a pace off.

"I'm drawn," she said.

"You're drawn."

"You let the strut go and killed it," said Katori. "I watched." She did not look at him. "That was right. It was the only thing you could do with nobody else here." She was quiet. "There's somebody else here. You hold. I kill."

"I hold," said Toren.

He understood it all at once, standing at the top of the lane. He had held and killed, held and killed, all the way from the Breach, because there had been nobody else to do the other half. At Caul Hill Callie had held the door with her back to all of them, and he had given her the lean. He had given it away.

He could give this away too.

"I'll hold," he said. "You kill."

"That's the lane," said Katori.

---

The big push came at dusk.

By then every one of them had a place. Dessa was on the crate. Otto and the other yard lifter were on the lamp-line with Wyck. The girl was at the rope on the lip path, and Ines was on the west rim. Toren and Katori were at the top of the lane. Jab and Dee were at the table, and Callie stood by them with the counter and Dee's book. Pell and Rook were on the cart.

Jab felt it first. He did not say *now* this time, or *pushing*. He said "*Dee*," and there was a thing in his voice that nobody had heard since the rope by the door at Caul Hill.

"*It's all of it,*" said Jab. "*The whole bottom. It's gathering and it's not stopping gathering. It's—*"

"*Everybody back!*" said Dessa.

The whole bottom of the Sag came up.

It came up the way the drifts at the wash had come up in the gust, only there was no gust. The fog heaved up out of the bottom and up the slopes. It came up past the lamp-line on the east track and onto the lip path, and up the made road toward the lip. Things stood up in it everywhere. Toren tried to count them from the top of the lane, and Callie tried from the table, and they both stopped.

The lamp-line was behind the lamps. Nobody was below them. The things came up at the light.

Otto and the yard lifter met them with bars. Wyck stood in Otto's old place with his bar planted and his weight in his heels, and a husk came at him, and he did not strike it. He took it on the bar, low, the way you take a sack, and let it come, and turned it off the end of the bar down the slope, and stepped back into his place.

"*Heels,*" said Wyck, down the line.

Ines was loosing from the west rim. They could hear the bow from the table. The girl was on the lip path with her blade.

And up the made road, toward the lip, toward the table where Jab was drawing, the fog came, with husks in it.

"*Callie!*" said Dessa.

"*I've got the road,*" said Callie.

She put Dee's book down on the table and went to the top of the made road, where it came up over the lip above the table. She did not stand behind the hook. She knelt beside it, as she had in the bottom, with the curve across the concrete and her right hand on the tail.

"*Holding,*" she said, flat, in the voice for a number.

And the dark lane filled.

They came up it three at a time. Hounds. They came up the lane at the dark top of it, where Toren was, and the first hit the strut, and the second hit the first, and the third went along the end of the strut into the pace of dark.

"*Holding,*" said Toren. "*Three. One through the end.*"

"*Mine,*" said Katori.

She did not step into the lane. She reached across the end of the strut with her blade lit and lying flat, and the hound coming through the pace of dark met the edge of it, and came apart.

"*Two on the strut,*" said Toren.

The strut took them. They hit it, and scrabbled, and fell back, and hit it, and the strut went down into the gravel and did not come up into him, because he was soft. His breath was going under it.

"*One coming round,*" he said. "*The end.*"

"*Mine.*"

Katori killed it.

"*One,*" said Toren.

It came through the pace at the end. Katori was there, and it came apart.

The lane was empty.

Toren stood at the top of it with the strut up and nothing on it. He felt the stone go. It went out from under him the way it had gone at the steel door, like water out of a basin, and he was on his own. He was two turns of his own and a bit, and the strut on that.

"*Stone's gone,*" he said. "*I'm on mine.*"

"*How much?*" said Katori.

"*Two turns.*"

"*Then let it down when it's empty,*" said Katori. "*Not before. I'll say.*"

He held it. He held a strut with nothing on it at the top of a dark lane while the fog heaved behind the hounds. It was not free. There was no free strut. But it was cheaper than a hound.

At the table the work had not stopped.

"*Jar,*" said Jab. He said it before it was dry.

"*The fence's fourth,*" said Dee. "*Not the home three.*"

"*Not the home three,*" said Callie, from the top of the made road, holding.

Dee put the fence's fourth against Jab's left palm, and Jab went on. He said *busy*, and kept saying it, and then *clean*. He said *next*, and the other yard lifter answered him in his slow voice, and Jab drew him.

Then Jab stopped.

His right palm was still flat on the yard lifter's chest, round the white place, and the man was clean. But the warm was in the wrong place. It was round the mark, past the heel of his hand, where the clean cold should have been holding it off, and it was in his wrist. It was his own hand, hot round the white place, and the hot was going up. On the rope by the door at Caul Hill he had kept his hand where it was, and Dee had had to take his wrist.

He took his hand off.

"*Off,*" said Jab.

Dee looked up from the jar. She had not said it yet.

"*I'm warm,*" said Jab. His voice was very steady. "*The palm. Round the mark. It's to the wrist. It's not the draw. It's mine.*" He breathed. "*I'm off. Vent.*"

"*Vent,*" said Dee.

He put his palm flat on the gravel at the edge of the lip and let it go. He stayed there. Ten breaths. Twenty.

"*The girl's not done,*" said Jab. "*Nor Wyck. Nor Ines.*"

"*I know who's not done,*" said Dee. "*You're off. Sit on the crate. I'll draw you.*"

"*Dessa's on the crate.*"

"*Then sit on the ground,*" said Dee. "*It's the lip. You can sit.*"

He sat on the ground with his back against the trestle, and Dee knelt in front of him and put her palm on his wrist, and drew. He did not ask to go back on.

At the top of the lane Toren could not look round. He had the strut. He had heard all of it behind him: the *off*, and the *vent*, and Dee telling Jab where to sit. Then he heard nothing from Jab for a long time, and it was the loudest thing he had ever heard him do.

At the top of the made road Callie was still holding.

"*I'm at my floor,*" she said.

Her voice was thick. There was blood in it.

"*I'm at my floor. I'm stopping.*"

"*Down,*" said Dessa. "*Callie. Down.*"

The hook lay flat on the concrete.

Toren looked down the lane. It was dark, and empty, and below it the fog in the bottom of the Sag was lying down.

---

It went down the way it had come up, with no count.

The fog sank back down the slopes into the bottom and lay there. It heaved once, a little, and lay down again. Things that had been standing in it came apart on their own, the way the husks in the corridor had come apart when the lifter died, and lay as grey. Or they went back down into the joints. Nobody on the lip could tell which.

"*Down,*" said Dessa. "*Stay on the line.*"

Nobody moved off the line.

"Now," said Katori.

Toren let the strut go. At the end. Not after. He stood at the top of the dark lane with nothing on his fist and his own down to a turn, and his arm aching in its cuff, and did not turn round yet.

"Callie," said Katori, beside him. "Read it."

Callie got up off her knees at the top of the made road with the hook for a stick, and held the counter out over the bowl. Her face was white. There was blood on her lip and down her chin.

"Forty," she said. "At the lip. Seventy on the slope. It was a hundred and ten in the push." She watched the counter. "It's coming down."

Toren turned round.

At the table, Jab was sitting on the ground with his back against the trestle and his right hand held palm up on his knee. Dee was kneeling in front of him with her palm flat on his wrist. The rag was off the white place. The blister had broken, and the skin round the mark was red and wet.

"Cool," said Dee. She took her hand away. "You're cool."

Jab looked at the hand.

"I stopped," he said.

"You stopped," said Dee. "You said *off* before I did. You vented long. You sat."

"The girl's not done," said Jab. "Nor Wyck. Nor Ines."

"No," said Dee. "They're not."

"I'm cool," said Jab.

"You're cool," said Dee. "And a hand. Over your floor. I measured you."

Jab got up. He did it slowly, one knee and then the other.

"I'm back on," he said. "If you'll watch."

Dee looked at him, and they watched her do the sum: the one with a price on every line, and the other one.

"I'll watch," she said. "Three. Then you're off for tonight, whatever's left."

"Three," said Jab.

He drew the girl. She stood in front of him with her plait down her back and the leather binding on her left arm and her blade dark on her right fist. She looked at Jab's hand on her chest the whole time, frankly, the way she looked at everything.

"Busy," said Jab. "In the arms." And then, "Clean."

He drew Wyck. Wyck stood with his bar and his strapped arm and his face set, and when Jab said *clean*, he said, "I'm cleared tomorrow," as if Jab had asked.

He drew Ines last. She had come off the west rim with her bow and her empty quiver, and she stood in front of him without a word.

"That's all," said Jab. "That's everybody."

"Jar," said Callie.

She was at the table with Dee's book, on her knees, because she could not stand.

"The fence's fourth," she said. "It had five. It's got three. Two out of it." She wrote. "My first's done. Seven and a half. The lifter's first's done." She looked up. "Nine and three quarters. On the watch and us. For the Sag."

"And the home three?" said Toren.

Callie looked at the rack on the cart.

"Not touched," she said.

---

Dessa counted them.

She did it from the crate, with the big book open on her knee and a lantern the gate crew had brought hung on the trestle beside her. She did it the way she had done the order every night on the road from the old station: out loud, one name at a time, with her eyes open.

"Katori," she said. "Ines. Otto."

She looked down the line at the two yard lifters. One of them was sitting on the lip with his trouser leg rolled up and a cloth tied round his calf, where a hound had got past a bar in the last of the push.

"The two of you," said Dessa. "The girl." She looked at Wyck. "Wyck."

Then she looked at the six of them.

"Pell," she said. "Rook. Dee. Jab. Callie." She looked at Toren last. "Toren."

"And a horse," said Toren.

"And a horse," said Dessa. She did not smile. She never smiled when she was counting. "Everybody back."

It was the call she had given across the Sag all day, for a push. Nobody moved for it now. They were all behind the lamps already, the watch and the six together.

Dessa turned the book to the back.

Toren knew the page before she found it. The departure page. *41st day. Morning. Out through the north yard gate, for Caul Hill, on the Director's narrow yes.* The names. The Flasks. *Home line: D. Cole.* And *S. V.: pull the door*, in her hand, because he had said it to her at the gate.

And under that, six lines. Empty.

She held the pencil over the first one for a long time.

"I said I'd write *back*," she said. "When you were back."

"We're back," said Toren.

Dessa wrote.

*55th day. Dusk. Back through the Sag, on a push, in the fog. Held by the home line and the six together:*

Then she stopped, and looked at the six lines, and at the names she had to write in them. She looked down the lip at the lamp-line: at Katori at the top of the dark lane, and at Otto, and the yard lifter with his calf tied up, and the girl.

"There's not enough lines," said Dessa.

Toren looked at the page. So did Callie, from her knees at the table.

"I left six," said Dessa. "One for each. I said I'd not need more than six." She did not look up. "I need more than six."

She wrote in the six lines anyway, one to a line, the way she had said she would, in her small neat hand. Rook first, as *E. Marsh*, the name Hallet had for him. Then Dee, *D. Wren*. Then Pell, and Jab, and Callie, each by the one letter she had always written for them. Toren last, *T. Voss*.

Then, under the last line, on the white of the page below it where there were no lines at all, she went on writing. Katori. Ines. Otto. *The weighbridge girl.* *The two yard lifters (one bitten, the calf, the last push).* Wyck, *cleared tomorrow*.

And at the bottom, *D. Cole.*

She looked at it.

"That's everybody," she said.

"That's everybody," said Toren.

Dessa shut the book. She held it against her chest the way she had held it at the gate on the forty-first, and she did not open her eyes for a while.

"Go and put them in the beds," she said. "All of you. Pell and Rook first. Sowerby's been standing at the yard gate since the fourth hour with a face like the end of the world." She opened her eyes. "The watch'll hold the night. It's lying down."

"Dessa," said Toren.

"What."

"It was yours," said Toren. "The push. *Everybody back.* It was the call."

"It was yours," said Dessa. "You wrote it."

"I wrote a thing," said Toren. "You made it a call."

Dessa looked at him in the lantern light.

"Pell said that," she said. "Didn't he. At the north lip. I heard him. The whole Sag heard him." She almost smiled. It was the first time. "He's right. Go on. You're letting the cold in."

They went up the made road from the lip toward the yard gate in the last of the light. Otto had Duchess's head. The cart went in the middle with the walking wheel knocking. Pell sat on the high side, leaning. Rook lay on the canvas with his eyes shut, hot. Jab walked at the tailboard with his right hand held palm up and open, cooling in the air. Callie walked beside the cart with her hand on the rail and blood on her chin and the hook for a stick. Dee walked at the wheel. Toren walked at the back, the way he always did.

Behind them the lamps stayed lit along the east track, and the watch stayed on them.

At the yard gate there was a lamp, and under it a man in a white coat, standing with his arms folded. He had the face Dessa had said. Behind him, a long way up, in the long window on the second floor of the yard office, there was a light, and somebody small and square standing in it, not moving.

Toren counted them in through the gate. Out loud.

He did not say *out* this time. He did not say where to. There was nowhere to.

"In," he said. "Six."
