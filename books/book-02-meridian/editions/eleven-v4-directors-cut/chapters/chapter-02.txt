At home they had had the same words as anywhere. You heard them at the smoking racks and on the log jams, in arguments about whose cousin was better at what. His father's aunt had used one about him when he was fifteen. She had used it once and never again, because in her opinion saying it twice made a boy tiresome.

He wrote Flame.

Then, because it was not the whole truth, he added: That's what they'd have said at home. I don't know what you'd say here.

Under CAPABILITY there was a great deal of empty space. Jab looked at it. He thought about a woman leaning past his father on a gravel bar, four years ago, with a baby on her back. She had looked at the ugliest blade anybody had ever pulled out of a river stone. Well, it looks like you, she had said. That's the whole of it.

He wrote.

One shape. It's a fish knife. Heavy, fat in the belly, hook on the end. I've had it four years. I can hold it all day. I can light it when I need it sharp. The light sticks at the belly every time and always has.

I've used it on wood, rope, hide, fish, snow, and a log jam.

Tonight I used it on a hound. That's the first time I've used it on anything that was coming at me. I got most of it wrong. A woman who knew what she was doing killed four while I killed one.

The light doesn't make them come. It makes them go the other way. It's a fence, not a weapon. I found that out tonight, and somebody here probably knew it already.

He read it back. It filled the front. So he turned the card over.

The girl two places down had shut her eyes again. Her name was Callie, and she had told him about the washing so he would stop touching his collar. He had understood that about a minute after she said it. He was glad she was not watching now.

On the back he wrote:

There's a second thing and I haven't got a word for it.

It tells me when my brother's worse. It's right about four times in five. When it's wrong, it's wrong in the direction I want. So I don't ask it when I want an answer.

Twice it has done more than tell me. Once in the snow, two nights ago. Once tonight. Both times it came on its own. Both times I couldn't do it again afterward. I can't do it now.

He stopped. Then he pressed harder with the pencil.

It does not touch his leg. I want that written down, because somebody is going to ask me, and the answer is no. It has never gone within a hand's breadth of the bone and it never will.

Ivor Hallet read the front. Then he turned it over, which Jab had not expected. He read the back. Then he went to the top and read the whole of it again.

He did not look up for some time.

"Sir," he said at last.

"Yes."

"This second thing. Has anybody taught you anything about it?"

"There's nobody to teach me," said Jab. "Where I'm from there's four people with a door open. All four have got this one." He put his hand flat on his breastbone, over the place where the metal was not. "Nobody in my valley has ever had two."

Hallet laid the card face up on the desk, square to the edge.

"I'm going to have to have somebody else look at this," he said.

"I thought you might."

"Most people say something else at that point."

"Most people haven't just handed you a piece of paper saying they can't do the only thing on the back of it," said Jab.

Hallet made a small sound. He wrote something in his book that Jab could not see.

Then he pushed a paper band through the hatch. It went round the wrist and fastened. There was a red thread run through the paper.

"Conditional admittance," said Hallet. "Fourteen days, reviewable. A household with a clinical admission comes up faster than most. The station likes to know where it stands." He said that part flatly, as if it were printed somewhere and he was only reading it off. "Yard C, second row, hut twelve. It's empty. Four beds and a stove. I'll write you into it tonight. The bathhouse is shut, but there's hot water at the pump end and nobody will stop you. Visiting at the long building is in the afternoons."

"No," said Jab.

Hallet looked at him.

"My brother's going into a room tonight," said Jab. "I'm not sleeping in hut twelve. Who do I talk to?"

"That's not how—"

"I'm not asking you to let me in. I know you can't. I'm asking who I talk to."

Ivor Hallet sat behind his window with his cardigan buttoned wrong. He looked at a boy of seventeen standing in his socks, with a paper band on his wrist and a hole in his forearm, and his collar open.

"The woman at the desk inside the doors of the long building," he said. "Mrs Lask. She'll tell you visiting's in the afternoons. She'll mean it. She has said it to four hundred people and never once enjoyed it." He picked up the pen. "Then you'll ask her what you've just asked me. She'll tell you Doctor Sowerby. You won't get Doctor Sowerby tonight, because he'll be busy. So you'll ask her who else. Eventually somebody will get tired of the sound of you. They'll sit you on the bench inside the doors instead of the one outside. The bench inside is warm."

He wrote one line and blotted it.

"North up the main street to the end," he said. "Four hundred yards. There's three lamps on it and they go off at the first hour. You'll be under them when they do, so don't be frightened. There's a written reason."

He was under the second lamp when it happened.

The street was four hundred yards of wet concrete between long low buildings with turf roofs, with a drain down the middle and three lamps on poles, each putting down a circle of flat white light the colour of bone. Between them the dark was complete.

He was walking in his socks, because his boots were in a bin. The girl in the pen had lent him nothing and told him nothing. But when he stood up from the bench she had said, not opening her eyes, "Walk in the lit bits. The drain's got a grating that's loose between the second and the third."

So he walked in the lit bits.

Halfway through the second circle, all three lamps went out at once.

They did not flicker. They went, the way a held breath goes. The dark came down over the whole street and over him, and for a moment there was nothing to see at all.

Jab stood still, and found that he was not frightened; he was listening instead, the way you listen on a river at night when the fire has gone out.

He could hear the station: a stove ticking somewhere, a door, water running in a pipe underground a long way down, and nothing anywhere that came at a person on four legs.

He counted forty paces to where the third lamp had been, and stepped wide of the loose grating, and went on.

Mrs Lask told him visiting was in the afternoons.

She told him without one grain of malice. She was about fifty, with a cardigan and a list, and she stood behind a desk just inside the doors of a long low building. The corridor behind her went away in flat white light further than he could see.

"Who do I talk to?" said Jab.

"Doctor Sowerby."

"When?"

"Not tonight. He's in with your brother, and he'll be in with him a while."

"Then who else?"

Mrs Lask looked at him.

"There isn't an else," she said.

"Then I'll wait for him."

"Visiting," said Mrs Lask, "is in the afternoons."

"I know," said Jab. "Who do I talk to about waiting?"

It went on like that for a while. He was not rude and he never raised his voice, and he had nothing whatever to threaten anybody with. Somewhere around the third exchange he discovered that the only thing he had left to spend in the world was that he was still standing there.

At the second hour, Mrs Lask put down her pen.

"There's a bench outside the doors," she said. "I'm told it's very cold."

"All right."

"And there's one inside them, along that wall. It's for people waiting to be sent for." She wrote something on her list without looking up. "I haven't sent for you. I don't want you thinking I have."

Jab sat down on the bench inside the doors.

It was warm. There were pipes behind it.

He did not sleep.

He sat with his hands open on his knees and his back against the pipes, and he went through the ledger. He had done it every night on the road for eighteen days. His head would not do anything else.

Three twists of bark in a paper, somewhere in a bin. His boots in another bin. The frame in two pieces. The metal on its cord in a third bin, with a tag. A band on his wrist with a red thread. A hut with a number.

And a brother behind a door.

Somewhere in there, under the ledger, he went looking for the other thing, without meaning to, only to see.

He had felt it go out of him at the culvert head. It had not been a refusal. It had simply stopped, the way a bucket stops pouring when it is empty. He went down now to where it lived and found the bottom of the bucket. Dry. Not a finger of anything.

He had known it would be. He went and looked anyway, the way you go back to a snare you know is empty.

Don't ask it when you want an answer, he told himself.

He wanted an answer more than he had ever wanted anything. So he did not ask.

At some time in the small hours, a young woman in white came along the corridor with a tray. She stopped in front of him.

"Right," she said. "Arm."

She said it in a voice that expected no discussion. She cut the field dressing off his forearm and looked at what was under it. Then she cleaned out the hole, which hurt more than the hound had, and he watched her do it, because watching was better than not watching.

"That's a bite," she said.

"Yes."

"What bit you?"

"A hound."

"When?"

"About the fifth hour. Sixth."

She stopped with the needle in her hand and looked at him properly for the first time.

"You've been sitting on this bench for six hours with a hound bite," she said.

"I've been waiting to be sent for," said Jab.

She put eleven stitches in it. Then she found the other place, on his thigh, where something had taken a fold of his coat and a good deal of skin under it. She said a word he had not heard a nurse use before, and put six more in that.

"Is there anything else on you I ought to know about?"

Jab thought about it.

"No," he said. "That's the lot."

"You'd tell me."

"I'd tell you," said Jab. "I've got a rule about it."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she went away down the corridor and came back with a dry shirt and a blanket, and put the blanket over his knees, and went away again without saying anything at all.

Doctor Sowerby came out at the eighth hour with a folder under his arm and found him on the bench.

He was small and bald and in a hurry. Jab recognized the hurry at once, and trusted it at once, because it was the way his father's aunt had been in a hurry. It was the hurry of somebody with a great deal to do, none of which was going to get done by standing still and being kind about it.

He did not sit down.

"You're the brother."

"Yes."

"Right," said Doctor Sowerby. "I do this in the order I always do it in. The bad thing, then the good thing, then the bill. I'm told I do it too fast. Stop me whenever you like. I won't mind."

"Is he alive?"

Doctor Sowerby stopped.

"Yes," he said. "I should have started there, and I didn't. That's the second time this month. You may take it as read for the rest of this conversation: if anything of that kind changes, I'll say it before anything else."

He put the folder down on the bench and sat on the arm of it. It was as close to sitting down as he seemed to come.

"He's alive. He's warm. He's got fluid in him and the first of the medicine, and he's been asleep since the fourth hour. That's the best thing that's happened to him in a week. Now the bad thing. There's a lot of it.

"The break's the least of it. I want you to hear that first, because everybody gets it the wrong way round. It's a bad break in the shin bone. It's shifted. It's spent seven weeks knitting itself into a shape that isn't the shape of a leg. If that were all, I'd have it straightened this morning, and you'd have a cross child in a frame for three months, and I'd be talking to somebody else by now.

"The trouble is what got in through the skin when it happened. There's an infection in the bone. Not round it. In it. It's been there the better part of two months, and it has killed a piece of that bone. The piece is dead. Dead bone doesn't come back. Medicine can't reach into it, because there's nothing alive in there for the blood to carry medicine along. So it sits in his leg being a home for the infection. It'll go on doing that as long as it's in him. And while it's in him, he'll keep having the nights you've been sitting up through, until one of them doesn't stop."

Jab sat on the bench with his hands on his knees.

"How long," he said.

"How long what?"

"If nobody does anything. How long has he got."

"I'm not going to guess at that."

"Guess," said Jab.

Doctor Sowerby looked at him.

"Weeks," he said. "Possibly not many. And I'll say the other half of it. You got him here at about the last edge of the time when anything I say next is worth saying. I'm told you did it on your back. I'd rather say that to your face than have you find out in six months that everybody in this building said it to each other."

Jab did the arithmetic, because his head would not do anything else. It never had.

Four years old, and done growing at seventeen or eighteen: thirteen years, thirteen winters of coming back to this building, thirteen years inside a wall that reviewed a household on what it reckoned that household was worth.

He had carried his brother out of the home valley to save his life. He had. And the price was that neither of them was ever going home.

"Good," said Doctor Keel. "Now go and eat something, because you're grey. I do not operate on children whose people fall over in my corridor."
