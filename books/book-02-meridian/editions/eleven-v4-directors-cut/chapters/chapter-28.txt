Chapter 28 — Callie: The Still Point

[Steady travel narration; watchful tension beneath the words]

On the forty-fourth morning Callie walked into her own hook.

She had thought about it all night, between her watch and the light, lying on the canvas with the stone settled at the bottom of the well and the hook on the ground beside her where she could put her hand on it. It wants ground. The Ward on her tore the moment one foot came up. So she would not put it on her. She would put it in the steel, where it was cheap, where it had held a rack and a stair and the mouth of a pipe. She would carry the steel.

She tried it at the first halt, on the old line west of the water, with the wind coming across the bare fields from the west and bringing the grit with it again.

She held the hook out in front of her by the conduit, with the curve at the end of the four feet of pipe, up off the ground at the height of her waist. Curve outward. She reached for the stone and let it go into the steel.

The hook went pale.

It went fixed. It went exactly as fixed as it went across a doorway or a pipe mouth. It stopped being a thing she was holding and became a thing that was simply there, in the air, four feet in front of her, at the height of her waist, and nothing on the earth was going to move it. The grit hissed against the pale steel and went round it.

She took a step forward.

The hook did not.

She walked into it. She walked into the end of her own conduit, which was fixed in the air like an iron bar set in a wall. It stopped her at the ribs, on the left side, on the place that had been healing since the scarp. She heard herself make a noise. She stood with the conduit against her ribs and her hands still on it and her eyes watering.

Rook laughed.

He laughed out loud this time, not the low careful laugh. He was at the tailboard beside Jab, and he laughed and then put his hand on the tailboard and stopped, because laughing hurt his back. He laughed anyway, a little, into his collar.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. I'm sorry."

"You're not," said Callie.

"I'm not," Rook agreed. "I'm sorry about the ribs."

She let the Ward go. The hook came alive in her hands again, heavy and ordinary, and she stood holding it with her other hand pressed flat on her side.

Miss Wren had not laughed. She had watched it with the glass in her hand and her face perfectly still, and when Callie let go she came round the tailboard.

"Ribs," she said.

"Same place."

Miss Wren put her hand flat on Callie's side through the coat and pressed, not hard. Callie breathed in and it hurt, and breathed out and it hurt less.

"Bruised," said Miss Wren. "Not cracked again. You'll know about it for a week." She took her hand away. "How much?"

"Nothing. It was there for a breath."

"Then it cost you a rib and nothing else. That's cheap." She looked at the hook. "It's still. That's what it is. That's what it's always been. You put it in the steel and the steel stops. You can't walk a thing that's stopped."

"I know that now."

"I knew it yesterday," said Miss Wren. "I didn't tell you. You'd not have believed me till you'd walked into it."

She walked in front of the cart all morning and thought about it with her side aching.

The Ward was being still. That was what it was, in the steel. Not strong. Still. The rack had not been held up by strength. It had been held by the hook being somewhere and not moving. On her it was the same. It was her being still, and the moment she lifted a foot she was not still any more, and it tore.

So a Ward that moved was a Ward that was not a Ward.

She tried it in her head every way she could think of. Plant the hook, walk up to it, let it go, carry it forward, plant it again. That was what Toren had done with the lead box in the pen, walking it on its edge. But in the gap every time she let go there would be nothing at all. The handoff gap was two, with somebody under the load, and three without. Two, every few steps, with nothing. It would be a fence with holes in it, walking.

She tried it anyway, at the next halt, three times, with the hook. Plant, walk, let go, lift, plant. It worked the way a fence with holes works. The grit came through every time she lifted, in a gust, and hit Jab in the face.

"That's worse than nothing," said Jab mildly, wiping his eyes. "That's nothing with a wall in it."

"I know."

"How much?" said Miss Wren.

"A little. Every time I plant it, it takes a bit." Callie went down to look. "An eighth. Near enough. For three tries."

"Settled, seven eighths," said Miss Wren, writing. "You're down to less than a Handful and you've not got a thing for it."

"I've got that it doesn't work."

"That's a thing," said Miss Wren. "It's not a thing you can hold a stair with."

At noon Pell greased the axle.

He had been listening to it all morning. Callie had heard him listening. The right wheel had begun to sing at the hub, a thin dry note on every turn. It had started when the cart came out of the washout on the forty-second, and Pell had been walking at Duchess's head with his head a little on one side, the way her grandfather had held a sack when he was listening to it.

At the noon halt he got down on his knees on the stone of the old line beside the wheel. He took the grease pot out of the box under the driver's bench and a stick. He worked the grease in round the hub where the axle went through. He did it slowly, the way he did everything, with the wheel turning under his hand a little at a time as he rocked the cart.

Callie sat on the edge of the bed with her side aching and watched him.

She watched the wheel. She did not mean to. It was a thing that was moving, and she watched moving things. It was a big wheel, as high as her shoulder, with twelve spokes and an iron tyre. There was a puddle under it where the frost had melted on the stone in the sun. When Pell rocked the cart, the wheel rolled forward a hand's width into the puddle and back again. The bottom of it went into the water and out of the water.

"It's the washout," said Pell, to the hub. "She got grit in there in the stream. It'll sing till it's out."

"It goes into the puddle," said Callie.

Pell looked up at her.

"The wheel," said Callie. "When you rock it. It goes into the water and out again." She was looking at the bottom of the tyre, where it met the stone. "It doesn't splash. When you roll it forward it goes into the water and it doesn't splash at all."

"It wouldn't," said Pell.

"Why not?"

Pell sat back on his heels with the grease stick in his hand and looked at her the way he had looked at her on the first morning, when she had told him to take the high side of the culvert. The way you look at somebody who has asked the right question.

"Because a wheel doesn't move where it touches," he said.

Callie looked at him.

"The bit of the tyre that's on the ground," said Pell. "Right there. Where it's in the water. That bit's not going anywhere. The top of the wheel's going forward twice as fast as the cart. The middle's going as fast as the cart. The bottom—" He put his hand flat on the stone beside the tyre where it touched. "The bottom's still. For a moment. Every bit of it, in turn. It comes down onto the ground and it stops, and then the next bit comes down and stops, and the one before it lifts off. It never slides. That's why it doesn't splash." He put the grease stick back in the pot. "That's all a wheel is. A lot of still, one after another. My father told me that when I was ten, and I thought he was mad, and he wasn't."

Callie sat on the edge of the stone bed of the old line and did not move.

A lot of still, one after another.

She looked at the hook across her knees. At the curve of it, a foot of it, the grain hook her grandfather had used at the hopper gate, bent like a new moon. She looked at the bottom of the curve.

"Pell," she said.

"Mm."

"If I rolled it. The hook. On the ground, on the curve, like the bottom of a wheel. The bit that was on the ground—"

"—would be still," said Pell. "For a moment. Then the next bit." He looked at the hook, and then at her. "I don't know anything about your Ward, Callie. I know about wheels."

"The Ward wants still," said Callie. "It wants ground."

"Then give it both," said Pell, and went back to the hub.

She tried it before the glass ran out.

She stood up on the old line with her side aching and turned the hook over in her hands. She held it by the end of the conduit, with the four feet of pipe going down and away from her at a slant and the curve at the bottom, the way you might hold a long-handled hoe. She put the curve on the stone. Not the point of it. The back of it, the outside of the bend, the way the bottom of a tyre sits on a road.

She pushed.

It rolled. It did not roll the way a wheel rolls, all the way round. It was only a foot of curve. But it rocked forward along its own bend, and the part of it that touched the stone moved along the bend from the back of the curve toward the front, like the bottom of a rocking chair. The rest of the hook went forward with her hands.

She did it again. She pushed it forward and let it rock along its curve, and when it came to the front of the curve she lifted it a hand's width and set the back of the curve down again, further on. Rock, lift, set. Rock, lift, set. Like a man walking on one long foot.

Then she reached for the stone and did not put the Ward in the hook.

She put it in the part of the hook that was touching the ground.

She did not know if she could. She had never asked the Ward to go into part of the steel and not the rest. She had always asked it for the hook, and got the hook, all of it, fixed. But she looked at the place where the curve met the stone, the way Toren had looked at the heel of his spike in the first lane. She kept her eyes on it, the small bright line where the back of the bend sat on the grey. She asked for the Ward there.

It came.

She felt it come up out of the stone at the bottom of the well and down through her arms and along the pipe and into the curve. Into the bottom of the curve, where it touched. The steel there went pale. Not all of it. A hand's width of the bend, where it was on the ground.

And from that hand's width something stood up.

It stood up off the curve of the hook like a sheet of frost standing on its edge. It was pale, and she could see through it, and it went up from the steel where the steel touched the stone to the height of her shoulder. It was a wall, a short wall. It stood on the outside of the curve, the side the bend faced, and it leaned a little over nothing.

She pushed.

The hook rocked forward along its curve. The pale hand's width at the bottom went with it, sliding along the bend as the touching part moved. It stayed where the steel met the stone, and the wall stood up off it and went with it. It did not tear. It did not tear because there was always a part of the steel that was on the ground and still. The Ward was always in the still part.

She walked.

She walked one step, rocking the hook forward on its curve, and the wall went with her. Two steps. She lifted the hook at the front of the curve and set it down again on the back of the bend a hand further on. For the length of a breath the wall shivered and thinned, and did not go, because she set the back down before the front came up.

Three steps. Four.

The grit came across from the west in its low brown haze and hit the pale wall and hissed against it and went round, and did not get to her.

Five. Six.

"Callie," said Miss Wren.

She did not look round. She did not dare.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

On the tenth the back of the curve came down on a loose stone in the bed of the old line. It skidded. The hook rocked sideways instead of forward, and for a moment there was no part of it that was still on the ground. Only a part of it that was sliding.

The wall went out like a blown lamp.

She stood on the old line with the hook in her hands and the grit in her face.

"Ten," said Toren, from the tailboard. He said it in the voice he used for a number he was writing down. "That was ten."

Then Rook said, "Again," and Miss Wren said, "How much?" in the same breath. Pell, on his knees by the wheel with the grease pot, said, "Mind the loose stones," without looking up.

Callie went down to look.

"About a tenth," she said. "For ten steps." She heard it. It was more than the hook held still across a door. It was much less than on her. "Somewhere in between."

"Settled, three quarters and a bit," said Miss Wren. She wrote it. "Somewhere in between is a price, Callie. It's not a number. Again, and we'll find the number."

She did it again. She did it five more times that afternoon, walking in front of Duchess on the old line with the grit coming across, and she found the number, and the other numbers that went with it.

She found that it wanted the stone of the old line. On the packed grey stone of the bed the curve rocked true and the wall stood. Where the bed had gone to earth and grass, the curve sank a little and rocked slow, and the wall thinned. Where it had gone to loose sand, at a place where the old line crossed a dry stream on a bank of it, the curve skidded on the first step and there was no wall at all.

"Footing," said Rook, from the tailboard. "Same as everything. Same as the strut."

She found that it wanted her slow. At the frame's pace, at Jab's pace, it held. When she walked faster the curve came off the ground at the front before the back was down, and there was a moment of nothing, and the wall shivered and went. She tried to run with it once. She got two steps.

"Speed," said Rook.

She found that it stood on one side only. It stood off the outside of the curve, the side the bend faced, and nowhere else. If she turned the hook to face the other way, the wall stood on the other side. It did not go round. It was not a room or a dome or the sheet the day holder had laid across the whole bottom of the Sag. It was a wall, and it went where the curve was pointing.

"It's a lee," said Pell, at the fourth halt. He was standing behind it with his collar down for the first time all day and the grit going past a foot from his face. "That's all. It's the lee side of a hedge. It'll keep what's blowing off whatever's behind it. It'll not keep anything off that comes round the end."

"How much?" said Miss Wren.

"A twentieth," said Callie. "For fourteen steps."

"Half the price for more steps," said Miss Wren, writing. "It's getting cheaper."

"I'm getting better at it."

"That's what cheaper is," said Miss Wren.

At the fifth halt she put people in it.

She had not done it on purpose until then. The wall had stood beside her and kept the grit off her, and anybody who happened to be walking close behind it had got the lee of it too. Pell had, and Jab once. Now she wanted to know.

"Jab," she said. "Walk behind it. Close. On this side."

Jab came off the tailboard and walked behind her, a pace back, on the lee side, with the frame on his back. She rocked the hook forward on its curve along the stone and the wall stood up off it. She could feel it stand. She could feel it standing between the grit and Jab's face, and she knew where his face was, because she could see his shoulders out of the corner of her eye.

"Rook," she said. "You too."

Rook came. He walked beside Jab, on his left. Callie felt the wall change.

She did not ask it to. It changed on its own. It got longer at the back, to put Rook's shoulders behind it as well as Jab's. As it got longer it got thinner, like dough being pulled. She could feel the grit hissing against it harder, as if the wind had found where it was weakest.

She held it for eight steps. On the ninth it tore at the far end, behind Rook, and the grit came round the end and hit him in the side of the head.

"That's two," said Rook, wiping his ear. "Two's shorter."

"Two's shorter," said Callie.

She tried the cart. She knew before she tried it that it was stupid, and she tried it anyway, because she had to know. She stepped out wide on the windward side of Duchess and the cart. She rocked the hook forward on the stone of the bed and asked the wall to stand up off the curve and go along the whole side of them, horse and cart and all, to the tailboard where Jab walked.

It went.

It went long and thin and pale, along Duchess's shoulder and her flank and the whole side of the cart, like a sheet of ice on a pond in the first night of frost. It held for four steps. On the fifth it went from end to end all at once, like a pane going in a window.

Callie stopped. She put the back of her hand to her lip, and it came away red.

"Nose," said Miss Wren, already beside her with a cloth.

"I know."

"Head back. No. Head forward. Pinch it." Miss Wren held the cloth. "That's the widening. Same as the twentieth, with Wyck. Eleven seconds and a nosebleed." She looked at Callie over the cloth. "You can't have the whole cart. You can't have all of us. I'd like that said out loud, by you, now, so it's said."

"I can't have all of you," said Callie, through the cloth.

"How many can you have?"

Callie thought about it with her head forward and the blood going into the cloth.

"Two," she said. "Close. On good stone. At his pace." She nodded at Jab. "For a while. Not long. Or one and the frame. Or—" She stopped. "It's who's behind it. Every body I add, it's thinner. It's shorter. I've got to choose."

"Then choose," said Miss Wren. "Every time. Before you put it up. Who's inside." She took the cloth away and looked at it. "That's the whole of it. That's what nobody who's ever thrown up a wall over a room has had to learn, because they could have the room. You can't. You'll have to decide who's worth the lee."

She did the third one alone, with nobody in it, to see.

It was a long way off. It was at the edge of the world, west, where the land went flat and grey toward the last of the light. It was the only thing on the whole of that flat that was not flat. Callie did not know how else to say it. It was the end of the day, and the sky in the west was going the colour it went, grey and pale yellow and then grey again, with long thin clouds lying across it. And over the hill, over the flat top, there was a place where the sky did not go the colour it went. It was a patch, round, the size of her thumb held up at the end of her arm. It was darker than the sky round it. Not black. Bruised. The clouds that lay across the sky went into it and came out of the other side of it not quite where they should have been, the way a stick looks bent where it goes into water. Round the edge of it the light was a colour she did not have a word for. It was a little green, a little grey, like the light in the bottom of a pond.

It did not move. She watched it and it did not move. But she had the feeling, watching it, that it was not still either. The way you have the feeling that a sleeping animal is breathing before you can see its side go up and down.

"Two days," said Pell.

Nobody else said anything at all.

Callie read the land in front of her, because it was what she did, and because she could not look at the sky over the hill for long.

The land between the rise and the hill was flat and bare and long. The old line went across it straight as a ruler, a thin grey stripe with its posts along it. And in the low places, all across the flat, there was grey. It lay in the hollows and along the bottoms of the ditches and against the sides of things, in long soft drifts. It lay the way snow lies a week after a fall, when the rest has gone and only the drifts are left in the places the wind put them. But it was not white. It was grey. It was the grey she had lifted every morning of her life off the plain, the grey in the jars on Jab's back.

There was a great deal of it.

"That's ash," she said. "In the low places. All the way to the hill."

"Lying where it fell," said Rook.

"Nobody's lifted it," said Callie. "Nobody's been near it to lift it."

Beside her, Jab had put his hand on his coat, over the left side. He did not say anything. He was looking at the patch of wrong sky over the flat top of the hill, and his face was very still, the way it went when he put his attention down on a table and took his hands off it.

"Jab," said Callie.

"I don't know," said Jab. He did not look away from the hill. "It's a long way. I don't know what it is. I don't feel anything." He was quiet. "I don't like it."

"Nobody likes it," said Pell, and said a word to Duchess, and walked on down the rise toward the flat. Callie went out twenty paces in front of him with the hook and read the road for the wheels. She did not look up at the sky again until it was dark, and then she could not see it.
