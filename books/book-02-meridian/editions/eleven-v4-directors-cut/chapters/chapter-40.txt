Chapter 40 — Graded

[Measured, lucid narration; give every name, number, and ledger turn room to land]

Jab drew them at the river, all of them, alone.

There was nobody else to do it. Dee was at her floor. Rook was off, and hot, and lying on the canvas with his eyes open. So Jab stood on the gravel below the pump house in the last of the sun, with a jar of the lifter's ash against his left palm and his right hand flat on one chest after another, and drew. He had Toren's jar first, the half one. When it went thin he said jar before it was dry, and Callie took his own second jar out of the frame and pulled the stopper for him.

Callie held the counter for him too. She held it in her right hand, because her left arm would not go out from her side without the ribs saying so, and she held it at arm's length to each chest in turn and said the number, and Jab drew until she said a smaller one.

"Ninety," she said, at Toren. And later, "Twenty." And later, "Twelve. Off."

He did Toren. He did Callie, with his palm flat on the inside of her wrist, because the ribs were where the rest of it would have been. He did Dee.

He did Dee longest.

She stood in front of him with her hands at her sides and let him. Her face was white, and for the first time since the gate it was not steady. It had been steady the whole time under the hill, over the jar and the glass and Jab's back, nineteen times. It had stopped being steady on the bridge.

"A hundred and ten," said Callie, at Dee's chest. "Ten breaths."

Jab did not say anything. He drew. He held it high and vented it into the gravel downstream of the pump house, away from the water, onto the road's ground and not the river's, and went back on.

"Sixty," said Callie, after a long time. "Thirty. Fourteen."

"Off," said Dee. Her voice was thin. "That's enough. That's the room."

Jab came off.

Then he stood with his hands held out a little from his sides, the way he had stood in the cut at the wash, and nobody took them. There was nobody to take them.

"I'll vent," he said. "Long."

He went down the gravel to the far end, past the stone with the chalk cross on it and the two hot jars from the day before standing beside it, and knelt there, and put both palms flat on the stones. He stayed like that while the sun went down behind the hill.

Callie graded the jars at the fire.

Nobody asked her to. She took the counter and went to the frame where it stood against the pump house wall on its two flat stones, and she held the counter to each jar in the rack at a hand's width, one at a time, and waited ten breaths for each. And she said each one out loud, because that was what you did with a number.

"The fence's last whole one," she said. "Eight. The room. It's clean. Eight in it." She moved along. "The fence's fourth. The one under the line. Eight. Clean. Five in it."

She weighed it on her palm. Five, near enough.

Then the lifter's.

"Jab's first," she said. "Nine. Clean. Full. Eight." She moved along. "Jab's second. Eight. Clean. Six in it. He's drawn two out of it at the river."

"Mine," said Toren, from the fire.

"Yours is empty," said Callie. "It went on the four of us first. It was only a half." She moved along. "Mine. The first." She held the counter to it for ten breaths, and then for ten more, because she wanted to be sure. "Ten. It's clean. Full. Eight."

She came to the last one.

"My second," she said. "Nineteen."

She held it up to the fire and looked through the glass at the grey in the bottom. It was grey. But there were darker grains in it, fine, like pepper in flour, all through.

"It's dirty," she said. "There's grit in it. The hill. It came up with the rest. I felt it come, and I didn't know it was coming." She put it in the rack apart from the others, in the socket at the end where jar B had been, with the broken glass taken out of it now. "Four in it. Dirty. It'll burn wrong."

"It'll burn wrong," said Dee, from the canvas by Rook. "It'll not go on anybody's back unless there's nothing else. Write it dirty."

Toren wrote it.

Callie stood by the frame and said the whole of it. She said it the way her mother said the store book, flat, for somebody else, the way she had said it at every halt on the old line and every night since.

"Inside," she said. "The fence's, thirteen. Five in the fourth, eight in the last. The lifter's, twenty-two, clean. Four, dirty. Thirty-five clean. Four dirty." She stopped. "Home, twenty-four. On the cart. Not touched. The road jar's empty. The vent jars are under the hill, and two by the stone."

"Say what we went in with," said Toren.

"Forty," said Callie. "Clean. On the forty-seventh."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Thirty-five and four," said Pell, from the cart. "Against forty." He was quiet. "Five short, clean. For two boxes."

"And three under the line, inside two turns," said Dee. "That goes in front of her with our names on it. Not as a thing we'll do again. As a thing we did." She did not stop there. "And my floor. And his." She nodded at Toren. "And her ribs. And his hand." She did not look toward the far end of the gravel, where Jab was still kneeling. "And the rest of it on the floor of that room."

"How much is on the floor of that room?" said Pell.

Callie thought about it. She thought about the long slope of grey from the carrier to the threshold, knee-deep in the middle, and the bank of it along the corridor wall in the shape of a rope, and the tray full of it the whole length of the corridor into the dark, and the stair.

"More than we lifted," she said. "Four times. Five." She shook her head. "I don't know. It's not a number. It's more than we could lift in a day, with nobody hurt."

"Then write that," said Toren. "More than we could lift."

"What was it?" said Pell.

He said it to Rook. He said it the way he would ask the weighbridge about a jar, not unkindly. Rook was lying on his side on the canvas with his face to the fire and his eyes half shut.

"The Director'll want a word for it," said Pell. "On the paper. They grade them. A Flask, or a Barrel. She'll want to know which." He looked across the river at the hill in the dusk. "It came along a rope and had its hand in Jab. It mended everything anybody did to it every thirteen breaths. It had the whole hill." He looked at Rook. "That's a Barrel, surely."

Rook opened his eyes.

"A Barrel's a thing on its own," he said.

He said it slowly, because he was hot and his mouth was dry. Dee held the cup for him, and he drank, and went on.

"A Barrel comes out of a tear all by itself, and it's big on its own account. What's in it is its own." He looked at the fire. "That wasn't big. That was a Flask. Maybe. A Flask with its hand in everybody's pocket." He shut his eyes again. "All that grey on the floor wasn't it. It was everybody else's. The husks'. The hounds' at the wash. The jar that broke on the rail. Twenty years of whatever came out of that sky and fell on the hill and lay there. It went and got it all, and put it on, like a coat that's too big." He was quiet. "Write it a Flask. Write the rest as what it had."

Toren wrote it. Its own: a Flask (R.). What it had: more than we could lift.

"You'd know," said Pell.

"I'd know," said Rook. "It had my job."

Dee was sick in the night.

Callie heard it before she saw it. She was on the first watch with Pell, because Pell was awake and Callie could not lie down without the ribs, and she was standing by the cart wheel in the dark with the hook for a stick. Somebody got up off the gravel by the wall, fast, and went down toward the river and was sick, and it went on for a long time.

Jab was already there. He had not been asleep either. He went down the gravel to Dee in the dark, and Callie could see the two of them against the grey of the water: Dee on her knees with her hand on a stone, and Jab crouched beside her.

"It's that room," said Dee, when she could. Her voice carried along the water. "It's twenty turns in that room after, at a hundred and thirty, with nobody drawing me. It's that room coming out." She was sick again. "It'll be that room for a day."

"Hold still," said Jab.

"I'm holding still."

"You're not. You're shaking."

"I'm allowed to shake," said Dee. "I'm not allowed to be drawn at my floor. There's nobody to draw you after."

"There's nobody to draw me now," said Jab. "You're busy. I can feel it from here. Hold still."

Callie did not hear what Dee said to that. She saw Dee sit back on her heels on the stones, and she saw Jab put his palm flat on Dee's back, between the shoulders, and his other hand on the jar he had brought down with him, and bend his head.

Callie watched them for a long time. Then she looked away, because it was not hers to watch.

Pell was counting the sky.

He sat on the cart with his back to the rack and his leg out, the way he had sat for all three days, with the sheet on his knee that he could not see to mark in the dark. He was looking across the river at the hill. Over its flat top there were no stars, and round the edge of the place with no stars there was the light the colour of the bottom of a pond.

"It's not ten and three," said Pell.

"I know," said Callie. "We saw it at the top of the road."

"I've been counting it since dusk." He did not take his eyes off it. "It's not anything. Eight, and five. Then twelve and two. Then fourteen and nothing, and it goes again. It's thickening and thinning, the same as it was, but there's no count to it. It's like a man breathing in his sleep who's having a bad dream." He was quiet. "It had a count for three days. Every one. I'd have put my name to it."

"It had the lifter," said Callie.

"Or the lifter had it," said Pell. "Jab doesn't know which. I don't. I'd like to know which." He moved his leg on the boards and made a small sound. "Write it down. Tomorrow. That woman'll want it more than the boxes, I think. Or as much."

"She'll want the boxes."

"She'll want the boxes," Pell agreed. "And she'll want to know why a thing that counted to thirteen for three days stopped counting the afternoon somebody put a point in a heap of grey under it." He looked at Callie in the dark. "I wouldn't like to be the one who has to decide what to do about that. With a Sag held and a machine that can see."

Callie looked at the sky over the hill. It thickened for a long time, and thinned, and thickened again at once. She did not count it. It had nothing to count.

[Steady, watchful narration; let distance and reserves register]

She thought about the door.

She had not let herself think about it all afternoon. There had been the stair, and the road, and the bridge, and the river, and the jars. Now there was only the dark and Pell and the cart wheel, and she thought about it.

She had held it. She had stood in it with her back to the room for twenty turns and more, with the stone going out of her into a door and a bit until it was gone, and then her own, and a husk pushing against the pale a foot from her face. She had never once seen what was happening behind her. She had heard it. She had heard the sack hit the wall. She had heard Toren say holding with his teeth together, and Jab say busy, and Dee say off, over and over, and the glass going over. She had heard the line said. She had heard two turns, and said I've got two turns, I've not got three, and she had not known, saying it, whether two would be enough. And she would not have been able to do anything about it if they were not.

She had looked round once. Rook had said she would.

She had turned her head and seen Jab on the floor with his hand out, and Dee behind him with the jar, and the room behind that, grey. And Jab had looked at her and said busy. Just that. It was the word he said to tell you he was working and how long you had to hold.

And she had turned her head back and held.

She had not carried them. That was what she understood, standing by the cart wheel in the dark with her arm across her side. She had carried people all her life. She had carried her mother across four hundred miles in her head, every breath, every step, counting her. She had held a wagon on a hook with her mother in it. On the lip at the wash she had held a lee off her own stone over two bodies and taken a third in and out of it with her eyes. She had thought that was what it was for: holding people.

Under the hill she had held a door, and the three of them had done the work behind her back. She had not been holding them. She had been holding the edge of them. And they had held the rest.

Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.

When her mother said it, she had thought it meant letting go. It did not mean letting go. It meant choosing what to hold, and holding that, all of it, and trusting the people on the other side of it to hold their own.

She had not known it would feel like this. It did not feel like forgetting. It felt like the ache in the palm after lifting, when you have put down a thing that was worth carrying.

In the morning Dee measured them.

She did it sitting on a stone by the cold fire. She was not inside a fence, so she was allowed, and her legs would not have done it standing. Her face was grey, and there were marks under her eyes like bruises, but her hands were steady again, and they all watched the hands go to each wrist in turn.

"Your own's back," she said to Toren. "A turn. Not two. It'll be two by tonight if you do nothing."

"I'll do nothing."

"You'll carry a wheel," said Dee. "But not today." She did Callie. "Your own's back. Half. You slept?"

"No."

"You slept an hour," said Dee. "Jab said. Your own's half." She put her palm flat against Callie's side, over the coat, not pressing. "And that's cracked. Two, I think. The same two." She took her hand away. "I'll strap it. You'll not lift anything heavier than a counter for a week. You'll not lean on that hook."

"I'll lean on it to walk."

"You'll lean on it to walk," Dee agreed. "That's what it's for this week."

She did Jab. She held his palms in both of hers, turned up. The white place on the heel of the right one was red round its edges now, and shiny, and there was a small blister at one side of it.

"They're warm," she said.

"I vented half the night."

"I know you did. I watched you. I was being sick, and I watched you." She put her palms flat on his, palm to palm, and they saw her find it. She drew. It was the watch taking it off the one who had worked, the way she had done it in the cut at the wash, and her face went quiet while she did it. "There," she said. "Cool."

"You're at your floor," said Jab.

"I'm a finger over it," said Dee. "I slept too. After you'd finished with me." She let go of his hands. "That's enough for a watch. It's not enough for anything else. Not today."

She did her own last, with two fingers on the inside of her crooked wrist.

"A finger," she said. "Over." She wrote it.

Rook was holding. That was Dee's word for it, and she said it the way she had said it about the post on the arch. The red round the stitches had not gone further out, and it had not come in. He was hot, but not as hot as he had been on the first morning, and he drank the third of Sowerby's papers in boiled water and made the face.

"One left," said Dee.

"Keep it," said Rook. "For whoever's worse."

"You're the worst."

"Then keep it for when I'm worse than this," said Rook. He looked at her across the cup. "You were sick."

"I was the watch," said Dee. "Nobody watched me." She took the cup. "I'll not do it that way again."

"Yes you will," said Rook. "If it's the same thing again, you'll do it the same way." He shut his eyes. "I'd like it written down that I said so."

"I'll write that you said so," said Dee. "I'll not write that you were right."

He laughed. It was the low careful laugh of a man with a hole in his back. Dee's mouth moved at the corner, once, and stopped.

They decided at the cart.

Pell had the atlas open on his knee, and Toren had the paper. Everybody else stood, or sat on what they were allowed to sit on, round the tail of the cart, where the carrier sat on its board behind the home jars with two live boxes in it and the lid in its groove.

"Say the clock," said Toren.

"The forty-ninth," said Pell. "Today. The cycle's the fifty-fourth. Five days." He put his finger on the atlas. "It was six days from the gate to here, with the siding. With the road whole."

"It isn't whole," said Callie.

"It isn't whole," said Pell. "The crown's gone into the wash, and the post, and the arch. There's a gap in the embankment four paces wide, with the wash running through the bottom of it and the drifts heaped on both sides." He looked at the three of them. "North round the head of the drifts, toward the hill. Or south through the marsh to the river and down this bank. Two days more, either way, and there's no bed for the cart on either. That's what I said on the forty-sixth. It's still true."

"That's the fifty-seventh," said Toren. "Home."

"Or later," said Pell. "With me on the cart and not walking."

"And the other thing?" said Toren. "What you said at the fire. That it'll take people across the bottom on foot."

"On foot," said Pell. "Through the drifts. Quick, and wash after. It won't take the cart." He looked at the cart under him, and the carrier on it, and the three home jars. "Nothing takes a cart across that."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Nothing takes a cart across that," he said again, slowly. He put his hand flat on the boards of the cart bed. "A cart's not a thing. It's a lot of things. Two wheels. An axle. A bed. Two shafts. Pins." He looked up. "A cart in pieces goes across in pieces."

Nobody said anything.

"The wheels roll," said Pell. "You roll them, down one side of the gap and up the other. They're as high as her shoulder." He nodded at Callie. "The axle's two men. The bed's four, or two and a horse with a rope on it. The shafts are one each. The carrier's on its board, and a board slides on a drift. It's flour." He looked at Toren. "And there's a bank on each side of the gap, loose. And you set steps on a loose bank at the wash. On stones."

"I set them on my own," said Toren. "The last of it."

"You'll have a day's worth by tomorrow," said Pell. "Miss Wren says two turns by tonight. And there's a jar of that thing's ash to settle, if it's clean."

"And you?" said Toren.

"I'm a bed and two poles and a bit of canvas," said Pell. "Two of you. I'll not say anything about the camber."

"Price it," said Toren.

He said it to Callie. She stood by the tail of the cart with her arm across her side and the counter in her other hand, and she priced it out loud, because that was the rule, and because saying it was how she found out whether it was true.

"The drifts were eighty, ninety at the foot of the bank," she said. "In the wind. With no wind, lying still, they'll be less. Sixty, seventy. Where the crown went in, where it's turned over, more. A hundred." She looked east, toward the ridge and the cut and the wash behind it, a mile off. "An hour. For all of it. Down, across, up, the pieces, and Pell, and the horse, twice over for the wheels. An hour at eighty. All of us, and the horse."

"How much?" said Dee.

"Ten," said Callie. "Maybe twelve. For the drawing after, in the cut on the far side. And the settling for Toren's steps." She looked at the frame against the wall. "Out of the lifter's."

"Out of the lifter's," said Toren.

"Out of the lifter's," said Callie. "It's got twenty and a half clean. A Handful and a half went on Miss Wren in the night." She did not look at Dee. "It'd have ten and a half left, and the fence's thirteen, and the dirty four. And home, twenty-four, not touched." She stopped. "That's half the lifter's, for one hour in the wash. The rest is for the road." Then she went on. "We'd be on the old line east of the gap by the fiftieth, with the cart back together. And it's five days from there, on the road we know. With the siding." She did the last sum. "The fifty-fifth. Home."

"Not the fifty-fourth," said Toren.

"Not the fifty-fourth," said Callie. "Nothing's the fifty-fourth. The fifty-fourth was gone before we crossed the bridge."

Toren looked at the paper for a long time.

"It's the fifty-fifth, or the fifty-seventh," he said. "Or later." He looked up. "That's Sowerby dosing her off her blood, badly, a day past the cycle. Or three. And whatever the fifty-fourth does, with a man guessing." He did not say Gran. "And ten Handfuls of that thing's ash, and an hour in the drifts, and Callie's ribs, and Dee a finger over her floor."

"Say which," said Dee.

"The wash," said Toren. "In pieces. Tomorrow, first light, if the wind's down. Not today. Nobody carries a wheel through a drift at their floor."

"Not today," said Dee, and wrote it. "And if the wind's up?"

"Then we sit at the cut and wait for it to drop," said Toren. "And I'll write what it costs."

Pell shut the atlas.

"Then today we go to the cut," he said. "It's a mile. The cart'll do that whole. It's this side of the gap." He looked at Duchess, tied to the wheel with her rope halter, eating. "She'll pull it to the cut. And tomorrow she'll walk through a drift, because somebody she knows is walking in front of her. And then she'll hate every one of us for a week."

"She'll be right," said Rook, from the canvas.

"She's always right," said Pell. "She's a horse."

They loaded the cart.

The frame went on the cart this time, in the rack behind the driver's bench beside the home jars, because Jab's palm was blistered and Dee said he would not carry a frame of jars a mile on a blister. Rook went on the cart on the canvas, on his side, with his back off the boards. Pell stayed where he was. The carrier sat behind them on its board with the lid in its groove.

Toren went round the cart once, the whole of it, and counted what was on it. Then he counted them.

"Pell," he said. "Dee. Rook." He stopped on Rook the way he always did, longest. "Jab. Callie." He looked at the mare. "Six. And a horse."

He wrote it on the back of the signed paper, standing at the pump house wall.

49th. The pump house. Out: 6. To the cut. The wash tomorrow, in pieces (Pell's). Home the 55th, if the wind's down.

He looked at it.

"Dessa left six lines," he said. "At the foot of the page. For back."

"I know," said Callie.

"I'll not write in them yet," said Toren. He folded the paper and put it in his coat beside the plate.

Callie went out in front. She did it without being asked, the way she had done it on the first morning on the old north road: twenty paces in front of Duchess, with the hook for a stick and her left arm strapped across her side under her coat. She began to read the road for the wheels.

It was the road they had come on, three days ago, at dusk: the old line going up from the river on its long bare slope to the ridge, and the cut through the ridge, and the wash behind it. She knew it. She read it anyway, for the wheels. She read the loose stones on the bed, and the place where the frost had heaved it, and the mark where the cart had come down it on the forty-sixth with Pell on it and his foot on a coil of rope.

At the top of the slope, at the mouth of the cut, she stopped and turned round.

She looked back once. Everybody does.

The river lay low and dark in its stony bed, with the pump house on its near bank, roofless, and the two broken pipes going into the water. The footbridge went across on its posts, with two grey poles of wrack wood still lying along it over the crack. At the far end stood the gate, with a twist of green wire through its chain, left so it would come.

Beyond the gate there was the yard, and the road going up the hill in its long Z, and the long building standing along the rim with its windows black. There was the drum behind it with its broken top. And over the whole of the top of the hill there was the sky.

The edge of it thickened. She did not count it. It thinned, and thickened at once, and thinned for a long time. It had no count. It was a thing breathing in its sleep, having a bad dream.

Under it, in a room under a hill, there was more ash lying on a floor than she could have lifted in a week. There were hundreds of boxes, and a third live one talking to the room, and three stoppered jars with a chalk cross beside them. She would put the number of every one of them on the Director's table.

In the cart behind Duchess there were two.

It was the forty-ninth day. At home it would be the ninth hour, near enough. Her mother would have walked to the ward for her pills at the eighth hour, with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. Senna would be on the fourth bed on the right, with five days to the narrow bed and the machine that sang flat. Tonk would be in the fifth bed, with a cord round his fist. They were all breathing without her. They had been breathing without her for eight days, and nothing had happened to any of them because she was not there counting.

She did not know what would happen on the fifty-fourth. She did not know if the wind would be down tomorrow.

She knew what was in the cart, and what was in the frame, graded. She knew who was on the cart and who was walking. She knew the road to the cut, and she knew the wash.

She turned round and went on into the cut, twenty paces in front of the horse, reading the road for the wheels. Behind her Duchess came on with the cart, with Pell and Rook riding on it beside the boxes and the home jars, and Jab and Dee walking at the wheel. At the back, where he always walked now, Toren was counting them.
