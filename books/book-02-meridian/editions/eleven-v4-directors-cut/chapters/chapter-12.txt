Chapter 12 — Toren: From the Ground Up

[Focused, grounded narration; keep physical instruction clear]

By the third morning the yard had a shape.

Toren could see it from the corner of the east shed, where he left the chair for Senna, out of the wind with its back to the stove pipe. He saw it the way he saw everything now, as weight, and where it went. The first lane ran along the shed wall for sixty paces, and Rook had spent two afternoons putting things in it out of the scrap bins: three posts sunk in the cinders at uneven distances, a length of old rope strung between two of them at the height of a man's hip, a timber frame at the far end made from two uprights and a crossbar and four iron brackets that did not match. Nothing in the first lane was straight, and nothing was where you expected it. Rook said that was the point.

The second lane ran to the sand heap by the berm. It was old ballast sand, grey and coarse, dumped there when the wagon lane closed and never moved, and Wyck had claimed it the way a dog claims a step. He sat on top of it most of the day with his bar across his knees and his strapped arm across his chest, and told people how to stand, and how to fall, and where they were putting their weight, and why that was stupid. There were four straw sacks at the foot of the heap and a post wrapped in old blanket. Otto spent most of his mornings there, falling over on purpose.

The third lane was the shed. It was not a lane at all, but Rook had painted a line to its door anyway, so that it would have one. Inside it was the stove and the cot and the table with the brass scale, and at the far end the bucket under the drip, going tick. Dessa's table stood against the wall outside the door, where the light was, with the big book open on it and a stone on each page to keep the wind off.

That was all of it. Three lines of whitewash on the cinders, some scrap, a heap of sand and a shed that leaked. Toren had been in it for two days. He had already found that he walked differently when he crossed from one lane into the next, and that he could not have said why.

"Two," said Dee. "Know how much of yours there is."

She had a glass in her hand. It was small, the height of her palm, with a brass frame round it and grey sand inside, and when she turned it over the sand began to run through the waist of it in a thin grey thread.

"One turn's a minute," she said. "Near enough. I've had it since I was fourteen. It's the only thing I took off the wire that wasn't on my back."

Toren saw Rook, on the shed step, look at the glass and then look away.

"This isn't a Reading," she said. "Elias has taught some of you Readings. That's how much of the room's lit. It's a good question. This is a different one. This is how much oil's left in the lamp, today." She set the glass on Dessa's table. "Everybody does the smallest thing their door does, and holds it, from wherever you are now, down to your floor. You feel for the floor. You don't go through it. When you feel it, you stop, and I write down how many turns of the glass that took. Tomorrow we do it again. It's not a race. The numbers don't compare between doors. They compare between days."

"What's a floor?" said the weighbridge girl.

"The last place you can stop," said Jab, before Dee could answer. "And still walk home, and still do it again tomorrow."

The woman looked at him. Then she nodded, and did not say anything else about it.

Callie went first, because she was nearest. She stood in the first lane with her feet planted and her hands at her sides, and a shine came up over her like old glass, a skin's width off her coat, and she stood in it. Her lips moved. Toren knew she was counting. The woman watched the glass.

At thirty-eight, by Callie's lips, the shine went off her.

"Floor," said Callie.

"Thirty-eight seconds, standing," said the woman, and Dessa wrote it. "That's today."

The weighbridge girl did her blade, lit. It stood out of her fist straight and level with its frost-pale line along the edge, and she held it very still and did not look at it, and her face got whiter and whiter, and at a turn of the glass and most of another she said, "That's it, I think, is that it?" and the woman said, "You tell me," and she said, "That's it," and the blade went.

Jab lit his knife and held it. He held it through one turn of the glass, and two, and three, standing easily in the lane with the amber line lying along the edge of that fat ugly blade, and at the fifth turn he yawned.

"That'll do," said the woman. "I believe you. Put it out before you fall asleep."

"I can do it all day," said Jab. "If it's only the light."

"I know. I read your card." She turned to Toren. "You."

Toren took the stone out of his pocket.

He had been waiting for this since the morning before. He had lain awake in hut eleven for part of the night waiting for it, with his hand in his coat pocket on the stone and not closing on it, which was the only way he had found to keep his hand there at all.

The stone was warm. It had been warm for fifteen days, since a night on a step when he had stopped checking. It came and went under his fingers like something breathing, the least impressive thing anybody had ever felt. He did not know how full it was. He had not asked. He had been afraid, every day for fifteen days, that asking would be the same as checking, and that checking would put it out, and that this time it would stay out.

"It's not a question you can ask me," he said.

The woman waited.

"The last time it went out," said Toren, "I didn't know when it was coming back. The time before that, it was two days. I asked it, every forty steps, for two months, if it was still there. And the asking put it out." He stopped. "Rook says so. Callie says so. It's the one thing I've learned in this place that I know is true."

"Checking puts it out," said the woman. "Yes. I'm not asking you to check."

"What's the difference?"

"Checking's asking every forty steps whether it's still there," she said. "Because you're afraid it isn't. It's asking the same question over and over and not listening to the answer, because the answer isn't what you're asking for. You're asking to be told you're not going to lose it." She did not say it unkindly. "Measuring is asking once, properly, and writing the answer down, and then leaving it alone. You don't have to believe the answer. You only have to write it."

Toren looked at the glass on the table.

"One," said the woman. "The spike. Dark. Not lit. Not the other thing with your legs, and not both, and I know about both, and Dessa's told me she told you not this year. Just the spike, dark, until you feel the floor. Then stop. And don't look at the stone. Look at the glass."

He held the stone in his right hand, with the seam across his palm, and let the spike come.

It came the way it always came, not the way he wanted it. It came up out of his fist crooked and ugly, as long as his forearm, thick at the root with a bad heavy heel to it, grey and blunt and dark. Garrick had looked at it on the day it first came, in a stone cut with husks coming up the road, and said keep the ugly shape, stop trying to be good at it. Rook had looked at it for two months and not let him improve it. It sat on his fist now like a thing that had been dropped there, and he held it.

The woman turned the glass.

He did not look at the stone. He looked at the sand. It ran through the waist of the glass in a grey thread, and piled up in the bottom in a little grey hill, and the hill got taller.

Holding the spike cost something. He had never let himself notice what. He noticed it now, because he was looking for it, and it was a small steady draw from somewhere behind his breastbone, like water going out of a barrel through a pinhole. It was not frightening. It was only a thing that was happening.

The woman turned the glass again.

Somewhere in the second turn, he felt it. It was not the stone going cold. It was not a door closing. It was a thinning, the way a rope thins when you feel the strands begin to go under your hands before it parts, and he knew the way you know you have been walking too long that there was a place a little way below here where he would not be able to get up again.

"There," he said.

He let the spike go. It went back into the stone. The stone was warm in his palm. It was still warm. It was exactly as warm as it had been before he started, which was to say it was faint, and breathing, and there.

"Two turns and a quarter," said the woman. "Dark." Dessa wrote it. "That's today."

It was not much. Toren knew it was not much. Callie's thirty-eight seconds had been standing in a Ward, which cost everything, and the weighbridge girl's turn and three-quarters had been lit, and Jab had yawned at five. Two and a quarter, dark, was the smallest number anybody had said, for the cheapest thing anybody had done.

He stood in the first lane with the stone in his hand and found that he did not mind.

He had asked it how much. He had asked it once, and it had answered, and it was still there. For the first time since the Breach he had put a question to it and it had not gone out.

"Thank you," he said to the woman.

She looked at him over the glass for a moment. Then she wrote something in her own book, not Dessa's, and Toren did not see what.

"Write why," said Senna, from her chair by the stove pipe.

"Because tomorrow," said the woman, before Dessa could look at her, "you'll need to know whether today was a good day."

"You fall like a dropped plate," said Wyck.

He said it from the top of the sand heap, in the flat voice he used for things that were simply true. Toren was lying at the bottom of the heap on his good side, in the sand, where Wyck had told him to fall.

"I fell like you said."

"You fell like you'd been told to fall. That's different. You went down like a plate off a shelf, all at once, and you put your hand out." Wyck pointed with the bar at Toren's right hand, which was flat in the sand where it had landed. "You put your hand out and that's how people break their wrists. I know. I've done it." He did not say which time. "You fall on the road every time you do the thing with your legs. Dessa says eleven times since you started it. She counted. So you might as well learn to do it properly."

"How do you do it properly?"

"Like a sack," said Wyck. "A full sack. You don't reach for the ground. You let the ground come and you give it something round." He thought about it. "Tuck the chin. Round the back. Take it on the meat, not the points. Roll the way it wants to go. Don't put your hand down, ever, for any reason, even if your mother's at the bottom of the heap."

"And you know how to fall."

"I don't fall," said Wyck. "I've had a lot of time to watch other people do it."

Toren fell eleven more times. The last three he did it like a sack, or like something that had once seen a sack, and Wyck said "Better" once and "That'll do" once and said nothing at all the third time, which Otto told him afterward was the best thing Wyck said to anybody.

The first lane ended at the timber frame, and in front of the frame there was a post with a rag tied round it at the height of a man's chest.

"Twelve paces," said Rook. He was sitting on an upturned crate at the side of the lane, because he had held the Sag at first light and his hands were still doing what they did afterward. "With the struts. To the post. And stop."

"Stop?"

"Stop," said Rook. "Everybody practises going. Nobody practises stopping. You've gone past everything you ever ran at and fallen over on the other side of it." He looked at the post. "Have somewhere to be. Be there. Stay."

Toren stood at the start of the lane with his left arm strapped against his chest.

The cinders were wet. There had been frost in the night and the sun had got to it, and the whitewash lines Rook had painted had gone slick and grey where the damp had got into them. Toren looked at the line he would have to cross two paces before the post, and thought about it, and decided it did not matter.

He had somewhere to be.

The struts came. They came the way they always came, thin and uneven and bright along the outside of his shins, and the left one late, the way the left one was always late, half a breath behind the right as if it had been called from further off. There was the flat crack of them loading and a scatter of grit, and he went.

Twelve paces went by in about the time it takes to say twelve. The post came up at him. He started to stop.

His right boot came down on the whitewash line and went out from under him like a boot on ice.

He went left. There was nothing he could do about it. His weight was all going forward and to the left, and the left was where the arm was, strapped across his chest with the break in it, and he saw the cinders coming up at it and knew exactly what the noise would be. He had made it on the slabs at the bottom of the Sag on the night of the north road. His whole body knew it.

And the left strut arrived.

It was late. It was always late. It had been coming up his shin the way it always came, half a breath behind, and his foot was already gone out from under him when it got there, so it did not come up along the leg at all. It came up from the ground. It came up out of the cinders a foot to the outside of where his boot should have been, at a slant, like a prop kicked under a falling wall, and it met his leg at the knee coming the other way, and it locked.

He stopped.

He did not stop the way a person stops. He stopped the way a ladder stops against a wall. He hung there, tipped over to the left at a slant, with his right boot skidded out on the whitewash and his left leg braced into the cinders along a line of light that ran from his knee to the ground outside his foot, and his broken arm a foot off the ground, and nothing moving.

For about as long as it takes to breathe in.

Then it went out, and he dropped, and at the last moment, without thinking about it at all, he tucked his chin and rounded his back and went down onto his good shoulder like a full sack, and rolled, and lay there in the cinders on his back looking at the sky.

Nobody said anything.

Somewhere down at the sand heap Otto had stopped in the middle of falling over. Dessa had stopped with her pencil on the page. At the shed door, Senna had turned her chair by leaning.

"Do that again," said Wyck.

Toren lay on his back.

"Do what?" he said.

Callie was already walking over. She did not come to him. She came to the place on the lane where he had been, a pace and a half short of the post, and crouched, and looked at the ground. She did not touch it. She held the hook's handle out over it, flat, the way you hold your hand over a track to feel whether it is warm.

"Don't walk here," she said, to nobody. "Anybody. Don't walk here."

Rook had got up off his crate. He came down the lane slowly, with one hand out to nothing, and stopped over Toren and looked down at him.

"What did you do?" he said.

"I fell."

"No," said Rook. "You nearly fell."

"That wasn't Ward," said Dee, from the shed door.

"No," said Rook. He had not taken his eyes off Toren. "It's not his door. That was the struts. That was his hardware." He said the word as if he had never said it about a door before and was trying the taste of it. "That was his hardware doing something I've never seen it do."

"That's a guard," said Wyck.

They looked at him. He was on top of the sand heap with his bar across his knees.

"When you stand to take a hit," said Wyck. "Arms up, weight down. That's your guard. That's what the men at home called it, when they fought on a pay night. You put your guard up." He pointed with the bar. "That was his legs putting a guard up. That was a strut stood there to take the ground instead of pushing off it."

Dessa had her pencil on the page.

"What do I write?" she said.

"Guard," said Wyck.

"Stride, held," said Rook.

"Write what he did," said Senna from her chair, "not what you think it was. You'll only have to cross it out. Write: the strut stood up."

Dessa looked at the three of them. Then she bent over the book and wrote for some time. When Toren got up and came and looked, later, she had written all three, one under the other, with a line drawn down the side to join them, and at the bottom, in smaller letters, Toren fell like a sack after. Wyck's lane.

Rook would not let him do it again that day.

"You'll try to," he said. "You'll try to do the thing you just did, and you'll try to do it on purpose, and it'll put you on that arm. I'm not having you on that arm. Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow what?"

"Tomorrow we'll see if it'll come when you ask," said Rook, "and I'll have Wyck build you something to ask it with that won't break anything. And her in the shed will want to measure you first. Sit down."

Toren sat down on Rook's crate. His hands were shaking. He looked at them for a while. It was the first time they had shaken like Rook's.

At the end of the lane, a pace and a half short of the post, Callie was still crouched with the hook's handle held out flat over the cinders, looking at the ground as if it were a page she had not finished reading.

Wyck built it overnight.

He could only use one arm, so Otto did the lifting and Wyck did the telling. By first light there was a rope hanging from the crossbar of Rook's timber frame at the end of the first lane, and on the end of the rope a straw sack stuffed so full it was nearly round, hanging at the height of a boy's knee. You pulled it back along the lane on a second rope and let it go, and it swung down and through, and hit whatever was standing under the crossbar.

"Knee height," said Wyck. "Not higher. It's not to hit him. It's to hit what he puts in front of him." He looked at Toren. "Keep the arm in. You put that arm anywhere near the sack and I'll take it off you myself, and I've only got one hand to do it with."

"Nobody pulls it back further than that mark," said Dee. She had painted a line on the cinders herself, short, in whitewash, six paces out. "That's as hard as it hits. That's today."

"You measure everything," said the weighbridge girl.

"I do."

"Even how hard you hit somebody with a sack."

"Especially that," said Dee.

Otto pulled the sack back to the line. Toren stood under the crossbar with his feet set and his arm strapped against his chest.

"When you're ready," said Rook.

The first time, he built the struts the way he always built them.

They came up the outside of his shins, the right and then the left, late, and he felt them load the way they loaded before a run, a coiled feeling like a held breath, and he held them there and tried not to let them go. He tried to keep them the way they had been in the fall. Rigid. Held.

"Now," said Rook, and Otto let the sack go.

It came down the lane with a noise like wind. Toren watched it come and held the struts, and at the last instant, as the sack arrived, they did what they had been built to do.

They fired.

He went sideways out from under the crossbar like a stone out of a sling, and the sack went through the place where he had been, and he landed in the cinders two paces off to the right, and rolled, like a sack, and ended up sitting.

There was a small silence.

Then the weighbridge girl laughed. It came out of her all at once, high and startled, and she clapped both hands over her mouth and went scarlet and said through her fingers, "Sorry. I'm sorry. You went so far."

Toren sat in the cinders and looked at the place he had been.

He found that he was laughing too. It was not much of a laugh. It was the first one that had come out of him in Meridian that was not a sound in place of something else. He sat there with his arm strapped to his chest and laughed at the place he had been, and at the sack swinging back and forth through it, and at himself, and nobody in the yard told him to stop.

"You loaded them," said Rook, when he had finished. "You built them to go, and then you asked them not to."

"I know."

"Again."

The second time, he built them rigid from the start. He did not let them coil. He made them straight and hard and held them the way you hold a bar across a door, square under his body, and his knees locked with them, and his hips, and everything he had.

Otto let the sack go.

It hit. It hit the struts dead square at the knee, and the struts held, for about as long as it takes to blink, and all the weight of that swinging sack went straight through them into his legs, and his legs went straight into his hips, and his hips went nowhere, because he had locked them, and so the whole of it went up through his spine and into the strapped arm and came out of him as a noise he had not made since the slabs.

He was on his back. He did not remember going there.

The arm. It was the arm; something in it had jarred against the splint, deep, along the line he could have drawn on paper, and the dull ache there had become a bright one. He lay in the cinders and held it against his chest with his good hand and breathed through his teeth.

Dee was kneeling beside him. He had not seen her come.

"Where?"

"The arm," said Toren. "It jarred. Inside the splint. It's—it's not like the first night. It's not as bad as that. But it's there."

She looked at him before she touched him. Then she put two fingers along the splint, lightly, from the wrist to the elbow, and watched his face while she did it.

"That's today," she said.

"I can go again."

"You can. You won't." She sat back on her heels. "You told me where it hurt and how much. That's the most useful thing anybody's done in this lane all morning. If you'd lied about it, I'd have let you go again, and you'd have been on your back in Keel's room this afternoon with that splint off." She stood up. "Sit on the heap with Wyck. The two of you can watch other people do things."

Rook, on his crate, said, "Good."

Toren looked at him.

"You said it," said Rook. "Out loud. Where it hurt." He looked at the sack, still swinging on its rope, slower and slower. "It's harder than taking the sack."

So he sat on the sand heap with Wyck for the rest of the morning, two boys with one good arm between them, and watched other people do things.

He watched Callie stand in her Ward in the first lane, and lift one foot, very slowly, and watched the shine go off her like water off a tipped plate. He watched the weighbridge girl cut a straw sack in half with her straight blade, clean, in one stroke, and then stand looking at the two halves on the cinders as if she had not known she could do it. He watched Jab light his knife and let it go dark and light it again, over and over, a dozen times, faster each time. He watched Dessa write all of it.

"It held for you," said Wyck, after a long time. "When you weren't trying."

"Yes."

"Same as mine." Wyck turned the bar over in his left hand. He did not drop it. "Rook says I'm a person who decided, over and over, for years, not to be moved. I never decided anything. "I don't know yet," said Wyck. "I'm finding out. Keel says six weeks." He was quiet for a while. "You locked your knees. The second time. I watched. Toren thought about that.

"That's not all of it," he said.

"No," said Wyck. "It's my bit. Somebody else'll have the rest."

Somebody else had the rest.

He found it at the end of the day, when the yard had emptied and the light had gone long and orange across the cinders from the west. Callie was at the end of the first lane, by the post, crouched where she had crouched the morning before. She had not been anywhere near him all afternoon. He stood up.

He did it slowly, and stood with his feet set, on the cinders beside her three drawings, with no sack and no frame and nobody watching but her.

He did not build the struts. He did not let them come up his shins the way they always came. It came.

It was thin and uneven and bright, and it came up out of the ground at a slant like a prop under a wall, and it met his knee, and for about as long as it takes to breathe in, it held. There was nothing pushing on it. It was only there, standing, the way a strut stands under a roof.

Then it went.

He stood on the cinders. He did not fall over.

"That's the late one," said Callie. "That's the one that holds."

"It went out."

"It came, though." She stood up too, with one hand on her ribs. "You asked it and it came. It didn't come yesterday when you asked it. It only came when you fell." She looked down at the drawings. "Now you know where to ask it from."

Toren looked at her. She was looking at the ground.

"Wyck said lock the struts and not myself," he said. "Stay soft behind them."

"Then that's two things," said Callie. "From the ground up. And soft behind." She put the hook over her shoulder. "Tomorrow you'll have three. Everybody always wants you to have three."

Toren sat down on the step of eleven. The stone in his pocket was warm.

"How do you light it?" he said.

Jab showed him. The amber line came out of the grip end of the blade, thin as a hair, the colour of a coal with the ash still on it, and ran along the edge, and at the swell of the belly it stopped, and guttered, and hung there for one breath, and caught, and went on round to the hook.

"Now you," said Jab.

[Focused, grounded narration; make the physical instruction legible]

Toren let the spike come.

It came crooked and ugly, the way it always came, as long as his forearm, with its bad thick heel. He looked at it beside Jab's knife in the dusk. Jab's knife was ugly too, as ugly as a door hinge, but it was a shape. It was a shape somebody had meant. Everything about it, the heavy spine and the fat belly and the little hook, was for something. Toren's spike was not for anything. It was a thing that had come out of his fist on the second day of his life with it because a husk was coming, and had never changed since.

He tried to make it a little better.

He did not decide to. He was looking at Jab's knife, and at his own spike, and something in him leaned toward the one from the other, the way you straighten a picture on a wall without thinking, and he tried to take the heel off the spike and make the line of it cleaner, a little more like a thing somebody had meant.

The spike guttered like a lamp in a draught and went out.

He sat on the step with his fist empty.

"Don't make it nice," said Jab.

Toren looked at him.

"I watched you do it," said Jab. "You looked at mine and you tried to make yours like it. It went." He put his own knife out, and it went back into the metal on his neck cord, and he sat with his hands on his knees. "When I was thirteen, on a gravel bar, with sixty people watching, I pulled this out of a river stone. It was the ugliest thing anybody in the valley had ever seen come out of a Kindling. My father's was long and straight and narrow, like a reed. Mine was a fish knife. The kind every house has two of." He was quiet for a moment. "My mother came up behind my father with my brother on her back, and she looked at it, and she said, Well, it looks like you."

Toren said nothing.

"That's the whole of it, she said. Don't spend too long." Jab looked down the row at the lamp, which was not lit yet. "They're both dead. The bridge went. I never asked her what she meant by the last bit. I thought I had years."

[Steady, watchful narration; let distance and reserves register]

The lamp at the end of the row came on, flat and white.

"My father said keep the ugly shape," said Toren. "Stop trying to be good at it. In a stone cut, with husks coming up the road. It was the last thing he ever told me about it."

Jab turned his head and looked at him.

They sat on their two steps, one door apart, and neither of them said anything for a long time. Toren thought about a woman on a gravel bar he would never see, with a baby on her back, looking at a fish knife, and a man in a stone cut with husks coming, looking at a spike. Two people who had never met, four hundred miles apart, saying the same thing to two boys about the shape of what came out of them. He did not know what it meant. He thought it meant something.

"Light it," said Jab. "The ugly one. The one that looks like you."

Toren let the spike come.

It came crooked and ugly and heavy in the heel, and this time he did not look at Jab's knife. He looked at his own. He let it be what it was. And then he asked it for the light, not the way he would ask a door to open but the way you would ask somebody sitting next to you on a step to pass you something, without looking, because you knew they were there.

The light came.

It came along the edge of the spike from the root, thin and hard and white-grey, a cold line lying along the inner curve of the crooked blade. It came all at once and it shook. It shook the way his hands had shaken on Rook's crate. It flickered at the heel and at the point and along the middle, and it would not settle, and he held it and it shook.

"You're holding it like somebody's going to take it off you," said Jab.

Toren did not answer.

He thought about a grey coat's glove at the north gate, held out for the stone. About a paper with nineteen days on it, and his father's hand on his shoulder. About a greenish light no brighter than a jar of fireflies, held up on a channel wall, that did not go out. About everything that had been taken off him since the spring, one thing after another, until he had stopped being able to hold anything without waiting for the hand that would come for it.

Nobody was going to take this. It was his. It was the ugly one. It looked like him.

He let go of it a little. Not all the way. A little, the way you ease your grip on a rope you know will hold.

The light steadied. It lay along the crooked edge of the spike, thin and white-grey and cold, and it did not shake, for one breath, and another, and a third.

Then it flickered, and he let it go before it could go out on its own, and the spike went back into the stone, and the stone was warm.

"Three," said Jab.

"Three," said Toren.

"You look at the belly," he said, after a while.

Jab turned his head.

"When you light yours. I watched your eyes. You start at the grip, and your eyes go along with the light, and when it gets to the belly your eyes stop there. Because that's the part that cuts. That's the part you've used for four years, on the wood, on the hound." Toren nodded at Jab's fist. "The light stops where you're looking. Look at the hook."

Jab was silent for a long moment.

Then he let the knife come again, dark, on his fist, and started the light at the grip, and Toren watched him fix his eyes on the little hook at the end of the blade and hold them there, and not let them go to the belly at all.

The amber line came out of the grip. It ran along the edge. It came to the swell of the belly, and it did not stop. It went through the belly like water going through a gap in a wall, with a flicker in it, half a breath, less, and on round to the hook, and the whole edge was lit.

Jab stared at it.

"Four years," he said.

"You never had anybody watching your eyes," said Toren.

Jab sat on his step with the whole edge of his knife lit in the dusk, amber and even from the grip to the hook, and looked at it. Then he looked at Toren. Toren had seen his face do a great many things in eighteen days, most of them in corridors, and he had not seen it do this.

"Don't tell Dee," said Jab. "She'll want it measured."

"She'll want it measured," Toren agreed.

They sat on their two steps in the light of the lamp, and neither of them went in for a long time.
