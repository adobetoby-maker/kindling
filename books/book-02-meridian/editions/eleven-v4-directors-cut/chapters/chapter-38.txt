Chapter 38 — Off Before Ten

[Taut, spatially clear narration; build urgency without rushing]

"Husks," said Dee. "Two."

The corner heap had stood up in the same three, and so had the heap at the far end of the cabinets, three cabinets along from the frame. There were two husks in the room now, grey and faceless, the colour of old wasp nests, and they turned toward the worked things in it: toward Toren's hands, with a strut just gone out of them; toward Callie's back in the doorway; toward Jab's palm, which had been worked a breath ago and was open now.

"I've got the cabinet one," said Toren.

He went at it, and he did not go at it with the heel. He set a strut from the floor in front of it, slanting back toward the cabinets, and the husk walked into the strut and stood against it, pushing, pinned between the pale line of the strut and the steel of the cabinet door behind it. Toren stood behind the strut, soft, and paid by the breath.

"And the corner one?" said Dee.

It was coming along the heap of boxes toward Jab.

Jab let the knife come. It came on his left fist the way it always came, dark, heavy in the spine and fat in the belly: the fish-knife, the one shape he had lived inside for four years. He did not light all of it. He lit the last inch, the hook, and felt the draw of it behind his breastbone.

The husk came along the boxes at him, low.

He did not want to kill it. Every one you killed lay on the floor, and then it was in the air, and then at the three it was standing up again; he had watched it happen five times the day before. But it was coming at his hands, and Toren was on the strut, and Dee had the jar, and Callie had the door, and there was nobody else. He killed it. He put the hook into it low as it came, the way he would have cut a rope, and it came apart, and the grey fell on the boxes and the floor at the foot of the racks in a heap.

"A hundred and twenty," said Dee, with the counter.

Jab let the knife go dark. He crouched by the heap and did not try to lift it, because there was no time, and because he knew now what lifting against the lifter was like. He watched it instead. For ten breaths it lay there, and then it drew out into threads and went into the strands at the foot of the racks, and along them into the corner, into the heap the husk had first stood out of.

He went after it. He went along the foot of the racks at a crouch with his right hand out, all the way to the corner, and he put his left hand up behind him without looking. Dee was there. She had followed him, and she put the mouth of the jar against his palm.

"Three," said Dee.

The corner swelled.

Jab put his hand flat on it, on the top of the heap where it swelled, where the husk was going to stand, and it pushed into him. It was the same as at the cut: one push, a palm's worth, warm and gritty and loose, all at once, and then nothing behind it. He held it high. The clean cold came into his left palm from the jar to hold it off his wrist, and it came thin, like the pour at the bottom of the jug.

"Jar," said Jab.

He said it before the jar was dry. Under his hand the heap did not crack and did not stand. It sank back a little, like a loaf that has been knocked.

"Off," said Dee.

He was already off. The vent jar, and the grey going down into the glass, and the stopper.

"Two caught," said Dee.

The corner heap lay in the corner. It was smaller than it had been, and it did not stand up.

For a moment Dee did not answer the jar. She looked at the empty in her hand.

"That's jar two done," she said. "The open one. It went on the first two catches, and they were the biggest." She turned the empty over. "They'll not all be that big. It's less every time."

She crossed to the frame on the cabinet top, and Jab watched her hand go to it. There were three full jars in it, and three empties, and the broken socket. She put the empty in beside the others, which made four empties, and then she stopped with her hand on the neck of the first full one.

"That's the last one above the line," she said.

Nobody said anything. Toren was behind his strut with the husk against it and his face wet. Callie was in the door. The lifter lay over the carrier.

"Open it," said Toren.

"It's the last above," said Dee again. "When it's done, it's the line. I'm saying it now so it's said."

"It's said," said Toren. "Open it."

She opened it. She lifted it out of the frame by the neck and pulled the stopper, and came back to the corner, and put the mouth of it against Jab's left palm.

"Jar three," she said. "Open. Eight in it. Two whole behind it, and that's sixteen. The line." She looked at the hump. "Say it, Jab."

"Eight above the line," said Jab. "In this one. Then it's the line."

"It doesn't matter where," said Jab.

He said it to Dee, crouching in the corner with his hand held away from him. The lantern was behind him on the cabinet, and one side of Dee's face was yellow with it.

"It doesn't matter where I take it," he said. "The cut. The corner. Wherever it pushes. It's all out of the drum." He looked at the hump over the carrier. "Every time I take one, there's a palm's worth less of it. Anywhere."

"Then take it where it matters," said Dee.

She said it at once. Jab thought she had known it before he said it, and had been waiting for him to find it.

"Where it's going to stand up," said Jab. "Where it's mending something Toren did. Where it's going to hurt somebody." He looked at Toren behind the strut with the husk against it. "There."

"Then that's the order," said Dee. "You'll not get all of them. It pushes into every hole at once. You'll get one." She looked at the glass in her fist. "Pick the one."

It learned.

Jab felt it learn. Afterward he did not know how else to say it, and he did not try. It had nobody in it and it did not want anything, but it did what it did, and when what it did stopped working, it did something else. The way water does.

At the next three it did not push into the corner. It pushed into the husk on Toren's strut.

He felt it go out of the hump across the floor along the strands, not to the corner but to the thing pinned against the cabinet at the end of the row, and he saw the husk get bigger. It swelled against the strut like a sack being filled, taller and thicker, and the strut had to take more of it, and Toren made a noise through his teeth.

"It's feeding it," said Jab. "The one on your strut."

"I can feel it's feeding it," said Toren. "It's heavier."

Jab went along the cabinets fast, crouching, with his hand out behind him for the jar. But the three was over before he got there, and the ten had begun, and the husk on the strut was as big as a man now, and it leaned.

"I'm paying for that by the breath," said Toren. "It's more than a wheel."

"Next three," said Jab. "I'll be on it."

"On what? On the husk?"

"On whatever it pushes into."

He crouched at the foot of the strut, at the husk's feet, with his right hand an inch off its grey where it stood on the floor. He did not touch it. You did not touch a husk. But it was not only a husk now. It was a place the lifter pushed into. It was a hole in the lifter, walking about, and the lifter was trying to fill it.

"Clean in," said Dee, behind him, with the jar at his left palm.

"The three," said Jab, before she did.

It pushed. He put his palm flat on the foot of the husk where it stood on the concrete, and the push came into him instead. It came along the floor from the hump and up into the husk, and at the husk's foot it found his palm drawing, and came into his palm, warm, a palm's worth. The husk did not grow. It shook against the strut the way a sack shakes when you pull something out of the bottom of it.

"Off," said Jab, and came off. The vent jar. The grey. The stopper.

"Three caught," said Dee.

The husk leaned on the strut. It was as big as it had been, and no bigger.

"It's not heavier," said Toren. "It's not getting heavier."

Toren began to cut.

He did it on the tens, and he did it one thing at a time, because he could not keep a strut up and light the heel together. For Toren it was one or the other. So at the top of a ten he let the husk's strut go, and the husk fell forward off the cabinet onto its front, the way they did when the thing they were leaning on went out from under them. It would take it a breath or two to get up. He went to the hump.

There had been three arm-ropes, and there were still three. One lay where it had slid off the strut behind Callie, on the floor between the hump and the door. The other two lay across the boxes, out from the far side of the hump, toward the corner.

He went to the near one first. He lit the heel and put it across the arm-rope a pace from the hump, low, and dragged it through. It came apart. The long end lay on the floor toward the door, spilling, and the short end stuck out of the hump like the stump of a branch.

He let the heel go dark. The husk was getting up by the cabinets, and he set the strut in front of it again as it came, and it walked into the strut and stood against it, pushing.

"Cut," said Toren. "Near one."

"The three's in four breaths," said Dee.

Jab went to the stump.

He was learning the room. He did not know that he was doing it until he was doing it: where the lantern light fell and where it did not; where the boxes were, and the carrier under the hump with its iron handle sticking out, and the cabinets; where Callie's heels were, and the curve on the threshold, and Toren. He went round all of it at a crouch with his right hand out in front of him and his left hand back, and Dee came behind him with the jar and the bag. She was always there when he put his hand back.

He put his palm an inch off the stump.

"Nine," said Dee. "The three."

It pushed. The stump grew at him, the way the cut strands had grown toward each other on the floor the day before, a finger, two fingers, to mend. He put his palm on the end of it, and it came into him instead of growing.

"Off."

The jar. The grey. The stopper.

"Four caught," said Dee. "That's the first vent jar full. Near enough." She put it in the bag. "Two vent jars left. They'll hold more than that one did. It's giving less every time."

The stump stuck out of the hump, short, a hand long, and it did not grow. The long end of the arm-rope lay on the floor by the door with its spill going away from it in threads.

Toren looked at it.

"That's one," he said. "It's got two."

It learned again. At the next three it threw.

It did not push into a hole. It lifted the second arm-rope, the one that lay across the boxes toward the corner, and swung it, and it swung it at Jab.

Jab did not see it come. He had his hand an inch off the stump of the first arm-rope and his eyes on it, and Dee's jar at his left palm, and all his attention down under his palm in the grey. He heard the long heavy dry sound of the rope coming up off the boxes. He heard Toren say "Jab."

Something went over his head.

It went over him the way a roof goes over you: pale and hard, a line of light going up from the concrete at a slant beside his knee and on over the top of him and Dee, like a plank leaned against a wall with the two of them under it. The arm-rope hit it. Jab felt it hit through the concrete and up through his boots, with the sound of a sack hitting a wall, and the strut took it and stood over him and Dee, pale, while the rope lay over the top of it and began to slide.

"Holding," said Toren. "Both."

Jab looked round.

Toren was standing between the hump and the cabinets with his left arm against his chest, and he had two struts up. One went from the floor to the husk at the end of the cabinets, pinning it. The other went from the floor over Jab and Dee. He was soft behind both of them, and his face was grey and wet in the lantern light. He had held two at once on the arch at the wash for as long as it took to walk a cart onto a post, and Jab had heard him say what it cost. Two struts while it moves. That's twice the price.

"Toren," said Jab.

"Get the stump," said Toren. "I've got the roof."

The arm-rope slid off the strut onto the boxes. The ten had begun.

At the next three Jab had his hand on the stump of the first arm, and it did not push there. The lifter pushed at the second arm instead, the one that had hit the roof, because the rope was cracked across where it had struck the strut and the lifter was mending it.

Jab felt it go the wrong way. He felt it go past him.

"It's gone to the other," he said.

"Then leave it," said Dee.

"It'll swing again."

"Then Toren'll roof you again." She did not raise her voice. "You can't be on all of them. It pushes into every hole at once. You're on one. Pick the one."

"I picked the wrong one."

"Then pick the next one," said Dee. "That's all a watch is."

He came off late once.

It was the seventh. He had his palm on the corner heap, which the lifter was trying to stand a husk up out of again, and the push came into his hand, a palm's worth, and he held it high. But he was thinking about the second arm-rope lying across the boxes behind him, and where it would swing. He was thinking about Toren's grey face. He was thinking about Callie's back.

"Off," said Dee.

He was not off.

The ten began. He felt it begin under his palm. He felt the lifter draw, flat and open and patient, the way it had drawn all afternoon the day before, at everything loose in the room, and the palm's worth in his hand was loose. It was held high and open in his palm, and it was not his, and it was not in a jar.

It came out of his hand. It went out of his palm back down into the corner heap in threads, the way the heap on the floor had gone out from under his palm the day before. And his palm went with it. Not his hand: the drawing. The part of him that was reaching down into the grey went after the threads toward the hump, toward the middle, the way you lean after a rope that is being pulled away from you.

For a breath he was going.

"Off," said Dee.

He took his hand off.

He crouched in the corner with his palm up and empty. Half the palm's worth had gone back into the heap. He could feel where it had gone, and he could feel where he had nearly gone after it.

"Off before the ten," said Dee. "Not at the ten. Before."

"I know."

"You know now," said Dee.

He looked at his palm. Round the white place the rest of it was warm, a little. It was not lodged. It was warm.

"Vent," said Dee, and held the jar, and he vented the half into the glass, and she put the stopper in.

"Seven caught," she said. "Half of it."

He did not come off late again. At every three after that he said off himself, before she did, and was off, the way he had said vent before she did at the mouth of the cut. And every time, as he lifted his palm, he felt the ten begin a breath behind it. It was like stepping off a step just as it gives.

[Steady, watchful narration; let distance and reserves register]

He looked at Callie once.

He could not help it. He was crouched by the stump of the first arm-rope, near the door, waiting for a three, and she was a pace and a half from him with her back to him. He had not looked at her properly since they came in. He looked now.

She was standing in the doorway with the curve on the threshold and her hands on the conduit, and the lee stood up out of the curve on the corridor side. He could see it past her: a pale sheet standing up off the threshold and leaning back up the wall over the door, like a lean-to the wrong way up. It was grey on the outside. The grey of the corridor's cut rope had heaped against its foot in a long ridge as high as Callie's knees now, piled against the outside of the pale wall, and up by the hole the cut end of the ceiling rope had heaped against it too.

And there was a husk against it. It stood in the corridor on the other side of the lee, pushing. It had come from the stair door, at the worked thing in the doorway, and walked into the lee, and it stood pressed against the pale wall a foot from Callie's face, pushing, with no face of its own. Every time it pushed, Jab saw Callie's shoulders take it.

She was holding it off her stone, her whole stone, two Handfuls: the husk on the outside, and the heaps, and a door and a bit.

There was blood on her lip.

She turned her head.

She did not turn round. She turned her head a little, over her shoulder, the way you turn your head at a noise behind you, and for a breath her eyes found him: Jab crouched on the floor with his hand out over a stump, and Dee behind him with the jar, and the room behind that.

He did not know what she saw. He did not know what her own face did. He looked at her and said the only thing he had.

"Busy," he said.

Callie looked at him a breath longer. Then she turned her head back to the corridor, and she did not turn it again.

It was smaller.

Jab saw it as he went round the hump for the eleventh catch. He had been too near it to see it before, the way you do not see a heap of sacks going down while you are lifting off the top of it. The hump had been as high as Callie's waist. It was as high as her knee now at the high end, and the other end had gone down to nothing, and he could see the carrier's lead lid under it, grey, and the iron handle sticking all the way out.

"It's smaller," he said.

"It's less," said Toren. "I can feel it on the husk. The husk's less."

It was. The husk on Toren's strut had been as big as a man. It was the size of a boy now, pinned against the cabinet, and it pushed with less.

And the three was different. Jab felt it before he knew what it was. The line of light opened along the top of the hump at the three, the colour of the edge of the sky, and it stayed open longer. It opened at the three and did not shut at the end of the three. It shut at the end of four. Then five.

"It's pushing longer," said Jab.

"Why?" said Toren.

"Because there's less in the push." Jab knew it the way he knew a floor. "It's pushing everything it lifted, and it didn't lift much, so it keeps pushing. Looking for more." He looked at the light. "It's opening wider because it's got nothing to put through."

"Pumping itself dry," said Dee.

"It doesn't know how to stop," said Jab.

He crouched by the stump of the first arm-rope with his hand out, and while the ten ran he thought about it. He thought about the rope by the door the day before, and the push that would not stop, and the whole of the hill behind it. And himself, with his palm on it, not able to find his floor.

It could not find its floor either. There was nobody in it to find it. There was nobody in it to say off.

The difference between him and it was one word, and Dee.

The lifter moved.

It had not moved since they came in. It had lain over the carrier like a dog over a bone. It had thrown its arm-ropes and pushed into its holes and stood its husks up, and it had not moved.

At the seventeenth three it moved.

It pulled itself, the way it had come along the corridor the day before, drawing itself along its own rope. But the only rope it had now that went anywhere was the cut end of the big one: the stump that ran from the hump across the floor to the threshold, to the curve of the hook, to Callie's heels. It came along that. It slid off the lead lid of the carrier and came along the floor toward the door, the whole grey hump of it, low, knee-high, pulling itself along the stump the way a man pulls himself along a rope in a flood. It came toward the threshold, and the lee, and the river on the other side of it.

"It's going for the door," said Jab.

Toren saw it. Jab saw him see it. Toren had the husk on his one strut, the size of a boy now, and there was no roof over Jab, because Jab was not under anything. And Toren could not hold the husk and the lean both. Not on what he had left.

"Jab," he said. "The husk. I'm letting it go."

He let it go. And he put the foot of a strut down on the floor in front of the hump, a pace short of Callie's heels.

The lifter came against the strut. It came up against the pale line of it, low, the whole grey weight of it, and it leaned. It did not push the way a husk pushed. It leaned the way the cart had leaned on the arch, and they could all see it in Toren's face.

"Holding," said Toren. And then, not to anybody: "That's the lean. That's the cart."

"Toren—" said Dee.

"I've got it," said Toren. "That's the last of the stone. I'm on mine." He did not move. "Two turns of mine, near enough. One strut. Get the husk, Jab."

The husk had fallen forward off the cabinet when the strut went out from under it, and it was getting up. It was the size of a boy, and it came at the nearest worked thing, which was Jab's open hand.

Jab let the knife come on his left fist and lit the hook of it, and waited for the end of the ten. Then he put the hook through the husk low as it came, and it came apart on the floor by the cabinets. He let the knife go dark and put his left palm back, and Dee was there with the jar.

The grey had not lain two breaths on the floor before the three came. The lifter pushed into it to stand it up again, and Jab's right palm was flat on it.

"Off," said Jab.

The vent jar. The grey. The stopper. The heap lay on the floor by the cabinets and did not stand.

"Get whatever it's pushing," said Toren, through his teeth. "Get it, Jab."

"Jar," said Jab.

He heard himself say it. It was the eighteenth three, or the nineteenth. He had lost count of the threes. He had not lost count of the jar. He felt it go thin.

Dee had it against his palm, and she held it there a breath longer than she needed to, as if she had not heard. Then she took it away.

"That's the line," she said.

It went round the room. Toren was on his one strut under the lifter, with his face grey and running and his own reserve going under it by the breath. Callie was in the door with her back to them and blood on her lip, holding. Dee had the empty jar in one hand and the glass in the other. And Jab was crouched by the lifter with his palm an inch off its edge.

"Sixteen in the frame," said Dee. "Two whole. That's the line. Everybody out."

Nobody moved.

"If we go," said Toren, "it's at the door. It's against my strut. When I let go, it's on Callie's hook. When Callie lets go of the hook, it's through. It's in the corridor with the rope. It's got the river."

"I know where it is," said Dee.

"It's got nothing," said Toren. "Look at it. It's knee-high. It's opening five breaths. It's got nothing left to push but itself."

"I know what it's got," said Dee. "I've been counting it into jars."

She did not look at Toren. She looked at Jab.

"What do you feel?" she said.

Jab knew what she was asking. It was not the counter, and it was not the jar. It was Rook's question, the one Rook had asked at the ditch, and at the pen with his hand on Jab's shoulder doing nothing. What's there? She was asking him what she could not measure.

He put his palm an inch off the edge of the lifter, where it leaned on Toren's strut, and shut his eyes.

It was there: grey, loose, with nobody in it. It was drawing, in the ten, on what little was loose in the room: the spill of the cut arms, and the husk's grey, and the ridge along the foot of the racks. It was drawing at the rate it came, and it was not coming, because there was so little left lying anywhere in the room. It was drawing on nearly nothing.

And under the draw, at the middle of it, where the light opened at the three, there was a place. It was a hole, and nobody had made it. It was the place the push came from, the vent, and it was open wider every time. It was a hole in the middle of the lifter that the lifter was pushing out of, and it had nearly nothing left to push.

"It's missing," he said. "The three. It's pushing, and nothing's coming back in the ten. It's nearly dry." He opened his eyes. "One more, maybe. Two. Then it'll open, and there'll be nothing to shut it with."

Dee looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the counter on her shoulder.

"A hundred and thirty," she said. "Not red."

Then she looked at Toren.

"Say it," she said.

Toren said it.

He said it from behind his one strut, with the lifter leaning on it and his own reserve going under it. He said it with his teeth together, flat, in order, the way Pell said a distance.

"We cross it," he said. "The line. Now. On purpose. Inside two turns of the glass. Not three. If it's not open for the point inside two turns, everybody out, finished or not, and it has the door." He breathed. "It's mine. I'm saying it. Write it. And the rule's still the rule. This is once."

"It's mine as well," said Dee. "It's my line."

She did not write it, because she had no hand for the book. She said it instead, out loud, to the room, so that it was said. "Inside two turns under the line, and not a breath more. His call and mine. Written tonight, and put in front of her at home with both our names on it." She turned the glass in her fist. "The glass is running."

She reached back to the frame on the cabinet and took the fourth jar out by its neck, one of the last two whole ones. She pulled the stopper and put the jar against Jab's left palm.

"Jar four," she said. "Open. One whole left behind it. That's under." She looked at him. "Holding on an open's not a catch, Jab. It'll pull on this the whole time you're on it. A Handful and a half a turn, near enough. Three, for two turns. Not a grain more."

Jab felt the clean cold come in.

It was the fence's. It had been for the stair, and the rooms, and the road home. It was under the line, where it was never supposed to be opened except to get somebody out. He knew that. He knew what it cost. Every Handful that came out of this jar was a Handful that nobody would have. He felt it come in at his left palm and go through him, and wait.

"Callie," said Toren. "Two turns."

"I heard," said Callie, from the door. She did not turn her head. Her voice was flat and thick, the way it was with a nosebleed. "My stone went at the fifteenth three. I'm on my own. I've got two turns. I've not got three."

At the next three the light opened, and stayed open.

It opened along the top of the lifter where it leaned on Toren's strut, the colour of the bottom of a pond, and it opened wide. Jab could see into it, and there was nothing in it. There was only the light, green-grey, pushing out like breath out of a mouth, with nothing behind it.

It pushed at every hole in the room at once. Jab felt it go: at the stumps; at the corner; at the husk's grey by the cabinets, which shivered and did not stand; at the cut arm by the door. He did not go to any of them.

He put his palm on the lifter.

He put it flat on the edge of the grey where it leaned against Toren's strut, a hand from where the light opened. He had never put it on the lifter itself. He had been on its ropes and its holes and its stumps the whole fight. He put it on the thing now.

It was not warm. That was the first thing. It had been warm all the day before, warm all through, with the whole of the hill in it. It was not warm now. It was grey and loose and cool and thin, like flour in the bottom of a sack.

It pushed into him. It pushed into him because he was drawing, and he was a hole, and it had never known anything else to do with a hole. It pushed what it had, and it had very little.

He held it high. The clean cold came in at his left palm hard and fast, faster than it had come all the fight, to hold the push off his wrist, and he could feel the jar going down under it. He did not let the push into his wrist. He held it in his palm, the last of what the lifter had, and felt it go on pushing, five breaths, six, with nothing, into his hand. It was like breath going out of a man who has no more breath.

Off before the ten.

He did not take his hand off. Not yet. He knew he had to. He knew the ten was coming a breath behind the three, and that it would draw, and that what he had in his palm was loose and would be drawn back, and him after it. He knew all of that.

But the light was open, a hand from his palm. It was open because he was on it, taking the push, so that there was nothing to shut it with. If he came off now, the ten would come, and it would draw what little was lying in the room, and at the next three it would have something to push, and it would shut.

"Toren," he said.

Toren was there. He was standing over the lifter with his one strut still under it, soft, and the spike on his fist, dark.

"It's open," said Jab. "It's open and I'm on it. It's got nothing to shut it with."

"How long?"

"Till the ten," said Jab. "Then I'm off. Whatever you've done."

Toren did not light the spike. Not with the strut up. He looked past Jab at Callie's back.

"Callie," he said. "I'm giving you the lean. It's coming onto your hook. A pace. Then it's on your steel till I'm done."

"Give it me," said Callie. She did not turn her head.

"Letting go," said Toren.

The strut went out. The lifter slid the last pace along its stump, low, the whole thin grey of it, and came up against the curve of the hook on the threshold, on the room side, and stopped as if it had come up against a wall. Because it had. Jab went with it, crouched, with his palm flat on it and the jar at his other hand, and he did not come off.

In the door Callie made a small sound.

"Mine," she said.

Toren lit the spike.
