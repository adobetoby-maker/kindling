His grandmother was lying on her back in the fourth bed on the right with her hands folded on the blanket and her eyes on the ceiling. The shed had taken a great deal out of her. It always did now. Her face had the grey, scraped look it had on the days the machine sang flat, and she did not turn her head when he sat down.

In the next bed Tonk was asleep under his frame, with the bar hanging over him on its chain and the long cord running from his wrist to Jab's. He looked at the book, and then at Senna, and then at Toren, and went back to looking at the floor, in the way that meant he would hear every word and pretend he had not.

"Open it. Find a clean page. Not near the front. Dessa's got something on the front she doesn't want anybody reading, and I'm not going to be the one who reads it." She did not move her eyes from the ceiling. "Have you got a pencil?"

"Yes."

"Then write what I say. As I say it. Not tidied." She was quiet a moment. "I've been deciding for ten days. I've decided."

"In the spring of 2130," said Senna, "I was fifty-three, and I went in for the copper."

Toren wrote it. His right hand did not want to close on the pencil tonight. The seam across the palm had pulled all day, from the jam and the brush and the spike, and it pulled now, and he wrote slowly and did not stop.

"There were eleven of us. Out of Sallow Creek and two other places. The man who ran the crew had been in before, twice, and come out, and that was how you got to run a crew, then. You'd been in and come out." She paused. "Write come out. It's important."

He wrote it.

"It was six days from here. North, and then west along the old line, by cart, the way we went. You'll find the old line on the man with the atlas's atlas; it's in red. At the end of it there's a hill with the top taken off, and a fence round the whole of it, eleven miles of fence, and inside the fence there's a plant. That was the word. The plant. We never called it anything else." She shut her eyes. "Caul Hill. That's the hill. The plant's on it."

Toren's pencil stopped.

"Write it," said Senna.

He wrote it. Caul Hill. His hand made the C badly and he did not go back over it.

"We were inside the fence line eleven days. We knew what was in there. Every soul on that crew knew. We'd been told the whole of our lives. There'd been something in the ground there since before the Fall, and the Fall had cracked whatever they'd put round it, and it had been leaking for fifty years into the dirt and the water and the air." She said it without any change in her voice at all. "We went in anyway, because it was copper, and copper was bread that year, and nobody else would go."

"Gran."

"I haven't finished. Write."

"The copper was in the instrument rooms," said Senna. "Deep in. Under the main building, down stairs, where the walls went thick. Rooms and rooms of them. And in every room there were racks. Floor to ceiling. Steel frames. And in the racks there were grey boxes, row after row, with little windows in the front, all dark." She opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling. "Like the ones in that shed. Exactly like. I've been lying here eleven days at a time for three weeks looking at the back of that shed through a door, telling myself it wasn't. It is."

Toren did not write. He could not. He sat with the pencil on the page.

"We pulled the backs off the racks for the wire," said Senna. "That was where the good copper was. Behind the boxes, in bundles, thick as your arm. We cut it out and rolled it. And the boxes we threw on the floor, because they were in the way." She moved her folded hands, very slightly, on the blanket. "They were heavy. Far too heavy for what they were. And they were warm. Not hot. Warm, like a stone that's been in the sun. The man who ran the crew said never to carry one. He said they'd cook your back through your coat in a day. So we left them where they fell. On the floor. Hundreds of them, in the end. We had eleven days."

"Hundreds," said Toren.

"On the floor. Where we dropped them. Nobody's been back that I ever heard of." She turned her head on the pillow, at last, and looked at him. "The mark. On your paper. The wheel with four spokes and the bar under it. It was on every door on the way in. It was stamped on the racks. I walked past it eleven days running and never once wondered what it was, because it wasn't copper."

He looked at the paper, in his coat pocket, on the other side from the stone. He did not take it out. He did not need to.

"You knew," he said. "On the tenth day. When you said it in the corridor."

"I knew on the tenth day that there were racks. I knew the day you showed me that it was the same racks." She held his eyes. "I've been deciding since then whether to tell you where they are. Because I know you, Toren Voss. And I knew your father, and I knew his father, and I know what every man in that line does with a number once he's got it."

"What does he do?"

"He goes and gets it," said Senna. "With a paper and three ifs."

In the chair between the beds, Jab had not moved. He was looking at the floor with his elbows on his knees. Toren could not see his face. He could see his hands, hanging loose between his knees, and one of them had closed.

"Listen to me," said Senna. "This is the part to write down most."

"I'm not telling you to go," she said. "I'm telling you where it is. Those are different. You'll want to make them the same. Don't." He had not expected it. Rook had sent him round with a message for Pell, who was at the south lip with the atlas, and on the way back Toren stopped for a moment beside her, because his leg was pulling where the stitches had been and she was standing where there was room to stop. The argument happened in the shed that evening, and Toren had never heard adults argue like it. Not on the road. On the road, when adults argued, it was about which way to go, and it ended when somebody went.

This did not end.

The Director's card had come at the fourth hour, to the shed, not to the huts. It said the yard was requested to support the Sag lift on the twenty-fifth day, at first light, under the day holder's line and Mr Marsh's, on terms to be agreed by the yard and written down. It said attendance is not compulsory. Wyck had read it out loud and said nothing afterward, which was worse than anything he could have said.

"Not ready to hold. Nobody's asking that. Ready to be behind a line with a crew, doing a thing they already know how to do, with real ash and real ground. They've had a week of the yard." He put it back on. It was warm. Not hot. Warm, like a stone that has been in the sun. The iron cap of a post that had stood in a frozen dip all night in fog was as warm as a cup of tea somebody had held.

He knew it. He knew it the way you know a smell. He had put his hand on warm iron once before, on the south road, on the approach, with the ground moving under all of them and Rook's face going still; and he had seen clean ash lying in a ditch with nothing killed to make it; and he had seen—

He looked at the post. The lamp on its pole stood above and behind him on the track, and the post threw a shadow down across the stone and into the fog.

It threw two.

"Callie," he said.

She looked up from the drift.

"The post's warm," said Toren. "The iron. And look at the shadow." He did not know how to say the rest of it. "I've seen that before. On the south road. Before the Breach. It's the ground. It's the ground going thin."

Callie looked at the post. She looked at the two shadows lying across the stone from one post and one lamp. Then she did not look at the post at all. She looked past it, down, into the fog, toward where the sixth post was and where the slab was, and she went very still.

It was not the stillness of somebody reading. Toren had watched her read. This was the other stillness, the one she had on the bench outside the scan room when the knocking stopped.

"It's going," she said.

She was on her feet. "The slab. At six. It's going. The water's gone off it—the fog's different—" She did not finish. "Katori."

Katori was already moving. She had heard the name the way Katori heard everything, and she came down the track with her hand up, flat, and said in a voice that did not go loud but went everywhere, "Back. All of you. Ten paces up. Now. Leave the jars."

The lifters got up. The weighbridge girl got up, looking at Callie. Otto was already moving, pulling one of the yard lifters by the coat.

Toren turned and shouted up the track to the lip, into the white, as loud as he had ever shouted anything.

"Six! The slab at six!"

He heard Pell's whistle. One blast, short. Not the three. The one that meant listen.

Then Pell's voice came down the track, pitched to carry. "Minute twenty. Holder, do we change?"

The day holder answered from her hollow without turning her head. "Not with six moving." Her voice was low and rough, and it carried the way voices carry over still water. "I let go to hand it over, there's a gap, and the gap's on six. I've got it down. I'll keep it." Then, louder than Toren had known she could speak, across three hundred paces of white to the north lip: "Keep your hands down, Marsh."

From post five Toren could not see the north lip. He would learn afterward that Rook had kept them down, and that nobody had said when they would try the change again.

It was not the crack he had expected. It was a long low sound from under everything, like a door the size of a barn being opened very slowly on hinges nobody had oiled in seventy years, and it came up through his boots and into his shins where the struts lived and into his teeth. The fog moved. It did not blow. It rose, all at once, in the bottom of the dip, the way a sheet rises when somebody underneath it sits up.

At the south lip, the day holder shouted a single word.

"Weight!"

Toren did not understand the word. He understood her voice. It was the voice of a woman holding a door against something on the other side of it, and the something had just leaned.

Then the fog in front of him was full of shapes.

They came up out of the white below post five in a rush, low and many-legged and fast, the colour of an old wasp nest, and he knew them. Husks. He had killed them on a road with an ugly spike on the second day of his life with it. There were more of them than he could count and they were coming up out of the ditch below the track like water coming up out of a drain, and they went, the way husks always went, at the nearest living thing.

The nearest living things were Callie and the weighbridge girl, ten paces up the track from where they had been, and Toren, with a canvas bag of jars on his shoulder.

She was ten paces above Callie and twenty above him, and between them the husks were coming up onto the track out of the ditch in a stream, and he saw her see it, and see that she could not get down to them through it, and see what that meant, all in the time it took her to cut two more.

"West!" Katori's voice, not loud, everywhere. "The door! Across!"

Across was the bottom of the dip, in the fog, where the line was.

Toren took the canvas bag off his shoulder and dropped it.

He heard the jars break on the stone as he went. He did not look. He had a Flask and more in that bag, the whole of the morning's first hour, weighed and written, and it went down onto the track with a sound like somebody dropping crockery, and he did not look at it. It was like going into a lake.

The white came up to his waist and then his chest and closed over the tops of the lamps, and there was nothing to see but white, and under his boots the slabs of the bottom of the Sag tilted and slid and had black water between them. He could not see the other side; he could not see anything. He went the way he had seen the map go: across, and west, toward the gap in the shoulder with Jab's name in it.

Somewhere behind them something was coming. He could hear it, the scrabble of too many legs on wet stone.

The weighbridge girl went down.

He felt it through Callie's arm. Something came out of the white on the left and hit the girl low, at the hip, and she went down sideways with a cry. And then—he saw it; he would remember seeing it—she did not put her hand out. She tucked her chin and rounded her back and went onto the slab like a sack, the way Wyck had made Otto fall a hundred times on the heap, and rolled, and came up onto her knees with her left arm held against her chest and her face white.

The husk that had hit her was turning.

Toren let go of Callie's sleeve and let the spike come.

It came crooked and ugly, and he did not ask it for the light, because there was no time to ask for anything. He put the heel of it into the husk where its shoulder was, the bad thick heel that he had used to hook branches out of a ditch, and dragged it round and off the girl, and then he put the point in, and it came apart into grey.

"Up," he said to the girl, and got his good hand under her arm, and hauled.

She came up. She had her left wrist against her chest and her face was the colour of the fog, and she said, very clearly, in the voice of somebody who wants it known, "I fell right. I fell right, he'll want to know, I fell right—"

"I know," said Toren. "I saw. Go."

They came out of the white at the foot of the west shoulder.

The fog was thinner there, going up the slope, and he could see the gap in the shoulder above them, the long easy slope that came down from the rim to the ditch, the one door. Jab was in it.

Jab had lit his knife.

The amber line lay along the whole edge of it, from the grip to the hook, without a gutter at the belly, and Jab had stepped out of the door onto the open slope with the knife held up high in front of him like a lantern, and the husks that had come across the bottom behind them, following them through the fog, were going to him.

Not to them. To him.

Toren understood it all at once, the way Jab had once understood a fence. Husks came at anything worked, anything lit. A light held up in the fog was the brightest worked thing on the whole Sag, and every husk in the bottom of the dip turned to it like water turning to a drain. Jab had not lit it to fight them. He had lit it to call them. He was standing out on the open slope with the whole of his light held up, calling them off three people coming out of the fog behind him.

A husk reached him. He put the knife through it, belly first, the fat of the blade, and it came apart. Another. On the rim above him Ines stood up off her stone and loosed, and the arrow went into the fog, and something in the fog stopped moving.

"Go up," said Toren to the weighbridge girl. "Up the door. Along the rim, to the lip. Dessa's there. She'll count you."

The girl went. She went up the long slope past Jab with her wrist against her chest, and Jab moved as she passed him, half a step, so that his light was between her and the bottom, and did not look at her.

Callie had not gone up.

She was standing at the foot of the door, at the bottom of the slope, beside the stone wing wall where the west mouth of the pipe came out under the road. She was not looking at Jab, or at the husks, or at Toren. She was looking at the pipe.

"The water's stopped," she said.

Toren looked. The west mouth of the pipe was a stone arch as wide as a man's shoulders, set in the wing wall, with a fringe of dead grass hanging over it. Since the rain it had been running: a steady low pour of black water out of the arch and into the ditch, day and night, loud enough to hear.

It was not running. Water stood in the bottom of the arch, black and still, and nothing came out of it.

"Something's in it," said Callie. "Something big enough to stop it." She turned her head and looked up the slope, at Jab's lit knife, and then east, across the fog, toward the ditch on the other side, where the husks had come up and where the pipe began. "They didn't come at the light."

"What didn't?"

"Whatever came up after the husks," said Callie. "It didn't come to the light. It went to the dark. And the only dark there is between there and here is—"

She did not finish; she was already moving. She went along the foot of the wing wall to the arch with the hook in both hands, bolt in, the long reach, and Toren saw what she was going to do a heartbeat before she did it, and saw what it would leave her with, and could not stop her, and would not have.

She put the curve of the hook across the mouth of the pipe, corner to corner, the way the fence rail had lain across the other end six days ago. She planted her feet in the ditch. She did not grip.

Jab was already looking east again. Into the fog. His knife had gone dark on his fist; Toren had not seen him put it out.

Jab did not answer. He did not know how, Toren thought afterward; or he knew and there was not time. He went down off the shoulder into the fog on the far side of the door, dark, with no light on him at all, and the white closed over him and he was gone.

Toren stood in the one door with the spike lit on his fist.

He knew who. He had seen her go. When the ground spoke and the fog sat up and her voice shouted weight, he had been looking straight up the track at the south lip, and he had seen the grey shape there leave its hollow for the first time in two years. She had gone down alone into the white, toward the corner where the slab had gone, to lay back down a line that had folded. Then the husks had come up out of the ditch, and he had taken Callie by the sleeve and run, and he had not called it to Pell or to Katori or to anybody. He was the runner. He was the one who was meant to know where everybody was.

Nobody else had been looking at her. Nobody ever was. He had been, and he had said nothing, and now Jab was going to her in the dark on his own.

A husk came up the slope out of the fog at his light.

He put the spike through it. The light shook. He did not let it. He held it like a thing that was his, that looked like him, that nobody was going to take.

Another. Another. His arm was in its sling and his right hand was doing all of it, and the seam across the palm was on fire, and the third one got past the point and hit him at the knee, and he went down.

He went down like a sack. Chin tucked. Back round. Onto the meat of his good shoulder, not the arm, never the arm, and rolled, and came up on one knee in the gap in the shoulder with frost and ash all down one side of him.

The spike was still lit.

He looked at it. He had fallen, and it had not gone out. It had never not gone out. It lay along the crooked edge, thin and white-grey and cold and steady, as if falling were a thing that happened to other people.

He got up. He stood in the one door, where Jab had put him, with the light held out where he did not want them. Below him, at the foot of the slope, a girl with nothing on her stood with her feet in a ditch and her hands on a pale hook across the mouth of a pipe, counting out loud.

"Forty-one," said Callie. "Forty-two."

Beyond her, the fog lay across the bottom of the Sag, white and flat and silent, and somewhere in it, going east without a light, was Jab.
