Chapter 34 — The Yellow Stair

Inside the fence the ground was a floor, and Callie treated it like one from the first step.

"Stop," she said, three paces in.

They stopped. Toren was on the front rope beside her, Jab stood with the frame on the outside of the sledge, and Dee was at the back with the back rope and the glass. Nobody asked her why. She had read the pen on her knees for a quarter of an hour with a counter, and they waited while she read this standing up.

The yard was concrete, cracked into slabs, with weeds in the cracks. The combed lines of grey ran across it from the south and the east toward the foot of the road. Most of them were no wider than a finger, but they were everywhere, and there was no straight way from the gate to the road that did not cross forty of them.

"We'll drag the board over them," said Toren.

"We'll drag the board through them," said Callie. "And then it'll have grey on its bottom all the way up the hill, and down the stair, and into the room. And back." She looked along the yard. "And we'll walk in them. And it'll be on our boots."

"Then where?"

Callie looked for the clean way, one slab at a time, the way she had read the pen.

The pipes ran across the yard on their left, side by side on their concrete blocks, from the river bank to the foot of the hill. Each block was the size of a crate, and there was one every five paces, with the pipes lying rusted in the saddles on top. The combed lines went under the pipes, through the gaps between the blocks. But along the north side of the blocks, in their lee, the concrete was bare. The lines went round the end of each block and under it, and on the north side of every block there was a strip a pace wide where the grey had not settled, because the lines had somewhere else to be.

"Along the pipes," said Callie. "On the north side. Close to the blocks. It's clean there. Mostly." She took a step and held out the counter and waited ten breaths. "Eighteen. At the first block. It was twenty-four in the middle."

"Along the pipes," said Dee.

They went along the pipes.

It was a hundred paces to the foot of the road, and Callie read the counter every ten breaths and said the number out loud. Eighteen. Twenty. Nineteen. Twenty-two, where a line of grey came round the end of a block and lay across their way, and she made them lift the front of the board over it.

At the gatehouse she stopped for a breath and looked in at the window.

It was dark inside. There was a concrete floor with a stove on it, cold and rusted, and two old bottles lying on their sides by the stove, and a black ring on the floor where somebody had made a fire that was not in the stove. On the back wall there were marks in white chalk. They were strokes in a row, the kind you make to count days, and Callie counted them without meaning to. Eleven. And a twelfth, begun: a short stroke that went down half as far as the others and stopped.

She knew whose they were. She thought about the two men who had slept inside on the second night because they were drunk, and been sick in the morning, and thought they had got away with it. She thought about somebody on the last morning picking up the chalk to make the twelfth stroke, and stopping halfway down, because there was not going to be a twelfth day.

"Twenty-two," she said, holding the counter to the window. "At the hut."

"Callie," said Dee.

"Twenty-two," said Callie again, and went on. She did not say anything about the strokes.

The road was the hard part.

It went up the side of the hill in the two long slants they had seen from the gate. It was concrete too, and it had been good concrete once, wide enough for a lorry. The pipes went up beside it on the inside on their blocks, and the lines of grey went up the middle of it like the ruts on the old north road.

The board would not go up it on its own. Toren and Callie pulled with the front rope over their shoulders, leaning into it, while Jab walked on the inside beside the carrier with his hand not on it and Dee walked behind, never below. Every twenty paces Toren said "Turn," and Callie took the end of the front rope once round the nearest pipe block, the way Pell had said, and the four of them stood and breathed with the rope holding the weight instead of them.

"Round something that doesn't move," said Toren, at the third one.

"You don't have to say it every time," said Callie.

"I do," said Toren. "Or I'll forget, at the one that matters."

At the first bend the road had gone.

Not all of it. Something under the outside half had washed out, and the concrete had cracked along the middle of the road, and the outer half had tilted down the hill like a lid half off a box. The inside half, against the pipes, was still flat. In the corner of the bend, on the inside, there was a pile of grey heaped against the foot of the pipe blocks, where the lines came round the bend and bunched up. It was the first thing like a drift they had seen inside the fence.

"Forty," said Callie, from four paces off. "At the heap." She looked at the slumped half. "The inside's good. The outside's hollow. You can see the crack. There's nothing under it but air."

"Then between," said Dee. "Not the heap. Not the hollow."

There was a pace and a half between the heap and the crack, and the board was a pace wide.

They took it through. Toren went first with the rope, and Callie went on the heap side, walking with her left boot a hand's width from the grey and her eyes on the crack. The board came round the bend with its outside edge on the crack and its inside edge a finger from the heap, and it touched neither.

"Good," said Dee, behind them. It was the first time she had said it all day, and all three of them heard that it was.

At the top of the second slant Dee stopped them for the halt. It was twenty turns by the glass since the gate. They stood on the road below the last bend with the rope round a pipe block, and the wind did not come, and the grey lay still in its lines. Dee put her back to the hill and made them stand in a row in front of her, not sit, and she drew them.

She did it off the open jar, with the mouth of it against her left palm and her right hand flat on each chest in turn: Toren first, then Callie, then Jab, who stood with the frame on and his hands held out from his sides and watched her do it.

"Busy," said Dee to herself, on Toren. And, after a while, "Clean."

There was a little on all of them. It was what the yard had given them, and the heap at the bend. She drew it and held it high and vented it into the concrete of the road, which was the road's to have and not hers. Then she held her own hands out to Jab, palms up.

He held his palm over them an inch off. "Right," he said. "A bit." And he took it.

"Say it," said Dee.

Callie weighed the jar on her palm, the way she weighed everything now.

"A Handful gone," she said. "And a bit. For the four of us and the vent." She gave it back. "Six and three quarters in it."

"Six and three quarters," said Dee. She turned the glass. "Twenty more, and we'll be down the stair."

The building was bigger than it had looked from the gate.

It stood across the top of the road like a cliff, three rows of windows high, flat and grey, with long streaks of rust running down from the window frames like old tears. Every window was black. Some still had glass in them, and most did not. The door at the top of the road had been a pair of doors, and the doors were gone. There was only a square of dark as high as two men, with a step in front of it and the hinges hanging off the frame.

The grey went in at the door.

Callie stood on the step with the counter out and watched it. The combed lines came up the road and across the step and over the edge of it into the dark, all of them. Ten breaths, and they slid a hand's width over the edge of the step. Three, and they lay still.

"Thirty-eight," she said. "At the door."

She did not go in yet. She stepped back off the step and looked up.

From here they could all see past the north end of the building. The round wall stood up behind it, very near now, higher than the building and grey. It was not a wall so much as a great drum, round, with its top broken off in jags. And over the drum, over the whole top of the hill, there was the sky.

It was not the size of a cart wheel now. It was too big to be any size at all. It was over them, and its edge was a long way up and a long way round, the way the edge of a cloud is when you are standing under the middle of it. On one side the clouds went into it, and on the other side they came out, not quite right. All the way round its edge ran the light the colour of the bottom of a pond, thickening, and thinning.

Callie had promised herself on the road that she would not look at it for long. She looked at it now on purpose. Somebody had to, and this was the kind of looking she was good at.

"I'm counting it," she said. "For the paper."

Toren had the paper out already, and the pencil.

The edge thickened, and Callie counted. Ten. It thinned. Three.

Ten, and it thickened. At the end of the ten, as it began to thin, something came out of the bottom of it.

It came out of the underside of the dark, over the drum, and fell. It was not a thing. It was a long thin fall of grey, like the rain you see coming down out of the bottom of a cloud a long way off, when it is raining over there and not here. It came down out of the sky in a veil and went down behind the broken rim of the drum, and was gone.

"Something came out," said Callie.

Toren's pencil stopped.

"A fall of grey," said Callie. "Out of the bottom of it. At the three. Into the round one." She did not stop looking. "One."

She watched ten more counts, and then twenty more. The edge thickened and thinned, and nothing fell.

"One," she said. "In twelve counts. Say that. I'll count it again when we come out."

Toren wrote it, and they all heard the pencil. Top of the road. The sky: ten and three. 1 fall of grey out of the bottom, into the round wall, at the three. In 12.

"That's what comes out of it," said Toren quietly. "She asked."

"That's what came out of it while I was looking," said Callie.

Inside it was dark after ten paces.

There was a hall first, with the light from the door lying across its floor in a long pale square. The floor was grey all over, not in lines here but in a skin of dust with the lines drawn on it. A long counter ran across one side of the hall with nothing on it. On the wall above the counter there was a sign whose letters had gone to nothing, and in the middle of the sign there was the mark: the wheel with four spokes and a bar under it. It was the mark on Toren's plate.

Toren stopped and looked at it. He touched his coat on the side where the plate was, and went on.

On the left of the hall a corridor went away south along the length of the building. It was wide and dark, with doors down both sides of it, and the grey lines went across the hall and into it and along it.

"Lanterns," said Dee.

She lit them from the box of lights in her bag. The tallow caught slowly, and the light came up yellow and small, and the corridor came out of the dark round them in a circle ten paces wide. The walls were concrete, painted a colour that had once been green, with a line of grey dust along the foot of each wall and the combed lines down the middle of the floor.

"Husks come at a light," said Toren.

"Husks come at a light," said Dee. "And we can't see without one. That's two things that are true." She hung one lantern from Toren's belt and gave the other to Jab, who hung it from the frame. "If they come, they come at the lantern. I'd rather they came at the lantern than at somebody's hands."

They went along the corridor.

Ten paces along it, on the left, there was a gap in the wall, wider than a door, with a rail along its edge.

The rail was iron. Somebody had painted it yellow a long time ago, and most of the paint had flaked off in patches the size of fingernails, and what was left was pale and dirty. But it was yellow. It was the yellow of the line on the floor of the shed at Meridian: the line Tonk had been rolled across on his bed, and Callie's mother on hers, and Senna on hers every eleven days. Each of the three of them had somebody who had been rolled across it.

The stair went down beside the rail into the dark.

Toren stood at the top of it with the lantern on his belt and his good hand on the yellow rail, and for a while he did not say anything. Jab's left hand had gone to his coat, over the left side, and stayed there.

"Gran's been looking at that line for five weeks," said Toren.

"I know," said Callie.

It was a long stair.

The first flight went down east, toward the front of the building, thirty steps along the right-hand wall. It came to a landing against the building's east wall and turned back on itself, and the second flight went down the other way, west, twenty steps more, back under the corridor above. The walls got thicker as they went down. They could see it at the landing, where the corner of the wall was cut back and the concrete was as deep as Callie's arm was long.

Beside the steps there was a slope.

Callie saw it at the top and did not understand it at first. It was a smooth ramp of concrete a pace wide, running down beside the steps against the wall all the way to the landing, and then on down beside the second flight. There was an iron ring set in the wall at the top of it, as thick as her thumb.

"For barrows," said Toren. "For bringing things up. Things you don't carry."

"For wire," said Callie. "Hundreds of rolls of it. For eleven days."

The grey lines went down the stair. They went down the steps and down the ramp a hand's width at a time, into the dark below the landing.

They took the sledge down the ramp on the back rope. Toren put the rope through the iron ring at the top and took the end of it round his waist, over his good hip, and Callie took it behind him. Dee and Jab stayed at the top with the lantern, above the sledge and not below it, until it was down, because that was the rule: nobody below a load on a slope. The board went down the ramp slowly, a pace at a time, with the rope paying out through the ring and round Toren's waist and through Callie's hands, while the carrier sat on it and did not shift. At the landing it stopped against the turn of the wall with a soft knock. They went down the steps beside it and did the same again from a second ring in the landing wall.

"Fifty," said Callie, at the landing. And at the bottom, "Fifty-six."

The door was there.

The last of the steps came down to a flat place four paces across, and the ramp ended there too. On the far side of the flat place, facing them, set in a square of concrete wall, was the door.

It was steel. It had been painted grey once, and now it was the colour of a knife that has been left out. It sat in a steel frame in the concrete. In the middle of it, at the height of Toren's face, the wheel was stamped into the steel, big, as wide as a hand, with its four spokes and its bar. Below the wheel there was a handle: a bar of iron as long as a forearm, bolted across the door at both ends.

Against the foot of the door there was a heap of grey.

It had come down the stair in its lines, and down the ramp, and across the flat place, and it had met the door and stopped. It had heaped up against the bottom of the steel the way snow heaps against a shut door in a yard, a hand deep at the door and thinning out toward the stair. While they watched, the lines coming down the stair slid a hand's width into the heap. At the very bottom of the door, where the steel did not quite meet the concrete, there was a crack no wider than a knife blade, and the grey was going under it. It went very thin, in threads. Ten, and three.

"It's been getting under that for twenty years," said Dee quietly.

Nobody went near the door.

They stood at the bottom of the stair in the lantern light, all four of them, and looked at the steel. Nobody needed to say whose words were in the stair with them. They were an old woman's, from a pillow. Every man on that crew put his shoulder to it on the first morning and it didn't move. We stood at the bottom of the stair shoving at it for a quarter of an hour, eleven of us, in the dark.

"Fifty-eight," said Callie. "At the heap."

"Pull it," said Toren.

He said it quietly, to the door.

He put his good hand on the iron bar.

Callie put the point of the hook through the gap between the bar and the steel, from the side, and took the conduit in both hands. Jab came and put his hand on the bar beside Toren's, and did not take the frame off, and set his feet.

"On three," said Toren. "Not the three. My three."

Jab made a sound that might have been a laugh.

"One," said Toren. "Two. Three."

They pulled, and the door did not move. It did not move at all. Callie felt the hook bite into the bar and the conduit go hard in her hands, and nothing gave anywhere. It was like pulling on a wall.

"Again," said Toren. "It sticks. She said it sticks."

They pulled again. Toren leaned back on the bar with his whole weight, and Jab with his, and Callie with hers on the hook, and the door came. It did not come the way a door comes. It came the way the slide had come on the box in the pen, all at once, with a shriek of steel on steel that went up the stair and round the landing and on up into the building over their heads. It swung toward them a hand's width, and stuck, and came again. As it came, the bottom of the door swept the heap at its foot back across the flat place toward the stair in a long grey fan, and they stepped back out of the way of it.

The door stood open toward them, as wide as a man.

Nobody said anything. The shriek went on in the stair above them for a long time after the door had stopped.

"How long?" said Toren.

"About thirty breaths," said Dee. "From pull it."

Toren stood with his hand on the bar. His face in the lantern light was not a face the others had seen on him before. It looked as if somebody had said something to him in a room where nobody else was, and he had heard it.

"Thirty breaths," he said.

"Write it," said Dee. "Tonight. She'll want it."

Beyond the door there was the long corridor.

It was the one Senna had meant; they could tell by how long it was. The lantern on Toren's belt went into it and did not find the end. It was lower than the corridor upstairs and narrower, three paces wide, with a concrete floor and concrete walls and a concrete ceiling not much higher than Toren could have reached with his good arm, and it went straight away from them into the dark.

"West," said Callie.

The others looked at her, and she said the rest of it out loud so that they had it too.

"The first flight went down east," she said. "Toward the front. The second came back west, under the corridor. We stepped off the bottom step facing west, with the door in front of us." She held the counter out into the dark. "It goes the same way. Into the hill. Under the building, and then on under the top. Toward the drum."

"West," said Toren. "Three doors."

On the left of the corridor, a little way along, there was a door, open, a black oblong in the concrete. Further along, the lantern found another. At the very edge of the light there was the dark shape of a third.

On the right-hand wall there were no doors. There was a tray.

It ran along the wall high up, a hand below the ceiling, as far as the light went. It was steel, like a long open trough the width of two hands, held to the wall on iron brackets. It was the kind of thing wire had lain in, in bundles as thick as an arm. Senna's crew had come down this corridor and cut the copper out of it, and rolled it, and taken it up the stair on the ramp.

There was grey in it now. At first it looked like dust: a thick soft line of grey lying in the tray all along, the colour of the dust on the floor, the dust of twenty years fallen into a trough.

"Callie," said Dee. "The floor."

Callie read the floor. There was a heap on this side of the door too, a hand deep, against the steel where the gap was. It was twenty years of what had come under the gap in threads. Now it was spreading out on the concrete in threads again, and the threads were going west. They went along the floor at the foot of the right-hand wall, under the tray. And then they went up the wall.

They went up the wall.

The four of them watched them do it. Thin grey threads, finer than hair, went up the bare concrete from the floor to the tray a hand's width at a time, as if the wall were a floor. Ten, and they climbed. Three, and they stopped, stuck to the wall. And they went into the tray.

Callie lifted the counter and held it out toward the tray at the length of her arm and waited ten breaths.

"Seventy," she said. "At the tray. It's sixty on the floor."

While she was waiting, while she was counting the ten, the grey in the tray moved.

It was not dust. The whole long soft line of it in the trough moved at once, the way the threads moved. It slid west along the tray a hand's width in ten breaths, and stopped for three. It was not dust lying in a trough. It was a rope, a rope of grey as thick as Callie's wrist, lying in the tray the whole length of the corridor, and the threads were going into it off the floor and the wall, and the whole rope was going west.

"Toren," said Callie.

"It's not lying there," said Callie. "It's going. It's all going. The floor's going into it, and it's going—"

"West," said Jab.

He said it very quietly. He had not said anything since the door. He was standing behind them with the frame on his back and the lantern hanging from it and his hand flat on his coat over the left side, and he was not looking at the rope. His eyes were half shut.

"It's the same," he said. "It's the ten and three. It's the same as the wash." He opened his eyes. "It's pulling harder here."

The first door was twelve paces along, on the left. They went to it along the left-hand wall, the side away from the tray, with Callie in front reading the floor and the sledge scraping behind them over the threads, because there was nothing else to do with it.

Over the doorway there was a place where the tray sent a branch across. A thinner rope, like a branch off a trunk, went across the ceiling in the small trough and in through the hole.

The room was not large. It was six paces deep and five across, and the air in it was colder than the corridor. Along the right-hand wall there were big grey cabinets as tall as a man and twice as wide, with doors on their fronts, standing in a row. Along the left-hand wall there were racks. The boxes were on the floor.

They lay on their sides and on their faces and on each other, tumbled, the way stones lie at the foot of a wall that has come down, and some had rolled out into the middle of the room. So we left them where they fell. On the floor. Hundreds of them, in the end.

"There," said Toren. His voice was not steady. "There they are."

The rope came down through the hole above the door and down the wall inside the room, just to the left of the doorframe, a line of grey as thick as two fingers, crawling. It reached the floor and went along the foot of the wall to the left, into the heap of boxes. In the heap it split. It went in among the boxes in finer strands, like roots going in among stones, under them and round them and between them, all through the heap. They could see the strands on the grey steel, grey on grey, where one lay over a box and went down the other side.

It was lying where the wire had been. It had gone where the crew had pulled the copper out, behind the racks and down, and through the boxes the crew had thrown on the floor to get at it.

"Eighty," she said. "At the boxes. From the door."

Then she said the thing she had seen, plainly, the way her grandfather would have said it at the scale house in one line.

In the far corner of the room, where the racks ended at the back wall, there was a heap of grey. Callie had seen it and not thought about it. It was larger than the others, heaped up in the corner against the back wall at the end of the boxes, and the rope went into it. All the fine strands from the heap of boxes ran along the foot of the racks and into it, the way the roots of a tree go down into the ground.

At Jab's three, it moved.

It moved outward. Everything else in that place had moved inward all afternoon, and this moved out. The grey in the corner swelled as if something under it had drawn a breath. It heaved up, and cracked along the top, and something came up out of it the colour of an old wasp nest.

It stood.

"Husk," said Callie.

It had no face. It turned toward her, toward the tongs in her hands and the lantern and the worked things in the room, and it came along the foot of the racks at her.

Toren went past her. He had the spike on his fist before she saw it come, dark and heavy and crooked, and he lit the heel of it, the hand's width along the hook, and put it into the husk as it came, low. He dragged it round against the racks and put the point in.

It came apart. It came apart all at once, the way they did, into grey. There was no wind down here, so the grey did not go up in a burst the way it had at the wash. It fell. It came down on the boxes and the floor in a heap, a husk's worth of loose grey, and lay there.

"A hundred," said Callie, with the counter out. "It's a hundred, near the corner."

"Back," said Dee. "Everybody back from it. To the door."

They went back to the door. Toren let the spike go dark. The four of them stood in the doorway, with the lantern hanging from Jab's frame, and looked at the heap of grey on the floor where the husk had been.

It lay there for ten breaths.

Then it began to go. It went in threads, the way everything went. They came out of the heap and went along the floor toward the foot of the racks, toward the strands of the rope, and into them. It was faster than the threads in the yard, much faster. The whole heap drew out into lines and slid into the rope and was taken along it into the corner, into the big heap, in ten breaths, and the floor where the husk had come apart was bare concrete again.

Three.

The heap in the corner swelled.

None of them could have looked anywhere else. It swelled the way it had before, all at once, as if it had drawn a breath. It heaved up, and cracked along the top, and the thing that came up out of it was the colour of an old wasp nest. It stood, and turned toward the lantern.

"It's the same one," said Callie.
