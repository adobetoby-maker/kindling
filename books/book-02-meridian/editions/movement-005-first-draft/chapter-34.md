# Chapter 34 — Callie: The Yellow Stair

Inside the fence the ground was a floor.

Callie knew it the moment she was through the gate. It was not a road any more, and not a field. Everything she looked at, she looked at the way she had looked at the concrete of the pen: where the dust lay, where the water had gone, which way the grey was going, and what was clean. She had read the pen for a quarter of an hour on her knees with a counter. She would read this with the sledge rope on her shoulder, walking.

"Stop," she said, three paces in.

They stopped. Toren had the front rope beside her. Jab stood with the frame on the outside of the sledge, and Miss Wren was behind with the back rope and the glass.

The yard was concrete, cracked into slabs, with weeds in the cracks. The combed lines of grey ran across it from the south and the east toward the foot of the road. They were thin, most of them no wider than a finger. But they were everywhere. There was no straight way from the gate to the road that did not cross forty of them.

"We'll drag the board over them," said Toren.

"We'll drag the board through them," said Callie. "And then it'll have grey on its bottom all the way up the hill, and down the stair, and into the room. And back." She looked along the yard. "And we'll walk in them. And it'll be on our boots."

"Then where?"

She looked for the clean way. She did it the way she had read the pen, one slab at a time.

The pipes came out of the river bank on her right and went across the yard to the foot of the hill, side by side, on their concrete blocks. The blocks were each the size of a crate, one every five paces. The pipes lay in the saddles on top of them, rusted, and between the blocks there was the gap under the pipes where the concrete was in shadow.

The combed lines of grey went under the pipes, through the gaps between the blocks. But along the north side of the blocks, in the lee of them, the concrete was bare. The lines went round the end of each block and under, and on the north side of each one there was a strip a pace wide where the grey had not settled, because the lines had somewhere else to be.

"Along the pipes," said Callie. "On the north side. Close to the blocks. It's clean there. Mostly." She took a step and held out the counter and waited ten breaths. "Eighteen. At the first block. It was twenty-four in the middle."

"Along the pipes," said Miss Wren.

They went along the pipes.

---

It was a hundred paces to the foot of the road. Callie read the counter every ten breaths and said it out loud. Eighteen. Twenty. Nineteen. Twenty-two, where a line of grey came round the end of a block and lay across their way, and she made them lift the front of the board over it.

At the gatehouse she stopped for a breath and looked in at the window.

It was dark inside. There was a floor of concrete with a stove on it, cold and rusted. There were two old bottles on their sides by the stove, and a black ring on the floor where somebody had made a fire that was not in the stove. On the back wall, in white chalk, there were marks. They were strokes, in a row, the kind you make to count days. She counted them without meaning to.

Eleven. And a twelfth, begun, a short stroke that went down half as far as the others and stopped.

"Twenty-two," she said, holding the counter to the window. "At the hut."

She did not say anything about the strokes. She thought about two men who had slept inside on the second night because they were drunk, and been sick in the morning, and thought they had got away with it. She thought about somebody on the last morning picking up the chalk to make a twelfth stroke. They had stopped halfway down, because there was not going to be a twelfth day.

"Callie," said Miss Wren.

"Twenty-two," said Callie again, and went on.

---

The road was the hard part.

It went up the side of the hill in two long slants, the way she had seen it from the gate. It was concrete too, and it had been good concrete once, wide enough for a lorry. The pipes went up beside it on the inside, on their blocks, and the lines of grey went up the middle of it like the ruts on the old north road.

The board would not go up it on its own. Toren and Callie pulled, with the front rope over their shoulders, leaning into it. Jab walked on the inside beside the carrier with his hand not on it. Miss Wren walked behind, not below. Every twenty paces Toren said "Turn," and Callie took the end of the front rope round the nearest pipe block, once, the way Pell had said, and they stood and breathed with the rope holding the weight instead of them.

"Round something that doesn't move," said Toren, at the third one.

"You don't have to say it every time."

"I do," said Toren. "Or I'll forget, at the one that matters."

At the first bend the road had gone.

Not all of it. The outside half had slumped. Something under it had washed out, and the concrete had cracked along the middle of the road and the outer half had tilted down the hill like a lid half off a box. The inside half, against the pipes, was still flat. There was a pile of grey in the corner of the bend, on the inside, heaped against the foot of the pipe blocks where the lines came round and bunched up. It was the first thing like a drift she had seen inside the fence.

"Forty," said Callie, from four paces off. "At the heap." She looked at the slumped half. "The inside's good. The outside's hollow. You can see the crack. There's nothing under it but air."

"Then between," said Miss Wren. "Not the heap. Not the hollow."

There was a pace and a half between the heap and the crack. The board was a pace wide.

They took it through. Toren went first with the rope. Callie went on the heap side, walking with her left boot a hand's width from the grey and her eyes on the crack. The board came round the bend with its outside edge on the crack and its inside edge a finger from the heap, and did not touch either.

"Good," said Miss Wren, behind them, and it was the first time she had said it all day.

---

At the top of the second slant Miss Wren stopped them for the halt.

It was twenty turns by the glass since the gate. They stood on the road below the last bend with the rope round a pipe block, and the wind did not come, and the grey lay still in its lines. Miss Wren put her back to the hill and made them stand in a row in front of her, not sit, and she drew them.

She did it off the open jar, with the mouth of it against her left palm and her right hand flat on each chest in turn. Toren first. Then Callie. Then Jab, who stood with the frame on and his hands out from his sides and watched her do it.

"Busy," said Miss Wren to herself, on Toren. And, after a while, "Clean."

It was a little, on all of them. It was what the yard had given them and the heap at the bend. She drew it and held it high and vented it into the concrete of the road, which was the road's, not hers. Then she held her own hands out to Jab, palms up.

He held his palm over them, an inch off. "Right," he said. "A bit." And took it.

"Say it," said Miss Wren.

Callie weighed the jar on her palm, the way she weighed everything now.

"A Handful gone," she said. "And a bit. For the four of us and the vent." She gave it back. "Six and three quarters in it."

"Six and three quarters," said Miss Wren. She turned the glass. "Twenty more, and we'll be down the stair."

---

The building was bigger than it had looked from the gate.

It stood across the top of the road like a cliff. It went up three rows of windows high, flat and grey, with long streaks of rust running down from the window frames like old tears. Every window was black. Some of them had glass still in them and most did not. The door at the top of the road was a pair of doors that were not there any more. There was only a square of dark as high as two men, with a step in front of it and the hinges hanging off the frame.

The grey went in at the door.

Callie stood on the step with the counter out and watched it. The combed lines came up the road and across the step and over the edge of it, and went in, into the dark, all of them. Ten breaths, and they slid a hand's width over the edge of the step. Three, and they lay still.

"Thirty-eight," she said. "At the door."

She did not go in yet. She stepped back off the step and looked up.

From here she could see past the north end of the building. The round wall stood up behind it, very near now, higher than the building, grey. It was not a wall so much as a great drum, round, with its top broken off in jags. And over the drum, over the whole of the top of the hill, the sky.

It was not the size of a cart wheel now. It was too big to be any size. It was over them. Its edge was a long way up and a long way round, like the edge of a cloud when you are standing under the middle of it. She could see the clouds going into the edge of it on one side and coming out on the other, not quite right. And round the edge, all the way round, the light the colour of the bottom of a pond, thickening, and thinning.

She had promised herself she would not look at it for long. She looked at it now on purpose, because somebody had to, and it was the kind of looking she was good at.

"I'm counting it," she said. "For the paper."

Toren had the paper out already.

The edge thickened. She counted. Ten. It thinned. Three.

Ten. It thickened. And at the end of the ten, as it began to thin, something came out of the bottom of it.

It came out of the underside of the dark, over the drum, and fell. It was not a thing. It was grey. It was a long thin fall of grey, like the rain you see coming down out of the bottom of a cloud a long way off, when it is raining over there and not here. It came down out of the sky in a veil and went down behind the broken rim of the drum, and was gone.

"Something came out," said Callie.

Toren's pencil stopped.

"A fall of grey," said Callie. "Out of the bottom of it. At the three. Into the round one." She did not stop looking. "One."

She watched ten more. Twenty more. The edge thickened and thinned. Nothing fell.

"One," she said. "In twelve counts. Say that. I'll count it again when we come out."

Toren wrote it. She heard the pencil. *Top of the road. The sky: ten and three. 1 fall of grey out of the bottom, into the round wall, at the three. In 12.*

"That's what comes out of it," said Toren quietly. "She asked."

"That's what came out of it while I was looking," said Callie.

---

Inside it was dark after ten paces.

There was a hall first, with light from the door lying across the floor in a long pale square. The floor was grey, not with lines here but all over, a skin of dust with the lines drawn on it. There was a long counter across one side with nothing on it. There was a sign on the wall above it, the letters gone to nothing, and in the middle of the sign the mark, the wheel with four spokes and a bar under it. It was the mark on Toren's plate.

Toren stopped and looked at it. Then he touched his coat, the side the plate was on, and went on.

On the left of the hall a corridor went away south along the length of the building. It was wide and dark and it had doors down both sides of it.

The grey lines went across the hall and into it, and along it.

"Lanterns," said Miss Wren.

She lit them from the box of lights in her bag. The tallow caught slowly, and the light came up yellow and small, and the corridor came out of the dark round them in a circle ten paces wide. It was concrete, painted a colour that had once been green, with a line of grey dust along the foot of each wall and the combed lines down the middle of the floor.

"Husks come at a light," said Toren.

"Husks come at a light," said Miss Wren. "And we can't see without one. That's two things that are true." She hung one lantern from Toren's belt and gave the other to Jab, who hung it from the frame. "If they come, they come at the lantern. I'd rather they came at the lantern than at somebody's hands."

They went along the corridor.

Ten paces along it, on the left, there was a gap in the wall. It was wide, wider than a door. There was a rail at the edge of it.

The rail was iron. Somebody had painted it yellow a long time ago. Most of the paint had gone, flaked off in patches the size of fingernails, and what was left was pale and dirty. But it was yellow. It was the yellow of the line on the floor of the shed at Meridian, the line Tonk had been rolled across on his bed, and Callie's mother on hers, and Senna on hers every eleven days.

The stair went down beside it, into the dark.

Toren stood at the top of it with the lantern on his belt and his hand on the yellow rail. He did not say anything for a while.

"She's been looking at that line for five weeks," he said.

"I know," said Callie.

---

It was a long stair.

It went down in a straight flight along the right-hand wall, thirty steps, and turned on a landing, and went down again the other way, twenty more. The walls got thicker as they went down. Callie could see it at the landing, where the corner of the wall was cut back, and the concrete was as deep as her arm was long.

Beside the steps there was a slope.

She saw it at the top and did not understand it at first. It was a smooth ramp of concrete a pace wide, running down beside the steps against the wall, all the way to the landing, and then on down beside the second flight. There was an iron ring set in the wall at the top of it, as thick as her thumb.

"For barrows," said Toren. "For bringing things up. Things you don't carry."

"For wire," said Callie. "Hundreds of rolls of it. For eleven days."

The grey lines went down the stair. They went down the steps and down the ramp, a hand's width at a time, into the dark below the landing.

They took the sledge down the ramp on the back rope.

Toren put the rope through the iron ring at the top and took the end of it round his waist, over his good hip. Callie took it behind him. Miss Wren and Jab stayed at the top, above the sledge, not below it, with the lantern, until it was down. That was the rule. Nobody below a load on a slope.

The board went down the ramp slowly, a pace at a time, with the rope paying out through the ring and round Toren's waist and through Callie's hands. The carrier sat on it. At the landing it stopped against the turn of the wall with a soft knock, and they went down the steps beside it and did it again from a second ring on the landing wall.

"Fifty," said Callie, at the landing. And at the bottom, "Fifty-six."

---

The door was there.

It stood across the bottom of the stair in a square of concrete wall. The last of the steps came down to a flat place four paces across, and the ramp ended there too, and on the far side of the flat place, facing them, was the door.

It was steel. It had been painted grey once and was now the colour of a knife that has been left out. It was set in a steel frame in the concrete. In the middle of it, at the height of Toren's face, the wheel was stamped into the steel, big, as wide as a hand, with its four spokes and its bar. Below the wheel there was a handle. It was a bar of iron as long as a forearm, bolted across the door at both ends.

Against the foot of the door there was a heap of grey.

It had come down the stair in its lines, and down the ramp, and across the flat place, and met the door, and stopped. It had heaped up against the bottom of the steel the way snow heaps against a shut door in a yard, a hand deep at the door and thinning out toward the stair. As she watched, the lines coming down the stair slid a hand's width into the heap. And at the very bottom of the door, where the steel did not quite meet the concrete, there was a crack no wider than a knife blade, and the grey was going under it. Very thin. In threads. Ten, and three.

"It's been getting under that for twenty years," said Miss Wren quietly.

Nobody went near the door.

Callie understood that they were all doing the same thing. They were all standing at the bottom of the stair in the lantern light, looking at the steel, and every one of them was thinking of an old woman on a pillow. *Every man on that crew put his shoulder to it on the first morning and it didn't move. We stood at the bottom of the stair shoving at it for a quarter of an hour, eleven of us, in the dark.*

"Fifty-eight," said Callie. "At the heap."

"Pull it," said Toren.

He said it quietly, to the door.

---

He put his good hand on the iron bar.

Callie put the point of the hook through the gap between the bar and the steel, from the side, and took the conduit in both hands. Jab came and put his hand on the bar beside Toren's, and did not take the frame off, and set his feet.

"On three," said Toren. "Not the three. My three."

Jab made a sound that might have been a laugh.

"One," said Toren. "Two. Three."

They pulled.

The door did not move.

It did not move at all. Callie felt the hook bite into the bar and the conduit go hard in her hands and nothing give, anywhere. It was like pulling a wall.

"Again," said Toren. "It sticks. She said it sticks."

They pulled again. Toren leaned back on the bar with his whole weight, and Jab with his, and Callie with hers on the hook. And the door came. It did not come the way a door comes. It came the way the slide had come on the box in the pen, all at once, with a shriek of steel on steel that went up the stair and round the landing and up and up into the building over their heads. It swung toward them a hand's width, and stuck, and came again. The heap of grey at its foot went over as the bottom of the door swept through it, and slid, and spilled into the dark on the other side.

The door stood open toward them, as wide as a man.

Nobody said anything. The shriek went on in the stair above them for a long time after the door had stopped.

"How long?" said Toren.

"About thirty breaths," said Miss Wren. "From *pull it*."

Toren stood with his hand on the bar. Callie saw his face in the lantern light. It was not a face she had seen on him before. It was as if somebody had said something to him in a room where nobody else was, and he had heard it.

"Thirty breaths," he said.

"Write it," said Miss Wren. "Tonight. She'll want it."

---

Beyond the door there was the long corridor.

Callie knew it was the one, because it was long. The lantern on Toren's belt went into it and did not find the end. It was lower than the corridor upstairs, and narrower, three paces wide, with a concrete floor and concrete walls and a concrete ceiling not much higher than Toren could have reached with his good arm. It went straight away from them into the dark.

West.

She had worked it out before she had finished looking. The stair had come down along the building's east wall and turned, and the door faced back the way they had come in. The corridor went away from it into the hill. Under the building. And then on, under the top, toward the drum.

"West," she said.

"West," said Toren. "Three doors."

On the left of the corridor, a little way along, there was a door. It was open, a black oblong in the concrete. Past it, further along, the lantern found another one. And further, at the very edge of the light, the dark shape of a third.

On the right-hand wall there were no doors. There was a tray.

It ran along the wall high up, a hand below the ceiling, as far as the light went. It was steel, like a long open trough the width of two hands, held to the wall on iron brackets. It was the kind of thing wire had lain in, bundles of it, thick as an arm. Senna's crew had come down this corridor and cut the copper out of it, and rolled it, and taken it up the stair on the ramp.

There was grey in it now.

Callie thought at first it was dust. It lay in the tray all along, a thick soft line of grey, the colour of the dust on the floor. It was the dust of twenty years, fallen into a trough.

"Callie," said Miss Wren. "The floor."

She read the floor. The grey that had spilled through the door when it swept open was spreading out on the concrete in threads, and the threads were going west. They went along the floor at the foot of the right-hand wall, under the tray. And then they went up the wall.

They went up the wall.

She watched them do it. Thin grey threads, finer than hair, going up the bare concrete from the floor to the tray a hand's width at a time, as if the wall were a floor. Ten, and they climbed. Three, and they stopped, stuck to the wall.

They went into the tray.

She lifted the counter and held it out toward the tray at the length of her arm and waited ten breaths.

"Seventy," she said. "At the tray. It's sixty on the floor."

And while she was waiting, while she was counting the ten, she saw the grey in the tray move.

It was not dust. The whole long soft line of it in the trough moved, all together, the way the threads moved. It slid west along the tray a hand's width. Ten breaths. It stopped. Three.

It was not dust lying in a trough. It was a rope. It was a rope of grey as thick as her wrist, lying in the tray the whole length of the corridor, and the threads were going into it off the floor and the wall. And the whole rope was going west.

"Toren," she said.

"I see it."

"It's not lying there," said Callie. "It's going. It's all going. The floor's going into it, and it's going—"

"West," said Jab.

He said it very quietly. He had not said anything since the door. He was standing behind them with the frame on his back and the lantern hanging from it and his hand flat on his coat over the left side. He was not looking at the rope. He had his eyes half shut.

"It's the same," he said. "It's the ten and three. It's the same as the wash." He opened his eyes. "It's pulling harder here."

---

"Three doors," said Miss Wren. "We don't need the third. We need the first."

The first door was twelve paces along on the left. They went to it along the left-hand wall, the side away from the tray, with Callie in front reading the floor, and the sledge on its board scraping behind them over the threads. There was nothing else to do with it.

Over the doorway, on the right-hand wall of the corridor opposite, there was a place where the tray sent a branch across. It went across the ceiling in a smaller trough and through the wall above the door, into the room, through a square hole in the concrete the size of a hand. The rope went with it. A thinner rope, like a branch off a trunk, went across the ceiling in the small trough and in through the hole.

Callie stood in the doorway and held the lantern up.

The room was not large. It was six paces deep and five across, and the air in it was colder than the corridor. On the right-hand wall there were big grey cabinets, as tall as a man and twice as wide, with doors on their fronts, standing in a row. On the left-hand wall there were racks.

They were steel frames, from the floor to the ceiling, the way Senna had said. But their backs had been torn off. She could see the ends of torn metal sticking out at the edges where somebody had levered the backs away from the frames to get at what was behind them. The wall behind the racks was bare, with holes in it where things had been fixed.

And the boxes were on the floor.

They lay all along the foot of the left-hand wall in a long low heap, under the empty racks, where they had been thrown. Grey steel loaves. There were dozens of them. More than dozens. They lay on their sides and on their faces and on each other, tumbled, the way stones lie at the foot of a wall that has come down, and some had rolled out into the middle of the room. The dust of twenty years lay on all of them. Here and there she could see one lying with its front up, the little dark window in it and the slide across the window, and the plate riveted on beside it.

*So we left them where they fell. On the floor. Hundreds of them, in the end.*

"There," said Toren. His voice was not steady. "There they are."

The rope came down through the hole above the door. It came down the wall inside the room, just to the left of the doorframe, a line of grey as thick as two fingers, crawling. It reached the floor and went along the foot of the wall to the left, into the heap of boxes. And in the heap it split. It went in among the boxes in finer strands, like roots going in among stones, under them and round them and between them, all through the heap. She could see the strands on the grey steel, grey on grey, where they lay over a box and went down the other side.

It was lying where the wire had been. It had gone where the crew had pulled the copper out, behind the racks and down, and through the boxes the crew had thrown on the floor to get at it.

Callie held the counter out at the length of her arm toward the heap and waited.

"Eighty," she said. "At the boxes. From the door."

She understood it then, standing in the doorway with the lantern up. It was very simple. It was the kind of thing her grandfather would have said at the scale house in one line.

The boxes were in the grey. The grey was in the boxes. You could not put a hand on one without putting it in the other.

---

"Callie," said Miss Wren. "Read them. From the edge. With the counter. Don't touch."

She read them.

She went along the edge of the heap with the counter at a hand's width, bent over, never touching. Toren came behind her with the lantern and the plate in his hand, the paper he had copied the plate onto on the eleventh day, and held it to each one that lay face up. Most of them had the wheel. Some did not. Some had a different mark, a square with a line through it, and those were bigger, and the counter at them said what the room said.

"Sixty-eight," said Callie. "It's dead. Seventy. Dead." She moved along. "Sixty-five. That's the room. Dead." She held the counter to a box lying on its side with its window toward her. "A hundred and ten."

She stopped.

"That's warm," she said. "At a hand. That one's alive."

"Wheel," said Toren, holding the plate to it. "Four spokes. And the bar. It's the same."

"Is the slide shut?"

Callie looked. The steel slide lay across the little window, all the way across, in its groove.

"Shut."

"One," said Toren.

She went on. There were a great many dead ones. She read eleven more before she found another that said a hundred and six, lying face up at the edge of the heap with a strand of grey lying across its top like a hair across a cheek. Its slide was shut.

"Two," said Toren.

And a third, deeper in, half under two others, that made the counter jump before she had it at a hand. A hundred and forty. She held it there for ten breaths and did not like it.

"Its slide's not shut," said Toren, bending with the lantern. "It's rusted. It's stuck halfway. The window's half open."

"Then it's not talking only to itself," said Miss Wren, behind them. "It's talking to the room." She did not come nearer. "Leave that one."

"Leave it," said Toren, and wrote it.

---

They did the halt in the room, because it was twenty turns.

Jab worked. He stood in the middle of the room with the frame on his back and drew them one at a time, at the chest, off the open jar in Miss Wren's hand, and vented into the concrete by the door, which was the room's floor and already dirty. Miss Wren watched, and said *vent* before he needed it, and when he had done the three of them she took the leftovers out of his hands. It took longer than it had on the road.

"Two," said Callie, weighing the jar. "Near enough. For the four of us, and the room." She gave it back. "Four and three quarters."

"We've been in forty turns," said Miss Wren. "That's three and a quarter spent, and the jar says so. We've got twelve for today." She looked at the heap. "Get the two. Then we go."

---

It was Callie who got the first one into the carrier.

Toren brought the sledge to the edge of the heap, as close as he could get it, with the carrier on it. He lifted the lid out of its groove one-handed and set it on the board. Then he stood at the carrier's side and put the foot of a strut down on the concrete floor at a slant. He let it come up out of the floor to the lip of the carrier like a plank laid from the ground to the tail of a cart.

"Set," he said. "Walk it up that."

Callie took the tongs.

They were heavy, and long. She got the jaws round the box that said a hundred and ten, the first one, on its sides, and closed the rings, and the jaws bit. She did not lift it. She could not have lifted it at the end of a yard and a half of iron, and she did not try. She dragged it out of the heap along the floor to the foot of Toren's strut, and turned it, and set its end on the strut.

*Get the weight onto something that is not you.*

It went up the strut. She pushed it with the tongs and it slid up the pale hard line of the strut toward the lip of the carrier, grating a little. The strut took all of it. Toren stood behind the strut and was soft and counted under his breath. The box reached the lip and tipped, and went over into the carrier, and landed on the lead bottom with a sound like a door shutting in another house.

"One," said Toren. He let the strut go. "Four breaths."

The rope did not move.

Callie had been watching it. She had been watching it the whole time, out of the side of her eye, the way you watch a dog you do not know. It lay in the heap in its fine strands and crawled west, ten and three, and did not care about the box or the tongs or the strut or anything. It had been lying across the next box, the one that said a hundred and six. It was still lying across it.

"The second one's got a strand over it," she said.

"Can you get the tongs under it?"

"I can get the tongs round the box," said Callie. "The strand'll be on top of the jaw." She looked at it. "It's only grey."

"It's only grey going somewhere," said Miss Wren. "Do it slowly. If it does anything, let go of the tongs."

---

She was reaching for it when she heard Jab say "Three."

He said it quietly, behind her, by the door. Counting.

She looked up.

In the corner of the room, the far corner, where the racks ended at the back wall, there was a heap. She had seen it and not thought about it. It was a heap of grey, larger than the others, heaped up in the corner against the back wall at the end of the boxes. The rope went into it. All the fine strands from the heap of boxes ran along the foot of the racks and into it, the way the roots of a tree go down into the ground.

At Jab's *three*, it moved.

It moved outward. Not in, the way everything else in that place had moved all afternoon. Out. The grey swelled in the corner as if something under it had drawn a breath. It heaved up, and cracked along the top, and something came up out of it, the colour of an old wasp nest.

It stood.

"Husk," said Callie.

It had no face. It turned toward her, toward the tongs in her hands and Toren's strut and the lantern. It came along the foot of the racks at her.

Toren went past her. He had the spike on his fist before she saw it come, dark and heavy and crooked. He lit the heel of it, the hand's width along the hook, and put it into the husk as it came, low, and dragged it round against the racks, and put the point in.

It came apart.

It came apart all at once, the way they did, into grey. There was no wind down here. The grey did not go up in a burst the way it had at the wash. It fell. It came down on the boxes and the floor in a heap, a husk's worth of loose grey, and lay there.

"A hundred," said Callie, with the counter out. "It's a hundred, near the corner."

"Back," said Miss Wren. "Everybody back from it. To the door."

They went back to the door. Toren let the spike go dark. They stood in the doorway, the four of them, and the lantern hung from Jab's frame, and they looked at the heap of grey on the floor where the husk had been.

It lay there. Ten breaths.

Then it began to go.

It went in threads, the way everything went. They came out of the heap and went along the floor toward the foot of the racks, toward the strands of the rope, and into them. It was faster than the threads in the yard. Much faster. The whole heap drew out into lines and slid into the rope and was taken along it, into the corner, into the big heap, in ten breaths. The floor where the husk had come apart was bare concrete again.

Three.

The heap in the corner swelled.

Callie watched it. She could not have looked anywhere else. It swelled the way it had before, all at once, as if it had drawn a breath. It heaved up, and cracked along the top. And the thing that came up out of it was the colour of an old wasp nest, and it stood, and it turned toward the lantern.

"It's the same one," said Callie.
