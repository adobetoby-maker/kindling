# Chapter 36 — Toren: The Door That Opens Toward You

Toren came out of the room last, the way Dee had said, and turned east.

The others were ahead of him along the left-hand wall of the corridor. Jab first, with the frame on his back and the lantern swinging from it, walking fast and holding his right hand away from his side. Dee behind him with the open jar in the crook of her arm and the glass in her fist. Callie behind her with the hook. They were going toward the steel door, twelve paces off, where the stair was.

He did not run. Running was how you went down on a wet floor, and he had one arm.

Behind him, in the room, the short husk had got up off the floor. He heard it come after him, the dry soft sound of it on the concrete. He did not look round. He knew where it was the way he knew where the others were.

He looked west instead, once.

The heap was at the second door now. It filled the corridor from wall to wall, grey, higher than his waist, with the rope in the tray going down into it from above like a rope going into a well. It came on. It was slower than a man walking. It was faster than he had thought anything that size could be.

At the three the line of light opened in the middle of it. It was the colour of the edge of the sky. It stayed open for three breaths, and shut.

Toren turned his back on it and walked east.

---

"Door," said Callie, ahead of him.

She had stopped. They had all stopped. Jab was standing a pace short of the steel door with his lantern held up, and he was not going through it.

The heap of grey that had been against the door was on the corridor side now. The door had swept it through when they pulled it open. It had lain on the floor of the corridor in a spill, a hand deep, all afternoon, while its threads went west.

It was not lying now. It had swelled.

It had drawn up into a hump against the wall beside the door, between them and the door, the way the corner in the room had drawn up. As Toren came up behind them, the three came, and the hump cracked along the top. Something came up out of it.

"Husk," said Jab. "In front."

It stood up in the corridor a pace from the steel door, faceless, between them and the stair. It turned toward the lantern on Jab's frame.

Toren looked back. The short husk was coming along the wall behind him. The heap was coming behind it, past the second door, toward the first.

He did the sum. It was not a long one. A husk in front and a husk behind, and a thing he did not have a name for behind that. And every one of them, if it was killed, would lie on the floor for ten breaths and stand up again.

"Callie," he said. "Hold the front one."

She did not ask how. She put the back of the hook's curve down on the concrete in front of the husk, still. It went pale. The husk came at the lantern and walked into the hook and stopped.

"Holding," said Callie.

"Jab. Dee. Round it. Through the door. Up."

There was room. The hook stood across the corridor at a slant, from Callie's hands to the curve on the floor, and the husk stood against the curve pushing. Between the curve and the steel door there was a gap, a pace wide, against the left-hand wall.

Jab went through it. He went sideways, with the frame on his back scraping the concrete, and the husk turned its blank front toward him as he passed, and did not come, because the hook was in its way. Dee went through behind him with the jar. They were in the flat place at the foot of the stair.

"Callie," said Toren. "Now you."

"If I let go, it comes."

"Let it come." He had the spike on his fist. He did not light it. "I'll hold the door."

---

She let go.

She lifted the curve off the floor and went through the gap sideways past the husk, and the husk came after her. It came to the doorway, to the steel frame where she had gone through.

Toren went past it, on the other side.

He did not go past it the way Callie had. He went the way he had gone past the husk on the strut in the room, close, on its blind side, the side away from where it was turning. He came through the doorway into the flat place a breath after it did, and it was in front of him, going for the lantern on Jab's frame at the foot of the stair.

He lit the heel.

It turned. It came at the light on his fist, because it was nearer than the lantern. He put the lit hook into it low, dragged it round against the steel of the door, and put the point in. It came apart against the door, and the grey fell at the foot of the steel in a heap.

He did not look at the heap. He knew what it would do.

"Up," he said. "All of you. Don't wait."

"Toren—"

"*Up*," said Toren. "I'll shut it."

He put his good hand flat on the steel of the door, at the edge, and pushed.

---

It did not want to go.

It had opened toward them. It had come off its stick with a shriek and swung toward them as wide as a man, and stood there. Now it had to go back the other way, away from him, into the corridor. And it was heavy, and it was stiff, and he had one arm.

He put his shoulder to it. It moved. It went a hand's width, and stuck, and he set his feet and leaned his whole weight on it the way he had leaned on the rack in the yard, and it went again. It swept the heap of the husk he had just killed along the floor in front of it, into the corridor.

Through the narrowing gap he saw the short husk. It was at the door of the first room, coming. And behind it, filling the corridor at the first door now, the heap.

At the three, in the middle of the heap, the light opened.

He put his shoulder to the door and it shut. It went the last hand's width with a sound like a great weight set down, iron on iron, in the frame.

He stood with his shoulder against it, breathing.

Above him he could hear them on the stair. Jab first, his boots slow and heavy with the frame. Dee's quick ones. Callie's. They were at the landing. They were going on up.

The door moved.

It moved against his shoulder. It did not open. It moved, a finger's width, in its frame, the way a door moves when somebody on the other side of it puts their hand flat on it and leans.

The husk.

He had forgotten. He understood it now with his shoulder against the steel. It had opened *toward* them. It pulled from this side. So from the other side, from the corridor, it pushed. Anybody in the corridor who leaned on it would open it.

It moved again. A finger's width. It came back.

---

He did not decide. There was nothing to decide.

He took his shoulder off the door and stepped back from it, and put the foot of a strut down on the concrete of the flat place, two paces from the door. He let it come up at its slant from the floor to the steel, at the height of his chest, the way you set a plank against a door you want kept shut.

It met the steel just as the door began to open.

The door stopped. It stood a finger open in its frame, with the strut against it, and something on the other side pushing it. The push came down the strut into the floor. It did not come into him. He was soft.

"Holding," he said, to nobody.

It was the husk. He could feel what it was. It was a husk's weight, a steady blank push without anything behind it, the same as it had been on the strut in the room. It pushed, and it pushed, and he paid for it by the breath.

"Toren!" Callie's voice, from the landing. "Toren, where are you?"

"The door," he said. "It opens from their side. I'm holding it. Go up."

"Toren—"

"Go *up*. When you're at the top, say."

He heard her go.

He counted breaths. He looked at the steel in the lantern light that was going away up the stair, and he counted, and he went down once to look. The stone was less than half. It had been less than half when he came out of the room. He had held the husk on the strut in the room for the whole of Jab on the rope, and Jab on his knees, and Dee drawing. He had not been able to think about it then. He thought about it now. There was less than half, and it was going.

Then the push changed.

It did not get stronger all at once. It got heavier, the way the weight had got heavier on the strut at the wash when the crown went. Something else had come up against the other side of the door, behind the husk. It was not a husk's weight. It was slow and enormous and it did not push the way a husk pushed. It leaned. It leaned the way the whole leaning cart had leaned on him over the arch.

He felt the strut take it. He felt it go down into the concrete of the flat place, and the concrete hold it, and his stone go under it like water going out of a basin with the plug pulled.

*Every breath there's a wheel on it, I pay for.*

This was not a wheel.

"We're up!" said Callie's voice, a long way above him, at the top of the stair. "Toren! We're up!"

The stone was gone.

He felt it go. He had felt it go at the wash, at the ring stone, and he knew what it felt like. There was nothing at the bottom of him now but his own, two turns and not the bit, and the strut was on that. And the weight on the other side of the door did not care whose it was.

He counted three more breaths. He did not know why. So that it was a number.

He let go.

---

He was on the fourth step when the door opened behind him.

He heard it. It came off the strut's place and swung toward the flat place with the shriek of the first time, all the way, and hit the concrete wall at the end of its swing with a sound he felt in his teeth. He did not look round. He went up the stair. He went up it the way he had gone up the loose bank at the wash on his own strut-steps, only there were real steps here, concrete, and he took them two at a time with his left arm against his chest and his right hand on the yellow rail.

The lantern was on his belt. It swung and knocked against his hip and threw his shadow up the stair in front of him, enormous.

Husks come at a light.

He heard it behind him on the stair. The dry soft sound, on the concrete, coming up. Not fast. Faster than him.

He came round the landing. He went up the second flight. At the top, in the square of grey light from the hall, there was Callie. She was on her knees on the top step with the hook laid across the top of the stair from wall to rail, the curve on the concrete, and it was pale.

"Over it," she said. "Step over. It's only the steel."

He stepped over it.

The husk came up the last of the stair behind him and walked into the hook, and stopped.

---

It stood on the top step of the yellow stair with the pale curve across its front and pushed, and pushed. Its grey went up the stair behind it in the light from the hall. Toren stood in the hall with his back to the wall and looked at it.

"Kill it," said Callie. "Against the hook. It'll go down the stair."

He lit the heel. He did not have much to light it with. He lit it anyway, off his own, a hand's width of thin white-grey along the hook of the spike. He came round the end of the rail and put it into the husk from the side, low, and dragged it round against Callie's curve, and put the point in.

It came apart against the Ward.

The grey fell. It fell down the stair behind the hook, down the steps, in a long slow slide, toward the landing and the door and the dark. None of it came over the curve.

"Down," said Callie, and lifted the hook, and the Ward went out.

They stood in the hall of the long building, all four of them, in the light from the door, and breathed.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"It opens toward you," said Toren at last. He heard his own voice. It was not steady. "From the other side, it opens away. It pushes." He looked at the stair. "Gran never said."

"Your gran was never on the other side of it," said Dee.

---

They went out of the long building and down the road with nothing to drag.

It was the middle of the afternoon. The sun was over the drum behind them, going down into the wrong sky, and the light on the yard below had gone yellow. The wind had not come. The grey lay in its combed lines going up the road, and they walked down beside them on the inside, along the pipes, the way they had come up.

At the top of the road Callie stopped and turned round and looked up. She had said she would.

Toren stopped with her. He took the paper out.

She counted. He watched her lips. Ten. Three. Ten. At the end of the fourth ten, as the edge thinned, the long grey veil came down out of the bottom of the dark over the drum and went down behind the broken rim.

"One," said Callie.

She counted on. At the ninth count there was another.

"Two," she said. "In twelve." She looked at him. "It was one in twelve going in."

He wrote it. *Coming out: 2 in 12.*

"Is that more?" said Jab.

"It's two," said Callie. "It's not enough counts to say more. It's what I saw."

---

Rook was where he had said he would be.

He was sitting at the foot of the first post at the near end of the bridge with his coat over his shoulders and his face grey and wet. He had seen them come out of the long building at the top of the road. Toren saw him see them. He watched them all the way down the Z, and across the yard along the pipes, and through the gate.

At the gate Toren stopped.

The green copper wire was hanging on the fence where he had put it. He took it down. He brought the gate round on its hinges, scraping, and put the chain round the frame and the post, twice. Then he twisted the wire through the two ends of the chain, round and round, one-handed, with Callie holding the chain so it did not swing. It took a long time. He did not tuck the ends in. He left them so they would come.

"Why?" said Callie.

"Because we're coming back," said Toren.

They crossed the bridge on the outside planks, not the middle one.

At the near end Rook looked up at them from the foot of the post, one after another, as they came off the planks onto the gravel. He did not get up. He counted them out loud, which Toren had never heard him do.

"Dee," he said. "Jab. Callie." He looked at Toren last, longest. "Toren."

"Four," said Toren.

"Four," said Rook. "In." He looked at Jab's hand, held away from his side. He looked at it for a long time. "Wash," he said. "Then tell me."

---

They washed.

Coat off. Shake it. Wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat. Toren did it one-handed on his knees at the edge of the water with Callie's counter going along them afterward. Jab did it with his right hand held flat on the surface of the river and not put in it. Dee had said not to put the white place in anything cold till she had looked at it again.

Then they stood in a row on the gravel by the cold fire, and Dee drew them.

She did all three. Jab could not work. He could watch. He stood at her side with his left palm out and his right held against his chest like Toren's arm, and said *vent* before she needed it, and when she had finished the three of them he took the leftovers out of her hands with his left palm only.

"Callie," said Dee.

Callie weighed the jar.

"Three out of two," she said. "For the four of us at the stair, and here, and Jab's arm again." She set it down. "Five in it. Three whole." She did not wait to be asked. "Twenty-nine. One over today's line."

"One over," said Dee.

She measured their floors.

"Your stone's gone," she said to Toren. "Your own's a turn. And a bit." She looked at him. "You held that door on your own."

"I held it on the stone," said Toren. "Then I held it on my own. Then I let go."

"When?"

"When they said *up*." He looked at the hill. "Three breaths after."

Dee wrote it.

Callie's stone was a quarter. Her own was where it had been in the morning. Dee's stone was half, where the watch had taken it. Jab's floor was a finger. His own was less than a hand, and his arm was warm from the elbow down, and the heel of his right palm was white and shiny and would not feel.

Dee held his hand in both of hers, turned up, in the last of the sun.

"It's fixed," she said. "You know it's fixed."

"I know."

"It'll not come back." She did not let go. "It might go red. It might blister. If it blisters I'll do what Sowerby would, and keep it clean, and it'll close. But it'll not feel, there. Not that bit." She looked at him. "Can you draw with it?"

Jab put his right palm flat on his own left forearm and shut his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "Round it. Not with the bit."

"Then it's a mark," said Dee. "Not a hand." She let him go. "It's the first one the work's put on you that I can't take off. It won't be the last." She wrote it. "Vell had one. On the heel of his hand. The same place."

---

Pell had been counting all day.

He was sitting on the cart with his back to the rack and his leg out, with the atlas open on his knee. There was a sheet of Senna's route paper beside it, and on the back of the sheet Pell had made marks, the way the crew had on the gatehouse wall. Strokes, in rows. Some of them had a cross through them.

"I counted it on my own heart," he said. "I've no glass. It's sixty to a turn if you sit still, near enough. I sat still." He held out the sheet. "It never missed. Ten and three. All day. From the time you went through the gate till you came out of that door at the top. Every count."

"And what came out of it?" said Toren.

"Grey." Pell put his finger on the strokes with the cross through them. "Out of the bottom. At the three. Down into the round one. It's like watching rain a long way off, over there, when it isn't raining here." He looked up. "Seven. Seven falls, from the sun on the gate till you came out at the top. That's four hours, near enough."

"Callie saw one going in," said Toren. "In twelve. And two coming out."

"Then she saw what I saw," said Pell. "It's more in the afternoon than the morning. I'd not swear to it." He folded the sheet. "Write it. I'll count it again tomorrow. Somebody will want to know if it's more."

Toren wrote it. *Pell's count, from the cart. Ten and three all day. 7 falls of grey, gate to the top of the road coming out, about 4 hours. More in the afternoon (Pell's not sure).*

He looked at it. It was the Director's count. It was what she had asked for. *Count what comes out of it and write it down.* It was the first thing on the paper that was hers.

---

They told it at the fire.

Dee lit it with the last of the wrack from the morning, and put the pot on for Rook's back, and they sat round it in the dusk. They did not sit on the ground inside the fence. They sat on the gravel outside it, on the right side of the river, and Toren said it. He said it the way he had said the evidence at the edge of the water on the forty-third, in order, all of it.

The yard. The lines going up the road. The door, and the stair, and the yellow rail. The door at the bottom that opened toward you, and pulled in thirty breaths. The corridor going west. The tray, and the rope in it, crawling. The first room, and the boxes on the floor, hundreds, the way Gran had said, with the rope gone in among them like roots. Three live ones, and one of them open. One in the carrier.

"One in the carrier," said Pell.

"In the room," said Toren. "With the lid on. Where we left it."

He told the rest. The heap in the corner, and the husk that stood up out of it, and that Toren had killed and that had come back. The same one. The cut in the strands, and how it had mended in the three. Callie's lee across the door, and how it had stopped the floor and not the rope. Jab's palm's worth, into a vent jar, and the husk coming back a hand short.

He told them about the rope.

He did not look at Jab while he told it. He told it the way Jab had said it on the floor of the room with his arm in Dee's hands. *It thought I was a hole in it. It thought I was a wound, and it was mending me.* He told them what Dee had done.

Rook, on the canvas by the fire, did not say anything. He had his eyes on Jab's hand.

Then Toren told them about the thing on the rope. The heap as high as a waist, coming along the corridor, pulling itself along its own rope with nothing to pull with. The light that opened in it at the three. The red. The door, and what the door did from the other side.

"It came along the rope," he said. "From the west. From the round one, or from somewhere between. It came to where we were. To where the husk had been killed, and the grey was lying. And to him." He did not look at Jab. "It came to us."

"It came to you," said Rook.

He said it quietly, from the canvas, with his eyes half shut.

"*If it comes to you,*" said Rook, "*you do what you have to do and you come home.*" He opened his eyes. "You came home."

"We came home with one box," said Toren. "In a room, under a hill, behind a door that opens away from it."

"What is it?" said Pell.

Nobody answered for a while.

"It's lifting," said Jab.

He was sitting by the fire with his right hand on his knee, palm up, the white place catching the light. He had not said anything since the river.

"It lifts like we lift," he said. "Flat. At the rate it comes. It draws for ten, and it vents for three. Only it vents where it wants a thing. Into a heap, and a husk stands up. Into a cut, and the cut's not there." He looked at the hand. "Into a hand, if the hand's drawing on it."

"Then it's a lifter," said Rook.

He said it with the corner of his mouth moving, the way it moved when he was going to laugh and was not sure it would be allowed.

"It's got my job," he said. "Four seasons, on the worst of the north yard. And there it is, doing it for nothing, on its own hill, better than me." He looked at the fire. "A lifter."

Nobody laughed. But Toren saw Callie's mouth move, and Pell's.

He wrote it on the paper. *The lifter.* Because Jab had said what it did, and Rook had said what that made it, and nobody had a better word.

---

"You've a box," said Pell.

He said it after Dee had changed Rook's pad, in the dark, with the boiled water cooling in the pot and the fire going down. He had said nothing all through the telling. He had sat on the cart and listened, the way he listened to a hub.

"You've one box," he said, "in a lead box of its own, in a room, under a hill. You've twenty-nine inside. Sixteen's the line. That's thirteen to work with. You've a road home that's cut, and a man with a fever, and a man with one leg." He was not looking at anybody. "And in the room with the box, there's a thing that mends what you do to it, every thirteen breaths, and came along a rope to see you, and had its hand in Jab."

"I know," said Toren.

"I know you know," said Pell. "I'm saying it so it's been said by somebody who's not going in." He was quiet a moment. "A box and a half short, and all of you alive, is a thing I'd take back to that woman's office and put on her table. I'd not be ashamed of it."

"The box is in the room," said Callie.

"Then it's in the room."

"Then we go home with nothing," said Callie. "And that thing's got it. And the ash that went in the door today. And everything lying on the whole of that hill, going up it, ten and three." She said it flat. "And Senna's on the narrow bed on the fifty-fourth with a man guessing."

"She is," said Pell. "And she'd say the same as me."

Toren did not know if that was true. He thought about his grandmother on the pillow with her eyes shut, saying *pull the door.* She had been saving it. She had not said *come back without it*. She had not needed to. She had said it to the whole room with *I know what every man in that line does with a number once he's got it.*

He looked at the four of them round the dying fire. At Pell on the cart with his leg out, who had said it so it had been said. At Rook, hot, on the canvas, not asleep. At Dee with her book on her knee and her hands still. At Callie. At Jab with his hand palm up on his knee.

*Everybody back.*

He had written it. He had meant it. He had not known, when he wrote it, that it might one day mean leaving a thing behind that everybody at home needed, so that everybody could come home.

"Tomorrow," he said.

They looked at him.

"Tomorrow we decide," said Toren. "Not tonight. With the light." He heard himself say it, and knew he had said it before, on the forty-sixth. It had been the right thing then. He did not know if it was now. "Tonight we think about what it did. All of it. Every time it did something, and what we did, and what happened." He looked at Jab. "It mends in the three. You said. It vents where there's a hole. That's the whole of what we know. If there's a way in that, we find it tonight. If there isn't, we go home in the morning with nothing."

"And if there is?" said Dee.

"Then we go back for the box," said Toren. "And I'll say before we go what we'll pay for it. And if it costs more than that, we come out, finished or not."

Dee looked at him across the embers for a long time.

"Write that," she said.

He wrote it. Under the day, under the lifter, under Pell's count.

*47th. The pump house. In: 4. One box in the carrier, left in the first room, lid on. J.'s right palm: a white place on the heel, fixed. It mends in the three.*

And under that, because it was true, and because there was nowhere else to put it:

*Gran's door pulls. From the other side it pushes.*
