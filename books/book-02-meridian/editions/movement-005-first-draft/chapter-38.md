# Chapter 38 — Jab: Off Before the Ten

The first thing Jab did in the room was take the frame off.

He had never done it inside a fence. Nothing clean goes on the ground. But there were the cabinets along the right-hand wall, big grey steel ones as high as his shoulder, standing in a row with flat tops. He backed up to the nearest one the way he backed up to the tailboard at every halt, and let the rack come down on its top, and slid his thumbs out of the chest band and stepped out from under.

"It's a shelf," said Dee, beside him. "Not a floor."

"It's a shelf," said Jab.

His back came up. He had not known how much of him had been holding the frame until it was gone. Four full jars and two empties and a broken socket, and the lantern hanging off the side of it, lighting the room yellow from the top of a cabinet.

Dee put the canvas bag down on the next cabinet with a sound like a bell. Three vent jars. She had the open jar in the crook of her arm, and the glass in her fist, and Callie's counter on its strap over her own shoulder now. She had taken it off Callie's hip in the corridor before Callie turned round.

"Ten," said Dee. "Seven. Eight."

Jab crouched by the cut.

It was the big rope. Toren had dragged the heel through it on the threshold, and the grey had spilled out of it onto the concrete at the cut. The end of it on the room side lay across the floor from the threshold toward the lifter, three paces, as thick as Toren's thigh. At the far end it went into the grey hump that lay over the carrier.

At the near end, a pace from Jab's knee, it stopped. There was the spill, and then the concrete, and then the curve of Callie's hook lying on the threshold. Callie stood behind the curve with her back to him.

He could not see the lee from here. It stood on the other side of the curve, into the corridor. But he could see Callie's back, and her hands open on the conduit, and her shoulders. They were very still.

"Nine," said Dee.

Jab put his right hand out over the cut end of the rope. He did not put it down yet. He held it an inch off, the way he held it over a patient before he knew what was there. The white place on the heel of it caught the lantern.

Dee put the mouth of the jar to his left palm.

"Clean in," she said. "Three."

---

It pushed.

He felt it before he put his hand down. He felt the whole of the grey hump behind the rope do what it did at every three, the thing he had felt through his palm on the rope by the door. It drew breath. It vented. It pushed out into every place in the room where there was a hole.

The cut was a hole.

He put his palm flat on the end of the rope, round the white place, on the fingers and the side of the hand.

It came. It came up the rope at him from the hump like something shoved down a pipe, all at once, warm and gritty and loose, and into his palm. The clean cold came into his left hand from the jar and went through him and held it off his wrist. He held it high. It was not the hill. It was not the whole of everything lying where it fell, the way it had been on the rope by the door. It was one push. It was a palm's worth, and a little more, all at once.

It stopped.

It stopped because there was no more behind it. That was the thing he had not known until he felt it. On the rope by the door, the push had come and come. This came once, and it was all there was, and it stopped.

"Off," said Dee.

He took his hand off. He took it off with the grey held in it high and open, and turned, and Dee had a vent jar out of the bag with the stopper out. He put its mouth to his palm and tipped it. The grey went down out of him into the glass.

Dee put the stopper in with her thumb.

"One," she said.

Jab looked at the cut.

The end of the rope lay on the floor a pace from Callie's heels. It had not grown. It had not reached for the threshold. It lay where Toren had left it, with its spill, and the spill was going in threads back along the rope toward the hump. The ten had begun.

"It's where it was," he said.

"It's where it was," said Dee. She wrote it with the vent jar under her arm. "Jab. It's where it was."

---

He had heard the other things while his hand was on the rope. He looked at them now.

One of the arm-ropes was up.

It had been lying across the heap of boxes, as thick as an arm, flung out from the hump. It was not lying now. It had come up off the boxes at the three the way a wet rope comes up when somebody lifts the end of it, heavy and slow, and swung. It had swung across the room at the door. At the worked thing standing in the door with its back turned.

And Toren had been there.

He was standing a pace behind Callie on the room side with his left arm in its cuff against his chest. There was a strut going up from the concrete beside his boot at a slant, over Callie's shoulders, the way you lean a plank over a door. The arm-rope lay across it. It had hit the strut at the height of Callie's head and stopped there, draped over the pale line of it, and it was sliding off slowly, the way a wet rope slides off a rail.

"Holding," said Toren. His teeth were together.

Callie had not turned round. Jab saw that. He saw her shoulders move at the sound of it hitting, and not turn.

The rope slid off the strut onto the floor and lay there.

"Letting go," said Toren, and the strut went.

"Husks," said Dee. "Two."

The corner heap had stood up. So had the heap by the cabinets, at the far end of the row, three cabinets from where the frame was. Two husks, grey, faceless, the colour of old wasp nests. They turned toward the worked things in the room. Toren's heel, still lit a hand's width. Callie's back. Jab's palm, which had been worked a breath ago and was open now.

"I've got the cabinet one," said Toren.

He went at it. He did not go at it with the heel. He set a strut from the floor in front of it, slanting back toward the cabinets, and it walked into the strut and stood against it, pushing, pinned between the pale line and the steel of the cabinet door behind it. He stood behind the strut, soft, paying by the breath.

"And the corner one?" said Dee.

It was coming along the boxes toward Jab.

---

He let the knife come.

It came on his left fist, the way it always came, dark, heavy in the spine, fat in the belly. The fish-knife. The one shape he had lived inside for four years. He did not light it all. He lit the last inch of it, the hook, and felt the draw of it behind his breastbone.

The husk came along the heap of boxes at him, low.

He did not want to kill it. Every one you kill is lying on the floor. And then it's in the air. And then at the three it's standing up again. He knew that. He had watched it happen five times yesterday. But it was coming at his hands, and Toren was on the strut, and Dee had the jar, and Callie had the door.

He killed it.

He put the hook into it low as it came, the way he would have cut a rope, and it came apart. The grey fell on the boxes and the floor at the foot of the racks in a heap.

"A hundred and twenty," said Dee, with the counter.

Jab let the knife go dark.

He crouched by the heap. He did not lift it. There was no time to lift it and he knew what lifting against the lifter was like. He watched it. Ten breaths. It drew out into threads and went into the strands at the foot of the racks and along them into the corner. Into the heap the husk had stood up out of.

He went after it. He went along the foot of the racks, crouching, with his right hand out, to the corner. He put his left hand up behind him without looking and Dee was there with the jar and put its mouth against his palm. She had followed him.

"Three," said Dee.

The corner swelled.

He put his hand flat on it. On the top of the heap, where it swelled, where the husk was going to stand.

It pushed into him.

It was the same. A push. A palm's worth, warm, gritty, loose, all at once, and then nothing behind it. He held it high. The heap under his hand did not crack. It did not stand. It sank back a little, like a loaf that has been knocked.

"Off," said Dee.

He was already off.

The vent jar. The grey going down into the glass. The stopper.

"Two," said Dee.

The corner heap lay in the corner. It was smaller than it had been. It did not stand up.

---

"It doesn't matter where," said Jab.

He said it to Dee, crouching in the corner with his hand held away from him. The lantern was behind him on the cabinet. Dee's face was yellow on one side.

"It doesn't matter where I take it," he said. "The cut. The corner. Wherever it pushes. It's all out of the drum." He looked at the hump over the carrier. "Every time I take one, there's a palm's worth less of it. Anywhere."

"Then take it where it matters," said Dee.

She said it at once. She had known it before he said it, he thought. She had been waiting for him to find it.

"Where it's going to stand up," said Jab. "Where it's mending something Toren did. Where it's going to hurt somebody." He looked at Toren on the strut with the husk against it. "There."

"Then that's the order," said Dee. "You'll not get all of them. It pushes into every hole at once. You'll get one." She looked at the glass in her fist. "Pick the one."

---

It learned.

Jab felt it learn. He did not know how else to say it afterward, and he did not try to. It had nobody in it. It did not want anything. But it did what it did, and when what it did stopped working, it did something else. The way water does.

At the next three it did not push into the corner.

It pushed into the husk on Toren's strut.

He felt it go. It went out of the hump across the floor along the strands, not to the corner, but to the thing pinned against the cabinet at the end of the row. He saw the husk get bigger. It swelled against the strut like a sack being filled, taller, thicker, and the strut took more of it. He heard Toren make a noise.

"It's feeding it," said Jab. "The one on your strut."

"I can feel it's feeding it," said Toren. "It's heavier."

Jab went. He went along the cabinets fast, crouching, with his hand out behind him for the jar. But the three was over before he got there. The ten had begun. The husk on the strut was as big as a man now, and it leaned.

"Stone's half," said Toren. "I'm paying for that by the breath. It's more than a wheel."

"Next three," said Jab. "I'll be on it."

"On what? On the husk?"

"On whatever it pushes into."

He crouched at the foot of the strut, at the husk's feet, with his right hand an inch off its grey where it stood on the floor. He did not touch it. You did not touch a husk. But this was not a husk now, not only. It was a place the lifter pushed into. It was a hole in the lifter, walking about, and the lifter was trying to fill it.

"Clean in," said Dee, behind him, with the jar at his left palm.

"Three," said Jab, before she did.

It pushed. He put his palm flat on the foot of the husk, where it stood on the concrete.

It came into him instead.

He felt it come. It came along the floor from the hump and up into the husk and, at the husk's foot, it found his palm drawing, and came into it. Warm. A palm's worth. The husk did not grow. It shook, against the strut, the way a sack shakes when you pull something out of the bottom of it.

"Off," said Jab.

He came off. The vent jar. The grey. The stopper.

"Three," said Dee.

The husk leaned on the strut. It was as big as it had been, and no bigger.

"It's not heavier," said Toren. "It's not getting heavier."

---

Toren began to cut.

He did it on the tens. He left the husk on its strut, pinned, and went to the hump.

The arm-ropes lay out from it across the boxes and the floor. There had been three. One lay where it had slid off the strut behind Callie, on the floor near the door, across the room from the hump. The other two lay across the boxes, out from the far side of the hump, toward the corner.

He went to the near one first. It lay on the floor between the hump and the door. He lit the heel. He put it across the arm-rope a pace from the hump, low, and dragged it through.

It came apart. The long end lay on the floor toward the door, spilling. The short end stuck out of the hump like the stump of a branch.

"Cut," said Toren. "Near one."

"Three in four," said Dee.

Jab went to the stump.

He was learning the room. He did not know that he was until he was doing it. Where the lantern light fell and where it did not. Where the boxes were, and the carrier under the hump with its iron handle sticking out, and the cabinets. Where Callie's heels were, and the curve on the threshold, and Toren. He went round all of it at a crouch with his right hand out in front of him and his left hand back, and Dee came behind him with the jar and the bag. She was always there when he put his hand back.

He put his palm an inch off the stump.

"Two," said Dee. "Three."

It pushed. The stump grew at him, the way the cut strands had grown toward each other on the floor yesterday, a finger, two fingers, to mend. He put his palm on the end of it. It came into him instead of growing.

"Off."

The jar. The grey. The stopper.

"Four," said Dee. "That's the first jar full. Near enough." She put it in the bag. "Two left."

The stump stuck out of the hump, short, a hand long. It did not grow. The long end of the arm-rope lay on the floor by the door with its spill going away from it in threads.

Toren looked at it.

"That's one," he said. "It's got two."

---

It learned again.

At the next three it threw.

It did not push into a hole. It lifted the second arm-rope, the one across the boxes toward the corner, and swung it. It swung it at Jab.

He did not see it come. He had his hand an inch off the stump of the first arm, and his eyes on it, and Dee's jar at his left palm, and his attention down under his palm in the grey. He heard the long heavy dry sound of the rope coming up off the boxes. He heard Toren say "*Jab*."

Something went over his head.

It went over him the way a roof goes over you. Pale, and hard, a line of light going up from the concrete at a slant beside his knee and on over the top of him and Dee, like a plank leaned against a wall with the two of them under it. And the arm-rope hit it.

He felt it hit. He felt it through the floor, through his boots, through his knees on the concrete. It was the sound of a sack hitting a wall. The strut took it. It stood over him and Dee, pale, and the rope lay over the top of it and began to slide.

"Holding," said Toren. "Both."

Jab looked round.

Toren was standing between the hump and the cabinets with his left arm against his chest. He had two struts up. One went from the floor to the husk at the end of the cabinets, pinning it. The other went from the floor over Jab and Dee. He was soft behind both of them. His face was grey and wet in the lantern light.

He had held two at once on the arch at the wash, for as long as it took to walk a cart onto a post. Jab had heard him say it. *Two struts while it moves. That's twice the price.*

"Toren," said Jab.

"Get the stump," said Toren. "I've got the roof."

The arm-rope slid off the strut onto the boxes. The ten had begun.

---

Jab put his hand on the stump of the first arm at the next three and it did not push. The lifter pushed at the second arm instead, the one that had hit the roof, to mend where the strut had broken it. The rope was cracked across where it had struck, and the lifter was mending it.

He felt it go the wrong way. He felt it go past him.

"It's gone to the other," he said.

"Then leave it," said Dee.

"It'll swing again."

"Then Toren'll roof you again." She did not raise her voice. "You can't be on all of them. It pushes into every hole at once. You're on one. Pick the one."

"I picked the wrong one."

"Then pick the next one," said Dee. "That's all a watch is."

---

He came off late once.

It was the seventh. He had his palm on the corner heap, which the lifter was trying to stand a husk up out of again. The push came into his hand, a palm's worth. He held it high. He was thinking about the second arm-rope, which was lying across the boxes behind him, and about where it would swing. He was thinking about Toren, with his face grey. He was thinking about Callie's back.

"Off," said Dee.

He was not off.

The ten began.

He felt it begin under his palm. He felt the lifter draw. It drew the way it had drawn all afternoon yesterday, flat and open and patient, at everything loose in the room. And the palm's worth in his hand was loose. It was held high and open in his palm, and it was not his, and it was not in a jar.

It came out of his hand.

He felt it go. It went out of his palm back down into the corner heap in threads, the way the heap on the floor had gone out from under his palm yesterday. And his palm went with it. Not the hand. The drawing. The part of him that was reaching down into the grey went with the threads, toward the hump, toward the middle, the way you lean after a rope that is being pulled away from you.

For a breath he was going.

"*Off*," said Dee.

He took his hand off.

He knelt in the corner with his palm up and empty. Half the palm's worth had gone back into the heap. He could feel where it had gone. He could feel where he had nearly gone after it.

"Off before the ten," said Dee. "Not at the ten. Before."

"I know."

"You know now," said Dee.

He looked at his palm. Round the white place, the rest of it was warm, a little. Not lodged. Warm.

"Vent," said Dee, and held the jar.

He vented the half into the glass. She put the stopper in.

"Seven," said Dee. "Half of it."

He did not come off late again. At every three after that he said *off* himself, before she did, and was off, the way he had said *vent* before she did at the mouth of the cut. And every time, as he lifted his palm, he felt the ten begin a breath behind it. It was like stepping off a step just as it gives.

---

He looked at Callie once.

He could not help it. He was crouched by the stump of the first arm-rope waiting for a three, near the door, and she was there, a pace and a half from him, with her back to him.

He had not looked at her properly since they came in. He looked now.

She was standing in the doorway with the curve on the threshold and her hands on the conduit. The lee stood up out of the curve on the corridor side, and he could see it past her. It was a pale sheet standing up off the threshold and leaning back up the wall over the top of the door, like a lean-to the wrong way up. It was grey on the outside. The grey of the corridor's cut rope had heaped against its foot in a long ridge, as high as Callie's knees now, piled against the outside of the pale wall. And the ceiling rope's cut end had heaped against it up by the hole.

And there was a husk against it.

It stood in the corridor on the other side of the lee, pushing. It had come from the stair door. It had come at the worked thing in the doorway and walked into the lee and stood there, pressed against the pale wall a foot from Callie's face, pushing, with no face of its own. Every time it pushed, he saw Callie's shoulders take it.

She was holding it off her stone. Her whole stone. Two Handfuls, and the husk on the outside, and the heaps, and a door and a bit.

There was blood on her lip.

She turned her head.

She did not turn round. She turned her head, a little, over her shoulder, the way you turn your head at a noise behind you. And for a breath her eyes found him, crouched on the floor with his hand out over a stump, and Dee behind him with the jar, and the room behind that.

He did not know what she saw. He did not know what her face did. He looked at her and said the only thing he had.

"Busy," he said.

Callie looked at him a breath longer.

Then she turned her head back to the corridor. She did not turn it again.

---

"Jar," said Jab, at the tenth.

He said it before it was dry. He felt the clean cold coming thin into his left palm, the pour at the bottom of the jug.

Dee did not answer for a moment.

"That's two done," she said. "The open one's done."

She turned. The frame was on the cabinet top behind them. Jab saw her hand go to it. There were two full jars in it now, and two empties, and the broken socket. And the jar that was empty in her arm.

She stopped with her hand on the neck of the third.

"That's the last one above the line," she said.

Nobody said anything. Toren was on his two struts, the husk and the roof, with his face running wet. Callie was in the door. The lifter lay over the carrier.

"Open it," said Toren.

"It's the last above," said Dee again. "When it's done, it's the line. I'm saying it now so it's said."

"It's said," said Toren. "Open it."

She opened it. She lifted it out of the frame by the neck and pulled the stopper and put the mouth of it against Jab's left palm.

"Three," she said. "Open. Two whole. And that's sixteen, in the two." She looked at the hump. "Say it, Jab."

"Eight above the line," said Jab. "In this one. Then it's the line."

---

It was smaller.

Jab saw it as he went round the hump for the eleventh. He had been too near it to see it, the way you do not see a heap of sacks going down while you are lifting off the top of it. The hump had been as high as Callie's waist. It was as high as her knee now, at the high end. The other end had gone down to nothing, and he could see the carrier's lead lid under it, grey, and the iron handle all the way out.

"It's smaller," he said.

"It's less," said Toren. "I can feel it on the husk. The husk's less."

It was. The husk on Toren's strut had been as big as a man. It was not now. It was the size of a boy, pinned against the cabinet, and it pushed with less.

And the three was different.

He felt it before he knew what it was. The line of light opened along the top of the hump at the three, the colour of the edge of the sky. And it stayed open. It stayed open longer. It opened at the three and it did not shut at the three. It shut at the end of four. Then five.

"It's pushing longer," said Jab.

"Why?"

"Because there's less in the push." He did not know how he knew. He knew it the way he knew a floor. "It's pushing everything it lifted, and it didn't lift much. So it keeps pushing. Looking for more." He looked at the light. "It's opening wider because it's got nothing to put through."

"Pumping itself dry," said Dee.

"It doesn't know how to stop," said Jab.

He crouched by the stump of the first arm-rope with his hand out. He thought about it, while the ten ran. He thought about the rope by the door yesterday, and the push that would not stop, and the whole of the hill behind it. And himself, with his palm on it, not able to find his floor.

It could not find its floor either. There was nobody in it to find it. And there was nobody in it to say *off*.

The difference between him and it was one word, and Dee.

---

The lifter moved.

It had not moved since they came in. It had lain over the carrier like a dog over a bone. It had thrown its arm-ropes and pushed into its holes and stood its husks up, and it had not moved.

At the fourteenth three it moved.

It pulled itself. It did it the way it had come along the corridor yesterday, drawing itself along its own rope. But the only rope it had now that went anywhere was the cut end of the big one, the stump that ran from the hump across the floor to the threshold. To the curve of the hook. To Callie's heels.

It came along it.

It came off the carrier. It slid off the lead lid and along the floor toward the door, the whole grey hump of it, low, knee-high, pulling itself along the stump the way a man pulls himself along a rope in a flood. Toward the threshold. Toward the lee. Toward the river on the other side of it.

"It's going for the door," said Jab.

Toren saw it. Jab saw him see it. He had two struts up, the husk and the roof over Jab, and he let the roof go, because Jab was not under it now. He put the foot of it down on the floor in front of the hump, a pace short of Callie's heels.

The lifter came against the strut.

It leaned on it. It came up against the pale line of it, low, the whole grey weight, and leaned, and did not push the way a husk pushed. It leaned the way the cart had leaned on the arch. Jab saw it in Toren's face.

"*Holding*," said Toren. And then, not to anybody: "That's the lean. That's the cart."

"Toren—"

"I've got it," said Toren. "Stone's gone. I'm on mine." He did not move. "I've got a turn of mine. And a bit. Get it, Jab. Whatever it's pushing, get it."

---

"Jar," said Jab.

He heard himself say it. It was the eighteenth three, or the nineteenth. He had lost count. He had not lost count of the jar. He felt it go thin.

Dee had it against his palm. She held it there a breath longer than she needed to, as if she had not heard.

Then she took it away.

"That's the line," she said.

It went round the room. Jab felt it go. Toren on his two struts, one on the lifter, one on the husk, with his face grey and running and his own reserve going under both of them by the breath. Callie in the door with her back to them, blood on her lip, holding. Dee with the empty jar in her hand and the glass in the other. And himself, on his knees by the lifter with his palm an inch off its edge.

"Sixteen in the frame," said Dee. "Two whole. That's the line. Everybody out."

Nobody moved.

"If we go," said Toren, "it's at the door. It's against my strut. When I let go, it goes against the lee. When Callie lets go of the lee, it's through. It's in the corridor with the rope. It's got the river."

"I know where it is," said Dee.

"It's got nothing," said Toren. "Look at it. It's knee-high. It's opening five breaths. It's got nothing left to push but itself."

"I know what it's got," said Dee. "I've been counting it into jars."

She did not look at Toren. She looked at Jab.

"What do you feel?" she said.

He knew what she was asking. It was not the counter. It was not the jar. It was Rook's question, the one Rook had asked at the ditch and at the pen with his hand on Jab's shoulder doing nothing. *What's there?* She was asking it of him. She was asking him what she could not measure.

Jab put his palm an inch off the edge of the lifter, where it leaned on Toren's strut. He shut his eyes.

It was there. Grey, loose, nobody in it. It was drawing, in the ten, on what little was loose in the room. The spill of the cut arms, and the husk's grey, and the ridge along the foot of the racks. It was drawing at the rate it came and it was not coming, because there was so little left lying anywhere in the room. It was drawing on nearly nothing.

And under the draw, at the middle of it, where the light opened at the three, there was a place. It was a hole. It was not a hole anybody had made. It was the place the push came from, the vent, and it was open wider every time. It was a hole in the middle of the lifter that the lifter was pushing out of, and it had nearly nothing left to push.

"It's missing," he said. "The three. It's pushing, and nothing's coming back in the ten. It's nearly dry." He opened his eyes. "One more, maybe. Two. Then it'll open, and there'll be nothing to shut it with."

Dee looked at him for a long moment.

Then she looked at the counter on her shoulder.

"A hundred and thirty," she said. "Not red."

Then at Toren.

"Say it," she said.

---

Toren said it.

He said it from behind his two struts, with the lifter leaning on one and the husk on the other and his own reserve going under both. He said it with his teeth together, flat, in order, the way Pell said a distance.

"We cross it," he said. "The line. Now. On purpose. Two turns of the glass. Not three. If it's not open for the point in two turns, everybody out, finished or not, and it has the door." He breathed. "It's mine. I'm saying it. Write it."

"It's mine as well," said Dee. "It's my line." She did not write it. She had no hand for the book. She said it instead, out loud, to the room, so it was said. "Two turns under the line. His call and mine. Written tonight." She turned the glass in her fist. "The glass is running."

She reached back to the frame on the cabinet. She took the fourth jar out by its neck, one of the last two whole ones. She pulled the stopper. She put it against Jab's left palm.

"Four," she said. "Open. One whole. That's under."

Jab felt the clean cold come in.

It was the fence's. It had been for the stair, and the rooms, and the road home. It was under the line, where it was never supposed to be opened except to get somebody out. He knew that. He knew what it cost. Every Handful that came out of this jar was a Handful nobody had.

He felt it come in at his left palm and go through him and wait.

"Callie," said Toren. "Two turns."

"I heard," said Callie, from the door. She did not turn her head. Her voice was flat and thick, the way it was with a nosebleed. "I've got two turns. I've not got three."

---

At the next three the light opened, and stayed.

It opened along the top of the lifter where it leaned on Toren's strut, the colour of the bottom of a pond. It opened wide. Jab could see into it. There was nothing in it. There was only the light, green-grey, pushing out, like breath out of a mouth, with nothing behind it.

It pushed at every hole in the room at once. He felt it go. At the stumps. At the corner. At the husk on the strut, which shivered. At the cut arm by the door.

He did not go to any of them.

He put his palm on the lifter.

He put it flat on the edge of the grey where it leaned against Toren's strut, a hand from where the light opened. He had never put it on the lifter itself. He had been on its ropes and its holes and its stumps all morning. He put it on the thing now.

It was not warm. That was the first thing. It had been warm all yesterday, all through, the whole of the hill in it. It was not warm now. It was grey, and loose, and cool, and thin, like flour in the bottom of a sack.

It pushed into him.

It pushed into him because he was drawing, and he was a hole, and it had never known anything else to do with a hole. It pushed what it had. It was very little.

He held it high. He did not let it into his wrist. He held it there, in his palm, the last of what it had, and felt it go on pushing, five breaths, six, with nothing, into his hand. Like breath going out of a man who has no more breath.

*Off before the ten.*

He did not take his hand off.

Not yet. He knew he had to. He knew the ten was coming a breath behind the three, and that it would draw, and that what he had in his palm was loose, and it would draw it back, and him after it. He knew all of that.

But the light was open. It was open wide, a hand from his palm. And it was open because he was on it, taking the push, so there was nothing to shut it with. If he came off now, the ten would come, and it would draw what little was lying in the room, and at the next three it would have something to push, and it would shut.

"Toren," he said.

Toren was there.

He had let the husk go. Jab heard the strut go out and the husk fall forward onto the floor by the cabinets and not get up. It did not get up, because there was nothing coming to it. Toren was standing over the lifter with his one strut still under it, soft, and the spike on his fist, dark.

"It's open," said Jab. "It's open and I'm on it. It's got nothing to shut it with."

"How long?"

"Till the ten," said Jab. "Then I'm off. Whatever you've done."

Toren lit the spike.
