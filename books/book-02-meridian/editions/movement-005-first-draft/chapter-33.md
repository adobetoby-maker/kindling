# Chapter 33 — Toren: Out: Four

Toren woke before the light and counted them.

He did it lying on the gravel with his arm in its cuff against his chest and his eyes still shut. Pell, on the cart, asleep. Dee, awake. Rook. Jab, asleep at last, on his side by the dead fire with one hand flat over the left side of his coat. Callie, asleep, with her hand on the hook.

He went back to Rook, because something about Rook was wrong.

He opened his eyes. The sky over the river was grey, going white at the bottom. The pump house wall stood black above him. Across the river the fence ran north along the far bank, and beyond it the hill went up into the dark. Over its flat top there was still the place where the sky was wrong, and round its edge the light the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened, and thinned.

He did not count it. He knew what it would come to.

Dee was kneeling by Rook with two fingers on his wrist. She had not lain down again after her watch. Toren could see that from her coat, which had no gravel on it.

"He's hot," she said, without looking round.

---

Rook was lying on his side on the canvas, where she had put him the night before so his back was off the ground. His eyes were open. His face was wet, though the morning was cold enough to put frost on the cart.

"I'm warm," said Rook. "It's a warm morning."

"There's ice on the wheel," said Dee.

"Then I'm warm and the wheel's cold," said Rook. "We're both of us wrong."

Dee did not smile. She took the knot out of the canvas strip round his chest and lifted the pad off his back.

Toren came and knelt on the other side of the canvas, where he could see.

The stitches ran in a short dark line between Rook's shoulders, small and neat and tight, the way Dee had put them in at the cut. The skin round them was red. The red went out a hand's width on each side of the line, and it was swollen, and it was shiny. When Dee put the back of her fingers near it, not on it, she took them away again at once.

"It's declared," she said.

"That was quick of it," said Rook.

Jab was awake. Toren had not heard him get up. He was standing at the end of the canvas with his hand still on his coat, looking down at Rook's back.

"He's been hot since the fourth hour," said Jab. "I felt it. I didn't know if I'd dreamed it."

"Put your hand over it," said Dee. "Don't touch."

Jab knelt and held his right palm an inch above the red. He shut his eyes. Toren watched his face go still, the way it went at a table.

"There's nothing busy," said Jab after a while. "Not the grey. That's gone. We had all of that out." He did not open his eyes. "It's him. It's him, doing something. Fighting it. It's hot all through, not in bits." He took his hand away. "It's not the thing I can draw."

"No," said Dee. "It's the dog."

She said it to Toren, because Toren had written the dog down.

"The grey came out yesterday. What's left is what a hound's mouth puts in a man when it bites him. It's the same as a dog bite in a yard, or a nail in a foot." She laid the pad back on. "That's not Sustain. That's Sowerby's. And Sowerby's eighty miles off."

She opened her small bag. Out of it she took four folded papers, white, each the size of a thumb, tied together with thread. She untied one.

"Fever powder," she said. "He gave me four. For the road. For a hound bite, or a wound gone bad, or a fever in the night." She looked at the paper in her fingers. "I'm to put it in boiled water and see he drinks all of it. I'm to do it once a day. I'm not to do it twice to make myself feel better."

"And if it doesn't break?" said Toren.

"Then by tomorrow night he'll be worse," said Dee. "And then we'll not be waiting for anything."

---

The water was the first thing, then.

Dee's skin was empty. She had put the whole of it into Rook's back at the cut, a little at a time. Toren went to the cart and counted the others, because nobody had.

There were two, hanging from the rail behind the driver's bench. One was full. The other was a third full, near enough, when he hefted it.

"One and a third," he said.

"That's for drinking," said Dee. "Not for washing. Not for his back." She looked at the river. "The river's for the rest. If it's clean."

Callie was already up. She took the counter out of its bag and went down the gravel to the river's edge above the pump house, where the water came down out of the north fast and dark over the stones. She held the counter out over it at the length of her arm and waited ten breaths.

"Seven," she said. "The room." She moved down past the pump house and did it again, below the place where they had washed. "Nine. Our wash is in it."

"Above the pump house for drinking," said Dee. "Below it for washing. And nothing out of it goes in his back till it's boiled."

So they needed a fire, and the fire needed wood, and the pump house had none. It was stone, and the roof was gone. There was only the thorn they had brought on the cart and burned last night, and that was ash now.

Toren and Callie went down the bank below the pump house. The river had been high at some time, and it had left a line of wrack along the gravel, a hand's height above where the water ran now. It was sticks and reeds and bleached old branches, dry, jammed among the stones. They picked along it for half a mile and came back with an armful each.

"That's one pot," said Callie. "Maybe two."

"Then it's one now and one tonight," said Dee.

She boiled the pot at the pump house wall. When it had cooled enough, she stirred the powder into a cup of it and held the cup while Rook drank, lying on his side, with his hand round hers on the cup. He drank all of it. He made a face at the end.

"Sowerby's," he said. "It tastes like his office."

"Drink the rest of the pot," said Dee. "Slowly. It's the last boiled water you'll see till dark."

---

Pell had not waited for anybody.

He had got himself sitting up on the cart with his back against the rack behind the driver's bench and his splinted leg out straight along the boards. He had done it alone. Toren could see from his face what it had cost him and that he did not want it mentioned.

Dee knelt on the tailboard beside the foot. She did not undo the splint. She pinched the end of his big toe, where it came out of the canvas strips, and watched it go white and then pink again.

"The blood's going round it," she said. "That's good. It's swelled under the strips. That's not." She loosened one knot, a finger's width. "You'll not put it down."

"I've nowhere to put it down," said Pell. "I'll sit." He had the atlas open on his knee in its oilcloth. "A man on his back can't do much. A man sitting up can count."

"Count what?"

"Whatever wants counting." He looked across the river at the hill. "She said to count what comes out of that. The Director. It's on the paper." He did not look at Toren. "That's a thing a man can do sitting on a cart. I'll do it."

---

Dee measured their floors on the gravel by the cold fire, as if it were the gate.

She did Jab first, the way she always did now. "A hand," she said. "Floor's a finger." She turned his palm up and held her own over it, not touching. "Cool. They've come down."

"They came down about the third hour," said Jab.

"I know. I was awake."

She did Callie. "Your stone's what you settled last night. All of it." She kept her fingers on the wrist. "Your own's come back. Not all. Three parts, near enough."

"I slept," said Callie.

"You slept like a stone in a well," said Dee. "I watched you. That's the only reason it's three parts."

She did Toren.

He had settled last. He had sat by the fire after Jab lay down, with his palm flat on a square of paper and a Handful of jar C's ash on it, and let it come at the rate it came. That was most of an hour, for him. It had been full dark when the paper was clean. The stone had gone down slowly and lain there, warm and faint, a long way down.

"Stone's full," said Dee. "Your own—" She waited. "Two turns. Not the bit. The bit'll come back by noon, if you don't spend it before."

"I'll try not to."

"Don't try," said Dee. "Don't spend it."

She did her own last, two fingers on her own wrist, her eyes on the hill. She did not say anything about it. She wrote in her book.

---

Then there were the coats.

Senna's rules said coat off, shake it, wash at the river, and they had done that at dusk, in the last of the light. But it had been dusk. Now it was day, and Callie went round them with the counter.

Jab's coat had the long pale stain down the back of it where jar B had broken. Callie held the counter to it and waited.

"Eight," she said. "It's clean. It's only the jar." She looked at the stain. "It's the stair's ash. It's clean on you."

Dee's coat was twelve. Callie's own was fourteen. Pell's, on the cart, was twenty, and Dee made him take it off and hand it down, and shook it downwind herself.

Toren's was thirty.

He knew why. He had been outside the lee at the wash, killing two husks against it, with the grey going up over him in a burst each time. He stood in his shirt on the gravel with his arm in its cuff and watched Callie hold the counter to the coat. He was cold.

"Wash it," said Dee. "In the river. Below the pump house. Then beat it on the stones till you're tired, and then some more."

He did it one-handed. He knelt at the edge of the water and pushed the coat under and held it there, and it was so cold his hand stopped feeling after ten breaths. He pulled it out and beat it on a flat stone, and put it under again. Jab came down after a while and did the beating for him without being asked. The grey went off it into the water in a thin cloud and away down the river.

"Sixteen," said Callie, when it had been on the stones in the sun for an hour. "It's still in the seams."

"Sixteen's a coat," said Dee. "Thirty's a coat I'd not let inside."

Then the hook.

Callie held it up and held the counter to it herself. The curve read eighteen, where it had been down the loose bank twice and on the lip in the wind. Then she held the counter to the grip.

The top of the conduit was wrapped in rag where her hands went. The rag was old and grey and soft, and it had been wet with the wash and dried on her hands.

"Thirty-four," said Callie. "It's the rag."

She took out her knife and cut it off. She did it carefully, in one long strip, without letting it touch her other hand. Then she stood with the strip hanging from the point of the knife and looked at Dee.

"Not the river," said Dee. "Not the fire."

So Toren dug a hole at the far end of the gravel, away from the water, with the heel of his boot. Callie dropped the rag in it off the knife. He kicked the gravel back over it and put a flat stone on top, and Callie chalked a cross on the stone with Pell's chalk. *Don't touch.* It was the second one they had left on the road.

She washed the curve of the hook in the river with sand, the way her grandfather had scoured a scale pan. When she held the counter to it again it read eleven. The grip was bare steel now, cold and smooth. There was still a paper tag tied round the conduit below where the rag had been. The grey coats had tied it there in the pen. *pen, 35th.*

"You could cut that off too," said Toren.

"No," said Callie. "They'll want to write under it. When we get back."

---

They went to look at the bridge in daylight at the third hour of the morning. Three of them: Callie with the counter, Dee with the glass, and Toren. Nobody goes to look alone.

It was thirty paces across. It was three planks wide, laid side by side on cross-beams, and the cross-beams sat on posts that went down into the river. The planks were grey and split along the grain and furred with old moss at the edges. There was a rail on the upstream side, the north side, one long pole at the height of a man's hip, lashed to the posts with wire. There was no rail on the downstream side at all.

Below the bridge, downstream, there had been another crossing, beside it. Two iron pipes, each as thick as a man's body, had gone over the river on iron legs, side by side. Toren could see where they came out of the river wall of the pump house on this side, and where they went into the far bank on the other. In the middle the legs had gone. The pipes had broken and dropped into the river, and their ends lay in the water with the current going white round them.

"That was the pump," said Dee. "River water, over to the hill."

"What for?"

"I don't know," said Dee. "For something that needed water. Things like that always need water."

Callie walked out onto the bridge first, with the counter held out in front of her. She did not look at the water. She looked at the planks.

"Seven," she said, at the first post. "Nine. In the middle." She went on. At the far end she stopped, two planks from the bank, and crouched.

"This one's gone," she said.

It was the middle plank. It had cracked across, right through, between two cross-beams, and the two halves sagged down in the middle a finger's width. Toren could see the river through the crack.

"It'll take a person," said Callie. "On the outside planks. Not on this one." She put her hand flat on the plank beside it, and then on the one on the far side. "Those are sound. They're old, but they're sound. This one's rotten at the beam."

"And a load?" said Toren.

"What load?"

"The carrier." He looked back across the river at the cart. "It has to come over. It can't come over on a back. It's got to come over on something."

Callie looked at the crack.

"Not on that," she said.

She stood up and walked the last two planks to the bank, and stood at the gate.

---

The gate was a frame of iron, as tall as a man and a half and as wide as a cart. Wire was stretched across it, the same rusted wire as the fence, in squares as big as a hand. It hung from two hinges on an iron post on the right. On the left, where it met the other post, there was a chain.

The chain went round the frame of the gate and round the post, twice. Its two ends had been brought together and joined. But they had not been joined with a lock. They were joined with a twist of wire. It was copper, gone green, twisted round and round on itself in a tight thick knot the size of a fist, with the ends tucked in.

Toren looked at it for a long time.

Somebody had stood here, twenty years ago, on the last evening, and pulled the gate shut behind eleven people, and twisted a bit of copper wire through the chain so it would stay. Not locked it. Tied it. As if he had meant to come back and open it again.

"His," said Toren. "The man who ran the crew."

"Maybe," said Dee.

"Gran said he wouldn't let anybody sleep inside." Toren looked at the knot. "He'd not have left it open."

He did not touch it. Callie held the counter to it.

"Twelve," she said. "At the gate. It's fourteen through the wire." She held it out through one of the squares, into the fence, at the length of her arm. "Fourteen."

Then all three of them looked through the wire at Caul Hill.

---

The ground inside the fence was flat for a hundred paces, a wide yard of old concrete gone to cracks and weeds, with gravel in the cracks. There was a squat grey hut beside the gate on the inside, with a door and one window, both black. There were long low sheds along the south side of the yard with their roofs fallen in. There was a thing on four iron legs like a water butt on stilts, very tall, with its side rusted through in a long red wound.

The pipes came out of the river bank on the inside and went across the yard on low concrete blocks. They went to the foot of the hill and up it, side by side, beside a road.

The road went up the hill in two long slants, back and forth, a great Z cut into the side of it. At the top of the Z, on the rim of the flat top, there was the building.

It was long. It was the longest building Toren had ever seen that was not the Sag's own berm. It ran along the rim of the hill from north to south, three rows of windows high, grey, flat-faced, with every window black. It stood on the edge of the flat top like a wall somebody had built to keep the hill in. There was a door in the middle of it, at the top of the road. It was a black square.

And behind it, a long way up, over the flat top of the hill, the sky.

It was bigger this close. At dusk from the rise it had been the size of Callie's thumb at the end of her arm. From here it was the size of a cart wheel. It was not over the building. It was over what was behind the building. Toren could see now that there was something behind it. Past the north end of the long building there was the top of a wall that was not straight. It curved. It was a great round wall, higher than the building, broken off jaggedly at the top, like a pot with its rim knocked off.

The sky was over that. The dark patch hung over the round wall, and the light round its edge thickened and thinned over the broken rim.

"That's not the building," said Toren.

"No," said Dee.

"Gran never said anything about a round one."

"She said the main building," said Callie. "She said inside the main building. If you've got a main one, you've got others." She did not take her eyes off the yard. "Look at the ground."

Toren looked.

The yard was not bare. The concrete had grey on it, in the cracks and along the edges of things. It was not heaped in drifts, the way the wash had been. It lay in lines, thin, like hair that has been combed. Every line went the same way. They went across the yard from the south and the east, from the sheds and the fence, and they came together at the foot of the road and went up it. Toren watched the nearest line, beside the gatehouse.

It slid. A hand's width, toward the hill. It stopped.

He counted without meaning to. Ten. Three.

"It's going up the road," said Callie. "All of it. I can see it on the first bend." She pointed with her chin. "On the top bend. And at the top—" She stopped. "It goes in the door. Some of it. Some goes round the end of the building. Over the top. Toward the round thing."

"Some goes in," said Toren.

"Some goes in the door," said Callie. "Where we're going."

---

Dee turned her back on the fence.

She did it deliberately. She stood with her back to the gate and looked at the two of them, and she took out her book and did not open it.

"Say what she said," she said. "The Director. The narrow thing."

Toren knew it. He had written it at the long table, and on the back of the paper, and Dee had quoted it twice at the fire on the forty-sixth. He said it anyway, because she had asked.

"*You're going for the boxes. The live ones, with the wheel on the plate, in lead, on the cart. Two, three if you can. And you're going to look at that sky and count what comes out of it and write it down. You are not going into it. You are not going looking for whatever's under it. If it comes to you, you do what you have to do and you come home.*"

"*Into it,*" said Dee. "What's *it*?"

Toren looked back through the wire. He looked at the round wall with its broken rim and the dark over it.

"That," he said. "The round one. Whatever's in it. Under the sky."

"And the building?"

"The building's in front of it." He made himself say it slowly, in order, the way Pell said a distance. "The door's at the top of the road, on the east side. The stair's inside it. Gran said down the stair, a door, and then a corridor, and the rooms off the corridor on the left. Eleven of them. She didn't say which way the corridor goes." He looked at the building. "If it goes along the building, north or south, it's under the building all the way. If it goes back into the hill, west, it goes under the building for as long as the building's deep, and then it goes on under the top. Toward the round one."

"Which?"

"I don't know."

"Then we'll count doors," said Callie.

Dee looked at her.

"The building's about thirty paces deep," said Callie. "I can see its end from here, past the north corner. If the corridor goes west, and the rooms are eight paces apart, or ten, then three rooms are under the building. Four at the most. The rest are under the yard behind it." She did not look at the sky. "The rest are nearer to that."

Dee opened the book then, and wrote in it standing up, with the book against her forearm.

"Three doors," she said. "If the boxes are in the first three rooms, they're ours. If they're not, they're under that, and they're hers, and she can decide what to do about them with a Sag held and a machine that can see." She wrote. "Nobody goes past the third door. Not to look. Not for a box. Not for anything."

"And the ash that goes in the door?" said Toren.

Dee shut the book.

"The ash that goes in the door is lying where it's lying," she said. "If it's in the first three rooms, we'll be standing in it. We knew that yesterday. It's why we've got two healers and a counter and a line." She looked at him. "It's not a thing. It's ash, going somewhere. If a thing comes to us, then it's come to us. She said what we do then."

---

They said it all out loud at the pump house, because Dee would not have anything decided that had not been said.

Callie said the ash first. She stood by the cart with her hands open at her sides and said it flat.

"Inside, forty. Five whole, in the frame. The line's sixteen. That's two whole." She looked at Jab's frame, standing against the pump house wall on two flat stones. "Twenty-four to work with. Three jars. Home, three. Twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." And then, because Dee was looking at her: "And two empty in the frame. A and C. And the road jar in the rack, empty. And five vent jars on the cart. And one socket with glass in it."

"Settled," said Dee.

"A Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in you." Callie stopped. "Jab carries none. Jab's the jar."

"Four going in," said Dee. "Not five. Say it."

"Four going in," said Callie. "Two healers." She did the sum. Toren watched her do it with her lips not quite moving. "We priced forty-eight for five people. An hour in, two in the rooms, an hour out. The rooms were thirty." She looked at the hill. "It's not five. It's four. But it's four with two healers, not three, and one of them's got to be the jar and the watch in turns, all the time, and nobody's the reserve." She shook her head. "I can't make it come out better than it is. Twenty-four's four people for half the day, with nothing going wrong."

"Then we don't spend twenty-four today," said Toren.

They looked at him.

"Twelve," he said. "Half. We go in, and we find the stair, and the door, and we find which way the corridor goes. We read the first room. If the boxes are in it, we get them in the carrier and we come out. If they're not, we come out anyway." He heard his own voice. "Twelve spent is twenty-eight left. We come out at twenty-eight whatever we've got. Then we've still got a day."

Dee looked at him for a long moment.

"Twenty-eight's today's line," she said. She wrote it. "Sixteen's the line. Both."

"And the rest," said Toren. "Say them."

They said them. Red, and nobody trapped: everybody out, at once. Red, and a body trapped: the fewest who can do it stay, named. Two Flasks left inside: everybody out. A healer at their floor twice. A healer still warm after the watch. Jab below his floor once.

"One more," said Dee.

She said it to Jab, but she said it for all of them.

"There's two of us," she said. "You and me. If one of us goes down—hurt, or under, or anything—there's nobody to draw the one that's left. Not after. Not ever." She did not look away from him. "So if either of us is lost, for any reason, it's everybody out. That moment. Not when we've finished what we're doing."

"Everybody out," said Jab.

Toren wrote it on the back of the paper under the rest. *If a healer's lost, everybody out.*

Then, under that, the way he had written it on the step of eleven the night before they left:

*Everybody back.*

---

Rook had listened to all of it lying on the canvas with his eyes half shut. Nobody had asked him anything. When they had finished, he opened his eyes.

"And the outside," he said.

Dee turned.

"There's two of us out here," said Rook. "One can't walk and one's hot. We can't come in after you. We've both got that. I'd like it said, now, what we're to do if you don't come out."

"If we're not out by the time the sun's on the gate," said Pell, from the cart, "I'll know. I'll have been counting."

"And then?" said Dee.

Pell looked at Rook. Rook looked at Dee.

"Then I don't come in," said Rook.

"Say it again."

"Then I don't come in," said Rook. He said it flatly, to the stones of the pump house wall. "I sit at the near end of that bridge where I can see the gate. I'm the only one of us who can get to the near end of the bridge, and I can get there lying down if I have to. If something comes out of that gate that isn't you, I shout. If it's you, I count you." He was quiet a moment. "If it's dark and it's nothing, I don't come in."

Dee knelt down beside him on the gravel.

"It's the Director's word," she said.

"It's yours," said Rook. "I know whose it is." He shut his eyes. "Go on. You've a day."

---

Then there was the carrier.

It stood in the cart behind the three home jars, on its straw, where it had ridden for seven days. It was the size of a small chest. It was made of the depot's lead in sheets, folded and seamed, grey and dull, with a lid that sat down into a groove round the top and two iron handles, one at each end. It was so heavy empty that it had taken Pell and Toren and Jab together to set it on the cart in the north yard, and Toren had had only one arm to do it with even then.

"It doesn't go on anybody's back," said Toren.

"No," said Pell.

"And the cart can't cross the bridge."

"No," said Pell.

He was sitting on the boards with his leg out. He looked at the carrier, and at the bridge, and at the tailboard of the cart, which was hanging on its chains at the back with the two long pieces of its rail gone. Those two pieces were on his leg.

"Then it drags," he said.

He told them how. He told them the way he had told Toren where to cut the traces: once, slowly, with his finger on each thing.

The tailboard came off its chains. It was a single thick board, as long as a man was tall and as wide as the carrier with a hand to spare on each side. They set it on the gravel. They got the carrier down onto it off the back of the cart, Jab and Callie and Toren, sliding it on two poles of wrack wood with Pell saying *easy* and *easy* and *now*. They lashed it to the board with rope through both handles, crossed under the board and back, and pulled tight.

"Two ropes," said Pell. "One at the front to pull. One at the back to hold." He looked at Toren. "On a slope, down or up, there's a rope round something that doesn't move, and you pay it out. And nobody stands below it. Not ever. Not for a breath."

"Below it," said Toren.

"Downhill of it. If it's on a slope and you're below it, and it runs, you're under a box of lead." Pell put his hand on the edge of the tailboard. "If it runs, let it run. Lead's cheaper than you. There's more lead in the world than there is of any of you."

Callie had opened the lid. She was looking in.

"There's something in it," she said.

She lifted it out. It was a pair of tongs. They were iron, a yard and a half long, with two flat jaws at one end and two ring handles at the other, and they were wrapped in a piece of sacking with a label tied to them. The label was in a hand Toren did not know, square and neat. *For the boxes. Not your hands.*

"The shed," said Dee. "The old woman." She looked at the tongs. "She never said."

"She never said anything," said Callie.

She put them back in the carrier, and put the lid in its groove.

---

They went in at noon.

The wind had not come all morning. The grey lay still in the yard, in its combed lines. Rook said, from the canvas, "Go while it's down," and nobody argued with that either.

They ate first, at the pump house, bread and the last of the cold meat. They drank as much as they could hold of the boiled water that was left, and then a little from the full skin. Nothing to drink inside the fence. They went to the river below the pump house one at a time and made water, because Dee said so, and because nobody sits down inside the fence.

Dee opened the first jar.

She did it at the pump house, not at the gate. She lifted it out of Jab's frame by the neck, one of the five, and pulled the stopper, and looked in, and stoppered it again loosely so it would come out with a thumb.

"One," said Callie. "Open. Eight in it. Four whole."

"Four whole," said Dee, and wrote it.

They put two vent jars in a canvas bag, and Toren put the strap of the bag over his good shoulder. Callie had the hook and the counter. Dee had the glass and the book and the open jar in the crook of her arm. Jab had the frame, with four full jars in it and the two empties and the broken socket, and his knife not on his fist. The cart's two lanterns hung from Toren's belt and Callie's, unlit, with a twist of tallow wick each and a box of lights in Dee's bag.

Senna had said it went down stairs, where the walls went thick. There would be no windows.

---

Rook had got himself to the near end of the bridge.

Toren did not see him go. He only saw him there, when they came down the gravel with the sledge. He was sitting on the ground at the foot of the first post with his back against it and his coat over his shoulders. His face was wet again. He was where he could see the whole length of the bridge and the gate at the far end.

"You're off," said Dee, as she passed him.

"I'm sitting," said Rook. "Sitting's allowed."

They said the rules at the bridge end, because Senna's crew had said them at the gate every morning, like a grace. Nothing to eat inside the fence. Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, coat off, shake it, wash at the river. Never carry one of the boxes.

"Pull the door," said Toren, at the end.

Pell, on the cart, said it after him. So did Rook, from the post.

---

The sledge went onto the bridge easily.

Toren had the front rope over his good shoulder and Callie had it behind him, both of them pulling. Jab walked on the outside plank with the frame. Dee walked last, with the back rope loose in her hand. The tailboard slid on the old planks with a long dry sound, and the carrier sat on it without moving.

"That's the thing about a board," Pell called from the bank. "A board doesn't care."

At the far end Callie said, "Stop."

The cracked plank was the middle one. The sledge was as wide as all three planks together. Its front end had come up to the crack and was lying across it, over the sagging middle and onto the two sound planks on either side.

"It'll carry it on the sound ones," said Toren. "The board's wide."

"It'll carry it till the middle of the load comes over the crack," said Callie. "Then it all comes down on the middle. The middle's rotten at the beam."

She crouched at the edge of the bridge and looked down between the planks, at the post and the cross-beam and the water.

"There's stone under," she said. "The post's sitting on a big flat stone in the river. A foot under the water. Where the current's white on it."

Toren looked. He saw it. It was pale and square under the running water, beside the post, like a step.

"Set," he said.

He put the foot of the strut down through the water onto the stone beside the post, and let it come up at its slant under the middle plank, beside the crack. It came up thin and hard through the river and met the underside of the plank. He was soft behind it. He did not lock.

"Pull," he said.

Callie pulled. Jab put down the frame's weight against the rail and took the front rope with one hand. The sledge slid. The middle of the carrier came over the crack, and the weight came down onto the plank, and down the strut into the stone in the river. It did not come up into him. His knees gave a little and took it. He counted breaths.

Three. Four.

The back end of the sledge came off the crack and onto the bank.

He let the strut go. At the end. Not after.

"Sliding," called Pell, across the river, a breath late. Somebody laughed. It was Rook.

---

Toren stood at the gate with the green knot of wire in front of him.

He untwisted it one-handed. It took a long time. The wire was stiff with twenty years, and it did not want to come. He did it a turn at a time, with the ends of his fingers, and Callie held the chain for him so it did not swing. When the last turn came loose, he held the wire in his hand for a moment. It was only a bit of copper wire.

He put it in his pocket. He did not know why. Then he took it out again and hung it on the wire of the fence, beside the gate, where it would be when they came out.

The chain came off the post.

He put his hand on the frame of the gate and pulled. It did not open. He pushed. It went. It swung inward on its hinges, slowly, with a long scrape of iron on the concrete, and stood open.

Behind them, across the river, Pell was writing. Toren could see his head bent over the paper on his knee.

Toren took the signed paper out of his coat and the pencil. He wrote on the back of it, under the night before, standing at the open gate with the paper against the iron.

*47th. The east gate. Noon. Out: 4.*

He wrote *In:* under it.

He looked at the word for a moment. Then he folded the paper with nothing after it, and put it back in his coat, beside the plate.

"Twenty-four," said Callie, beside him. "Twelve today."

"Twelve today," said Toren. He picked up the front rope. "Everybody back."

They pulled the sledge through the gate into Caul Hill.
