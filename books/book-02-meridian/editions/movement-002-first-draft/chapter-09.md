# Chapter 9 — Toren: Test Weights

The lamp at the end of the row had gone out at the first hour. By the fifth, Toren was sitting on the edge of his bed in the dark of hut eleven with one boot on, listening to Rook put on his.

It took Rook a long time. He sat with his back very straight and worked the boot onto his foot with both hands, and every so often he stopped with his fingers on the laces and breathed out through his nose, and then he went on. Nobody in the hut offered to help. That argument had been had on the road, somewhere before the Level, and Rook had won it by not answering.

Toren had not slept much. He had lain on his bed with his coat on and looked at the dark, and in the dark there had been a little window in the front of a grey box, lit the wrong colour. Third row down, fourth box along. Every other light in that rack burned a steady pale green. That one was yellow, like the line painted on the floor to tell you where to stop.

He got his other boot on one-handed. His left arm lived in the sling against his chest now, and over eleven days the break under the splint had settled from a grinding, sickening ache into a dull one that he could have drawn on paper, from two fingers above the wrist to a place just short of the elbow. His right hand had come out of its wrapping three days ago with a pink seam across the palm where the Breach had opened it. The seam pulled when he made a fist. Every morning he made a fist anyway, to see how far it would close, and every morning it closed a little further than the morning before.

"You don't have to go every day," said Wyck, from the bed by the door. He said it with his eyes shut.

"I know."

"He doesn't need you out there."

"I know that too."

Rook stood up in three movements, one hand on the bed frame. "He's coming because he's got nothing better to do with his feet," he said. "Go back to sleep, Wyck."

Wyck did not go back to sleep. Toren heard him turn over, carefully, onto the side that was not strapped, and lie there listening to them go.

---

The row was frozen. The wet concrete had gone white overnight and it crunched, and the air had the thin, clean bite that meant the season had turned while nobody in Meridian was looking. They went up the main street in the dark under three dead lamps, past the long building, where one window was lit at the front and a shape that might have been Mrs Lask sat behind it, and out across the north yard to the yard gate. The gate crew knew them now. A man in a grey coat opened the small door in the big one without being asked and said, "Morning, holder," to Rook, and Rook said, "Morning," and they went through.

It was two hundred paces of made road from the yard gate to the south lip of the Sag. Toren had walked it three mornings now at Rook's pace, which was the pace of a man carrying something heavy that nobody else could see, and he had stopped wanting to walk faster.

This morning the Sag was full of fog.

He had not seen it do that before. The dip lay under the fog like a bowl under milk, and the top of the fog was as level as poured milk, so that the road went down into whiteness at the south lip and came up out of it again three hundred paces away at the north. Down in the white there were the shapes of things he knew were there: the tilted slabs at the bottom, the marker posts down the east side, the black water standing in the joints. He could see the tops of the posts, the nearest three or four, sticking up out of the fog with their white paint grey in the half-dark. He could see nothing else.

"It does this," said Rook, "when the nights get cold and the ground doesn't. The ditches are warmer than the air." He looked at it a while. "It'll burn off by the eighth hour."

The day holder was at the south lip, where she always was.

She stood at the edge of the made road in a grey yard coat, with her hands out from her sides and her feet planted, facing the fog. She had been there since the night crew went off. She had held the Sag alone for two years, Pell had said, and he had said it the way he said a distance, as if it were only a number.

Toren had watched her for three mornings. He watched people's weight now; he had started on the road, with Senna in the cart and Rook's shoulders and Dessa's leg, and he could not stop. The day holder stood with her weight on her left foot. Every few minutes she moved it onto the right, very slowly, without lifting either foot off the ground, and after a while she moved it back again. He had counted, the first morning, and it was every four or five minutes. Under her right heel there was a hollow worn into the gravel at the lip about the size of a saucer.

You did not wear a hollow like that into gravel in a week.

Nobody watched her. The night crew going off watched the road behind them. The lifters coming on watched the ground in front. Rook watched the dip. She stood at the edge of all of it with her hands out and her face shut, holding a road, and the only thing Toren had ever heard anybody say to her was *morning*.

"Morning," said Rook.

"Morning," said the day holder. She did not look round.

---

The lip path went round the west rim of the dip above the ditch, and at this hour the fog came up over it to their knees. Toren walked on Rook's left, half a step behind, where the hole in his back was. He did not know what he would do if Rook fell on that side. He walked there anyway.

It took them nine minutes to go round to the north lip. When they got there Rook stood for a while and got his breath, looking down the road into the white.

Then he put his hands out from his sides.

Nothing happened that anybody could have seen. Across the dip, at the south lip, the day holder let her own hands drop and stood where she was, resting on her feet. She did not sit. Toren had never seen her sit.

He had never been able to see the line either. Callie said it was like a sheet of clean paper laid over the ground, so that where it lay she could read nothing at all, not a track, not a crack, not the grain of the silt. Dessa said it was like somebody holding their breath in a room. To Toren it was only the set of Rook's shoulders, and the way his hands stayed out from his sides a little higher than a man's hands would hang, and the look on his face, which was the look of a man listening for a sound in another part of the house.

Somewhere down in the fog, a slab ticked as it cooled.

The lifters came down the east track in a line. Katori first, with her cap pulled down; then Ines with her bow on her back, counting something under her breath; then Otto with the sack of jars; then Callie, with the hook on her shoulder and her left arm held close against her ribs. The east track ran along the side of the dip above the fog, on dry stone, and there was a drift along it this morning where the night crew had put down whatever had come up in the small hours: a Handful, perhaps a little more, lying grey in the frost.

Callie stopped before she knelt. She stood on the track and looked down into the fog, at the place where the fourth marker post and the fifth and the sixth went down into the white, and she stayed like that for a while. Then she said something to Pell, who had come down behind them with the atlas under his arm. Pell opened the atlas and wrote.

"She's looking at that slab again," said Toren.

"She looks at it every morning," said Rook. His hands did not move. "So does Pell's book."

On the west side of the dip, halfway down, where the bank came down to the ditch in one long easy slope, Jab was standing in the only way up. He had come again. He had the fish-knife shape on his right fist, dark, with no light on it, and the fog came up round his knees like water. He did not look at the lifters and he did not look at Rook. He looked at the ditch, and at the fog, and at the one place anything could come.

Toren looked at the fog for a while, and then at Rook's hands.

"You said a thing on the step last night," said Rook.

Toren waited.

"About four hundred miles."

"I didn't know you were awake."

"The door was open. Nobody in this station's ever awake at the first hour, except everybody." Rook did not take his eyes off the dip. "It was a true thing. I'd not say it again till you've done the sums on it."

"What sums?"

"The ones you do," said Rook. "Your father's kind. With the ifs written down."

Toren thought about his father's paper. It had said nineteen days to Meridian, with three ifs written under the number, and he had carried it down the road for sixty-four days while the number broke and was crossed out and written again underneath. He had hated the ifs, afterward. He had thought they were the part of the paper that had gone wrong.

He had not thought, until now, that they were the only part of it that had been true.

"All right," he said.

Rook nodded, once. That was the whole of the conversation.

---

At the end of the hour the day holder put her hands out again and took the whole line back. Rook let his down. He stood very still for a moment, and then his hands began to shake, the way they did every morning now, a fine fast tremor from the wrists to the ends of the fingers that he neither hid nor mentioned.

Toren sat down on the stone on his left side.

Down on the east track, Callie and Otto were working along the drift on their knees, a span at a time, palms flat, the way Rook had shown them. Katori stood over them with her hands at her sides. The fog was going thin at the top and Toren could see down into it now, a little way, to the tops of the slabs and the black water between them and the white post with the number six on it, sticking up at a tilt out of a crack in the road.

"Go and do your sums, then," said Rook.

"You'll be all right?"

"I'll be sitting down," said Rook. "It's the same thing today."

---

The shed was not open on a morning when nobody was booked. Toren went down the long slope under the north end of the long building anyway, into the cool grey air where the walls went thick and the windows stopped, and he stood at the yellow line in front of the wide door and waited.

The door was open a hand's width. Through it he could hear somebody working, the small patient noises of a man with tools in a quiet room: a scrape, a click, a long breath out.

After a while the grey-haired man came to the gap and looked at him.

He had his sleeves rolled and a smear of fresh ink up the side of his hand to join the old one. His eyes were red at the rims. He looked like a man who had been awake since the first hour doing arithmetic and had not liked the answers.

"It's not a Friday," he said.

"I know."

"She's not due for ten days."

"I know. I'm not here for her." Toren looked past him through the gap, at the long room with the pale ring standing in it and the rack along the back wall. He could see the rows of little windows from here, most of them a steady green. He found the third row down and counted along. "I'm here about the fourth box."

The grey-haired man looked at him for a long moment.

"Why?" he said.

It was a fair question. Toren had walked down the slope trying to find the answer to it, and had not, and now he had to say something.

"Because nobody's asked," he said. "My gran's on it every eleven days for the rest of her life. There's a boy in the ward who needs a picture on the sixteenth, and a woman who's going to need them for as long as there is. And yesterday I sat outside that door and heard you tell the doctor there's never been a spare of anything in that room." He stopped. "So I thought somebody ought to know what the box is. Before it stops."

The grey-haired man rubbed the ink on his hand with his thumb. It did not come off. It had not come off in some years.

"Wait there," he said.

---

He came back with a box in his arms.

It was grey steel, about the size of a loaf, with a little dark window in the front and a plate riveted to the steel beside it. He carried it the way you carry a sleeping animal, close in against his chest, and when he came to the yellow line he stepped over it without breaking stride, because nothing in the rule said a thing could not come out.

"It's dead," he said. "Row one, nine along. It died before I came here, and I came here when I was twenty. It was a reference for a kind of picture nobody takes any more. I keep it for the screws." He held it out. "Mind. It's heavier than it looks."

Toren took it in his right hand and against his hip, the way Otto took the barrow. It was heavier than it looked. It was heavier than anything that size had any business being, dense and dead and cold, and his arm dropped three inches before it understood.

"Lead," said the grey-haired man. "There's a jacket inside, all round. It keeps what's in the middle from talking to what's next door."

"What's in the middle?"

"A thing we know the true value of." He sat down on the bench against the wall, which Toren had never seen him do, and put his forearms on his knees. "Have you seen the weighbridge? In the yard?"

"I've walked past it."

"Every morning, before he weighs anything, the weighman puts the test weights on. Iron blocks, with a number stamped in them. He knows what they weigh, because somebody weighed them against something else they knew, and so on back, a long way, to people who are dead. If he puts a fifty on and the scale says fifty-one, it's the scale that's wrong. Not the weight. The weight's the one thing in that yard nobody argues with." He nodded at the box in Toren's arm. "That's a test weight. So's everything in that rack. Every morning, before the machine looks at anybody, it weighs itself against every one of them, row after row. If they all agree, it's telling the truth. If one of them's off, the machine can't tell which of them is lying. It's too honest. So it tries to agree with all of them at once."

"And the fourth box is off."

"The fourth box has started to lie. Only a hair. It's not the thing in the middle; that's as good as the day they sealed it. It's what reads it that's going, and I can't open it to find out which part, because once it's open it isn't a reference any more. It's just a box." He rubbed his hand. "So the ones either side of it try to agree with it. And they start to lie too, a hair each. That's why every run takes longer. I've been sitting at that screen telling the machine it's wrong, by hand, because I know what it ought to say. That works as long as I know."

He looked at the floor between his boots.

"I'm starting not to know," he said.

---

Toren stood at the yellow line with seventy years of lead against his hip and did not say anything for a while.

He was thinking about the rack. About the way the green lights had gone pale one after another when the machine walked them, left to right, like somebody going along a row of windows with a candle. About Callie on the corridor floor saying *that's ten*. About the yellow light.

"What would a spare look like?" he said.

"Like that." The grey-haired man nodded at the dead box. "Not that one. Each of them's sealed round a different value; you can't put one in another's place. But the same kind. The same makers, the same plate, the same sort of room. If I had a live one of that kind, I could set it against the ones that still tell the truth, and learn what it says, and drop it in. A month's work. Less, if I didn't sleep."

"And where would there be one?"

The man laughed. It was the same laugh as the one in the corridor on the tenth day, which was not a laugh.

"Anywhere they built rooms like that one, before the Fall," he said. "Which was a great many places. Most of them weren't hardened. Those are dust and wire now. The ones that were hardened were hospitals, mostly, and places that did research. And the big plants." He looked up. "The power plants, the big ones. They shielded their instrument rooms the same as we are, because the same sort of people built them. It's in the manuals. I've never been in one. Nobody's been in one since my father's time, that I know of."

*The last time I stood this close to one of those racks, I was fifty-three. And I'd come for the copper.*

Toren did not say it. He stood very still with the box on his hip, and his face must have done something, because the grey-haired man looked at it and then looked away, the way you look away from a man counting money.

"Can I copy the plate?" said Toren.

The man looked back at him.

"Nobody's ever asked me that," he said.

"Somebody should have."

The man held his eyes for a moment. Then he felt in his shirt and found a stub of pencil and the back of a form with printing on the other side, and held them out.

Toren set the box down on the concrete on his side of the line, carefully, and knelt beside it, and copied the plate. He did it with his right hand, and the seam across his palm pulled every time he closed his fingers on the pencil, and he went slowly. There was a stamped mark in the top corner, a small thing like a wheel with four spokes and a bar across the bottom. There were three lines of stamped letters that meant nothing to him, and under them a long number, and under that a short one. He copied every letter and every digit. He copied the wheel twice, because the first one was wrong.

"Sowerby's looked," said the grey-haired man, watching him. "For years. You know that. For other people. He's written to every place that answers letters."

"He looked for a machine," said Toren, without looking up.

The man said nothing.

"Nobody's looked for a box," said Toren, and finished the number, and checked it digit by digit against the plate, and folded the paper twice and put it in his coat pocket, on the other side from the stone.

When he stood up, the grey-haired man was still looking at him. He picked up the dead box and held it against his chest.

"Ten days," he said. "The morning. Bring her early. I'll want to warm it up three times."

"I'll bring her."

"I know you will," said the man, and went back over the yellow line, and the door closed to a hand's width behind him.

---

His grandmother had mislaid her feet.

"They've gone somewhere," she said. "I stood up this morning to go to the chair, which I'm allowed to do if somebody's watching, and I looked down, and the left one was on the edge of the blanket and I hadn't the least idea. I'd have sworn it was on the floor. It was an inch off the floor, on a blanket, and I'd put my whole weight on it." She was sitting up against her pillows in the fourth bed on the right, with her hands in her lap, watching them. "The nurse caught me. She was very good about it. I told her so."

"Gran—"

"Don't do that face. It's the feet. It was always going to be the feet after the hands. Sowerby drew it on a page for us." She turned one hand over, slowly, looking at it. "It's early, that's all. The old business is in a hurry. I'm not."

In the fifth bed Tonk lay flat with the bar along his leg and the long cord knotted to his wrist, and the other end of it, Toren saw, was round Jab's. Jab sat in the chair between the beds with his elbows on his knees. The fever had come down in the night; you could see it in Tonk's face, which was only pink now and not red, and in Jab's, which had stopped doing arithmetic for the first time in two days.

"The nails are less hot," Tonk told Toren. He said it as if Toren had asked, which he had not. "They're only a *bit* hot now."

"That's good."

"The doctor said I'd be hot for two days and cross for three." Tonk considered. "I'm doing the cross ones now."

"He started early," said Jab.

Toren sat down on the end of his grandmother's bed, careful of her feet, although she could not have felt him if he had sat on them. He took the paper out of his pocket and unfolded it and held it out.

She did not take it. She could not have held it well. She looked at it where it was, in his hand.

"What's that?" she said.

"The plate off one of the boxes. In the rack. The man let me copy it."

She looked at it for a long time.

Her face did the thing it had done in the corridor on the tenth day, when she turned her chair by leaning and looked through the wide door at the rack. It was not fear. Toren had seen his grandmother frightened, twice, and it did not look like this. It was the face of somebody standing at the start of a road they have walked before and know the whole length of.

Then it closed.

"Put that away, Tor," she said.

"Gran. You've seen that mark."

"I've seen a great many marks. I'm seventy-three."

"You said rooms full of them. In the corridor. You said you went in for the copper."

"I said a great many things in a corridor with a machine singing flat at me through a wall." She did not look away from him. Her eyes were very pale and very steady. "Listen to me. I'm going to tell you what I know. I haven't decided when. I'm going to decide that for myself, once I've worked out what it'll cost you to know it, because I know you, Toren Voss, and I knew your father, and I know exactly what the pair of you do with a number once you've got it." She let out a breath. "I've a great deal of time and very little to do with it. Let me use some of it on this."

He folded the paper and put it back in his pocket.

"All right," he said.

"Thank you."

"But you're going to tell me."

"I said I would," said Senna. "I don't say things twice. You know that. It makes a woman tiresome."

---

"What's the paper?" said Tonk, who had watched all of this with great interest from his pillow.

"It's a picture of a box," said Toren.

"The *yellow* one?"

"One like it. The yellow one's still in the rack. It's still working. It's only working badly."

Tonk thought about this.

"Yellow's for *wasps*," he said, darkly.

Jab laughed, the short startled one that seemed to get knocked out of him. Then he looked at Toren. "What did he say? The man."

Toren told him. He told it plainly, the way it had been told to him: the test weights, the thing in the middle nobody argued with, the machine too honest to know which box was lying. The fourth box going wrong by a hair. The boxes either side of it working harder and harder to agree with it, until they started to go wrong too.

Jab listened to the whole of it without moving. When it was finished he looked at his brother, and then at the old woman in the next bed, and then out of the window.

"Like a family," he said.

Tonk turned his head on the pillow.

"Like *us*?"

"A bit," said Jab. "A bit like us."

Toren did not say anything. He was thinking about the clan on the road: Senna in the cart, and all of them going a little further every day for her than they had gone the day before, until Milo went further than anybody. He thought Jab had put it better than the man in the shed.

---

They talked at the window while Tonk slept, the two of them, low, so Senna would not hear. Senna heard anyway. She had shut her eyes and folded her hands in her lap, which was what she did when she wanted to hear things.

"I did the sums on your box," said Jab.

"What sums?"

"If it's weeks, like he said. Maybe less." Jab kept his eyes on the window. "If it's five weeks, that's three cycles for her. Ten days from now, and twenty-one, and thirty-two. Two pictures for him, if they give him the second. And then there's your friend's mum, and whatever happens to her. And then my head keeps adding whoever comes through the gate next, and I can't stop it, because I don't know their name yet so I can't make them into a number I can hold."

"Sowerby said four hundred miles."

"You said that's only as far as he's looked."

"He looked for a machine," said Toren. "Everybody's been looking for another machine. You can't carry a machine. Nobody's looked for a box."

Jab turned his head and looked at him properly.

He had a way of looking at a thing that Toren had noticed on the steps, a way of going quiet all through, as if he had put the whole of his attention down on a table and taken his hands off it. He did it now for about as long as it would take to count ten.

"That's a different sum," he said.

"Yes."

"Somebody would have to know where a box was."

"Yes," said Toren.

Behind them, in the fourth bed, his grandmother lay with her eyes shut and her hands folded, and did not move at all.

---

They did the bread without her that night.

Sowerby's nurse would not let her out after the feet, not over four hundred yards of frozen path in the chair, not tonight. Dessa had gone up to ask, and come back with her face shut, and said *tomorrow, maybe*, and cut the bread on the top of the stove-box in her poor thick slabs.

She did the order. She did it the way she had done it every night: Senna's name first, and a slab set aside on a plate for somebody to carry up in the morning; then *me*; then *Wyck*; then *Rook*; then *Tor*.

Then she cut the last one and put it on the cloth at the end of the row and folded the cloth over it, and did not say the name.

Nobody said it. That was Senna's, now; she had said she would say it every night until they carried her out, and none of them had to. Toren thought she was probably saying it at this moment, flat on her back in the fourth bed with her eyes on the ceiling, to a ward full of sleeping strangers and a four-year-old who would ask her in the morning who Milo was.

He found he could not eat his slab. He held it.

Across the stove, Wyck reached for the kettle with his left hand, and as he lifted it his right shoulder came up inside the strapping, a quarter of an inch, and stopped. It was a small thing. Toren would not have seen it on the road. He saw it now, because he watched weight, and because Wyck's face went white round the mouth for as long as it took him to set the kettle down on the stove, and then went back to Wyck's face.

Later, when the others had gone to their beds, Toren went outside to the step and found Wyck there before him, in the dark, standing up against the wall of the hut with his strapped side pressed flat to the concrete. He was leaning into it. He was putting his weight into the wall through the bad shoulder, slowly, the way you would lean on a gate to see if the post would hold.

He heard Toren and stopped.

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"Say nothing," said Wyck.

"I haven't said anything."

"Keep on not saying it." Wyck came away from the wall. He did it carefully, which Wyck never did. "It's mine."

Toren looked at him in the dark for a long time. Then he nodded, because it was, and sat down on the step, and Wyck went in.

---

The man in the long coat had been, while Toren was at the ward. Dessa told them at the stove, in her flat way, before they slept.

"He stood on the step," she said. "He wouldn't come in. He said the medical side's circuit came in off the river this afternoon, with the cart. He said there's somebody on it who's going to want to see the boy in twelve." She looked at Rook. "And you."

Rook was sitting on the floor against his bed with his forearms on his knees. He had the small grey stone out, the one that was not his, and he was turning it in his fingers, the way he did at night now that there was nobody on the road to keep watch for.

"Me," he said.

"He said she reads road Wards."

"Who does?"

"He didn't say. He doesn't give names. He doesn't give his own." Dessa put the knife away in its cloth. "He said she'd want to see your line."

Rook turned the stone over, and over again. Toren watched his face, because he had got into the habit, and there was nothing in it. There was only a man turning a stone.

"Then she'll see it," said Rook. "It's out there every morning. Anybody can look."

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour.

---

The fog came again in the morning, thicker than before.

This time they went out with the lifters, because Katori's crew had come to the yard gate at the same moment, in a clump of breath and lamps. Callie had the hook over her shoulder. She looked at Toren when they came through the small door, a quick look, the way she did, and then she looked at his right hand, and then she looked at the road.

At the south lip she stopped so suddenly that Otto walked into her.

"Somebody's been here," she said.

The day holder did not turn round. Katori did.

Callie was looking at the gravel of the lip, where the made road ended and the dip began. There was nothing on it that Toren could see except the frost and the day holder's hollow and a great many boot marks, the crews coming and going, the night crew's heavy yard boots in a trampled mess where they had stood around their lamps.

"There," said Callie. She crouched, and pointed with the hook's handle without touching. "Since before the gate opened. The frost's in the heel of it, not over it. Small. Smaller than mine." She moved the handle along a line Toren could not see. "Good boots. The heels are new; there's the nail pattern, every one of them sharp. That's not a yard boot. Yard boots are all worn to the same shape, because everybody in the yard walks on the same cinders." She stood up. "They stood here a long time. Then they went round."

"Round where?" said Katori.

Callie looked along the lip path, west, above the ditch, into the white. She followed something with her eyes the way Toren had seen her follow a husk line on the plain, or a track in the dark.

"There," she said.

On the west bank, above the one door, where the fog lay thin at the rim, somebody was standing with their hands in their pockets, looking down into the Sag.

---

Rook did not see her. He had already started round the lip path toward the north lip at his slow pace, looking at the ground in front of his feet. Toren went after him, on his left. When they went past the place above the one door he looked up, and the figure on the bank was a woman in a long oilskin coat, bareheaded, with dark hair cut short to her skull, standing very still.

She was not watching the lifters or the dip or the fog. She was watching Rook.

She watched him all the way round to the north lip. She watched him stand there and get his breath. She watched him put his hands out from his sides, and at the south lip the day holder's hands dropping, and the fog lying flat and white over the whole of the bottom of the Sag with a road shut underneath it that nobody could see.

For an hour she stood on the bank and watched him hold it.

Toren watched her watching. Once she took her hands out of her pockets and turned her left wrist over, slowly, the way you turn a wrist that aches in the cold, and then put the hand back. Once she looked across the dip at the day holder, for a long time, and then back at Rook. She never looked at Toren at all.

Below her, in the one door, Jab stood in the fog up to his knees with his dark knife and did not look up once. Toren was nearly sure he knew she was there. It was the way he stood.

At the end of the hour the day holder took the line back. Rook let his hands down. They began to shake.

And the woman on the bank came down.

---

She came round the lip path at a walk, not fast, and she walked the way nobody else Toren had seen in Meridian walked, as if the ground might not be where it looked and she had learned to check with her feet. Close to, she was younger than he had thought on the bank. Twenty-five, perhaps. Her face was all flat planes and it did not move much. Under the oilskin she wore the grey of the long building, and on her feet were good boots, brown, laced to the shin, with new heels.

Her left wrist, where it came out of the sleeve, had a lump on the outside of it the size of a thumbnail, where a bone had knit crooked a long time ago.

She stopped two paces from Rook.

She looked at his hands first. She looked at them for a long time, shaking in front of him at the ends of his arms, and her face did nothing at all.

Rook looked up.

Toren saw his face do something he had not seen it do, not on the step with Katori, not anywhere. On the step, Rook had looked like a man who hears a voice in the next room he has stopped expecting to hear anywhere. This was a different look. This was the look of a man who has expected to hear a voice every day for eleven years, and has been afraid of it every day, and hears it.

"Elias," she said.

And Toren knew who she was.

He had been at the fire on the lane when Rook told them. He had sat in the dark with the others while Rook said the whole of it, Homura and the wire and the long room, and he had heard the part that came after, which Rook had said quickly, as if it did not matter: *I walked south for two months with a fourteen-year-old girl called Dee Wren who had a broken wrist and no shoes.*

He looked at her wrist. He looked at her boots.

"Dee," said Rook.

---

For a while neither of them said anything else. The fog lay in the bottom of the dip. Down on the east track the lifters had stopped, Toren saw; Katori was standing with her hand up, flat, the way you stop a crew without a word, and all of them were looking up at the north lip.

"Your line's thin on the east edge," said the woman. "You're holding it with your shoulders. I could see it from the bank."

"Good morning to you too," said Rook.

"Who cleared you to hold?"

"Nobody."

"No," she said. "Nobody would have."

She took his right hand. She did not hold it; she turned it over, palm up, and laid two fingers on the inside of the wrist, where the pulse was, and looked away over the fog while she counted. Toren had seen that before. He had seen Rook do it a hundred times on the road, to Senna, to Wyck, to Dessa after the bridge. He had never once thought about where Rook had learned it.

"Sit down, Elias," she said, "before you fall down."

Rook sat. It took him three movements. She crouched in front of him with her forearms on her knees.

He was looking at her wrist.

"It set," he said.

"It set crooked. Two sticks and a bootlace on a riverbank, and you said you were sorry for four days." Her voice had not changed at all. "It's held eleven years. I've set a hundred since. Mine are straighter."

"I don't doubt it."

"Yours held," said the woman.

Rook looked down at his own hands.

"They told me at the river," she said, after a while, "that a man came asking for me. Four years after."

"I did."

"I was here by then."

"I know. I didn't follow."

"No," she said. "You didn't." She waited. "Why not?"

Rook was quiet for a long time.

"Because I'd have had to tell you what I was doing instead," he said.

"I knew what you were doing. Everybody off the wire knew what you were doing. We used to hear it on the river road from people who'd heard it from people." She looked at his hands again, which had not stopped. "You can't get the angle on yourself. You never could."

"Dee—"

She put her palm flat against his chest, over the breastbone, the way Toren had seen Jab put his hand on his brother.

Toren watched the shaking in Rook's hands slow. It did not stop all at once. It slowed like a bell slows after it is struck, the long fine tremor going longer and finer, until it was a thing he had to look for, and then it was not there. Rook's shoulders came down half an inch. His face did not change.

"Don't," he said.

"Too late," said Dee Wren.

She took her hand away and sat back on her heels. Toren saw that her own face had gone a shade paler round the mouth, and that she pressed her hands together, one inside the other, in her lap, for as long as it took to breathe out twice. Then she let them go. Whatever it had cost her, she had paid it and put it away.

"That was yours," said Rook. "It wasn't yours to spend."

"I'll decide what's mine," said Dee. "I've been deciding it for some years now."

---

Toren went down the slope.

He had stood there long enough. It was not his, and he had known it was not his since the first word; it was only that he could not make his feet move until something told them to. When he did go, he went down the east side of the north lip to the track, where the lifters had gone back to work, and knelt beside Callie at the drift. He could not lift with one hand very well, but he could hold a jar steady while somebody tipped their palm into it, and that was something.

Callie tipped her palm into the jar. The ash went down out of her into the glass and lay grey in the bottom.

"Who is she?" she said.

"Somebody he walked south," said Toren.

Callie looked up the slope at the woman crouched in front of Rook on the north lip. She looked at the boots, and then at the wrist, and then at Toren's face. She held it for a moment, the way she held a patch of ground.

Then she put her palm flat on the drift again and did not ask anything else.

He had liked her for that on the bench, the first day. He found he liked her for it more now.

---

"Tor," said Rook.

Toren stood up. Rook was on his feet again, with one hand on the woman's shoulder for the last part of it, which Toren had never seen him allow anybody. The woman had stood when he stood.

"Dee Wren," said Rook. "Toren Voss."

She looked at him. She looked at all of him, from the top down, taking her time: the sling, the splint, the hand with the seam across the palm, the way he was standing, which Toren supposed was a way that said he had stitches healing at the back of one leg. She looked at him the way Sowerby looked at people, and the way Keel looked at people, and the way the woman at the north gate had looked at Tonk, for about four seconds, before anybody touched anything.

"The one who does two at once," she said.

"Once," said Toren.

"Twice, on Mr Hallet's sheet." She did not smile, but something at the corner of her mouth moved. "Hallet's sheets don't lie. They're the only thing in this station I've never caught at it."

Then her eyes went past him, across the dip, to the west side, where the fog was going thin, and where the one easy slope came down from the rim to the ditch, and where a boy of seventeen with a bandaged arm stood up to his knees in the white with a dark knife on his fist, looking at the only place anything could come.

"And that one stands in doors," she said.

"That one's why you're here," said Rook.

"I'm here for both of you," said Dee Wren. "I only found out about you last night."

She went down off the north lip and along the lip path toward the one door, not fast. Toren watched her go. She put her feet down as if the ground might not be where it looked, and he understood, watching her, that she was somebody who had once walked for two months without shoes, and had not forgotten a single step of it.
