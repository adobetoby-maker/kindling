# Chapter 15 — Jab: Below the Line

It had started four minutes before he gave Toren the door.

He had been standing out on the open slope below the gap in the shoulder with the whole edge of the knife lit and held up high, calling the grey ones off three people coming out of the fog, and they had come to it the way water comes to a drain. He had been busy. He had been putting the belly of the blade through husks one after another, the fat part, the part for when you can't get behind a thing, and watching Ines's arrows go past him into the white, and keeping his light between the bottom of the Sag and a girl going up the slope behind him with her wrist held against her chest.

And under all of it, under his breastbone, a finger's width behind, the floor had tipped.

He had felt it tip the way you feel a boat take a wave from a side you were not watching. Not far, at first. A lean. The old lean, the one that woke him at the second hour, the one that had only ever said one thing about one person. It said the same thing now.

*Worse.*

It did not say it about Tonk. Tonk was six hundred yards behind him and a berm and a wall away, in the fifth bed on the right, under a frame with a bar hanging over it on a chain, and Jab knew exactly where that was the way you know where your own hand is in the dark. This was not behind him. It was in front of him. It was out there in the fog, east, across the bottom of the Sag, where there was nothing to see but white and nobody he knew.

It had never done that. It had never leaned toward somebody he could not see. It had never leaned toward anybody who was not his brother, except once, in a shed, with his eyes shut and a woman with a crooked wrist putting his hand where she wanted it.

He had not asked it. He had a rule about that. He had been too busy with the grey ones to ask it anything at all, and it had come on its own, the way it came at the second hour, and it was not wrong in the direction he wanted, because he did not want anything from the fog. He did not know who was in it.

*I don't know yet whether they're one thing or two*, she had said, in the shed, with the bucket going *tick* at the far end. *I'll know when I've seen both do something I didn't set up.*

Nobody had set this up.

---

He went down off the shoulder on the far side of the door, dark.

He had put the light out before he went. He did not remember doing it. The grey ones came at light. The whole of the bottom of the Sag was full of fog and there were grey ones in it still, he could hear them, and a boy going across it with a knife lit up like a lantern would have every one of them on him before he had gone twenty paces. So he went dark, with the metal on his fist heavy and blunt and hooked at the end, the shape the light did not change, a tool for splitting fish.

The fog closed over him.

It was cold. It was colder than the air above it, and wet, and it smelled of stirred ditch and old stone and something else he did not have a word for, sharp at the back of the nose, like the air after lightning. He could see his own hand in front of him and not much past it. Under his boots the slabs of the bottom of the Sag tilted one way and then another, with black water standing in the joints, and he went across them the way he had gone across the flooded creek on the road, a long time ago: slowly, from the bottom up, putting each foot down and waiting for the stone to tell him what it was going to do before he put his weight on it.

The lean pulled him. It did not pull like a hand. It pulled like the slope of a floor, so that he found he was walking a little to the left of where he had meant to, and then a little more, and he let it.

Something came at him out of the white.

He did not see it. He heard it, the scrabble of too many legs on wet stone, low and to his right, and he turned toward the sound with the knife coming up, and lit it. Not the edge. Not the belly. He lit the hook, the last inch at the end of the blade, curled like a bent finger, the way he had lit it for the rail at the jam, and he put the lit hook into the grey shape where its shoulder would be as it came out of the fog at him, and drew it through, and put the light out again before the ash had finished falling.

One breath of light. He counted it. It had cost him the price of one breath of light, which was nearly nothing, and he had the rest.

He went on.

---

There was something lying across the bottom of the Sag that he could feel.

He had not known he would be able to. Callie said it was like a clean sheet of paper laid over the ground, so that where it lay she could not read anything. Dessa said it was like somebody holding their breath in a room. To Jab, going across it in the fog with the lean pulling him left, it was neither. It was like walking across the floor of a house where somebody upstairs was holding a door shut against a wind, and you could feel it through the whole house, in the boards, a strain, a leaning, the way you feel a big animal's breathing through a wall.

It was held. Something was holding it. And the holding came from in front of him, from the east, from exactly the place the lean was pulling him toward.

He came to the place where the ground went down.

It went down all at once, in front of his boots: the edge of a slab, tilted, and beyond it the next slab a foot lower, gone down into black water at a slant like a door off its hinges. There was bright dirt along the broken edge where it had been hidden a long time and was not hidden now. There was a white post standing up out of the water beside it, at a lean, with an iron cap.

Six.

He went down onto the dropped slab. The water came up to his shins. It was very cold.

And there she was.

---

She was standing at the far corner of the dropped slab, in the fog, where the east ditch came in. She was standing the way she always stood, the way he had seen her stand every morning for two weeks from the one door, the one grey shape at the south lip that nobody watched: feet planted, arms out from her sides.

Except that one arm was not out. Her left hand was pressed flat against her side, under her ribs, and held there. Only her right arm was out. She was holding the line with one hand.

Her face was the colour of the fog.

She turned her head and saw him. He saw her see him, and see what he was, a boy of seventeen with a dark knife on his fist, knee-deep in a ditch in the bottom of her Sag.

"Get back to your post, boy," she said.

Her voice was very low and very rough, like a gate that wanted oil, and it did not shake. Nothing about her shook. That was the thing he would remember. She stood in the black water with one hand out, holding a road shut, and her face was grey, and there was a sweat on it like a sheen of wet on a stone, and nothing about her shook at all.

He put his hand on her arm. The one pressed to her side.

The floor under his breastbone went over.

It did not tip. It went. It went the way it had gone at the culvert on the north road with his brother going grey under his hand, all at once and all the way, so that he had to put his other hand out against her shoulder to stay standing, and it did not say *worse*. It did not say anything. It pointed. It pointed under his hand, under her hand, under the ribs on the left side, into the dark inside her, where something that should have been whole was not whole, and where her blood was going, a great deal of it, a great deal too fast, somewhere it should not go.

"You're bleeding," he said.

"I'm holding."

"Inside. Here." He did not take his hand away. "You're bleeding inside."

"Something came up at the corner," said the day holder, "when it folded. It hit me in the side. I went down." She did not look at him. She looked at the fog in front of her where the line was. "I got up. I laid it back down. That's what you do." Her mouth moved on something that was not quite a word. "Then I'll bleed and hold."

"You'll bleed out holding."

She did not answer.

He watched her not answer. He watched her stand there with her one arm out, and her feet in the black water in the corner of a dropped slab, a woman of fifty-six who had held this road alone for two years while crews came and went and nobody watched her, and he thought: *nobody saw.* She had been hit in the fog, and gone down, and got up, and laid a folded line back down over the whole bottom of the Sag with a hand pressed to her side, and nobody had seen any of it. Nobody had come.

"I've got two years of this road," said the day holder. "I know what it costs."

Her knees went.

---

He caught her. He did not catch her well. She was heavier than she looked and he had the knife on one fist, and he let the knife go back into the metal on his neck cord and got both arms under her and went down with her onto the dropped slab, into the water, and got her head up out of it against his knee.

The line went.

He felt it go. It went the way a held breath goes, all at once. The strain in the floor of the house was gone. The ground under his knees, which had been the ground under something, was only ground again, wet slab and black water and silt, and in the joints of the slabs around him something shifted, down in the dark under the stone, the way a thing shifts when a lid comes off it.

He had time to think: *it's open.*

And then something else came down.

It came from the north, from the far lip, from a long way off, and he felt it arrive the way you feel a second pair of hands take the other end of a heavy thing you have just dropped. It came down over the whole bottom of the Sag at once, over him and her and the dropped slab and the black water, and it was not the same as hers. Hers had been a door held shut. This was a road shut. He knew it. He had seen it done on the north road on the night he came in, from the angle of a wall, with his brother against his chest: a man with his feet planted and his hands out from his sides, and the road on either side of him gone wrong.

Rook had taken the whole of it.

The shifting in the joints stopped. The ground under Jab's knees was the ground under something again.

He did not have time to think about what that cost. He had a woman's head on his knee and her blood going where it should not, and her face was not the colour of the fog any more. It was going past that.

---

He put his hand flat on her chest.

Not on her side. Not where the bleeding was; he did not know anything about where the bleeding was, and he had a rule about that, written on a card, pressed hard with the pencil. On her chest, over the breastbone, the way he had at the culvert, the way he had on the tenth day in the ward with the pin going red. Over the heart.

The heart was going very fast and very small, like a bird's.

He did not push. He had learned that much on a road and it had cost him an hour of a bad night and the use of his hands. He did not reach, or lean on it, or ask it to run uphill.

And he could not stop wanting.

He tried. He had tried in the shed with a stranger's cold hand in his, and he had not been able to, and he could not now. He wanted this woman to live more than he had wanted anything since the culvert. He did not know her name. He had never spoken to her. He had watched her every morning for two weeks, the one shape at the south lip, and he had not once thought about whether anybody else was watching her, and now he knelt in a ditch with her head on his knee and wanted her to live so much it was like a stone in his chest.

It went out of him anyway.

It went out of him without asking his permission, the way it had at the culvert. There was no decision in it anywhere. It came up out of the place it lived and went forward out of his chest, down his arm, through his palm, into the chest under his hand, and it was not a blade and it had no edge and it was not any shape at all.

It was warmth. It was the plainest thing in the world.

---

Her heart slowed.

He felt it slow. The bird under his hand went from a flutter to a beat, and from a small beat to a bigger one, and settled, the way a horse settles when you put your hand on its neck in a bad stall. Her breathing, which had been quick and high and shallow, went down into her. It went deep, and ragged, and regular.

She shook. It came up through her from the shoulders to the heels, hard, and again, and her teeth knocked together, and he thought of the Elder in her kitchen with the lamp lit, four hundred miles north, telling him what to watch for on the road. *Shaking is the body fighting. Be glad when it shakes.*

He was glad.

Her colour did not come back. He knew it would not. He knew, the way he had known at the culvert, that whatever was broken inside her was still broken, and whatever was bleeding was still bleeding. He was not mending anything. He was not even touching it. He was holding her up. He was keeping the whole of her going, the heart and the breath and the warmth of her, while the part of her that was broken went on being broken, so that there would still be a her for somebody to mend.

That was all it was. He did not know how long it could be.

He counted.

He counted in breaths, because there was nothing else to count in, and every sixteen of hers he called a minute, because the Elder had taught him that a sleeping grown woman breathes about sixteen times in a minute, and he had checked it on the road against Tonk's twenty-four and his own. It was not exact. It was his.

One minute. Two.

The bucket was going down.

---

He could feel it go.

He had never felt it go before. At the culvert it had simply gone out, like a lamp, and he had found himself sitting down in the ash. In the snow he had not known anything until his hand would not close. This time he knew. This time he had been down to look at it every night for eleven nights, since a woman in a shed told him to, and he knew what it looked like: a hand of dark in the bottom of a bucket, and a finger up from the bottom, scratched on the inside, a line.

It was going down the way water goes down in a bucket with a hole in it, steadily, a little every breath.

Four minutes. Five.

He thought about the line.

*That's your floor*, she had said. *You never go below it. Not for anybody.*

*Especially not for anybody.*

He had not said yes. She had written that down.

Six minutes.

He thought about his mother on a gravel bar four years ago with his brother on her back, looking at the ugliest blade anybody in the valley had ever pulled out of a river stone. *Well, it looks like you. That's the whole of it. Don't spend too long.*

He had never asked her what she meant by the last part. He had thought he had years.

Seven.

The dark in the bucket came down to the line.

---

He could feel exactly where it was. That was the strange thing. He had never known where anything was in that bucket before, and now he knew the way you know the edge of a step in the dark with your foot: there. A finger from the bottom. If it went below that, he would not be able to get up off this slab afterward. He would not be able to light the knife tomorrow. He would be the one somebody had to carry.

He took his hand off her chest. Only a little. Only to see.

She went grey.

It happened under his hand, in the time it took to breathe out. The heart that had settled to a beat went back toward the bird, and the breath went high, and the shaking stopped, and the Elder's voice in his head said *a child that's gone quiet and easy in the cold is a child two hours from the end of it*, and he did not think she had two hours. He did not think she had one.

He put his hand back.

It was not a decision. He had thought, when Dee drew the line on the inside of his bucket, that if he ever came to it there would be a decision, a place where he stood and weighed one thing against the other and chose. There was no place like that. There was a woman on a slab who went grey when he took his hand away, and did not when he put it back, and there was a line a finger from the bottom of a bucket.

*You don't get out of owing*, he had told his brother, in the ward, with the squirrel four hundred miles north. *You just have to find a different heap.*

He had not thought it would be a ditch.

He went below the line.

---

It was cold.

That was the first thing. The cold came in through his hands and feet first, the way it had in the snow. His fingers on her chest went cold from the ends inward, and his feet in the black water, which had been cold already, went a different kind of cold, the kind that is not in the skin but under it. Then it was in his wrists. Then his shins.

It was not like the bucket going down. Above the line it had been water going out of a bucket. Below it, it was the bucket.

He did not stop.

Eight minutes. Nine.

The fog was thinning. He noticed it the way you notice things at the edge of a fever. It had gone from white to grey to a sort of wet silver, and he could see further than his hand now, he could see the white post with the six on it leaning out of the water beside the slab, and he could see its shadow.

It had two.

And past it, at the edge of the fog, east, where the ditch came in, something was standing very still, side-on, with its head low.

---

It was long in the body and short in the leg and the colour of wet ash, and the light came off its back with a shiver in it, the way air shivers over a hot stone.

There was another behind it.

They were not moving. They were doing the other thing, the stillness, the thing the first one had done in the ditch on the north road at thirty feet, from lope to stillness with nothing in between. They were not looking at him so much as standing there being aimed at him.

He understood it all at once, the way he had understood a fence.

They had gone into the pipe because the pipe was dark. Something had stopped them in the pipe; he did not know what. Callie, he thought, at the west mouth, with her hook. They had come back out of it, east, into the ditch, into the fog, looking for dark. And the whole of the Sag was lit now: Toren's spike in the door, Katori's grey blade somewhere, lamps on poles on the east track, Ines on the rim. Everywhere was lit.

Except here.

Here there was a boy kneeling in black water with his knife gone back into the metal on his neck cord, and a woman on her back on a slab with his hand on her chest, and no light at all. The darkest thing in the Sag.

*They come at the dark bit.*

He could not light the knife. He knew that without trying. There was nothing to light it with. Everything that had been in the bucket and everything that had been the bucket was going down his arm into her chest, and if he took any of it back for the knife she would go grey, and if he took his hand off her to hold the knife up she would go grey, and there were two of them and one of him and she could not move.

*They can only come at one place*, he had thought on the north road. *So pick it.*

He was the place.

"Mine," he said.

He did not say it loud. He did not have anything to say it loud with. He said it to the two shapes at the edge of the fog, the way he had shouted it on the north road into the dark with his brother behind him, only now it was not a shout, it was barely a word. *Mine. They come at me.*

The near one dropped its head.

---

There was a crack like struts loading, and a scatter of grit off the edge of the slab, and somebody came out of the fog from the west faster than a person could run.

He came badly. His right boot skidded on the wet stone when he stopped and he nearly went over, and then did not. He stopped from his heels. He stopped with his weight going down into the slab, the way a boy on a sand heap had made Otto stand a hundred times, a pace in front of Jab and the woman, between them and the edge of the fog.

He had one arm in a sling against his chest. On the other fist there was a crooked, ugly spike, thick in the heel, and along the inner curve of it lay a thin line of light, hard and white-grey and cold.

It did not shake.

Toren stood on the dropped slab with his light held out low across the space between the hounds and Jab, the way you hold a lantern across a gate, and did not move.

*Light where you don't want them.*

The near hound came off it like water off a plate. It slid. It went round to the right, away from the white-grey line, and the second one went with it, the way a flock of birds turns without anybody deciding, and Toren moved his light a hand's width to the right, not more, and they came back left, exactly as far, and stopped.

They did not come at him. They had never once come at the light.

And behind them, out of the grey at the edge of the fog, something small and fast came over the lip of the east ditch with a grey blade lying flat and lit along it, like a stove across a room, and the near hound was going one way and the ash of it was going another, and the small shape was already turning.

Two seconds. He counted it afterward. One of them was her getting up over the lip of the ditch.

The second hound came apart where it stood.

Katori stood in the east ditch up to her knees in black water with her grey blade lit, and looked at Jab, and at the woman under his hand, and at Toren in front of them with his light still held out across nothing.

"Where's the medic," she said. It was not a question. It was Katori saying a thing out loud so that somebody would hear it.

"Coming," said Toren. He did not take his eyes off the fog. "Dessa sent her. Wyck asked where the holder was. Nobody had her on the count."

---

She came out of the fog from the south at a run.

She came down the road from the lip, in her oilskin, with the canvas bag with the red cross on the flap banging against her hip and two men from the long building behind her with a stretcher between them, and she came down off the edge of the made road onto the dropped slab without slowing, into the water, and knelt.

She looked at the day holder for about four seconds without touching her. Then she looked at Jab's hand on the woman's chest. Then she looked at Jab's face.

"How long?" she said.

"Nine minutes," said Jab. "Ten. I counted her breaths." His voice was not doing what he told it. "Something's broken inside. Left side. Under the ribs. It's bleeding somewhere it shouldn't. I'm not touching that. I'm only—"

"I know what you're only," said the woman with the good boots. "Off."

He did not move his hand.

"Jab. Off."

"She goes grey," he said. "If I take it off. I tried. She goes grey."

The woman put her own hand flat on the day holder's chest, beside his. Her palm was cold from the run. He felt it arrive.

And then he felt what came out of it.

It was the same. It was warmth, the plainest thing in the world, going down through a palm into a chest. But it was not coming from her. He could feel that. He had felt it once before, in the shed, with his palm on a dish: the difference between a room with somebody asleep in it and an empty room. What was coming out of her hand into the day holder's chest was coming from an empty room. There was nobody in it. It was clean and cold and it belonged to nobody, and it went into the woman under their hands and did exactly what his had done, and the woman with the good boots knelt in the black water with it going out of her and did not go grey, or cold, or anything at all.

She was full. She stayed full.

"I've got her," she said. "Let go."

He could not.

She took his wrist in her other hand, the one with the lump on the outside where a bone had knit wrong, and lifted his hand off the day holder's chest, the way you lift a sleeping child's hand off a blanket.

The day holder did not go grey.

Jab fell sideways onto the wet slab.

---

He did not lose the whole of it. He lost most.

He was lying on his side on tilted stone with black water round his face, and he could see, a long way off, the woman with the good boots kneeling with her hand flat on the day holder's chest, steady, and the two men from the long building unfolding the stretcher, and Toren's boots, and Katori's. He could see the woman's face. It was calm. She was breathing the way you breathe when you are doing a thing you have done a great many times, in and out, even. She was not emptying. He could see she was not emptying.

*That's what I'd have needed*, he thought, very slowly, as if the thought were being carried to him from a long way off by somebody walking. *That. Whatever that is.*

Somebody was saying his name.

He was very cold. The cold was in his hands and feet and wrists and shins and now it was in his chest, under his breastbone, a finger's width behind it, in the place where the bucket was. He went down to look at the bucket. He always went down to look.

There was no bucket.

There was no bottom to find. There was no line scratched on the inside a finger up from the bottom. There was nothing there at all, not dry, not empty, not anything, the way there is nothing where a step should be in the dark.

"Jab," said somebody. Toren. "*Jab.*"

He tried to answer. He found he was not there to do it.

---

The first thing he knew was a bar.

It hung over him on a chain, a short smooth bar like the rail of a gate, and it was swinging a little, and on the end of it there was a hand. A small hand, holding on.

He turned his head.

He was in a bed. It was a white bed in a long room with a window over it, and there was a light in the ceiling that did not flicker, and the room smelled of soap and of something sharper underneath. He knew the room. He had sat in a chair in it for twenty-five days.

He had never been in one of the beds.

In the next bed, the fifth on the right, under its frame, his brother was sitting up.

Tonk had both hands on the bar that hung over his own bed, and he had pulled himself up on it until he was sitting, with his back off the pillow and his shoulders up and his face bright red with effort and outrage, and he was looking over at Jab with an expression Jab had never seen on him.

"You were *asleep*," said Tonk.

Jab looked at him.

"You were asleep for *ages*," said Tonk. "They brought you in on a *bed*. With *wheels*. You were all wet and your mouth was open and you didn't say anything, and I said *Jab*, and you didn't *say* anything." His voice went up. "So I sat up. By *myself*. On the bar. The doctor said a little further every day and I did it all at *once*, because you were *asleep*."

"You sat up," said Jab.

"By *myself*."

"On your own."

"*Yes*," said Tonk, enormously.

Jab lay in the white bed and looked at his brother sitting up, and something went through him that was two things at once, and he did not know which was bigger. The first was so big it filled his chest like water filling a boot. His brother was sitting up. His brother, who had lain flat on his back on boards and blankets and a carrying frame and a padded trough for ten weeks, was sitting up on his own under a bar, looking down at him.

The second was smaller and sharper and went in under the first, like a splinter under a nail. *He did it without me.* The first time. The thing Keel said he would do a little at a time, every day, the thing Jab had thought he would be sitting in the chair for, watching; and it had happened while Jab was lying on a wheeled bed with his mouth open, and he had missed it.

"That's further," he said. His voice did not work well. "That's much further."

"I *know*," said Tonk.

---

There was something round his wrist.

He looked down. It was the long cord. The twelve-foot one, the one from the road, washed and tagged at the hatch and knotted every night since the second day to Tonk's wrist at one end and the bed rail or Jab's wrist at the other. Tonk's end was on Tonk's wrist, where it always was. The other end was not on Tonk's rail.

It was tied to Jab's.

Somebody had tied it with a knot he did not know, clumsy and huge, a knot tied by somebody who had never tied a knot before and had been determined to make it hold.

"The nurse did the knot," said Tonk. "I told her how. She did it wrong. So I told her again." He looked at the cord with great satisfaction. "So you don't go *anywhere*."

On the pillow beside Jab's head, where he had not noticed it, there was a small black wooden fish with one fin broken off.

"Little's minding you," said Tonk.

Jab put his hand up, slowly, because his arm was very heavy and very cold, and touched the fish with one finger.

In the fourth bed, on the other side of Tonk, Senna lay on her back with her hands folded and her eyes on the ceiling.

"He's been sitting up for an hour," she said, to the ceiling. "He said he'd not lie down till you woke. I told him that was a great deal of sitting for a boy with nails in his leg. He told me I wasn't his gran."

"She's *not*," said Tonk.

"I'm not," Senna agreed. "I've got one of my own. He's no better behaved."

---

Rook was in a chair at the foot of the bed.

Jab had not seen him. He was sitting very straight with his back not touching the chair and his forearms across his knees and his hands hanging off the ends of them, and his hands were shaking. They were shaking the way they shook on the north lip every morning after his hour, a fine fast tremor from the wrists to the fingertips, only worse, and he was not hiding them, and his face was the colour of the white bed.

"Is she—" said Jab.

"Alive," said Rook.

Jab shut his eyes.

"Sowerby had her open by the ninth hour," said Rook. "There was a thing in her side that had torn, that nobody needs. He took it out. He says she'll live." His voice was very tired. "He says she'd have been dead before the stretcher got to the lip. Without the nine minutes. He wanted me to tell you that. He says he'll tell you himself, and you won't hear him, because nobody hears the good thing the first time."

"She won't hold," said Jab. It was not a question.

"Not this winter. Maybe not again. Sowerby doesn't know. She doesn't either." Rook looked at his hands. "She's asked for her coat. They won't give it to her."

"The girl. With the wrist."

"Broken. Clean. Keel set it before noon. Keel says she fell right, and the girl's told everybody in the long building that she fell right, and that Wyck Tanner taught her, and Wyck Tanner's been sitting on the step of the shed all afternoon pretending he didn't hear about it." Something moved at the corner of Rook's mouth and did not get any further. "Toren's opened his hand again, the seam across the palm, holding that spike. Keel's stitched it. His arm's no worse, which she says is a miracle, and she doesn't hold with them."

"Callie?"

"At her floor," said Rook. "She stopped."

Jab lay still.

"She stopped," he said.

"The hounds left the pipe when Toren put his light across the mouth of it. She held the hook to her floor, counting, and when she got there she said so, out loud, and Katori came down the rim and said *out*, and she took it out." Rook's hands shook on the ends of his arms. "She's in fourteen with her back against the stove, and a finger of something left, and her mother's hand in hers."

"I didn't stop," said Jab.

"No," said Rook. "You didn't."

---

They did not say anything for a while.

Down the far end of the ward the nurse turned the small lamp lower. It was dark outside the window. Jab did not know what hour it was. He did not know what day. He found he did not want to ask.

"You held it," he said. "All of it. The bottom. When she went."

"I did."

"How long?"

"Thirty-one minutes," said Rook. "Until the fog was off it and the grey ones were down and the crew was up and there was nothing left in the bottom but her blood on a slab. Then I let it go." He looked at his hands. "Sowerby tried to put me in a bed. I said no. He said a word I hadn't heard a doctor say. I've been in this chair since."

"You shouldn't have."

"No," said Rook. "Nor should you."

Jab looked at the bar hanging over his brother's bed, swinging a little on its chain. Tonk had let go of it at last and lain back down, and was lying with his eyes open and the cord on his wrist, listening, the way he listened to everything, to file it.

"You said to ask you again," said Jab. "About what it is. In a while."

"I did."

"You said when I'd seen both halves do something nobody set up."

"I did."

"Is it a while?"

Rook looked at him. He looked at him for a long time from the chair at the foot of the bed, with his shaking hands hanging off the ends of his arms and his face the colour of the sheet.

"It's a while," he said.

---

"It's Sustain," said Rook. "All of it."

Jab did not say anything.

"The lean," said Rook. "That wakes you at the second hour and tells you your brother's worse. The warmth, that went into him at the culvert on the north road and into him in the snow. What you did today on that slab, in the fog, for a woman you'd never spoken to, at forty yards, before you could see her." He did not raise his voice. "You thought they were two things. You told her, in the shed. The lean and the warmth. You said it opened like an ear, not like a hand."

"I said that on the road. To myself."

"She wrote it down. She showed me." Rook's mouth moved. "She writes everything down. It's the best thing about her and it's the worst."

"Is it two things?"

"No," said Rook. "It's one. The ear's the hand. It's the same hand, reaching. The lean is it reaching before you know it's reaching; it goes out ahead of you, and it finds what's worse, and it tells you. The warmth is it arriving. That's all. It goes, and it gets there." He looked at Jab steadily. "Today it went forty yards through fog for somebody you didn't know, and got there, and held her up for nine minutes while her blood went where it shouldn't. That isn't two doors. That's one door, going and arriving. It's always been one. You just never saw it go that far before, because you only ever sent it to one place."

Jab lay with his hand on the fish on his pillow.

"How do you know?" he said.

"Because I've done it," said Rook. "Not like you. Worse than you. With less." He looked at his hands. "On a riverbank, with a girl of fourteen with a broken wrist and a fever that wouldn't come down, four days' walk from anywhere. I put my hand on her four nights running and went down past my floor every night and didn't know I had one. The fifth night I couldn't get up. She kept me breathing with her hands till morning. She was fourteen, and she didn't know how, and she did it anyway." He was quiet for a while. "I never had a word for it either. I had the doing. I've had the doing for half my life. She came back off the river with the word."

Jab thought about a woman with a crooked wrist kneeling in black water, calm, breathing evenly, with warmth going out of her hand that belonged to nobody.

"She didn't empty," he said. "Today. When she put her hand on the holder. I watched. She didn't go cold. She stayed full."

"No," said Rook. "She had something else to spend."

---

"I'd give her the same again," said Jab. "If it was tomorrow."

"I know you would."

"I'm not sorry."

"I didn't ask you to be." Rook leaned forward a little in his chair, and the shaking in his hands got worse, and he let it. "Listen. I'm going to say this once, because she'll say it better tomorrow and you'll want to have heard it from me first. It's not that you should have given her less. You gave her what she needed, and she's alive, and nothing anybody says in a shed takes that off you. It's that you gave her the only thing you had. You gave her you. Because there wasn't anything else in your hands to give." He sat back. "Next time there'll be something else in your hands. That's all she's trying to teach you. Not to care less. To carry more."

Jab lay still for a long time.

Then he went down, under his breastbone, a finger's width behind it, to look at the bucket.

There was nothing there. There was no bucket. There was only a place where a bucket had been, and cold.

"I can't find it," he said. His voice did something. "It's not empty. It's not there. I go down to look and there's nothing to look at."

"I know."

"When does it come back?"

"When it's ready," said Rook.

"What if it doesn't?"

Rook looked at him from the foot of the bed.

"Don't check," he said.

Jab turned his head on the pillow. In the next bed, his brother was lying with his eyes open in the dark under the frame, with the long cord running from his wrist across the gap between the beds to Jab's rail.

"Lie *down*," said Tonk. "I'm doing the sitting. You do the lying."

"I am lying down."

"Lie down *more*," said Tonk, and pulled the cord, once, a small pull.

Jab was already there.
