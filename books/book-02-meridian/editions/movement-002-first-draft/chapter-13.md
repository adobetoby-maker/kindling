# Chapter 13 — Callie: Three Answers

It rained in the night, hard, the way it rained on the plain in the spring, and by first light the bottom of the Sag was a lake.

Callie saw it from the south lip before anybody told her. The black water that usually stood in the joints between the slabs had come up and joined, and lay across the whole of the bottom of the dip in one sheet, flat and brown and still, with the tops of the tilted slabs sticking out of it like the backs of drowned cattle. The east ditch was full to the lip. The white post with the number six on it stood in water to its knees. There was a mist on it, low and grey, not the thick white fog of the mornings before but a thin one, the kind that lifts in rags.

"The pipe's blocked," said Pell. He had the atlas open against his chest to keep the rain off it. "The man at the gate said. It's jammed at the east end. It does it after a big rain, and the water backs up the ditch, and then the slabs shift." He looked at the post. "He said it moved another of them last year. Before anybody got down to it."

Callie looked at the slab by the post. She could not see its corner under the water. She could see that the water round it was a different colour, lighter, the brown of stirred silt.

"It's moving now," she said. "Or it did in the night."

Pell wrote.

---

Rook came round the lip path from the north with the day holder behind him, and it was the first time Callie had ever seen the day holder walk anywhere. She walked like somebody whose feet hurt, stiffly, and when she got to the south lip she did not stop and stand, as she always did. She looked at the water for a long time.

"I can't hold it with that on it," she said. It was the first thing Callie had ever heard her say that was not *morning*. Her voice was low and rough, like a gate that wanted oil. "The line lies on the ground. There's no ground. There's four foot of ditch on it."

"I know," said Rook.

"Somebody's got to get down there and clear the pipe before I can hold it. And nobody's getting down there with nothing held."

"I know," said Rook. "I'll hold it from here. What I can. The pipe mouth's out of the water on the east bank. I can get the ground round that."

The day holder looked at him sideways.

"With what you've got?"

"With what I've got," said Rook. He did not look at her. "And then there's the other thing. The yard's got three children who each think they can do something about a jam. I'd like to find out."

The day holder was quiet for a while. She looked at Callie, and at Toren behind her with his arm in its sling, and at Jab, standing a little apart with his hands in his pockets, and at the weighbridge girl, who had come too, and was looking at the flooded dip with her mouth open because she had never been outside the berm before in her life.

"On your head," she said to Rook, and went to the south lip, and planted her feet in her hollow, and waited.

---

The pipe mouth on the east side was a stone arch as wide as a man's shoulders, set into the bank at the bottom of the ditch. Callie had to go down the bank to see it properly, with Katori beside her and the woman with the good boots behind, and Rook at the lip above them with his hands out from his sides, holding the ground round it. She could feel where he held it. It was like walking onto a floor that somebody had swept.

The jam sat in the arch.

It was brush, mostly: dead branches and grass and a great mat of drowned weed, packed into the mouth of the pipe so tight that it had gone solid. The water behind it came up the ditch knee-deep and leaned on it. Callie could see the lean. The whole mat bulged inward toward the arch, and water came through it in a hundred small spouts at the bottom and went into the dark of the pipe with a sound like somebody hissing through their teeth.

Across the arch, from corner to corner, lay a length of old fence rail, split and grey. It had come down the ditch with everything else and caught across the mouth of the pipe on a slant, one end jammed up in the north corner of the arch and the other down against the south, and everything had piled up behind it.

"Right," said Rook, from the lip. "One problem. Three of you. Each of you tells me how you'd do it, with what you've got. Then you do your bit. Then we swap."

"Swap what?" said the weighbridge girl.

"Problems," said Rook. "Not shapes."

---

"It's the rail," said Jab.

He had come down the bank without being asked and was standing at the edge of the backed-up water, looking at the jam the way Callie had seen her grandfather look at a hopper gate that would not open. He looked at it for a long time. He looked at it, Callie thought, from the bottom up.

"The rail's holding everything," he said. "All that brush is only brush. It's piled on the rail. Cut the rail and it all goes at once." He pointed without moving his hand much. "It's bowed, look. Toward the arch. That's the water pushing on it. It wants to go. It's only holding because both ends are caught. You cut it at the bow and the two halves fold in and the whole lot goes into the pipe." He looked at the water, knee-deep, pressing. "And anybody standing in that ditch goes with it. Up to the knees at least. Into a pipe."

"How do you know?" said the weighbridge girl.

"I opened a jam once that would've taken six people a day," said Jab. "On the river at home. There was a man standing in the wrong place. My father got him out by the collar." He did not say anything else about it.

"Callie," said Rook.

Callie had been reading it the whole time Jab talked. She had been reading it the way she read ground, which was the only way she knew how to read anything: what was pushing, and where, and what it would do if it moved. The water was the weight. The rail was the key. That was Jab's, and he was right.

But there was something else.

"There's a root," she said.

They looked at her.

"There." She pointed with the hook, not touching. At the south end of the rail, where it jammed into the corner of the arch, something dark and knotted came out of the ditch bank and went round the end of it, once, like a rope round a post. "It's grown into the bank. From that tree up on the shoulder. It's got the south end of the rail. If Jab cuts the rail at the bow, the north half goes in and the south half doesn't. It hangs on the root. And then the brush goes half in and half not, and it jams again, lower down, inside the pipe where nobody can get at it." She stopped. "It's got two keys. Not one."

Jab was looking at the root.

"Hm," he said.

Callie did not know what the sound meant. She saw his face do something, though, as he made it, as if the sound had surprised him coming out.

"So how would you do it?" said Rook.

"I'd hold it," said Callie. "First. So nobody's in the way when it goes. I'd put the hook on the rail, over the bow, and put the Ward in the hook." She did not look at anybody. "Then nothing moves the hook. And nothing moves the rail, because it's on the hook. Then Jab can cut the rail and it won't go. It'll hang on me. Everybody gets out of the ditch. Then I let go of the Ward, and the moment I let go I put the edge on the hook and cut the root, and the whole thing goes at once. Clean."

"The moment you let go," said the woman with the good boots.

"The moment I let go."

"Can you do that?"

Callie looked at the jam.

"I don't know," she said. "I've never done it. It's on me or it's in the hook. It can't be both. And when I've got the Ward in the hook I've got nothing on me, and when I take it out there's—" She stopped.

"There's what?"

"There's a gap," said Callie. "Before the edge comes. There always is. I've never counted it." She looked at her hands. "I'll count it."

---

"Toren," said Rook.

Toren was standing on the bank above the backed-up water with his arm in the sling, looking up the ditch, not at the jam at all. The ditch went north from the pipe mouth for a long way, full to the lip, and all along it, in the brown water, there were things floating: branches, a broken crate, more weed, a dead crow.

"When it goes," said Toren, "all of that comes down the ditch after it."

They looked.

"The water drops, and everything that's floating up there comes down fast, and it goes into the pipe, and half of it's too big." He pointed with his chin at a branch as long as a man, turning slowly in the water forty feet up. "That goes in sideways, and you've got a new jam. Inside the pipe. Where nobody can get at it. Callie's thing, but worse." He looked at Rook. "Somebody's got to clear the ditch first. Get the big stuff out before it goes. That's mine."

"With what?"

Toren thought about it. Callie watched him think. She saw his eyes go to Jab's hands, where the fish-knife would come, and then to his own right hand, and she saw the thing he was about to do before he did it.

He let the spike come. It came crooked and ugly. And then he tried to make it better, for cutting branches; she saw it lean toward the shape of Jab's knife, a little cleaner, a little more meant, and it guttered like a lamp in a draught and went out.

He stood on the bank with his fist empty.

"Don't," said Jab, without looking round.

"I know," said Toren. "I know. I did it again." He let the spike come a second time, and this time he left it alone: crooked, and thick in the heel, and ugly. He looked at it. He turned his fist over, slowly, and looked at the heel of the spike, the bad heavy knob at the root of it that had never been any use for anything.

"That's a hook," he said.

It was. Turned over, the heel of the spike stuck out from the blade at an angle, blunt and hooked, the way the back of a boathook does.

"I'll hook them out," said Toren. "I don't have to cut anything. I only have to get them to the bank."

---

He did it with the struts.

He could not reach the big branch from the bank. It was forty feet up the ditch in the middle of the water. So he went along the bank at a run, with the struts up the outside of his shins, left one late, and at the place above the branch he stopped. He stopped a pace and a half short of the edge, badly, and Callie saw his right boot skid on the wet grass the way it had on the whitewash, and her stomach went. But he stopped. He stood there on the edge of the bank with his weight in his heels, the way Wyck made Otto stand, and reached down with the heel of his ugly spike and caught the branch by a fork and dragged it to the bank.

He did it eleven times. He cleared the ditch for sixty feet up from the pipe. Twice he had to light the spike to cut something that was caught on something else, and the light came along the crooked edge, thin and white-grey, and shook, and held for three breaths, and cut. The second time it did not shake as much.

The last time he stopped at the edge he fell over. He fell like a sack, and rolled, and got up with grass all down his good side, and nobody said anything, and on top of the sand heap four hundred yards away Callie knew that Wyck would have said *better*.

---

Then it was hers.

She bolted the hook onto its pipe, so that it was the long reach, four feet of conduit and a foot of curve, and went down the bank to the edge of the backed-up water. The woman with the good boots came down behind her. Katori stood on the bank above with her hands at her sides. Rook stood at the lip with his hands out, holding the ground round the arch.

Callie reached out over the water and put the curve of the hook over the rail, at the bow, where it bent toward the arch.

Then she did the thing she had done on the hill with the wagon.

She did not grip. Her mother had said: *your grandfather never once gripped a sack. You don't grip a sack. You get under it.* She got under it. She let the hook take the rail, and let herself stand behind the hook with her feet in the ground, and felt the Ward come up out of the ground through her heels, the way it always came, *down*, deeper than a girl goes, and then she did not let it close over her. She let it go past her. Into the steel.

The hook went pale.

She had seen it do that three times in her life. It went pale like frost on iron, from the curve back along the pipe to her hands, and then it stopped being a hook she was holding and became a thing that was simply there. Nothing could move it. The water leaned on the jam, and the jam leaned on the rail, and the rail leaned on the hook, and the hook did not move, and so everything else stopped moving round it.

There was nothing on her at all. She stood at the edge of the black water in her coat with nothing between her and anything, and her hands on a pipe.

"Jab," said Rook.

---

Jab went down into the ditch.

He went in up to his knees, which was where the water was, and the cold of it made him say a word Callie had heard the nurse say. He waded out along the face of the jam to the bow of the rail, where her hook was, and he let the knife come on his fist.

She watched him light it.

The light came out of the grip end, thin and amber, and she saw his eyes go to the little hook at the end of the blade and stay there. The light ran along the edge. It came to the fat of the belly, and it went through it, with only a flicker, and on to the point.

Then it went out along most of the blade, and stayed lit only at the hook. Only the last inch, curled at the end of the knife like a bent finger, glowing.

"You didn't light the belly," said the woman with the good boots, behind Callie.

"I don't need the belly," said Jab. "It's one rail. It's old. It only wants the tip."

He put the lit hook of the knife against the rail beside Callie's hook, at the bow, and drew it through. It went through the old grey wood like a hot wire through wax, in one pull, and the rail parted.

Nothing moved.

The two halves of the rail hung where they had been, one either side of the cut, held by the hook. The jam leaned on them. The water leaned on the jam. Callie felt it all come onto the hook, and the hook did not move. It did not even think about moving. She stood behind it with nothing on her and her hands on the pipe and felt the whole weight of a flooded ditch go past her, through the steel, down into the ground.

"Out," said Rook. "Everybody out of the ditch."

Jab waded back to the bank and came up it. Toren was already up. Katori had not been down. The woman with the good boots went up the bank behind Callie and stood at the top.

"Callie," said Rook. "When you're ready. Not before."

---

She read it one more time. The north half of the rail, hanging on her hook, with the bulk of the jam piled on it. The south half, hanging on her hook too, and on the root. The root, dark and knotted, going once round the end of it.

The gap. She had never counted it.

She took the Ward out of the hook.

She did it the way you take your hand off a thing that is balanced: all at once. The pale went out of the steel. And in the same instant, before the pale was gone, she reached for the edge, the long grey sweep that came off the curve when she leaned—*you lean and it comes*, Katori had said, *finish the pull*—and she leaned.

It did not come.

It did not come for a count of one. She felt the whole jam lurch as the Ward left the hook, felt the weight of it take the hook and pull, felt the pipe try to go out through her hands toward the arch, and her ribs, which were healing, told her about it in a voice she could not ignore.

Two.

She held on. She did not grip. She got under it.

Three.

The water was going. She could hear it change, the hiss becoming a roar as the north half of the rail folded in and the brush began to go into the dark of the pipe.

Four.

The edge came.

It came off the curve of the hook the way it always came, a grey shiver like breath on a window and then the long ugly sweep of it, the colour of nothing at all, cold the way the inside of a well is cold. She put it through the root. It went through like a wire through soft cheese. The south half of the rail swung free, and the whole jam went into the arch at once, rail and brush and weed and the drowned crow, and the black water went after it with a noise like a crowd shouting, and the ditch dropped.

It dropped a hand's width in the time it took to breathe in, and another, and the tops of the slabs at the bottom of the Sag began to come up out of the lake like drowned cattle standing up.

Callie let the edge go. She stood on the bank with the hook in her hands and her ribs on fire and the water going away from her down the pipe.

"Four," she said. "It was four."

"It went on three," said the woman with the good boots, behind her. "The jam. It went on three. You cut on four."

"I know."

"If somebody had been in that ditch—"

"I know," said Callie.

---

They did it again, the other way round. Rook made them.

He had Wyck's design for it, which Otto had carried out that morning in a sack: two stakes and a length of rail and a weighted rope. They drove the stakes into the bottom of the east ditch where the water had gone down, and wedged the rail between them, and hung the weight off the rail on the rope so that it leaned the way the jam had leaned. It was not a jam. It was a thing that behaved like one.

"Swap," said Rook. "Jab. You're holding it. Toren's cutting. Callie's clearing."

Jab could not put a Ward in anything.

He stood in front of the rail and looked at it for a long time with his hands in his pockets. Then he went back up the bank to the pile of brush Toren had dragged out, and came back with a length of green withy, and lashed the rail to the nearer stake with it. He did it fast, and tight, and he cut the ends short with the edge of his dark knife, and Callie heard him say under his breath, "*Tight and short. Tight and short.*" When he was finished the rail would not move.

"That's not a Ward," said the weighbridge girl.

"No," said Jab. "It's a knot. It's slower." He looked at the lashing. "My mother taught me. She said I didn't cut my ends short enough."

Toren cut the rail. He cut it with the spike, lit, and it took him three strokes, because the light shook on the second one and went dull, and he had to wait for it and ask again. On the third stroke it held.

Callie cleared the approach.

She did not do it the way Toren had. She could not run along a bank and hook things out. She stood at the top of the ditch and read it instead. The water was running again now, low and fast, and there were things in it coming down from further up, and she watched where they went. Husks went the way water went. So did everything else. She watched a branch come down and go against the east bank at a bend thirty feet up and turn and come off it and go straight into the middle of the channel.

So she went to the bend, and put the hook into the bank there, bolt in, and leaned on it until a lump of turf came away and went into the water, and the bend was not a bend any more. The next branch that came down did not turn. It went straight along the east side and past the stakes and into the pipe mouth lengthwise, the way you would feed a log into a stove.

"You didn't clear it," said Toren.

"No," said Callie. "I moved where it goes."

Rook, at the lip, put his hands down. The ground round the pipe mouth came back to being ground. Callie felt it come back under her feet like a floor that has been swept and then walked on again.

"Now you know what the other two are for," said Rook.

---

They did the handoff for the rest of the morning, on the stakes, with the weight on the rope. Callie put the Ward in the hook and took it out and reached for the edge, over and over, and counted.

Four. Four. Three. Four. Three.

"You're reaching," said Katori, from the bank. It was the first thing she had said all morning. "When you take the Ward out, you reach for the edge. You're asking it to come from somewhere else. It's not somewhere else. It's in the same steel."

"It's on me or it's in the hook."

"The Ward is. The edge isn't. The edge is always in the hook. It's the hook's." Katori did not raise her voice. "Don't take the Ward out and then ask for the edge. Lean *while* you take it out. Let one go as the other comes. Like changing hands on a rope."

Callie thought about it. She thought about her grandfather at the hopper gate, changing hands on a rope with a sack on the end of it, never letting go of one until the other had it.

Three. Three. Two.

"Two," said Callie.

She tried to make the edge shorter, the last time. The weighbridge girl had cut a branch clean in half that morning with her straight grey blade, and Callie had watched it, and the old wanting had come up in her like water in a well. She tried to make her own come out straight.

It guttered and went out.

"Keep the ugly shape," said Katori.

"I know," said Callie. "I kept it." She let it come again, the long grey curve, and then she did something she had not tried before. She did not make it straight. She made it short. She let it come out off the curve of the hook for a foot, not four, and stopped it there, and it stayed. A foot of grey curve, the colour of nothing, following the line of the steel.

It was cheaper. She could feel that it was cheaper. It came out of the well like a cup instead of a bucket.

"Mm," said Katori.

---

The next morning, in the shed, the woman with the good boots weighed out ash on the brass scale.

She did it with a small horn spoon, tipping grey powder from a jar into the little pan until the beam came level, and then tipping the pan out onto a square of paper. There were five squares of paper on the table in a row. On each there was a small grey heap that Callie could have hidden under her thumb.

"A tenth of a Handful," said the woman. "Each. Weighed. Clean Flask off the east track, the day before yesterday, which Callie called thin. It is thin. That's fine. You're not going to do anything with it." She stood back. "Three. Take in a little that you've measured. Put your palm on it, flat, like lifting. Don't haul. The only difference is that this time you don't hold it high. You let it go down."

"Down where?" said the weighbridge girl.

"You'll find out." The woman looked at them. "And then four. Let it settle. Don't touch it. It'll want to go somewhere. Don't let it. That's the whole of today."

Callie put her palm flat on the paper.

She knew this part. She had done it on the plain for two years, and on the east track for ten days. The ash came up into the heel of her hand, cold and heavy and slow, like water finding its way into a boot. It did not want anything. There was nobody home.

Then she did not hold it high.

She let it go down, the way Rook had told her never to on the track, and it went. It went down through her wrist and her arm and into her chest, cold, and further, and she felt it go down into the well. She felt it arrive there. It was like dropping a small stone into the bottom of a bucket that already had two fingers of water in it. It went through the water and lay on the bottom.

She wanted to count it.

She wanted to know how much. A tenth, the woman had said, and Callie wanted to go down and look and see whether the well had come up by a tenth, and whether a tenth was a hair or a finger, and how long it would last, and what it would buy.

She did not count it. She had done this before, with a harder thing, lying on a floor beside a stove listening to her mother breathe. She let it lie there.

And it settled. She felt it settle. The cold went out of it, slowly, the way cold goes out of a stone you have brought in from the yard and set by the fire, and after a while it was not a stone in the bottom of the well any more. It was only the well, a little deeper than it had been.

"Good," said the woman, who had been watching her face. "That's settled."

---

Jab's did not settle.

Callie saw it happen. He put his palm flat on the paper, and she saw the grey go up into his hand, and she saw the woman with the good boots put two fingers on the inside of his other wrist, to take his pulse. And she saw his face change, a little, a small surprised look, as if somebody had walked through a room he thought was empty.

"Where did it go?" said the woman.

Jab looked at her.

"To you," he said.

"Yes." She did not take her fingers off his wrist. "I felt it arrive. A tenth of a Handful, through your palm and out of your wrist and into my fingers in about the time it takes to swallow." She let go and wrote in her book. "You don't hold. You pass. That's your door. It's the first thing it does with anything it gets." She looked up. "You'll have to learn to hold before you pass. Or you'll pass everything you get, including the part of you that you need to get home."

Jab did not answer. Callie saw him look at his own palm.

Toren hauled.

She saw that too. He put his palm on the paper and she saw him lean on it, the way he leaned on a door, and the grey went up into his hand fast, too fast, all at once like water up a straw. And then he went grey. Not the ash; him. His face went the colour of the paper, and a sweat came out on his lip, and he took his hand off the table and held it against his chest.

When he opened it there was a grey dust on his palm again, fine, like flour. It had come back out.

"That's spill," said the woman. "You took it faster than you could settle it. It isn't in you. It's on you." She watched his face. "You'll feel full for about a minute. You're not. That's false fullness. It's the worst lie there is, because it's the one your own body tells you." She pointed at the door. "Outside, if you're going to be sick. Not in my shed."

Toren went outside. Callie heard him behind the shed, and then she heard Wyck's voice from the top of the sand heap, flat: "That'll be the ash, then."

The weighbridge girl had not put her hand on the paper at all.

"I don't want to," she said. She was very white. "I've been on the weighbridge a year and I've never touched it. The old man won't let me. He says it's for people with somewhere to put it."

"You've got somewhere to put it," said the woman. "You're on the register."

"I know I am. I don't want to."

The woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she took the horn spoon and tipped half the little heap off the girl's paper back into the jar.

"Half a tenth," she said. "Or none. It's yours to say."

The girl looked at the smaller heap. Then she put her palm on it, very slowly, flat, the way she had watched Callie do it, and her eyes filled up, and she did not take her hand away.

---

"On the road there's no settling," said Katori.

She was standing in the shed doorway with her cap pulled down. She had not come in, and she had not put her hand out, and she had watched all of it.

"You drink it, or you take it in, and you spend it, and you hope." She said it in her flat way. "There's no paper and no scale. There's a thing coming at you."

"On the road you die," said the woman with the good boots.

"I emptied myself once," said Katori. "Nine days."

"And were you any use to anybody," said the woman, "for nine days?"

"I was alive."

"That's the bottom," said the woman. "Alive and no use. I'm trying to teach them where the floor is. It's above that."

Katori looked at her from the doorway.

"Sometimes you spend past the line on purpose," she said. "Sometimes the thing in front of you is worth nine days. You don't know that in a shed."

"No," said the woman. "You don't. So you'd better know exactly where the line was. Then when you go through it on purpose, you know what you paid."

Neither of them said anything more. Callie stood between them at the table with a tenth of a Handful settled at the bottom of her well, and thought about her mother lying by the stove with her hand out, palm up, on the blanket. *Something left.* She thought Katori and the woman with the good boots were both right, and that she did not know yet what to do with that.

The woman was looking at Katori.

She had been looking at her, Callie realised, for a while. Not the way you look at somebody you are arguing with. She was looking at her face, at the line of the jaw under the cap and the way she stood, as if she were reading something in it that she had read once before, a long time ago, somewhere else.

Then she looked past Katori, out of the door, to the step of the shed, where Rook was sitting.

Rook looked back at her. He did not say anything. Callie saw him move his head, very slightly, down and up again, once.

The woman looked away.

Callie did not say anything either. She knew whose sister Katori was. She had sat behind a hut in the dark and been told. She did not know who the woman with the good boots was, or what she had been to anybody, or what she had seen in Katori's face. But she knew the look of somebody who has seen a thing they cannot say, because she had worn it herself on a bench outside the long building, and she knew it was not hers to ask about.

---

"Four," said the woman with the good boots, that afternoon. "Callie. Stand in it."

Callie stood in the first lane with her feet planted, and the Ward came up out of the ground through her heels and closed over her from her boots to her crown, a skin's thickness off her coat, like old glass.

"Now walk."

She lifted her back foot.

It went off her like water off a tipped plate. She stood in the lane with nothing on her.

"It always does that," she said.

"You walk like somebody with nothing on their back," said Wyck, from the heap.

They looked at him.

"You lift the back foot and the weight goes up with it," said Wyck. "Everybody does. You're walking like a person. Walk like you've got a sack on." He shifted the bar on his knees. "A full one. You don't lift a foot with a sack on. You slide the front one, and you put the weight on it, heel first, all of it, till the back one's got nothing in it. Then you bring the back one up. It's never got any weight in it when it leaves the ground. That's how you carry a sack across a yard all day."

Callie looked at him for a long moment.

*Stand a sack of meal on your back foot all day*, her grandfather had said. *You can feel it in your heels.*

She called the Ward again. It came up through her heels and closed over her.

She did not lift her back foot. She slid her front one, a hand's width, along the cinders, and put her weight on it, heel first, slowly, all of it, until she could feel the whole of her standing on that one heel and the back foot had nothing in it at all. Then she brought the back foot up.

The Ward stayed on.

She did it again. She slid, and loaded, and brought the back foot up with nothing in it, and the Ward stayed on her like a coat.

On the third step her weight wobbled, and the back foot came up with something still in it, and the Ward went off her.

"Two," said Callie.

"Two what?" said the weighbridge girl.

"Two steps," said Callie. She stood in the lane and looked at the cinders, at the two marks her front foot had slid, a hand's width each. "It walked two steps."

The woman with the good boots wrote it down.

---

"Now another person," said the woman.

"Me," said Wyck.

He was already getting down off the heap. He did it carefully, the way Keel had made him promise to do everything now, with his strapped arm held in against his chest. He came and stood in front of Callie in the first lane with his feet set, and held out his good hand.

"I don't move," he said. "You'll want somebody who doesn't move."

Callie took his wrist.

She did not know how to do it. She had never done it. She stood with her feet planted and her hand on Wyck's wrist and called the Ward, and it came up through her heels and closed over her, and stopped at her hand, where her hand stopped. It did not go onto him. It knew where she ended.

"It's yours," said the woman. "It fits you. It's always fitted you. It's never been asked to fit anything else."

"How do I ask it?"

"I don't know," said the woman. "Nobody's ever done this with a Ward like yours. I know how a wall's widened. You're not a wall." She was watching Callie's face. "What does it come up through?"

"My feet."

"Where's he standing?"

Callie looked down. Wyck's boots were planted on the cinders a stride from hers, heels in, the way he always stood. On the same ground. On the same cinders, over the same packed dirt, that went down under both of them to the same place.

She read it.

She did not read Wyck; she read the ground, the way she always did, and she found where his weight went into it. It went down into the cinders under his heels the way her own did, solid and set, like a post. It was not moving. It was not going anywhere. And she let the Ward, which came up out of the ground through her heels, come up through his as well.

It went over him.

She felt it go. It was like a coat that had fitted her all her life being pulled over two people. It went over Wyck from his boots to his crown, a skin's width off his strapping, and she saw the shine come on him like old glass, and she felt it thin. Not break. Thin, everywhere at once, the way a sheet thins when you stretch it over a bigger bed.

"Otto," said the woman.

Otto put both hands on Wyck's good shoulder and shoved.

Nothing moved. Not Wyck, and not Callie, who was holding his wrist. Otto shoved harder, going red, and his boots slid on the cinders, and neither of them moved an inch.

Callie was counting.

It was going out of her so fast. It was not like standing in the Ward alone; it was like having two buckets on the rope and both of them full. She could feel the well going down. She could feel the stone of ash she had settled that morning go, and the water above it, and she counted, and at eleven she felt the floor, the place a little way above the bottom where she would not be able to get up again.

She let go of it.

The shine went off both of them at once. Otto, still shoving, fell over.

"Eleven," said Callie.

She said it and then she found that there was something warm on her lip, and she put her hand up, and it came away red. A thin line of blood was running out of her nose. Her ribs ached all down the left side, deep, the way they had on the hill.

"Thirty-eight, alone," said the woman, writing. "Yesterday. Eleven with one other person. Standing still." She looked up. "Two people isn't twice. It's more than three times. Sit down, Callie."

"I want to try Toren."

"Sit down first."

---

She tried Toren after she had sat on the cinders for a while with her head back and her sleeve against her nose.

He came and stood in front of her in the lane and held out his good hand, and she took his wrist and planted her feet and called what was left above her floor, which was not much. She read the ground for his weight, and found it.

It was not like Wyck's. It was not set. It was in his heels and then it was not; it was in his toes, and then his heels, and then somewhere in the outside of his shins, a little lift and settle, over and over, like a horse that cannot stand still in a stall. His legs were ready to go. They were always ready to go. The struts were not up, but the thing that brought them up was there under his weight all the time, waiting, restless.

The Ward went over him and came off him in the same instant. It came off her too, all of it, like water off a tipped plate.

"It came off both of us," she said.

Toren looked down at his own legs.

"I didn't move," he said.

"You did," said Callie. "You didn't lift your feet. You moved." She let go of his wrist. "Everybody inside it has to stand like me. If they don't, it comes off. All of it. Me as well."

"Write that," said Senna from her chair by the stove pipe. "Just like that. *Everybody inside it has to stand like me.*"

Dessa wrote it.

---

She could not lift that afternoon.

She knew she could not, and so did Katori. The crew went out to the Sag at the fourth hour for the afternoon drift, which the night crew had called a Handful and a half, and Callie stayed in fourteen with her back against the stove and nothing in the well but the floor. She had kept the floor. She had kept it on the cinders with the blood running out of her nose and her mother's rule in her head. There was a finger of dark water above it, cold and clear and hers.

It meant she would not be on the board tonight. There would be Katori's name with the afternoon's weight against it, and Ines's, and Otto's, and not hers, and the sheet at the review in three weeks' time would have one less day on it.

She sat with that for a while. Then she got up, because her mother was at the weighbridge.

---

Her mother had been the tally for three days.

The old man in the leather apron had watched her read the beam for one morning without saying anything at all, and at the end of it he had written something on the housing woman's paper and taken the tally board off the nail and hung it round her mother's neck himself. She sat in the hut window on a high stool with her stick propped against the wall and the board on her knee, and when the crews came up onto the plate with their jars she watched the beam, and said the number, and wrote it with her left hand in big careful figures, and read it back.

"Thistle," she said, when Callie came round the side of the hut.

Callie stopped.

"Thistle," said her mother again. She was pleased. Her whole face was pleased. "I lost it on the scarp. I've been saying *the prickly one* since the scarp, and this morning I woke up and it was there, as if it had been in my pocket the whole time. Thistle. Thistle Ford." She laughed. "I've been saying the name of my own town for a month and calling half of it *the prickly one*."

"That's good," said Callie. "That's—"

"Don't do sums on my face, love."

"I'm not."

"You are. I can see you." Her mother looked out of the window at the plate. A yard lifter was coming up onto it, a heavy man Callie did not know, with a jar under his arm. He set it down. The old man moved the weight along the beam. The beam came up.

"There's water in that," said her mother.

The old man looked at her.

"The beam came up too fast," said her mother. "Wet ash comes up fast. It's heavy for what it is. There's a cup of ditch in the bottom of that jar." She did not raise her voice. "Tip it and see."

The old man tipped it. There was a cup of ditch in the bottom of it. The heavy man went red and said something about the rain, and the old man said nothing at all, and wrote something in his own book, and her mother wrote *Handful, less water* on the tally board in her big careful figures and read it back.

---

Later, at the end of the afternoon, when the plate was empty and the old man had gone to put the test weights on for the last time, her mother sat on the stool with the board on her knee and looked at the wall where they were kept.

"Put the—" she said.

She stopped. Callie watched her mouth move on nothing.

"The iron things," said her mother. "For checking the—the beam. The ones he puts on. With the number in the face." She shut her eyes. "I know what they're called. I've known what they're called since I was four years old. My father let me carry the little one."

"Test weights," said Callie.

"Test weights." Her mother opened her eyes. "Test weights. Yes. Thank you." She did not say it as if she were grateful. She said it the way you say thank you to somebody who has picked up a thing you dropped and handed it back to you, when you would rather they had not seen you drop it.

She put the pencil down on the ledge. It rolled. Her right hand went out after it, and did not close, and the pencil went off the ledge and onto the floor.

Neither of them said anything.

When the old man came back her mother stood up to go, and on the step down from the hut door her right leg went, the way it went now, as if a string had been cut. The old man had her elbow before Callie did. He did not hold it. He only had it, lightly, under his hand, the way the gate nurse had had it on the first morning, and her mother stood on the step with her stick in her left hand and his hand under her right elbow until the leg came back.

"Bad afternoon," she said.

"Bad afternoon," the old man agreed. It was the most Callie had ever heard him say.

"I'll be in at the eighth hour," said her mother.

"I'll be here," said the old man.

---

In the morning, at the eighth hour, in the long building, Doctor Sowerby asked Callie how the day had been.

Callie had lain awake doing the sum. She had lain on the floor by the stove and put a word found against a word lost, and a bad afternoon against three good mornings, and a leg that went on a step against a leg that had gone on a stair a week ago, and she had got as far as the second step of it, the one where you work out what it means, and stopped herself. It had taken a long time to stop. She had lain with her eyes open until she was sure.

"She found a word she'd lost," said Callie. "Thistle. In the morning. She'd lost it since the scarp. In the afternoon she lost one she's always had. Test weights. It took her about four seconds, and then I said it. Her right hand dropped a pencil. Her leg went on the step of the weighbridge hut. It came back in about half a minute. She finished her shift before that." She stopped. "No fits."

Sowerby wrote it all down.

"Anything else?"

Callie looked at her mother, sitting on the edge of the bed in the long room with her sleeve rolled up and her hat on the chair.

"No," she said. "That's all of it."

Sowerby looked up at her. He looked at her for longer than he usually looked at anybody, which was not long.

"That's exactly what I need," he said. "Thank you. That's exactly the right amount."

Her mother, on the edge of the bed, said nothing. But she put out her left hand, palm up, on the blanket beside her, and Callie went and put her own hand in it.

"Something left?" said her mother.

"Something left," said Callie.

---

At the ninth hour she sat on the bench against the wall at the bottom of the slope, with the hook across her knees, and counted.

Toren had brought Senna down early, in the chair, the way the grey-haired man had asked. Senna sat at the yellow line with a blanket over her knees and her hands in her lap where she could see them. She could see them. She could not feel them at all now, she said, except as weight, and she had started on the left foot as well as the right, and when the nurse had helped her from the bed to the chair that morning she had had to be told where the floor was.

"It's a very unreliable floor in this building," she said. "It's never where I left it."

The grey-haired man warmed the machine up four times. Then five. Then six.

Callie counted.

"Ten," she said. "Eleven. Ten. Ten." A gap. "Eleven. Ten. Ten. Ten."

Toren was standing by the chair with his hand on the handle. He did not look at her. She did not look at him. She went on counting, low, flat, the way she had for Jab, because the grey-haired man had asked her to and because it was the one kind of counting that was for somebody else.

"It's worse," she said, when it stopped. "Than the sixteenth day. It's ten more than it's eleven now. Before, it was eleven more than it was ten."

The grey-haired man came to the door and looked at her. He had heard. He had ink all up the side of his hand and his eyes were red to the rims.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

---

Senna was in there an hour and three quarters.

When she came out on the rolling bed she was grey and small, and she opened her eyes at the yellow line and found Toren.

"It sang flat," she said. "Twice. Somebody should know that."

"I know," said Toren.

"He looked at his screen for longer than last time before he said yes."

The grey-haired man had come out behind the bed. Doctor Sowerby had come down the slope, not running this time, and stood with him on the far side of the yellow line, and the two of them talked in low voices as if the corridor had nobody in it, because there was no curtain in the building worth the name, and they had stopped pretending otherwise.

"How long?" said Sowerby.

"Four weeks. Five." The grey-haired man rubbed his hand. "By the end of next month I won't trust it to measure her dose. I'll be able to run it. I won't know if what it's telling me is true."

"And then?"

"And then I stop," said the grey-haired man, "or I guess."

Callie did the sum. She did not decide to. Four weeks, five. A cycle every eleven days. The twenty-first day, today. The thirty-second. The forty-third. The fifty-fourth, if it was five weeks and a bit.

Three. Three cycles, maybe. Then a guess.

She stopped. She made herself stop at the second step, the way she had on the floor by the stove, and she sat on the bench with her hands on the hook and did not go on to the next one.

Toren had not moved. He stood at the handle of the chair with his face doing nothing at all, which was how Callie knew.

Senna, on the rolling bed, turned her head and looked up at him for a long time with her pale eyes.

"Tonight, Tor," she said. "Bring the book."
