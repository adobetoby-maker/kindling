# Chapter 11 — Jab: The Floor

The review room was on the first floor, at the top of fourteen stairs, and Jab went up them alone.

Otto had offered to come. He had stood on the step of twelve at the eighth hour with his hands in his pockets, going red, and said he could sit on the stair outside and not say anything, if that was a help. Jab had said it was not. Then he had seen Otto's face and said it was, a bit, and Otto had sat on the bottom stair with his big hands between his knees, not saying anything, and Jab had gone up past him.

It was the same room Callie had told him about on the step last night: a long table, three chairs behind it, two in front. The man in the long coat on the left. A woman with a red face and a ledger on the right. In the middle, an old man with a white moustache and pale eyes, looking at the window.

Jab sat in one of the two chairs. The other stayed empty. He looked at it once, and then did not look at it again.

"Household review," said the woman with the ledger. "Yard C, second row, hut twelve. Conditional admittance, fourteen days, reviewable." She read down the page with her finger. "Household, two. Head of household, the brother, seventeen. The other member a clinical admission, aged four." She turned the page. "On the sheet, a Flask of hound ash, lifted the night of admission, less station. A red thread. A declaration of capability, referred." She stopped, and frowned, and read the next line to herself before she read it out. "A request from the Director's office for a medical assessment, on terms, the terms attached. And a four-season contract, offered on the third day, for guard work on the Sag line." She looked up. "Withdrawn."

"I'd like it written that I said no," said Jab.

The woman looked at him.

"It's withdrawn, sir. You don't need to—"

"I know it's withdrawn. Somebody withdrew it yesterday, in a room upstairs, without me." He kept his hands flat on his knees. "A man told me on a step that I could say no. He said nobody here says it out loud, because everybody's busy being reasonable. I'd like it on the sheet that I said it. Not that it went away."

The woman looked at the man in the long coat.

"Write it," said the man in the long coat.

She wrote it. It took her a moment, because she had to find a place on the page where it would go, and there did not seem to be one. In the end she wrote it in the margin, small.

---

The old man with the moustache turned from the window.

"How old's the boy?" he said. His voice was thin and dry, like paper being folded.

"Four."

"And you carried him how far?"

"Four hundred miles," said Jab. "About. I counted it in rests, not in miles. It was eighteen days."

The old man looked at him for a long time with his pale eyes.

"Hm," he said.

It went into Jab like a hook. It was the sound his father had made. It was exactly the sound, a short low note in the nose, the one his father made at a rapid he had not decided about yet, or a knot somebody else had tied, or a boy standing in front of him with a question. It meant *I've heard you and I'm not finished*. Nobody had made it at Jab in two months. He had not known how much he had been waiting for somebody to.

He found he had to look at the table for a while.

"Twenty-eight days," said the woman with the ledger, writing. "Conditional residence extended, reviewable. The board notes the Flask. The board notes the Director's request and the terms attached." She paused. "And the no."

"Thank you," said Jab.

On the bottom stair, Otto stood up so fast he nearly fell over.

"Twenty-eight," said Jab.

Otto thought about it. His lips moved.

"That's the forty-second day," he said.

"It is," said Jab, and found, for the first time in several days, that something in him was nearly laughing. "You're getting as bad as the rest of us."

---

The east shed leaked at the north end.

There was a bucket under the drip. Jab saw it as soon as he came in out of the afternoon, standing by itself on the concrete in the half-dark at the far end, with a small grey circle of water in the bottom of it and a drop coming down every few seconds from a seam in the tin roof. *Tick*, it went, into the water. He watched it for a moment longer than he meant to.

The rest of the shed was long and low and cold, with a stove at the near end that somebody had lit an hour ago and that had not yet decided to be warm. There was a cot against one wall with a grey blanket folded on it. There was a table with a hand scale on it, brass, with little pans, and a row of stoppered jars, and a small brass-framed glass with sand in it, and a book open at a clean page.

The woman with the good boots was sitting on the edge of the table. She had taken her oilskin off. Under it she wore the grey of the long building, with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, and on the outside of her left wrist there was the lump where a bone had knit wrong.

Rook was sitting on the step outside the door.

"You can hear from there," the woman had said to him, when they came across the yard together. "You can't come in."

"It's my—"

"It's not yours. It's his." She had not raised her voice. "Sit on the step, Elias. If I do anything you don't like, you can say so through the door. You've got a loud enough voice for it. I've heard it."

Rook had sat on the step.

Inside there were three other people. Otto, standing by the stove, looking at everything with great care, as if he might be asked about it afterward. Dessa, sitting on the cot with her bad leg out straight. And Wyck, leaning against the wall by the door with his strapped arm across his chest and his bar in his left hand, looking at nobody.

"I asked for three people who wouldn't lie to me," said the woman. "Elias sent these. I've no idea yet whether he was right." She looked at Jab. "Sit down. Tell me what it is. Not what you think it is. What it does."

---

He told her.

He had told it once on a card and once to a man on a step. He had never told it out loud to somebody who wrote it down while he talked, and he found it came out differently. It came out in order.

The lean. How it lived under his breastbone, a finger's width behind it, and leaned, neither up nor down, like a floor in an old house. How it leaned toward his brother when his brother was worse. How he had first felt it on the road, three nights running, with his hand on Tonk. How it was right four times in five, and when it was wrong it was wrong in the direction he wanted.

"So you don't ask it," she said, writing, "when you want an answer."

"No."

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I made it a rule." He looked at his hands. "It was wrong the second time I asked it. I'd wanted it to say he was better."

She wrote.

The warmth. He told the snow shelter: four stones in the fire, and the four stones packed round his brother's back and feet, and pushing at the thing, pushing like a man leaning on a door, and nothing; and then stopping, and it coming. He told the culvert on the north road. He told the tenth day in the ward, sitting with his hand on his brother's chest while the pin went red, and nothing coming at all.

"It doesn't come when I call it," he said. "It never has."

"And the bucket."

He looked up.

"You said it went out of you *like a bucket going dry*," she said. "On your card. The back of it. I've read it four times." She turned the pages of her book back to one that was already written on, and he saw his own words copied there in her small square hand. "Tell me about the bucket."

So he told her that too. The bottom he went down to at night to look at, the way you go back to a snare you know is empty. Dry at the gate. A little in it by the tenth day. Something in it now.

"How much?"

"Some."

"How much is some?"

He thought about it. "A hand," he said. "About a hand deep. If the bucket were a bucket."

"Good," said the woman, and wrote *a hand*, and underlined it. "That's the first number anybody's given me about Sustain in a year. Keep giving me numbers. They don't have to be right. They have to be yours."

"Sustain," said Jab.

She put the pencil down.

"That's what it's called on the register," she said. "The line with very little written on it. Sensing and support. It's the other side of the same door Ward's on, the magic door. Most people who've got it have got a little of it, a feeling in the hands, a knack with a fever. A few people have got more." She looked at him. "I don't know yet what you've got. I know you've got something that goes out of you into another body and keeps it going. I read Tonk's chart for the night of the north road. I've never seen one like it."

"You know his name."

"I read his chart. I read yours." She picked the pencil up. "I read everything, Jab. It's the only thing I'm better at than him."

On the step outside the door, Rook said nothing at all.

---

"Now I want to see it," she said. "The lean. I'm going to set it up. I don't want you to want it. I'd like you to be bored, if you can manage it."

"I don't know if I can."

"Try." She stood up. "Shut your eyes. I'm going to put your hand on three people, one after another. I want you to tell me which of them is worse. That's all. Not what's wrong. Just worse."

"It only does my brother."

"Has it ever been asked about anybody else?"

Jab opened his mouth and shut it.

"No," he said.

"Then you don't know that it only does your brother," said the woman. "You know you've only ever asked it about him." She took his wrist, lightly, the way the nurse at the north gate had taken the boy's. "Shut your eyes."

He shut them.

She put his hand flat on somebody's chest, over the breastbone, through a shirt. The chest was very big and very warm and went up and down slowly, like a horse asleep. There was a heart under it going steady and heavy. He waited. He did not want anything; it was easy not to want anything about a chest this size.

"Nothing," he said. "He's all right. Something's itching him. On the arm."

Otto's voice, surprised, a long way up: "It *is*."

"Don't talk," said the woman. She moved his hand.

The next chest was narrow, and still, and sat very straight. The heart in it was quick and even. He felt, in the floor under his breastbone, something like a wall in an old house that has always leaned a little and always will. Not moving. Just there.

"There's something old," he said. "Low down. The leg, I think. It's not getting worse. It's not getting anywhere. It's just there."

On the cot, Dessa said nothing. But he heard her let her breath out through her nose.

The woman moved his hand again.

This chest was broad and hard and held itself very still against the wall, the way you hold something still that doesn't want to be. The heart under it was going harder than it should for a boy leaning against a wall doing nothing. And the floor under Jab's breastbone tipped.

It tipped the way it tipped at the second hour when Tonk was burning. Not as far. Not nearly as far. But it went, all at once, and it went one way, toward the right side of the chest under his hand, up under the collarbone where the strapping crossed.

*Worse*, it said.

He opened his eyes.

"This one," he said. "Here." He did not move his hand. "It's worse. Something's moved in there that was meant to be still."

---

"I'm fine," said Wyck.

"Take your coat off," said the woman.

"I'm fine."

"Then it won't take long." She had not moved. "Coat off, Wyck, or I'll send for Keel, and she'll take it off you, and she won't ask."

Wyck looked at her. Then he looked at Jab. It was a look Jab had seen on boys at home when somebody's little sister had told their mother where they'd been. He put the bar down against the wall and got his coat off his good shoulder, one-handed, and let it drop.

The strapping had slipped. Even Jab could see it: the bands that should have crossed at the back were riding high, and one of them had worked loose, and under the shirt the line of the collarbone had a bump in it that it had not had on the road.

The woman looked at it for about four seconds without touching it.

"Otto," she said, "go to the long building and ask Mrs Lask for Doctor Keel. Tell her it's a collarbone that's moved under strapping. Tell her it's Wyck. She'll know."

Otto went. He went fast, for Otto.

"How long?" said the woman to Wyck.

Wyck said nothing.

"How long have you been leaning on it?"

"Three nights," said Wyck at last. "Against the wall. To see if it'd hold." He looked at the floor. "It doesn't move me. Nothing moves me. I wanted to know if that was still true."

"It's still true," said the woman. "Nothing moves you. Your collarbone moved you. They're different things." She turned her head. "You knew?"

Jab realised she was asking him.

"No," he said. "I didn't know anything. It told me."

---

Doctor Keel came across the yard with her sleeves already rolled and her hair flat on one side. She looked at the collarbone for eleven seconds, and at Wyck's face for three, and then she took the strapping off and put it back on again in about the time it took Jab to understand what she was doing, and Wyck made one sound in the middle of it and no others.

"Six weeks," she said. "From today. Not from the road. From today." She stood back. "Pain tolerance isn't healing. I'd have thought a boy who'd been told what he's got by a man who knows would know the difference. Nothing moves you? Fine. That bone doesn't care. Leave it alone."

"Yes."

"Say it like you mean it."

"Yes, Doctor Keel."

"Good." She looked at the woman with the good boots. "You found it?"

"He did."

Keel turned and looked at Jab properly, the way she had looked at him in the corridor on the first day, when she told him about the nine.

"Did he," she said. She did not say anything else. She went back out across the yard with her sleeves still rolled, and Jab watched her go, and did not know what her face had done.

Wyck put his coat back on. It took him a long time with the new strapping, and nobody helped him, because nobody in hut eleven helped Wyck with anything he could do himself. When it was on he picked up his bar and stood holding it.

"You could've said nothing," he said to Jab.

"No," said Jab. "I couldn't."

"Why not?"

"I don't lie to people about their bodies," said Jab. "I've got a rule about it."

Wyck looked at him for a long moment. Jab waited for whatever was coming.

"Fine," said Wyck, and went out, and sat down on the step beside Rook, and the two of them sat there side by side and did not say anything to each other at all.

---

"Warm my hand," said the woman.

She held it out. She had been outside, crossing the yard, and it was cold; he could see that from here, the fingers white at the ends. He took it in both of his.

He thought: *come on.*

He thought it before he could stop himself. He wanted it to come. He wanted it very badly, because a man in a long coat and a woman in spectacles and a doctor with her sleeves rolled had all looked at him in the last three days as if he were a thing they might want, and he wanted, for once, to be the thing. He wanted her to feel it and write it down.

Nothing came.

He stopped wanting it. He tried to. He sat with her cold hand in his and tried to let go of the reaching part, the leaning-on-a-door part, the way he had at the culvert. But the culvert had had his brother going grey under his hand, and this had a stranger's cold fingers and a book open on the table, and he could feel himself wanting it the whole time, underneath, like a stone in a boot.

Nothing came. Her hand stayed cold.

After a while he let go of it.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be." She was writing. "Good."

"Good?"

"It doesn't come on demand." She did not look up. "Every Sustain I've ever met that came on demand was lying, or was about to kill somebody, usually themselves. That's the first honest thing I've learned about you." She put the pencil down. "You felt Wyck because you didn't care about the answer. You didn't feel my hand because you did. You told me that yourself. *Ask it when you don't care.* That's not a superstition. It's a description."

"Of what?"

"Of the door," said the woman. "I'll know more when I've seen more. I don't know yet whether the lean and the warmth are one thing or two. I think they might be. I'm not going to say it till I've seen both of them do something I didn't set up."

---

"One more thing," she said, "and then you can go, because it's nearly visiting."

"You know about visiting."

"It's on your terms. The man in the coat wrote them down. I've got them here." She tapped the book. "Show me the bottom of your bucket."

He looked at her.

"Don't tip it," she said. "Don't spend anything. Just go down and look, the way you do at night. And tell me how deep."

So he went down.

He shut his eyes, because it was easier, and went down under his breastbone to the place it lived, and found the bucket. It was the same as it had been last night: a hand of something at the bottom, dark and still, not moving, not wanting anything.

"A hand," he said. "Same as I said."

"Now find the floor."

"The bottom?"

"No. Above the bottom." She said it slowly, the way you say a thing to somebody walking a plank. "The last place you could stop. The last place you could take your hand off whoever's under it, and still walk home, and still light that knife of yours in the morning if something came up out of a ditch. It's not empty. Empty's the bottom. It's above that. It's where you'd have to stop to be any use tomorrow. Find it."

He looked.

At first there was nothing there to find. There was a hand of dark in the bottom of a bucket and the sides of the bucket going up. Then, looking, he found that there was a place a little way up the inside, not far above the bottom, where he knew, without knowing how he knew, that if the dark went below it he would not be able to get up off the floor afterward. He had been below it. He had been below it at the culvert, sitting down in the ash with people moving around him like rain. He had been below it in the snow, with a hand that would not close.

It was like a line scratched on the inside of the bucket, low down, by somebody who had been there before him and known.

"There," he said. "About a finger from the bottom."

"That's your floor," said the woman. "You never go below it. Not for anybody."

He opened his eyes.

"Not for anybody?" he said.

"Especially not for anybody."

He did not say anything.

She waited. She waited quite a long time, with the pencil in her hand, looking at him. Then she wrote something in the book, and he saw what it was, because she turned the book so that he could.

*Floor: a finger. Did not say yes.*

"I'll write that down too," she said. "Go on. It's visiting."

---

Rook walked back across the yard with him as far as the long building. It took a while, at Rook's pace.

"She's good," said Rook.

"Are you?"

Rook thought about it seriously, which Jab had come to understand was the only way he thought about anything.

"At some of it," he said. "Not at that. At the other end of it."

"What's the other end?"

"The end where there's no shed," said Rook. He stopped at the corner of the long building and got his breath, with one hand on the wall. "She'll teach you where your floor is. I've never known where mine was. I've only ever found out afterward, lying down."

"You said to ask you again in a while," said Jab. "About what it is."

"I did."

"Is it a while?"

Rook looked at him. The sun was going down behind the long building and the light came round it flat and orange and lit one side of Rook's face.

"No," he said. "Not yet. She's right about one thing, and I'll say it to you because I won't say it to her. You've got to see both halves do something nobody set up. Then ask me."

---

The north yard had lines on it in the morning.

Jab came through at first light on his way to the ward, because it was the quickest way from twelve, and found Rook in the corner by the east shed with a bucket of whitewash and a long brush, painting. He was doing it very slowly, bent over, with one hand on his knee. He had painted three lines across the cinders so far, long and straight, from the shed wall out toward the berm, and was starting a fourth that went round the end of the sand heap.

"What are they?" said Jab.

"Lanes," said Rook, without straightening up. "One's for doing things. One's for falling over. One's the shed." He finished the fourth line and stood the brush in the bucket. "I don't know yet if that's the right way round. We'll find out."

By the time the sun was up there were people in the lanes.

Wyck came first, in his new strapping, with his face like a shut door, and sat down on the sand heap at the end of the second lane with his bar across his knees. He did not say anything. After a while Otto came and stood near him, and Wyck looked at Otto's feet and said, "You stand like a man waiting for a cart that isn't coming. Put your weight in your heels," and Otto did, and Wyck said, "Better," and that was the second lane started.

Dessa came with a book under her arm. It was a big one, thick, with a cracked leather spine and ruled pages, and Pell came behind her looking pleased with himself in a way that told Jab exactly whose spare book it had been. Dessa sat down at a table somebody had carried out of the shed and set against its wall, and opened the book to the first page, and sat looking at it with a pencil in her hand.

Katori came with Callie, and stood where she could see everything, the way she always stood. Toren came pushing the chair, with Senna in it wrapped to the chin.

"It isn't Tuesday," said Senna, to everybody. "I'd like that known. I've been brought out on the pretext that it'll do."

And behind all of them, a little apart, with her plait down her back and her tally board left behind and nothing in her hands at all, came the girl from the weighbridge. She stopped at the edge of the first lane and looked at all of them, one after another, the way she had looked at the crew on the plate.

"Is this it?" she said.

"This is it," said Katori.

The girl looked at the three white lines, and the shed with a bucket under the drip, and the old woman in the wheeled chair, and the boy with his arm in a sling, and the big boy on the sand heap telling Otto about his heels.

"Right," she said. "I thought there'd be more."

---

The woman with the good boots came out of the shed carrying a tray.

There were three dishes on it, each covered with a square of cloth. She set it down on Dessa's table and stood behind it and looked at all of them.

"Four things," she said. "Before anybody spends anything in this yard. I'll say them once, and then I'll say them every day until you're sick of them, and then I'll say them some more." She held up a finger. "Know which is yours and which isn't." Another. "Know how much of yours there is." Another. "Take in a little that you've measured." Another. "Let it settle, and don't touch it." She put her hand down. "Then we'll talk about spending. Not before."

"That's Homura," said Rook, from the step of the shed.

"That's why I'm standing here," said the woman.

Rook said nothing to that. Jab saw his hands, on his knees, close slowly and open again.

"One," said the woman. "Which is yours. Put a hand out. Whoever's got a door."

They put their hands out: Jab, and Callie, and Toren, and the weighbridge girl, and after a moment Dessa from her table. Katori did not. Katori stood with her hands at her sides and watched, and the woman with the good boots looked at her once, for a long moment, and did not ask.

"Do the smallest thing your door does," she said. "The very smallest. And put your other hand flat on a dish."

Jab put the knife on his fist, dark. It came the way it always came, heavy in the belly, hooked at the end, the metal his mother had looked at on a gravel bar and said *well, it looks like you*. It cost nothing to hold. He had been holding it all day for four years. Then he let the light start along the edge from the grip, a hair's width of low amber, the colour of a coal with the ash still on it, and it ran along two-thirds of the blade and stuck at the belly, the way it always did, for one breath, before it caught.

He laid his other palm flat on the cloth over the first dish.

Under the cloth there was ash. He knew it before he felt it. It was cold and heavy and fine, and it did not want anything at all. It was like putting his hand on the floor of an empty house. There was nobody in it. There never had been anybody in it.

And in his other hand, in the knife, in the thin amber line along the edge, there was somebody. It was not warm, exactly. It was full. It was the difference between an empty room and a room with somebody asleep in it.

"Mine's got somebody in it," he said. "That hasn't got anybody."

"Good," said the woman, and made a mark in Dessa's book over Dessa's shoulder.

Beside him Callie had her right palm up, and a shine on it, faint, like old glass. She laid her left on the second dish. "Mine's cold," she said, "and it's mine. That's cold and it's nobody's." She thought about it. "Mine's cold like a well. That's cold like a stone in a well."

The weighbridge girl had her straight blade out, dark, and her other hand on the third dish, and her face screwed up. "It's like touching a dead fire," she said. "Like the grate in the morning. You know there was something and there isn't."

Toren had his stone out on his open palm. He was holding it the way Jab had seen him hold it at the gate on the first night, as if he were testing a stove. His other hand was on the first dish, beside Jab's. He did not say anything for a long time.

"I can't tell," he said at last.

"Try."

"I am trying. I can feel the ash. I can feel it's cold. I can't feel—" He stopped. His thumb had started to move on the stone. It stopped. "I can't feel the other. I don't know if it's there. If I look for it, I—"

"If you look for it, you're checking," said the woman. "And checking puts it out. I know. It's in Elias's book of things he says when he's tired." She did not sound impatient. "It's all right. It's the first morning. Write it, Dessa. *Toren, can't tell.* That's a result."

Dessa wrote it. Toren put his stone away in his pocket and stood with his hand on his knee, and did not look at anybody, and Jab saw what it cost him not to.

---

Then the woman swapped the dishes while they shut their eyes, and did it again, and again. Under one cloth there was ash, and under the others there was sand from the heap, and she moved them round, and they had to say which.

Jab found the ash every time. So did Callie. The weighbridge girl found it twice in three. Toren found it once, and knew he had found it by accident, and said so.

"*Toren, once, by accident, and said so,*" said the woman to Dessa. "Write the last part too."

Then Jab did something he had not meant to do.

The woman was standing behind him, between him and Callie, reaching past him to move a dish, and he felt her. He did not look for her. He felt her the way you feel somebody come into a room behind you. She was full. She was fuller than she had been on the north lip on the twelfth day, when she had put her hand on Rook's chest and come away pale; he had not been close enough then to feel anything, but he knew, now, the way you know a bucket has been refilled by the sound it makes when you set it down.

"You've got more than you had," he said, before he could stop himself. "Than on the north lip."

She went still, with her hand on the dish.

"That's rude," she said.

"Sorry."

"It's also right." She moved the dish. "Write it, Dessa. *Jab, unprompted, on a person.* And the time."

Dessa wrote it. Her pencil stopped, and she looked up at Jab over the top of the big book, and then down again.

---

"Write why," said Senna.

Everybody turned. She was sitting in her chair at the end of Dessa's table, wrapped to the chin, with her hands in her lap where she could see them.

"Why what?" said Dessa.

"Why it matters which is yours. You've written *Toren, can't tell*. You've written *Jab, on a person*. Nobody in forty years will know what any of it's for." Senna did not raise her voice. "I've been in a great many places where somebody wrote down what everybody did and nobody wrote down why. The why's always the first thing to go. You'll want it, one day. Or somebody will."

Dessa looked at the woman with the good boots.

The woman thought about it. Jab watched her think. She did it the way Rook did, seriously, as if the question deserved more than it had been given.

"Because you can borrow the one that isn't yours," she said. "Ash. Anybody's. You can take it in and spend it and it's gone and nobody's poorer. You can't borrow yourself back." She looked at Jab. "If you can't tell which is which, you spend yourself thinking it's the jar. That's how people end up on floors."

"Write that," said Senna.

Dessa wrote it.

When she had finished she turned back to the very first page of the book, which Jab had seen her write on before anybody came, and which she had covered with her hand when she saw him looking. She looked at it for a moment. Then she turned forward again to where they were, and went on.

---

The picture was at the ninth hour on the sixteenth day, and Jab asked Callie to come.

He asked her on the step of fourteen the night before, and he did not know how to ask it, so he said it badly.

"Would you count it?" he said. "Tomorrow. The knocking."

Callie looked up at him from her step.

"I always count it," she said. "I can't not."

"I know. I'd like it to be for me this time." He did not know how else to say it. "I can't hear it the way you do. I hear it and it's just noise, and I sit there and wait for the noise to stop. You hear it and you know what it's doing." He looked down the row. "I'd like somebody in that corridor to know what it's doing."

Callie was quiet for a while.

"All right," she said.

---

So she sat on the bench against the wall in the cool grey air at the bottom of the slope, with the hook across her knees, and Jab stood at the yellow line with the paper tray in his hand and his brother on the rolling bed beside him, and the grey-haired man came to the door and said, "I'll want to warm it up. Three times, maybe four."

"Four," said Tonk, from the bed. "You said four *last* time."

"I said three."

"You *did* four."

The grey-haired man looked at Tonk for a moment. Something happened in his tired face that might have been a smile, if he had had any left.

"I did four," he agreed, and went in.

The rack began to hum. Jab could not see it. He could see the edge of the machine through the door, the pale ring, and nothing else. Behind the wall the knocking started, long runs of it, fast and steady and mechanical, and he stood at the line and did not try to hear it, and let Callie hear it for him.

Her lips moved on the bench.

"Eleven," she said, low. "Eleven. Eleven." A gap. "Ten." Another run. "Eleven. Ten."

"Is that bad?"

"It's what it was on the tenth day." She kept her eyes on the door. "It's not worse. It's not better. It's the same bad."

The knocking stopped. Started. Stopped. Started. Four times, the man walked his candle along the row of windows, and Jab stood at the line and counted nothing, and Callie counted everything, out loud, low, in a flat voice like somebody reading a list of weights off a board.

Then the grey-haired man came to the door and said, "Now," and it was time.

Tonk looked at the yellow line. Then he looked at Jab.

He had the long cord in his fist, the loose end, the way he had taken it in on the tenth day. He had the wooden fish in the other hand. He held on to Jab's fingers with the cord hand, hard, for as long as it took Jab to count three, and then four, and then five.

Then he let go by himself.

"It's not metal," he said, holding up the cord. "So it can come."

"It can come," said Jab.

The door shut like a lid.

---

Doctor Keel came down the slope herself, when it was done.

She came from the room behind the desk at the top, where the picture came out, and she came down it fast, with her sleeves down for once and a sheet of something grey and shiny in her hand, and she stopped at the yellow line and looked at Jab, and Jab found that he had stopped breathing some time ago and not noticed.

"Clear," she said.

He did not understand the word.

"The bone's clear," said Doctor Keel. "I'm saying *clear*. I'm not saying *I think*. It was a picture worth the name for once; he got it on the fourth run, and it's clean. There's nothing in that bone that shouldn't be. The infection's gone out of him." She looked at Jab's face. "That's the four in five. He's in the four."

Jab sat down.

He did not decide to. He was standing at the line and then he was sitting on the concrete floor of the corridor with his back against the wall and the paper tray in his lap, and Callie was on the bench beside him, very close, not touching, and the grey-haired man was in the doorway, and Doctor Keel was standing over him with the shiny sheet, and none of it was quite real.

"Breathe," said Doctor Keel. "I'm not having you go over in my corridor. I told you that on the first day."

He breathed.

"Now the rest," she said, "because I don't do half of anything. I could see the growing plate on this one, properly, for the first time. It's damaged worse than I told you. Not gone. Worse than I'd hoped. That leg's going to be a bigger difference from the other one than I said, when he's grown. The boot's going to be higher. The operation in the middle is more likely than it was, not less." She did not look away from him. "I'd rather you had that today, in the same breath as the good one, than on a bad afternoon in three years when you're not expecting it."

"Yes," said Jab.

"The frame stays till the spring. That hasn't changed."

"No."

"And he'll start pulling." Keel turned to the rolling bed, which the nurse had brought out through the door, and on which Tonk lay flat with his eyes screwed shut and the cord in his fist, furious. "You. Can you hear me?"

"*Yes*."

"There's going to be a bar over your bed. Hanging from a frame. You'll pull yourself up on it. Every day. A little further than the day before."

Tonk opened his eyes.

"*Myself*?"

"Yourself."

"Not Jab?"

"Not Jab," said Doctor Keel. "Jab's done enough lifting. You're going to do some."

Tonk turned his head and looked at his brother, sitting on the floor of the corridor with his back against the wall.

"*Myself*," he said to him, with enormous satisfaction.

"Yourself," said Jab. His voice did not work very well. "Can you get up now?"

"No," said Doctor Keel.

"*When*?" said Tonk.

"Spring," said Doctor Keel, and went back up the slope as fast as she had come down it.

---

The grey-haired man was still in the doorway. He was looking at Callie, not at Jab.

"You counted," he said.

"Ten, four times in twenty-two runs," said Callie. "Same as the tenth day."

The man looked at her for a long moment.

"Same as the tenth day," he said. "That's the first good news I've had off that rack in a month." He rubbed the ink on his hand with his thumb. "It doesn't mean it's getting better. It means it's not getting worse this week."

"I know," said Callie.

"I'd like you to count it on the twenty-first day," he said. "When the old woman's in. If you're able."

Callie looked at Jab, sitting on the floor. Then back at the man.

"I'll be here," she said.

---

At the tenth hour the ward turned the visitors out.

Jab tied the end of the long cord to the bed rail, the way he had every night since the second, and checked the knot, and checked it again. Above Tonk's bed there was a frame now, two tall legs and a crossbar, and hanging from the crossbar on a chain there was a short smooth bar, like the rail of a gate, at the height of a small boy's reach. Tonk had pulled on it twice before he fell asleep. He had not got his shoulders off the pillow either time. He had said, both times, "*That's* further."

In the next bed, Senna lay on her back with her hands folded on the blanket and her eyes on the ceiling.

"Milo Faust," she said.

She said it to the ceiling, not loudly, in the voice of somebody stating a fact they have stated before and will state again. Jab had heard her do it last night, and the night before, at about this hour. He had not asked. It was not his.

Tonk was not asleep.

"Who's Milo?" he said, into the dark.

Senna did not answer at once. Jab stood by the bed with the knot under his fingers and did not move.

"A boy with a lamp," said Senna.

Tonk thought about this.

"Where is he?"

"He's what I say every night," said Senna. "So he's somewhere."

It was quiet for a long time in the ward. Down at the far end a nurse turned the small lamp lower. Jab thought his brother had gone to sleep.

"Can I say it?" said Tonk.

Senna turned her head on the pillow and looked across at the small shape in the next bed, under its frame, with the bar hanging over it like a rail.

"Yes," she said. "You can say it."

"Milo," said Tonk, to the ceiling, the way she had. And then, in a smaller voice, because he was four and it was dark and he had had his picture today: "Milo Faust."

"There," said Senna. "That's two of us."

Jab went out past Mrs Lask at her desk, who did not look up, and out into the frozen street under three lamps that would go out at the first hour. He walked home in the lit bits. At the loose grating between the second pole and the third he stepped wide without thinking about it.

On the step of twelve he sat down, and after a while he went down, under his breastbone, to look at the bucket. There was a hand of dark in the bottom of it, and a finger up from the bottom, scratched on the inside, a line.

*Not for anybody*, she had said.

He looked at the line for a long time. Then he came back up, and sat on his step in the cold with his hands on his knees, and did not decide anything.
