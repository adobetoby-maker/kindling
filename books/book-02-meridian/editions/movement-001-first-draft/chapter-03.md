# Chapter 3 — Toren: Four Chairs

Somebody had counted the chairs.

Toren saw them before he had taken three steps into the ward. Four chairs, set in a half circle round his grandmother's bed. Somebody had asked how many there would be. Somebody else had gone and fetched them and put them out and gone away. He looked at the four of them and did not like it, and could not have said why.

His arm hurt. It had hurt all night in a new way, a deep grinding way, since he landed on it at the bottom of the Sag. The gate nurse had said he would know by the noise he made. He had made a noise at about the third hour, getting up to go outside, and Dessa had heard it through the wall and said nothing, which was worse.

His stone was in his coat pocket. It had come back from the hatch at the seventh hour, washed, with a tag. It was cold.

It had been cold for two days after the Breach. Then last night, on the north road, running at a pack of hounds with no plan at all, it had not been cold. The struts had come up both legs. The spike had lit in his fist, hard and white-grey. It had been there for about a minute. Then he had fallen on the slabs, and it had gone out, and when he felt for the place where the asking went, the place was not there.

He took his hand out of his pocket.

The boy from the culvert was sitting in a chair by the next bed.

He had a dry shirt on that was too big for him and a bandage from wrist to elbow. His face had the look of somebody who has not slept in a very long time and has stopped expecting to. He looked up as they came in, and Toren looked back.

Toren had last seen him kneeling in the angle of a wall with his arm across a child, shouting *Mine! They come at me!* into the dark. Toren had not understood the words until Rook roared them back. Then everybody had understood them, all at once.

Neither of them said anything now.

---

Doctor Sowerby came in behind them fast, the way he always came, and went to the foot of the bed.

"Sit," he said. "All of you. The man with the back, especially."

Rook looked at the fourth chair. He lowered himself into it in three movements, with one hand on the bed rail. He did it without any expression at all. Dessa watched every inch of it. Wyck put his iron bar under his chair, where the nurse could not see it.

Toren sat nearest the bed.

His grandmother was sitting up. She had a cup on a tray across her lap. Her white hair had been washed, and it stood up at the back in a way it had never stood up on the road. She looked at the four of them and her face did the thing it did on the cart, when Toren came back from a watch with nothing to report.

"There you all are," she said. "He's counted you. Sit still."

Doctor Sowerby did not open the folder.

"Right," he said. "I'll do this in the order I always do it in. The bad thing, then the good thing, then the bill. I've been told I do it too fast, so stop me. I'll say first that I'm a day late with this. I wanted a second set of pictures before I said anything. And last night went on a four-year-old." He did not look toward the next bed. "I'd do that again. I'd still rather have been here yesterday."

"You're here now," said Senna. "Go on."

---

"The bad thing is that we cannot fix her."

He said it to Senna, not to them.

"I want that said in this room with her sitting up in it. In about a fortnight, somebody in a corridor is going to say the word *treated* to you. You'll hear *cured*. Then in six months you'll be angry with me, and I'd rather be got out of the way now. There is damage in this woman that was done in eleven days in 2130. It's had twenty years to go on being done. It is not coming out. Her marrow is failing. It's been failing for about nine years. Whatever kept her upright on that road was not medicine."

"It was a flask of road-ash and a man with a hole in his back," said Senna.

"It was. It shouldn't have worked. There's a paper in it for somebody who isn't me.

"The good thing. We can take out what's still in her that's still doing harm. That's a course. It takes about twenty weeks. It's unpleasant, and it works. We've run it two hundred and eleven times. I've got the numbers in this folder and you can read them. It starts this afternoon.

"And we can hold the marrow up from outside. Not replace it. Hold it up. That's a transfusion and a preparation we make here, measured on the machine in the shed, on a cycle. She had the first of it the morning after she came in. The cycle is eleven days. It doesn't stop.

"With those two things, this woman is not going to die this winter. Without them, she'd have died within about five weeks. I'd have given you six."

Nobody said anything.

"And then the bill," said Doctor Sowerby. "I'll let her ask for it. It's hers."

"Say it," said Senna Voss.

He said it. It took about ninety seconds, and he did not soften one word of it. Toren heard every word go into him and could not have repeated any of them afterward. When Sowerby had finished, he asked Senna whether she wanted him to stay in the room. She said no, thank you. He went. He did not look back.

The four of them sat in four counted chairs.

---

"Right," said Senna. "I'm going to say the whole of it once, properly, with all of you here. That's the rule in this family. I didn't make it, and I'm not going to be the first one to get out of it.

"Then I should like nobody ever to say it for me again. Not in any room, to anybody, for the rest of my life. If somebody asks, you send them to me. I have a great deal of time now and very little to do with it."

She looked past Toren then, at the next bed. At the boy in the chair beside it.

"You too, brother," she said. "You're in it now. I told you."

The boy said, "Yes, ma'am," very quietly. Toren saw him put both hands flat on his knees.

"First. I'm going to live." Senna let it sit. "That's the biggest one, and it's first, and nobody's going to take it off me by being sad in front of me before I've finished. A week ago I was five weeks from dying in a cart. Now I'm not. Three children and a man carried me three hundred miles to make that true. If any one of you looks at your boots while I'm saying it, I shall be extremely rude.

"Second. I'm going to live *here*.

"Not for the winter. Not until I'm stronger. Here. There's a machine in a shed at the north end of this station, and it's the only one of its kind anybody in this room has ever heard of. I have to be inside a mile of it every eleven days for the rest of my life. Eleven. Not a fortnight. Not if I'm careful. Eleven.

"So I won't see Sallow Creek again. I won't see Hobb's Wall, or that dreadful weighmaster I was so fond of, or the Level. None of you are to build me a chair on wheels and get clever about it. It isn't the distance. It's the eleven days. I've done the sums four times and so will you, and we'll all get the same answer."

Wyck said something under his breath. Senna did not stop.

"And I've been in a cart since the second of the month. I've crossed three hundred miles of the worst road in this country on my back, being no use to anybody. It's the only thing I was ever actually good at. I've done it for the last time. I'd like that noticed by somebody. It won't be in any of that man's folders."

"It's noticed," said Dessa.

"Thank you."

---

"Third," said Senna. "This is the one I'd rather not, so I'll do it quickly."

She put her right hand out from under the tray and laid it on the blanket, palm up.

"The course takes the nerves. Not all at once and not everywhere. It takes the hands and the feet, and it doesn't give them back. It's not a small number of people. It's nearly everybody. He showed me the number, and then he made me say it back to him.

"It's started already. It started before the course did, if I'm honest, which I'm trying to be." She looked at the hand. "The ends of the fingers. Like I've had them in snow. It'll get worse. By the spring I'll be able to move these and pick things up if I look at them while I do it. I won't know I'm holding them.

"And I won't walk. I can walk now, poorly, as far as that door. By the spring I shan't. That's the feet. That one's simple, and there's nothing to discuss."

"Gran—" said Toren.

"Don't do that face. Just say it."

"Is it worth it?"

"Yes," said Senna Voss. She said it immediately, without any weighing at all. "Yes, it is. I want you to hear how fast I said that. You'll want it one day, when somebody you love is in a bed and you're doing the same sum.

"I'm seventy-three. I've had twenty years of something that was always going to finish me. A man in a corridor has just offered me eight more. He told me to my face that he's seen twelve, and he's seen three, which he didn't have to do. Eight years. I'll watch that one—" her hand moved a little toward Toren "—stop being thirteen. I'll watch that one—" toward Wyck "—be told what to do by somebody and not do it. I'll watch Dessa Cole run a room. That's not a consolation prize. That's the whole thing I've wanted since the morning we left Sallow Creek. They're going to sell it to me for my hands and my feet and the rest of the road. I'd have paid more. I told him so. He said everybody says that in the first week."

She stopped and got her breath.

"I've finished," she said. "Somebody go and find out about the tea in this place. It isn't good, and I refuse to believe it's the best they can do."

---

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Wyck got up and went to find out about the tea, and took his bar with him. Dessa put her head back and shut her eyes. Rook sat in his chair with his hands on his knees and looked at nothing.

Toren looked at his grandmother's hand on the blanket.

He thought about the road. He had carried numbers down it for sixty-four days. Nineteen days, his father's paper had said, with three ifs. The ifs had killed his father. Then the number had broken and been rebuilt and broken again, and at the end of it they had come in under a lamp in the rain with the number at zero.

He had thought zero was the end of the sum.

It was not. It was the start of another one. Eleven days, and eleven, and eleven. There was no zero in it anywhere.

In the next chair, the boy from the culvert had his head down.

Senna looked across at him.

"Brother," she said. "Yours is at the ninth hour tomorrow?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then you'll want the tea as well," said Senna. "Wyck's gone for it. He won't bring enough. He never does."

---

Visiting was in the afternoons. The family meeting was allowed. Staying after it was not. At the half hour a nurse came and moved them out, kindly and without any possibility of argument.

There was a bench outside the doors, against the long wall of the building, in a strip of pale sun.

Somebody was already sitting on it.

It was the girl from the step. Callie. She had the hook across her knees again. It had come back from the hatch this morning a second time, washed a second time, and she was holding it by the conduit with both hands. She had her left arm tucked in against her ribs. There was the line of bald pink skin over her left ear, with the stitches in it, where no hair would grow.

She looked up as he came out.

"They told you it's afternoons," she said.

"They told me that the first night. I sat out here till the first hour." Toren looked at the bench. "Can I—"

She moved the hook. He sat down on the other end, slowly, in two movements, because the stitches at the back of his leg pulled if he did it in one. That was one fewer than Rook needed. He had started counting other people's movements lately and could not stop.

They sat in the sun and looked at the drain in the yard in front of them.

"Your mother's in there," said Toren.

"Fourth bed on the left. Opposite yours." Callie did not look away from the drain. "They're taking a picture of the inside of her head this afternoon. Then the doctor talks to the family tomorrow. At the ninth hour." She turned the hook over on her knees. "Your gran's got a loud voice. I heard some of it through the door."

"How much?"

"Eleven days," said Callie. "And the tea."

---

They were quiet for a while.

"You read that off the grass," said Toren. "The night before last. The mule. You knew it was a mule from the—"

"From the size of the mound." Callie kept her eyes on the drain. "And I read the other thing. Before that. On the big road."

He went very still.

She had not meant to say it. He could see that. She said it anyway, the way you set down something you have been carrying so you can use both hands.

"Something came across the pasture from the east," she said. "Heavy. Three legs to a stride, not four. Wide, the way a thing stands that's made for pushing. And a fourth one that wasn't a leg. It cut the grass off level. It cut a line in the turf a man long, clean as a knife through a loaf." She was not looking at him. "It hit the rail on the east side and bent it. Somebody was in the ditch under it. There's a hollow at the edge the size of somebody lying down. And it's dead, and somebody lifted it with bins."

Toren did not say anything for a long time.

"You got all of that," he said at last, "off *grass*."

"It's what I do. It's the only thing I'm good at." She turned the hook over again. "I'm not asking who. I just read it. And you were sitting here. And I didn't want to sit next to you knowing it, and you not knowing I knew."

He looked down at his wrapped hand.

"Yes," he said.

That was all. She did not ask anything else. He felt her not asking. It was the most restful thing that had happened to him in Meridian.

---

His hand went into his coat pocket.

It did it by itself. It had done it every forty steps for sixty days. It found the stone and stopped there, and then, as if it remembered something, it came out again and lay on his knee.

Callie was watching. He knew she was.

"I did that," she said. "With my hook. On a hill. I kept my hand on it and kept looking at it, to see if it was still there. Every time I looked, it went."

Toren turned his head.

"Somebody told me that," he said slowly. "On a towpath. That checking on it is what puts it out."

"Somebody told me something like it. After." Callie looked at her hands. "She said you can stop holding a thing and it goes on being held. She said she didn't know that herself for a long time."

He was quiet. Then it came out of him, low and flat, the way a thing comes out that has sat in a person with nobody to tell it to.

"It's out," he said. "Again."

She waited.

"It went out after the Breach. Two days. I couldn't find it at all. Then last night it came back, and I didn't ask it, it just—came. For about a minute. And now it's out again." He looked at the drain. "I don't know if it's going to do that every time. Go and come back. Or if last night was the last of it, and I spent it on a road."

Callie thought about it. He could see her thinking, the way you can see somebody read a track.

"It's not gone," she said. "It's empty. Those are different."

"How do you know?"

"I'm empty now. Nearly. I can feel it at the bottom. You can't use it for anything, but you know it's there." She stopped. "And you know more than me. Yours came back in two days. You've done it once already. I only know it about mine."

Toren had not thought of it that way.

"Katori's took nine days, once," said Callie. "The second time she emptied herself. She said she was sure it was gone. Absolutely sure."

"Nine days," said Toren.

"That was her. Yours was two." Callie stood up, slowly, one hand on her ribs. "I don't know anything about yours. I'm only telling you what somebody told me. Because it helped."

She did not look back as she went. He noticed that. He noticed that he was grateful for it.

He sat on the bench in the sun with his hand on his knee, and he did not put it in his pocket. It was harder than it should have been.

---

He came back at the start of the afternoon, when the woman at the desk let visitors through.

His grandmother was lying against her pillows with a new grey tiredness under her eyes. They were taking her down to the shed later, for the first of the course. She did not mention it. She was talking.

She was talking across the ward.

In the bed opposite, a woman was sitting up. She had brown hair, loose on the pillow, and a lean weathered face, and her right hand lay on the blanket like a glove. Callie was in the chair beside her. Callie had her hook across her knees again and was holding it very tightly.

"You're the grandson," said the woman in the bed, before Senna could say anything. "She's been telling me about you."

"Gran—"

"I've been telling her about Wyck," said Senna. "Mostly. You came into it."

"Your grandmother came in on a board," said the woman. "She'd like that understood. She'd have walked if she could. She's told me four times."

"I walked in," Senna told Toren. "This woman walked in. Through the south gate. On her own feet. They were talking about it at the desk yesterday, which they never do." She looked across the ward. "Tilda. This is my grandson. Tor, this is Tilda. She's the best company I've had since the spring, and she's been here two days."

Tilda looked at Senna for a long moment. Toren saw something go across her face. It was the look people got at a market when they met somebody worth talking to.

"I said that," said Tilda. "On the first morning. To a stranger, by a fire. *Tilda.* Nothing else. It was the whole of it."

"Did you."

"Almost exactly."

"Then we've got that settled," said Senna, "and we can get on to the interesting part."

---

The interesting part was a piece of bread.

A nurse had brought Senna a plate with bread on it. Senna picked it up. She did it slowly, looking at her own hand the whole time. She watched her fingers go round the bread the way you watch somebody else's fingers do a thing you have told them to do. When she had it, she lifted it, still looking, and took a bite, and put it down, and let go of it. Looking.

Tilda watched her do it.

"Your hands," said Tilda.

"They've started," said Senna. "The ends. Like snow. It's the treatment, and the old business under it. It takes the hands and feet and doesn't give them back. I can feel the weight of a thing. I can't always feel the thing." She looked across at Tilda's right hand on the blanket. "And yours?"

"It's stopped taking instruction. Since the winter."

"Can you feel it?"

"I can feel it. I just can't tell it anything." Tilda looked at her own hand. "It's like shouting at somebody in the next field."

Senna was quiet for a moment. Then her long weathered face did something that made her look about thirty years younger.

"Well," she said. "Which of us is going to cut the other's bread?"

"Neither," said Tilda at once. "We'll look at it."

Senna laughed. It was a short dry sound like a stick breaking.

"I've been saying that to three children and a man for a week," she said. "Not one of them has laughed."

"They're frightened of you," said Tilda.

"They are. I've worked very hard at it."

"I'll need to know how you do it," said Tilda. "I've got five of my own, and none of them are frightened of me at all. The horse is. The horse is the only one."

Toren looked at Callie. Callie was looking at her mother. Her hands had come open on the hook. She did not seem to know it.

"I heard you this morning," Tilda said to Senna, more quietly. "Through the ward. Saying the whole of it once."

"Everybody heard me. I meant them to."

"It's a good rule," said Tilda.

"It's the best rule anybody ever gave me," said Senna. "A boy on a wall made it up. I recommend it."

Tilda looked at the ceiling. Toren saw her put it somewhere, carefully, the way a person puts away a tool she means to use.

"Tilda has a daughter who reads ground," Senna told Toren. "That's what the gate nurse said. I thought you'd want to know before she read yours."

"She already did," said Toren.

Senna looked at him. Then she looked at Callie for a long time with her pale eyes.

"Did she," she said.

---

Rook was lying on the floor of hut eleven when Toren got back.

He was lying on his side on a blanket by the stove, because the bed was too soft and the floor was not. His eyes were shut. Dessa was sitting on her bed with her bad leg out, watching him with a face like a locked door.

"He's fine," she said, before Toren could ask. "He's not fine. He's lying down. It's the same thing today."

"He sat in a chair this morning."

"He sat in a chair. Then he walked to the yard office and stood at a counter for most of an hour, and he won't say why. Then he walked back and lay down on a floor. That's the whole of his day." Dessa did not look away from Rook. "He held the road last night. On both sides of him. For as long as it took. With a hole in his back that Sowerby says is a fortnight from standing." Her voice did not change. "I felt him do it. I was up on the bank with my eyes shut and I felt him go thin. That's the only word I've got. Thin. Like a rope with half the strands cut."

"I can hear you," said Rook, with his eyes shut.

"I know you can. I'm saying it so you can."

Wyck was on the step outside. He had the iron bar in his left hand. He had got it from a scrap bin at the yard office on the first day, eighteen inches of square-section steel that a man had cut down for him for nothing. He turned it over. He dropped it on the step. He picked it up.

Toren went out and sat down beside him.

"Say it," said Toren, after a while.

"Say what."

"You've been about to say something since the ward."

Wyck turned the bar over. He dropped it. He picked it up.

"He said he'd go," he said.

Toren did not answer.

"At the fire on the lane. He said *I'll walk you to Meridian, and when you're through the gate and she's in front of somebody with proper instruments, I'll go.* He said it to all of us. I wrote it down in my head, because I write down everything that man says, and so does everybody in this hut." Wyck looked at the bar. "We're through the gate. She's in front of instruments. She's had the whole of it said in front of her this morning. And he's lying on a floor and he hasn't said a word about it."

"Then ask him."

"I'm not asking him," said Wyck Tanner. "If I ask him, he answers. Then I'll never know if he'd have said it on his own."

---

Toren thought about the window at the south gate.

It had been two days ago, and it felt like a year. The pen with the hot pipes. The man with the pen on a string. *Persons in party.* And Toren saying *five*, and hearing his own voice do something, and Dessa putting her hand flat on his back.

Then Rook, asked for a name.

Rook had said *Rook*. Hallet had said that was not a name he could write. And there had been a silence that went on too long, and then Rook had said, "Elias Marsh."

Toren had known the name since the cairn. Rook had given it to all of them there, in a road, standing up. But he had given it to them. It had been a present. At the window it had been taken, the way the grey coats took boots.

And Hallet had turned back a great many pages in a very large book, and found it.

*You were at this gate eleven years ago. You gave a statement over two days concerning a research station and a company of men. The file says* referred.

*I found out at the time*, Rook had said. *A very polite young woman told me the word means it's gone to the right place. And it had. That's the part nobody ever believes. It had gone to precisely the right place, and it's still there.*

Last night, in the other pen, Hallet had read the name back again without being asked. *Mr Marsh.* And Rook had said *Present* as if it were nothing.

Toren had watched Callie hear it. He had watched her turn and look at him, and he did not know what his own face had done.

---

He said it on his own that night.

He said it at the eleventh hour, in the hut, with the stove going and all of them there. That meant Dessa had gone to the long building and got Senna in the chair with the wheels, over four hundred yards of wet path, with Wyck pushing and complaining. She had done it on the strength of a message that said *hut eleven, tonight, all of us*, and nothing else.

Rook stood with his back to the stove. He could not sit down and get up again twice in one day.

"I'm going to do this poorly," he said, "and I'm going to do it in one go. There's a rule in this room about all of us at once and nobody finding out second. It was set by somebody on a wall, and it's the best rule anybody's ever given me.

"On the lane I said I'd walk you to Meridian. I said that when you were through the gate and she was in front of instruments, I'd go. I meant it entirely. I had it worked out. There was a version of this week where I stood at that gate the day she went in and shook four hands and went east. I've carried that version about with me since before any of you were hurt. It's been a great comfort to me. I'd like to put it down now."

He stopped.

"I'm not going," he said.

Nobody moved.

"That's all of it. There's no second part. I'm not going, and there isn't a reason I can give you that would stand up in front of the man I was in that lane. I've stopped needing one." He looked at the stove rather than at any of them. "I went to the yard office this afternoon. I signed. It's ash-lifting on the Sag line, and it's the worst money in this station. Nobody else will take it, because you have to be able to hold a road shut. I've signed for four seasons, which in this place is a thing you sign."

Dessa made a sound. Rook went on.

"I'll say the other half out loud now, so nobody has to work it out on their own at four in the morning. Her course is clinical. It went through at the gate. It isn't conditional on one single thing any of the rest of us does. The man at that window told me so to my face, and made a promise about it he didn't have to make, and I believe him.

"But she has to be inside a mile of that shed every eleven days for the rest of her life. That means inside this berm. Being inside this berm is residency. Residency here is *reviewed*. A household gets reviewed on what the station reckons it's worth.

"So that's what I've signed for. Not for her medicine. For her address."

He looked at them then.

"If anybody here wants to say *you said you'd go*, now's when. I'll hear the whole of it standing up."

---

There was a long silence.

Toren looked at Wyck. Wyck was looking at the bar in his hand. He had it in his left fist, and he was holding it very still.

"Right," said Wyck.

That was the whole of it. The stove ticked. Dessa put her head back against the wall and shut her eyes.

And Senna said, "Well," in a voice that had a great deal in it. "I shall need somebody to push this chair on Tuesdays."

Toren thought: *I will.*

He did not say it. It did not need saying. He only thought it, and then he found he had decided it, and that it was the first thing he had decided in Meridian that nobody had asked him to.

He looked at Rook standing with his back to the stove. At the way he held his shoulders, which was not a way anybody held their shoulders on purpose. At his hands, which were not steady, and which he did not put away.

Four seasons. The worst work in the station. With half the strands cut.

Toren did the sum. He could not help it. He had been doing sums since the second of the month. It came out the way all of them came out now, without a zero anywhere in it.

Then he looked at his grandmother, who was looking at Rook. She was not doing any sum at all. She was only looking at him, with a face Toren had never seen her wear on the road.

He looked away. It was not his.
