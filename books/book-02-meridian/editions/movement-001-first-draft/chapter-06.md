# Chapter 6 — Toren: The Shed

The card came at the fourth hour. A boy of about ten brought it to the door of hut eleven and stood waiting to be told there was no reply.

It was buff, and the size of a hand. There were two lines on it.

*E. MARSH and T. VOSS are requested at the Director's office, second floor, yard office, concerning (i) the south approach incident and (ii) the north road incident. Attendance is not compulsory. Please bring your bands.*

The date under it was eleven days away.

Dessa read it over Toren's shoulder. Then she took it out of his hand and read it again.

"They've put them on the same card," she said.

"It's a card, Dessa. They've probably only got the one kind—"

"They have got more than one kind of card," said Dessa Cole. "I've been in this station five days and I've counted eleven kinds of paper. There's a new green one on the ward door." She held it up. "They put those two things on one card because somebody sat at a desk and joined them. The south approach is *you killed something nobody can kill*. The north road is *you did it again, with half the station watching, two days later*. Somebody's drawn a line between them."

Toren went to the door.

The boy had gone on down the row. He stopped at twelve and knocked, and waited, and nobody came. He slid a card under the door. Then he went on to fourteen.

Callie was sitting on the step there. Toren watched her take the card and read it. He watched her read it twice. Then she looked up the row, at the door of twelve, and at him.

"She's got one too," said Toren.

"Of course she has," said Dessa, behind him. "And the boy in twelve. That's three doors and one hour." She gave him the card back. "They haven't joined two things, Tor. They've joined three."

Wyck was on the step with his bar.

"*Attendance is not compulsory*," he said, without looking round.

"Yes."

"They wrote that down." He turned the bar over in his left hand. He did not drop it. "That's the bit I'd want a column for."

---

They did the bread that night, because Dessa said the day had to have a halt in it.

Senna came in the chair with the wheels. Dessa had fetched her again, four hundred yards over the wet path, with Wyck pushing and complaining about the camber. She had to be back in the ward by the tenth hour. Dessa had told the nurse the ninth. That was Dessa's idea of a margin.

It was Meridian bread. It was grey and even and came in a paper wrapper with a number printed on the side. Dessa cut it poorly, in slabs, on the top of the stove-box, with a great deal of concentration.

"I know the order," she said.

"I know you do," said Senna.

Dessa picked up the end crust.

"Senna."

She put it in Senna's hand. Then, without being asked, she folded the fingers over it.

Toren saw her do it. He saw Senna look down at her own hand as if it belonged to somebody sitting next to her.

"It's there," said Dessa.

"I know it's there. I can feel the *weight* of it. I can't feel the bread." Senna turned the hand over slowly, watching it. The crust stayed where it was. "It's like being handed things through a glove. A thick one. That's the whole of it. It isn't dramatic, and I'd be obliged if nobody made a face."

It had been the ends of her fingers yesterday. Like snow, she had said. Now it was the whole of both hands.

The course had started yesterday. Sowerby had drawn a line on a page for them. The hands would go over months, he had said. By the spring. And then he had said, in the same voice he used for everything, that sometimes it went early. That the old damage under it could make it go faster than the line. That he'd tell them if it did.

He had not needed to tell them. They could see.

"Do you want—" said Dessa.

"I do not," said Senna Voss, "want anybody in this room to hold my bread for me. Not tonight. Not in the spring. Not in eight years. I'd like that minuted." She lifted the crust the way you lift a thing with a tool. "I can see it perfectly well. I'll simply have to look at it. I've looked at worse."

She ate the crust, looking at it.

---

"Me," said Dessa. She put a slab down beside her own hand and did not eat it.

"Wyck."

Wyck took it, left-handed.

"Rook."

Rook took it. He was sitting on the floor with his back against his bed, because the floor was easier to get up from than a chair. He held the bread and did not eat it either.

"Tor."

Toren took it.

"Milo," said Dessa.

She cut the last slab. She put it on the cloth at the end of the row, and folded the cloth over it.

Then she did the thing she had not done on the bank at the Level, or on any night since. Toren could see she had decided it on the path over from the ward, with Wyck complaining behind her.

"He'd have hated this place," she said.

Wyck made a noise.

"He would. He'd have hated it for about four days. Then he'd have known everybody's name in the whole north yard. He'd have been telling that Hallet man his business. He'd have got a lamp. He'd have got one of those lamps off the pole somehow. He'd have found out who owns them." She stopped. She put the knife down. She went on anyway, the way you walk over a bad patch of road without slowing down. "He'd have stood in the middle of that street with three lights on it and said something completely idiotic about how it wasn't as good as his."

"It wasn't as good as his," said Wyck.

"It wasn't," Rook agreed.

"It was *terrible*," said Dessa. "It was a jar of fireflies. It was never once enough to see by, the whole road. And it's the only thing that ever got any of us anywhere."

Nobody said anything.

Senna put her right hand flat on the top of the stove-box, which was warm. She could only just tell it was warm. By the spring she would not be able to tell at all. She left it there anyway.

"Milo Faust," she said to the room, in a voice like a dry stick breaking. "There. It's been said tonight. It'll be said tomorrow. I'll say it every single night in this station until they carry me out of it, and none of you have to."

---

Toren sat on the step at about the twelfth hour. Wyck had pushed the chair back across to the long building and come back. The stove was banked. Everybody had stopped talking.

He put his hand in his coat pocket, the way he had done every forty steps for two months.

It was warm.

He did not take it out.

It was not hot. It was not much, and it was not steady. It came and went under his fingers like something breathing. It was the least impressive thing anybody had ever felt. It was the way it had felt on a towpath in the spring, with a sack of corn across his knees.

He had not checked since the bench. That was the truth of it, and he had told nobody. A girl who read ground had watched his hand go to his pocket and told him that checking was what put it out. She had said *you know more than me*. And he had sat on the bench after she left with his hand on his knee, and made it stay there.

So he had not checked for a day and a half. And at some point in that day and a half, while he was not looking, it had come back.

*It's not gone. It's empty. Those are different.*

He sat on a concrete step in a place with a written reason for everything. His arm was broken. His grandmother was four hundred yards away with a pair of hands that were already going. There was a summons on the table behind him that had joined him to two people he had known for less than a week.

He held a warm stone in his pocket, and did not look at it, and did not ask it for anything.

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour.

---

They brought Callie's mother home in the morning, in a barrow.

Toren was on the step with Wyck when they came along the row. The big boy from fourteen had the barrow—Otto. He had the strap crossed over his chest and one handle in his good hand and the other braced against his hip. The woman sat on a folded blanket on the boards with her back against the front of the barrow and her legs out. She had a battered wide-brimmed hat pulled down against the light.

Callie walked beside it with one hand on the rim. Not holding. Only there.

Wyck watched the whole length of it come. He watched the strap and the one-handed grip and the handle against the hip.

"You've got it on your hip," he said, when Otto was level with the step. "The other handle. You'll have a bruise the size of a plate by tonight."

Otto stopped.

"I've got one arm," he said.

"So've I," said Wyck. His collarbone sat wrong under his coat, and the strapped arm did not move at all. He held up the bar, as if that settled it, and then dropped it on the step and did not pick it up. "Put the strap under the handle, not over. It'll take the weight off your hip and put it on your chest. Your chest doesn't mind."

Otto looked down at the strap. He moved it. He took the handle again and walked two steps and stopped.

"That's better," he said, surprised.

"I know," said Wyck. "I've been carrying things with one arm longer than I'd like. I've had a lot of time to think about hips."

"Thank you."

"Wyck."

"Otto," said Otto, and went red, and walked on.

The woman on the boards had watched all of it from under her hat.

"He's cleverer than he looks," she said to Callie, not quietly.

"*Mum*."

"I'm only saying. Most people are."

---

Callie looked at Toren as the barrow went past.

She looked at his face first. Then her eyes went down to his hand. It was in his coat pocket. It was staying there. It did not come out and go back.

He did not know what she saw. He knew she saw something. He saw her see it.

He nodded.

She nodded back. She did not say anything, and neither did he. Then she went on down the row beside the barrow, with her hand on the rim, and did not look round.

"What was that," said Wyck.

"Nothing."

"It wasn't nothing. I'm sitting right here." Wyck picked up the bar. "It's all right. You don't have to tell me. I'll work it out. I'm cleverer than I look."

---

Senna's course was in the shed on Tuesdays and Fridays and some other days besides. That afternoon was one of the others. Toren pushed the chair.

Rook had said *Tuesdays*. It had not been a joke, exactly. Toren had decided to take it as a job.

The shed was not a shed. It was the long slope under the north end of the long building, going down, where the air got cool and the walls went thick and grey and there were no windows. At the bottom there was the wide door and the yellow line and the sign.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

"There's a tray," said the man who came out to meet them.

He was about forty, with rolled sleeves and grey hair cut very short, and ink on the side of one hand. He did not give a name. He looked at Toren's sling, and his wrapped hand, and at Senna in the chair.

"I know," said Toren.

He took the stone out of his pocket. It was still warm. He did not look at it. He put it in the paper tray and set the tray on the bench against the wall, and his hand did not want to let go of it, and he let go.

"It's not iron," said the man.

"No."

"Then it's the other thing." He did not seem to mind. "Same rule. Nobody's ever been able to tell me what the other thing does to the room, so the other thing stays out as well."

"What does the room do?"

The man looked at him. He seemed to decide something.

"Come to the line," he said. "You can look. You can't come in."

---

Toren stood at the yellow line and looked through the wide door while they took Senna in.

It was a long room with a low ceiling and a great deal of cable. In the middle of it was the machine. It was not beautiful. It was a thick pale ring standing on its edge, taller than a man, with a narrow bed running through the hole in the middle of it. Everything about it was old. The paint had worn off the corners where hands had gone for decades. There were patches on the casing where somebody had cut a panel out and bolted a different one in.

Behind it, along the back wall, there was a rack.

It ran the whole width of the room, floor to ceiling, in a steel frame. It was full of grey boxes, row on row. Each had a little window in the front, and a light behind the window. Most of the lights were a steady pale green. There were cables running from box to box along the bottom of the rack like the roots of a hedge.

"It reads," said the man, beside him. "It looks through her. That's the picture. The same thing looked at your friend's head, and the little lad's leg. And then, for her, it doses. It sets how much of the course goes in, and it measures the preparation for her marrow. It tells us how much is enough and how much would kill her."

"What's the rack?"

"The train." The man said it the way you name a horse you have worked for a long time. "Calibration train. Each of those is a reference. It's got a thing inside it that we know the true value of. The machine checks itself against them, one after another, before it does anything. If they agree, it's telling the truth. If they don't, it isn't."

"Do they agree?"

"Mostly," said the man.

---

They got Senna onto the narrow bed. She lay down on it with the blanket over her and her hands flat at her sides, looking at the ceiling.

"It sings at me," she said, to nobody. "I'd like that understood. It isn't knocking. It sings."

The man went to a desk by the wall inside the door. There was a screen on it that glowed a flat green. He did something with his hands, and the rack began to hum.

Toren felt it in his teeth before he heard it. The green lights along the rack went pale, one after another, left to right, like somebody walking along a row of windows with a candle. When the walk reached the end, the man looked at his screen.

He did not say anything. He did something else with his hands. The walk went along the rack again, left to right.

He looked at the screen a second time.

Toren watched his face. He had spent two months learning weather off people. This man's weather did not change much. But it changed.

"Is that right?" said Toren.

"It's right now," said the man. He did not look round. "It does that. The third row down, the fourth box along, wakes up slow. It did it twice last week. It did it in the summer, and it was fine for a month after."

"Is that bad?"

"It's old," said the man. "It's older than your grandmother. It was built before the Fall, in a place that knew what the Fall was going to do to things and built it anyway. Everything in it wakes up slow now and then. So do I." He did the thing with his hands again, and the machine began to sing. "Go and sit down, lad. Forty minutes. I'll call you."

---

Toren sat on the bench beside the paper tray.

He did not touch the stone. He listened to the machine sing through the wall. It was a high sound with a lower one under it, like wind in a wire.

He thought about his grandmother at the south gate, in the rain, on the tailgate with a stranger kneeling beside her in a grey coat. *What year did it happen?*

*2130.*

*Where were you?*

*Inside the fence line. Eleven days.*

*Which fence line?*

*The old plant at Caul Hill. I was fifty-three and I went in for the copper, and I knew what was in there, and so did every soul on that crew, and we went in anyway because it was copper.*

He had heard her say it and not thought about it since. There had been too much else to think about. Now he sat outside a room built by people who knew what the Fall would do, and listened to a machine older than his grandmother sing through a wall. He thought about her going into a place like that for copper at fifty-three, and coming out, and paying for it every day for twenty years.

He thought: *There's a room in this station she can't live without, and I've seen the inside of it now.* He did not know why that mattered. It seemed to.

He looked at the rack in his head. Third row down. Fourth box along. He did not know why he wanted to remember that either. He remembered it anyway.

---

At dusk, Rook sat on the step of eleven.

He had got himself down in three movements with his hand on the door frame, and he sat with his coat over his knees and his back very straight. Toren was on his bed inside with the door open, because the stove was hot and the evening was not. He could see Rook's back and the row beyond it.

Rook took something out of his coat and began to turn it over in his fingers.

Toren knew what it was. He had known since a campfire, weeks back up the road. It was a flat grey stone, small, rounded at the corners. It was the stone Vera Kess had carried at Homura, and died holding across a doorway, and Rook had taken up off the floor after. Rook turned it between his thumb and his first finger the way a man turns a coin he has carried so long he has forgotten it. His hand was too big for it. It went round.

Dessa had held that stone once. In a field, the morning her door came on.

Two doors down, past the dark door of twelve, somebody else was sitting on a step. It was the small woman from fourteen. Katori. Toren could just see her at the edge of the lamplight, with her back to her door frame and her cap pulled down. She was turning something over in her fingers too.

A slab of grey stone. Bigger than Rook's. The same way. Without looking.

Toren sat up.

---

Katori's hand stopped.

He saw it stop. He saw her head come up, very slowly, and turn. She looked down the row at the step of eleven. At the man on it.

Rook had the stone flat on his palm now, the way you hold a thing up to see it. The lamp at the end of the row was behind him. Toren could see what Katori could see. The stone was water-grey. On its two long edges there were two places worn smooth and a little darker than the rest. A thumb and a first finger had gone there, for years and years.

Katori stood up.

She put her own slab away inside her coat and buttoned the coat over it. She walked down the row. She went past the dark door of twelve. She stopped on the wet concrete a pace in front of the step of eleven, in the edge of the lamplight, with her hands at her sides.

Rook looked up.

Toren did not move. He did not breathe. He was in a doorway behind a man's back and it was not his, and he could not have looked away.

Katori was looking at the stone on Rook's palm.

"Where did you get that," she said.

---

Rook did not close his hand. Toren saw that. He did not put it away. He sat on his step with the stone flat on his palm and looked up at the small woman in front of him, a head shorter than most people, standing the way a fence post stands.

Something went across his face. Toren could only see the side of it. It was not surprise. It was the look of a man who has heard a voice in the next room that he had stopped expecting to hear anywhere.

"Off a floor," said Rook at last. "A long time ago."

Katori said a name.

It was one word. She said it quietly and without any weight, the way you say a thing you have not said out loud in a very long time, and have kept in the same place all that time so as to know where it was.

"Vera," said Katori.

Rook shut his eyes.

"Yes," he said.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Down the row, a stove ticked. The lamp stood at the end of the row.

"She had small hands," said Katori.

"She did." Rook opened his eyes. "I've had to hold it wrong."

"May I."

He did not answer. He only held his hand out a little further, flat, with the stone on it. The way you hold something out to a horse you are not sure will take it.

Katori took it.

She took it between her thumb and her first finger. Her thumb went to one of the two worn places on the long edge. Her finger went to the other. They went there by themselves, the way a foot goes into its own print in the mud. The stone lay in her small hand as if it had been cut for it.

Rook watched it sit there.

Toren could see his face now, a little. He saw it do something he would not have known how to describe. He did not try. He did not ever tell anybody he had seen it, and when Dessa asked him years afterward he said he had been asleep.

Katori held it for about three seconds. She did not look at it. Three seconds was about as long as either of them could stand.

"She was my sister," said Katori.

"I'm Rook," said Rook.

---

That was all he said for a while. Then:

"She talked about her people. She said they'd kept words. Their own words, for things, that nobody else had." He was looking at the stone in Katori's hand. "She never said she had a sister."

"She wouldn't have," said Katori. "She was the best of us. She'd not have wasted a word on the rest." Her voice did not change. "She was unbearable about it."

Rook made a sound. It was not a laugh. It was the place where a laugh would have been.

"She was," he said. "She was completely unbearable."

They were quiet. Then Rook said, very carefully, like a man putting something heavy down on a floor that might not take it:

"Her people gave the stone and the thing it's for the same name. The same word, on purpose. She told me so. In eleven years I've never met anybody who could tell me why."

Katori looked at him a long moment.

"I can tell you why," she said.

He waited.

"Not tonight," said Katori.

"No." He let out a long breath. "No. Not tonight."

---

She gave it back. She did it slowly. She held it out flat on her own palm the way he had held it out to her. He took it, and his hand closed round it the way it had learned to. Too big. Going round.

"You carried it," said Katori.

"Every day."

"Keep carrying it."

He looked up at her.

"I'm going to want to talk to you," said Katori. "I don't know when. Not soon. I've been holding this very hard for a long time, and I don't know how to put it down yet. I'm not going to learn it tonight on a step." She stood with her hands at her sides. "But I'm going to want to."

"I'm here four seasons," said Rook. "I signed."

Katori made a small sound. It was nearly a laugh too.

"So I've heard," she said.

She turned and walked away. She did not walk back to fourteen. She walked past it, past the doorway where Toren could see Callie sitting with her knees up, and round the end of the hut into the dark between the rows, where the lamp did not reach.

---

Rook sat on his step with the stone shut in his hand.

After a while he said, without turning round, "You're awake."

"Yes," said Toren.

"You heard."

"Yes."

Rook was quiet.

"She'll want somebody," he said. "Not me. Not tonight. Somebody who doesn't need anything from her." He was looking at the dark where Katori had gone. "It's not you either, Tor. You're mine. She'd see me on you."

Toren looked down the row. Callie had got up from her doorway. She stood there a moment, looking at the dark between the rows. Then she went round the end of the hut after Katori, slowly, one hand on her ribs.

"There," said Rook. "That's the one."

He did not get up. He sat on the step with Vera's stone in his fist and his back very straight, and Toren sat on his bed behind him in the doorway and did not say anything at all. There was nothing he could have said that would not have been his own, and none of it was his.

After a long time, Rook opened his hand and looked at the stone.

"Her sister," he said to it, very quietly. "Eleven years. Two doors down."

Then he put it away in his coat, and sat there until the lamp went out.
