# Chapter 22 — Jab: Terms

The Director came down the outside stair a second time on the thirty-eighth afternoon, and walked across the north yard and into Yard C, and stopped at the step of twelve.

Jab was sitting on it. He had come back from the long room at the middle of the day with everybody else and not gone in. Otto was inside, doing something to the stove that involved a great deal of careful noise. The row was empty. It was a cold clear day with the sun low over the berm, and the concrete of the row had not thawed since the morning, and the Director came along it in her grey coat with her spectacles on their cord. People in the yard behind her had stopped what they were doing to watch her go.

She did not have the man in the long coat with her. She had nobody. She stopped in front of the step and looked at him, and then she looked at the step of eleven next to it, which was empty, and sat down on it.

She did it carefully, the way a woman of sixty sits on a cold step, one hand on the door frame. When she was down she sat with her hands on her knees and looked along the row at the lamp on its pole at the end, unlit.

"I said I'd ask again," she said.

"You did."

"You were in a bed the first time. You're not in a bed now." She did not look at him. "I read Mr Hallet's sheet from the pen. And Sowerby's, which he wrote in his own hand again, and sent up the stair again, which he's now done twice in a fortnight and never before in thirty years. And Miss Wren's, which says you said *floor* at the forty-first minute and took your own hand off, and she didn't lift it." She took her spectacles off and held them. "My technician's alive. The station owes you for that. And for the holder. That's two."

Jab said nothing.

"So I'll ask," said the Director. "The same as I asked in the shed. The roll of the long building. A hut by it, four years settled, not twenty-eight days. Training under Miss Wren on her terms. And on call, when something comes through my gate that's worse and there's nobody else." She turned her head and looked at him. "That's what I want. It hasn't changed. I don't change what I want because a thing went well. I'd be no use if I did."

"And what'll you take?"

The corner of her mouth moved.

"You've been talking to the girl with the hook," she said. "Tell me what you'll give, and I'll tell you."

---

Jab looked at his hands.

They were steady. They had been steady for a week. There was a faint redness still in the creases of his right palm, from the pen, where Dee had drawn the last of the warm grit out of him and said *you'd have carried that home*. He looked at it for a while.

He had thought about this. He had thought about it on the cot in the shed at the first hour of the thirty-sixth morning, after Otto had come with the message about the ink, and on the step every night since. He had thought he would have it in order when she came. He did not. He had it the way you have a road you have walked once in the dark: every turn, and none of it in words.

"My brother," he said.

The Director waited.

"His bed," said Jab. "His care. That's clinical, you've said. Everybody's said. I believe it." He did not look up. "His bed's not the thing. It's where he sleeps after. When he comes out of that frame in the spring and he's done with the ward. Where he sleeps then. And every year till he's done growing, when he comes back to be measured." He made himself say the rest. "Whatever happens to me. On the hill. If I don't come back down that road. He's not reviewed. He's not conditional. He's not a household of one child that's got nothing on its sheet. He's settled. Till he's done growing."

"That's thirteen years," said the Director.

"Yes."

"You're asking me to settle a household of one child, who's four, for thirteen years, on the strength of a boy who's walking up a hill on the forty-first day that took an old woman's hands in eleven."

"I'm telling you that's the first thing," said Jab. "Before any of the rest."

The Director sat on the step of eleven with her spectacles in her hand and looked along the row for a long time.

"A child in a bed," she said at last, "is the cheapest thing this station does. It costs me less to keep your brother inside this berm for thirteen years than it costs me in paper to review him every twenty-eight days. I've done that sum. I did it the night you came in." She put her spectacles on and took a small book out of her coat, and a pencil, and wrote in it. "I'll not write it as a thing you've bought. I'll write it as a thing the station owes. There's a man in the east corridor with a white hand who's alive, and a woman in a coat in the next room who's alive. The station owes for both of them. I'll pay it to the boy in the bed." She finished writing. "That way it doesn't matter what you sign or don't. It's paid."

Jab looked at her.

"That's not what I asked," he said.

"It's better than what you asked," said the Director. "A debt I owe him can't be taken back if you go and do something I don't like. A bargain with you could." She closed the little book. "Go on. That was one."

---

"Dee," said Jab. "Miss Wren. I learn from her. I'll sit at her table every morning for as long as she'll have me. That's not signing. That's not your roll."

"Agreed."

"You write what I can do. On your line. The long one." He found the words coming more easily now, the way the first few steps of a road in the dark are the hardest and after that your feet remember. "You can write it all. The lean, and the warmth, and the pen. Write it the way Dessa writes things. What happened and how long." He looked at her. "You don't write that I'm yours."

"The register doesn't say whose," said the Director. "It never has. It's got eleven hundred names on it and not one of them says whose."

"Then write that it doesn't," said Jab. "On mine. Write that it isn't anybody's."

The Director looked at him for a moment. Then she opened the little book again and wrote a line, and turned it round so he could read it.

*His own.*

"Is that it?" she said.

"That's it."

"Go on."

"Nobody sends for me," said Jab. "Not in visiting hours. Not on the day of his picture. Not when he's had a bad night. You can ask. Mrs Lask can ask. Anybody can ask. I can say no, and it goes in the margin that I said it, and nobody's allowed to think it means anything but no." He stopped. "And I go to who I choose. Not who's on a list. I'll go to a lot of them. I'd go to most of them. I'll choose."

"You'll choose," said the Director. "And when you choose wrong? When you go to one and not the other, and the other one dies?"

Jab looked at the unlit lamp at the end of the row.

"Then I'll have chosen wrong," he said. "And it'll be mine. Not yours. Not the roll's. I'd rather carry that than be sent."

The Director was quiet for a long time.

"That's the one I'd have fought you on," she said at last. "In the shed, the first time. I'd have fought you hard. I'd have said a station can't run on a boy choosing." She wrote. "I'm not going to fight you on it. I've read what you did with nobody sending you, and I've read what the station did on the twenty-fifth with everybody where they'd been sent. I'd rather have you choosing."

"And the hill," said Jab.

"What about the hill?"

"I'm going because of the shed," he said. "Because of the machine, and Senna, and whoever comes through your gate next and needs a picture. Because a box is going yellow. Not because you've sent me."

"I haven't sent anybody," said the Director. "I've let. There's a difference, and I'd like you to know I know it." She closed the little book and put it away in her coat. "Is that all of it?"

"That's all of it."

"Then here's what I'll take," said the Director.

---

She did not say it at once. She sat on the step of eleven with her hands on her knees and looked along the row, and Jab saw her decide something, the way he had seen Dee decide things at the long table.

"I told them upstairs I had one more condition," she said. "That it wasn't Mr Voss's to meet. It's yours." She turned her head and looked at him. "When you come back, you'll sit at that table on the other side."

Jab did not understand.

"Miss Wren's table," said the Director. "The long one. In the shed that leaks. At Homura, she tells me, there was an old man who went up and down behind the benches with a stick. He's dead. She's the only one left who sat at his table, and she's twenty-five, and she can't sit on both sides of it for ever." She did not look away. "There'll be others. Not this winter. Next winter, and the one after. Somebody's going to come through my gate with a lean in them that tells them when their mother's worse, and a card that says it doesn't come when they call it. They'll need somebody at that table who's been where they are." She put her hand flat on her knee. "Once a week. Not on call. Not on my roll. At the table. You teach whoever comes, with whatever you've got."

"That's not yours to ask," said Jab.

"No," said the Director. "It's not. I'm asking it anyway. It's the only thing I want from you that I'm not allowed to take."

Jab sat on his step for a long time.

He thought about a woman on a gravel bar four hundred miles north with a baby on her back, looking at the ugliest blade anybody in the valley had ever seen, and saying *well, it looks like you.* He thought about his aunt at the smoking racks, who had not told him he was doing it wrong for the first hundred fish, because he had to find it. He thought about a room with a long table in it and a bucket going *tick* at the end, and somebody he did not know yet sitting on the bench with their hand on a paper, looking at a little grey heap and not knowing which part of the cold was theirs.

"Yes," he said.

The Director stood up. She did it carefully, one hand on the door frame of eleven.

"I'll ask again," she said. "About the roll. When you come back."

"Ask me again," said Jab. "When I'm back."

She nodded once and went away along the row toward the north yard. People in the yard stopped what they were doing to watch her go. At the corner of the row she stopped and turned round, small and square in the low sun.

"Mr—" she said, and stopped. "Jab. Come back."

Then she was gone.

---

The frame had been under his bed in twelve since the second day.

It had come back from the hatch in two pieces, washed and tagged, with the rest of the things they had taken off him at the north gate: the boots, the pack, the hide cover, the three twists of bark in their paper. He had put the boots on and the pack on the nail and the bark in the stove, a twist at a time, the way you use a thing you carried a long way for somebody else. He had put the frame under the bed. He had not taken it out. Every night for thirty-six nights he had lain on the bed with it under him and not taken it out.

He took it out on the thirty-eighth night, after the Director had gone.

The two halves lay on the floor of twelve by the stove, and Otto sat on his own bed and looked at them. They were ash, the whole lattice, split and shaved and lashed in two evenings in a house with nobody left in it. The hide bands were furred and pale where the wet had got at them on the road. The left shoulder lashing had three turns of boot-lace over a cord gone to fur. The curved ash rail that had carried Tonk's leg stood up off the lower half, and the hound had gone through the middle of the lattice on the north road with a crack like a shot and split it from the hip belt to the yoke.

"Can you mend it?" said Otto.

"I made it," said Jab.

It took him until the second hour of the morning.

He did not mend it to carry a person. He knew that before he began. He took the split lattice apart where the hound had broken it and did not try to put it back the way it had been. He cut new cross-pieces from a length of ash Otto went and got from the wood pile behind the long building, the same pile Pell had cut Callie's mother's stick from. He lashed them in, tight, and cut the ends short. *Tight and short.* He could hear his mother say it at the smoking racks, *he lashes tight and he doesn't cut his cords long enough*, and he cut them shorter than he had ever cut them, and she would have said nothing about it at all.

He made a rack of it. Eight sockets, two rows of four, from offcuts of yard timber Otto found in the scrap bins. Each one was lined with folded canvas, so that a jar would stand in it upright and not knock against its neighbour. He set the rack into the lattice where Tonk's seat had been, between the yoke and the hip belt, so that the weight of eight jars would ride on his hips and not his shoulders.

The curved ash rail he did not take off.

He looked at it for a long time. It stood up off the bottom of the frame at the angle it had stood at for eighteen days on the road, the rail that had carried his brother's bad leg so it did not hang. He did not need it. It was only in the way. He left it on and lashed the top row of the rack to it, so that the rail was the thing the jars leaned against.

At the first hour Wyck came in without knocking.

"I saw the light," he said, and stood in the doorway with his bar and his strapped arm and looked at the frame on the floor. He looked at it the way he had looked at Otto's barrow strap on the third day, all of it, and where the weight would go.

"You've got the belt too low," he said.

Jab looked up.

"For jars," said Wyck. "For a boy, that's where you want it. He's on your back. His weight's up high and it wants to go backward, so you want the belt low on the hips to hold it down." He came in and crouched beside the frame, carefully, with his arm against his chest. "Jars don't want to go backward. They want to go down. You want the belt up. On the bone. Here." He put his left hand flat on his own side, on the point of the hip. "And put the chest band under the yoke, not over. Same as the barrow. Your chest doesn't mind."

Jab moved the belt. He moved the chest band. Wyck watched him do it and did not say *better*.

"It was his," said Wyck, after a while. It was not a question.

"It was his."

Wyck nodded. He sat back on his heels and looked at the rail, lashed now with the top row of jars leaning against it.

"You kept that bit," he said.

"I kept that bit."

Wyck did not say anything else. He got up, carefully, and went to the door, and at the door he stopped.

"Six sand jars," he said. "Fill them tomorrow. Carry them round the yard all morning before you carry anything that matters. Your hips'll tell you what's wrong by the fourth hour." He went out. Then his voice came back through the door, flat. "I'd carry it for you if I could. I'd like that known."

---

Tonk did the chair on the thirty-ninth morning.

Jab had not known it was going to be that morning. Keel had not told anybody. She came down the ward at the ninth hour with her sleeves rolled and a nurse behind her pushing a chair he had never seen: low, with small wheels and a wooden board sticking out level from the front of the seat like a shelf, padded, at the height of a small boy's knee.

Tonk looked at it from his pillow.

"What's *that*?"

"That's a chair," said Keel. "It's yours. You're going to get into it."

"*Myself*?"

"Most of the way," said Keel. "The nurse is going to hold the board level, and hold your frame steady on it when it gets there, and that's all she's going to do. The rest is you. Your arms, and the bar, and your good leg." She looked at Jab in the chair between the beds. "And you're going to sit there."

Jab looked at her.

"You're going to sit in that chair with your hands behind your back," said Keel, "and you're not going to touch him. Not his arm, not his leg, not his frame. Not if he wobbles. Not if he shouts. If he falls, the nurse catches him, and she's caught more children than you've carried. If you put your hands on him, I'll send you out of the ward." She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way she said everything, fast and flat. "He's done this in his head eleven times this week. He told me. He's not done it with you there. So you're there, and you're not helping. That's your job this morning."

Jab put his hands behind his back.

---

It took eleven minutes.

Tonk reached up and got both hands on the bar and pulled, and came up off the pillow, the way he had every day since the twenty-fifth, shoulders and then back, until he was sitting. Then he did a thing Jab had never seen him do. He held on to the bar with one hand and put the other hand flat on the mattress beside him, and he shifted. He lifted his bottom off the bed on his arms and moved it three inches toward the edge, and set it down, and breathed, with his face red and his mouth shut in a line.

The frame came with him. The four pins and the bar on the outside of the leg moved three inches along the mattress. His face went white round the mouth when it moved, and then went red again.

"Good," said Keel.

He did it again. And again. Eleven inches, to the edge of the bed. Then he let go of the hanging bar and put both hands on the mattress behind him, and swung the good leg over the edge, and it hung down. The bad leg in its frame lay along the mattress still, pointing the wrong way.

"Now the leg," said Keel. "Both hands under the thigh. Not the frame. Never the frame. Under the thigh, above the knee, and lift it."

The nurse had wheeled the chair up close to the bed with its board level at the edge of the mattress. She stood with one hand on the board and the other hand ready, not touching.

Tonk put both hands under his thigh.

Jab sat in the chair with his hands behind his back. He could feel them there, both of them, knotted together at the wrists. They wanted to go forward. They wanted it more than he had wanted anything since the slab. His body knew this leg. It had carried this leg for eighteen days and every night for a month. It knew exactly where to put a hand under the knee and a hand under the heel, and lift, and turn. It knew how much it weighed with the frame on. It knew the sound his brother made when the pins moved.

He kept them behind his back.

Tonk lifted the leg. He lifted it with both hands under the thigh, an inch off the mattress, shaking, with the frame swaying, and he turned on his bottom on the edge of the bed, a little at a time, bringing the leg round with him. It took a long time. Twice he had to put the leg down and breathe. The second time he said a word he had learned from Wyck, and Keel pretended not to hear it.

The leg came round. It came off the edge of the mattress and onto the padded board, and the nurse put her hand on the frame to hold it steady on the board, and that was all she did.

"Now you," said Keel. "Down. Into the seat. On your arms."

Tonk put his hands on the arms of the chair. He lowered himself off the edge of the bed toward the seat, on his arms, with his good leg taking some of it and his bad leg on the board. His arms shook. He came down, slowly, and down, until his bottom was a hand's width off the seat and his arms would not do any more.

The nurse put her hand under him and took the last hand's width.

He was in the chair.

---

He sat in it.

He sat in it with his bad leg out straight in front of him on the board, in its frame, and his good leg hanging down, and his hands on the arms, and his face the colour of a tomato, and he looked round the ward. He had not seen the ward from this height in five weeks. He had seen it from a pillow. He looked at the window over his bed and the door at the end and Senna in the next bed and the ceiling, which was further off than it had been.

"I did the *chair*," he said.

"Most of it," said Keel.

"*All* of it."

"The nurse did the last hand's width," said Keel. "I watched."

Tonk thought about this.

"*Most* way," he said.

"Most way," Keel agreed. "That'll do." She looked at Jab. "You can take your hands out."

Jab took his hands out from behind his back. He found that his wrists hurt where he had been holding them. He sat in the chair between the beds with his hands on his knees and looked at his brother sitting up in a chair on his own, with a leg out on a board. He found he could not say anything.

It was the same thing as on the twenty-fifth day, when he had woken up in the sixth bed and seen Tonk sitting up on the bar. It was two things at once. There was the big thing, so big it filled his chest like water filling a boot. And there was the small sharp thing under it, like a splinter under a nail. *He didn't need me.* The first time, he had been asleep for it. This time he had been sitting a yard away with his hands tied behind his own back by nobody but himself, and his brother had not needed him. Not for the bar, or the edge of the bed, or the leg. Only the nurse, for the last hand's width.

"Jab," said Tonk.

"Mm."

"Did you *see*?"

"I saw," said Jab. "I saw all of it. I was right here."

"You didn't *help*."

"I didn't help."

Tonk looked at him from the chair with enormous satisfaction.

"Good," he said.

---

In the fourth bed, Senna lay against her pillows with her hands folded where she could see them.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "I counted. The nurse says eleven and a half. The nurse is wrong." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at Jab. "Your hands were behind your back the whole time. I watched them. I've never seen anybody hold their own hands so hard."

"I wanted to."

"I know you did," said Senna. "That's why it counted." She looked at Tonk in his chair. "I got into a chair once, the first week. By myself. Two steps. I can't do that now." She said it without any weight. "He'll do it by himself before the spring, and I'll be lifted by two men, and neither of us is going to make a face about it." She looked back at Jab. "That's how it goes, brother. Some of us are going up the hill and some of us are coming down it, and everybody gets a chair."

---

He should not have taken the frame to the ward. He knew that afterward.

He had not meant to take it to the ward. He had meant to take it across the north yard to the east shed, because Dee wanted to see it, and to load it with six jars of sand and carry it round the yard all afternoon the way Wyck had said. He had it on his back with the belt high on the bone of his hips and the chest band under the yoke, and six sand jars in the rack, and he came out of Yard C and along the side of the long building on his way to the yard.

The nurse had wheeled Tonk to the doors.

It was the first time he had been outside the ward since the night of the north road. Keel had said he could go as far as the doors in the chair, if it was not raining, and it was not raining. So the nurse had wheeled him down the ward past Mrs Lask, who had stood up, and out through the doors, and stopped the chair on the step at the front of the long building in a strip of low cold sun. And Tonk was sitting in it with his leg out on the board and a blanket over his knees and his face turned up to the sky, which he had not seen in five weeks.

He saw Jab.

He saw Jab coming along the side of the building with the frame on his back. And his face did the thing it had done at the gate on the first night, when he saw the lamp over the gate. It lit.

"You mended the *frame*!"

Jab stopped.

"You *mended* it," said Tonk. He was sitting forward in the chair, as far as the leg would let him. "It was *broken*. The hound broke it. It went in a *bin*. And you mended it." His voice went up. "It's got my *rail*."

"Tonk—"

"So I'm going," said Tonk.

He said it the way he said all the facts he had put together himself, with complete satisfaction, as if it were the end of a sum. Jab had mended the frame. The frame was for carrying Tonk. Jab was going somewhere. So Tonk was going.

"I've got *two* frames," said Tonk. "One on my leg and one on your back. So I can *definitely* go."

---

Jab took the frame off.

He took it off because he could not stand in front of his brother with it on his back and say what he was going to say. He undid the chest band and the hip belt, and slid out of the straps, and set the frame down on the cinders at the foot of the step with its six sand jars in the rack and the curved ash rail standing up at the top. Then he went up the step and crouched beside the chair, so that his face was lower than his brother's.

"No," he said.

Tonk looked at him.

"You're not going," said Jab. "I'm going. You're staying here."

"But you *mended* it—"

"I mended it to carry jars," said Jab. "Ash. In jars. Eight of them, in a rack, where you used to sit. Look." He pointed down at the frame on the cinders. "That's not your seat. It's a rack. It's for jars."

Tonk looked at the frame. He looked at the rack of six sand jars where his seat had been. He looked at the curved ash rail at the top, with the top row of jars leaning on it.

"That's my *rail*," he said.

"It's your rail. I kept it."

"Then why can't I *go*?"

Jab did the thing he had made a rule about on the road, and had kept every single time since, even when it cost him a night.

"Because of the pins," he said. "The nails in your leg. They go into the bone, and the bone's got to knit round them, and if the frame on your leg gets knocked about for six days on a road, the pins move, and the skin round them gets angry, like it did at the top one. And then you get hot, and it goes into the bone, and Keel has to go in again." He did not look away. "Six days there. Six back. On a cart. That's twelve days of the pins moving. Keel would say no. Sowerby would say no. I'm saying no."

"*You* carried me. On the road. With the nails."

"There weren't nails on the road," said Jab. "There was a broken leg on the road. And it went bad on the road, and it nearly killed you, and it took a woman with her sleeves rolled up two hours to take the bad bit out." He put his hand on the arm of the chair, near his brother's hand, not on it. "And where I'm going, there's a thing in the ground that makes people sick. It took Senna's hands. It'd take yours. I'm not taking you there. I'm not taking you anywhere you'd get sick."

"Then don't *go*," said Tonk.

---

His voice had gone high and cracked. His face was doing the thing it did now instead of crying, going red and very still, with his mouth shut hard.

"You'll be *asleep* again," he said.

Jab said nothing.

"You went *before*," said Tonk. "In the fog. And you came back on a bed with *wheels*. And your mouth was open. And I said *Jab* and you didn't say *anything*." He was not looking at Jab. He was looking at the frame on the cinders. "And I had to sit up by *myself*. And you weren't *there*."

"I know."

"And now you're going *again*. Further. For *twelve days*." He knew the number. He had heard it. He had been lying in a bed four feet from Senna for five weeks, and he heard everything. "And you'll come back on a bed with wheels. Or you *won't*."

Jab crouched on the step of the long building with his hand on the arm of the chair.

He did not lie. He had a rule about it. He did not say *I'll come back* the way you say it to a child, as if saying it made it so. He had said *they'd have to carry me out* on the first night at the gate, and it had been a lie, and his brother had said *you lied* with a four-year-old's terrible aim, and he had not lied to him since.

"I don't know," he said. "I'm going to try very hard. There's a paper with everything written on it, and a woman who'll turn us all round if I go below my floor once. Once. I said yes to that. I signed it." He looked at his brother's face. "That's the truest thing I've got. I'm going to try very hard, and there's somebody whose whole job is to make me come back."

Tonk did not answer.

"You did the chair," said Jab. "This morning. Most way. With me sitting there with my hands behind my back." He heard his voice go and let it. "You don't need me for the chair. You'll need me for other things. I'll be back for those."

Tonk turned his face away.

He turned it to the wall of the long building, as far as he could in the chair with his leg out on the board, and looked at the concrete, and did not say anything at all. Jab crouched beside him for a long time. The nurse stood behind the chair with her hands on the handles and looked at the sky.

"I'll take him in," said the nurse at last, quietly.

"Yes."

She turned the chair. Tonk kept his face to the side, away from Jab, the whole way back through the doors and down the ward. He did not look round once. Jab stood on the step and watched the doors swing shut behind the chair.

Then he went down the step and put the frame on his back again, and did up the belt high on the bone of his hips, and the chest band under the yoke. He carried six jars of sand round the north yard until the fourth hour, and his hips told him nothing was wrong at all.

---

Dee measured him at the long table that evening, three times.

It was the last measuring before the gate. She had said so in the morning. A quarter of a Handful each time: in, the way he did it now, holding it high in the palm with his other hand flat on the table, forty breaths before it tried to pass. Then settle. He could settle now, a little. Not the way Callie did it, all the way down. Enough that it lay in the bottom of the bucket and stayed when the drip went *tick* at the far end of the shed. Then spend, into Rook's hands, through the jar, the way he would at the fence. Then vent, into the sand. Then rest, for a quarter of an hour, with his hands on his knees.

Three times. Dee watched his face the whole time.

"Clean," she said, after the third. "All three. Nothing left in your palms. Nothing gone below the line." She wrote. "Go down and tell me."

He went down.

The bucket was there. A hand of dark in the bottom of it, and a bit more. The line scratched on the inside a finger up from the bottom, where it had always been, where somebody who had been there before him had scratched it and known.

"A hand and a bit," he said. "Floor's a finger. Same as the fourteenth."

"Same floor," said Dee. "More above it. Not because you're bigger. You're not. Because you've stopped spilling it on the way in, and you've stopped letting it lodge on the way out, and you rest." She closed the book. "That's working depth. Two weeks of it. At Homura it would have been twelve. You've done two, and you're going."

"Is it enough?"

"It's what there is," said Dee. "It's more than you had on the slab. It's more than Elias had at your age, on a riverbank, with me." She looked at him across the long table. "You'll work second. After me. Twenty minutes by the glass. Then watch. Then reserve. You'll call your own floor."

"I did."

"I know you did," said Dee. "I'm telling you I expect it."

Rook was on the step of eleven when he got back to the row.

He was sitting with his back against the door frame and his wrapped hands on his knees, and the lamp at the end of the row had come on, flat and white. Jab sat down on the step of twelve.

"Callie's mother told me," said Rook. "About the doors of the long building. Everybody in the station walks past her window twice a day." He looked at the lamp. "He's four."

"He's right," said Jab. "I will be asleep again. On a hill. Or I won't."

Rook did not say anything for a while.

"I went to Homura at fourteen," he said. "You know that. To buy my mother a doctor twice a year." He turned his wrapped hands over on his knees. "I told her I'd be back at harvest. She was at the table with her hands in flour, and she said *go*, and didn't get up. She didn't look round. I stood in the door for about a minute waiting for her to say *don't*. She didn't." He was quiet. "I was angry with her for that for about four years. I thought she didn't mind."

"Did she?"

"I found out later she'd sat at that table with her hands in the flour for an hour after I'd gone," said Rook. "My brother told me. She didn't move. She'd made up her mind to say *go* and not get up, because if she got up she'd have said *don't*." He looked at Jab. "I've been glad for sixteen years she didn't get up."

Jab sat on his step.

"He's not going to say go," he said. "He's four."

"No," said Rook. "He's not. He's going to be angry with you for twelve days and then some. You'll have to carry that as well as the jars." He shut his eyes. "It's lighter than the other thing. The thing where he says *don't* and you stay. I promise you that. I've carried both."

---

At the tenth hour, when the ward turned the visitors out, Jab went up anyway.

Mrs Lask did not stop him. She looked at him over her spectacles and went back to her list. He went down the ward between the beds in the half-dark with the small lamp turned low at the far end.

Tonk was lying on his back in the fifth bed with his face to the wall. His chair stood at the foot of the bed with the board sticking out. The long cord ran from his wrist across the gap to the rail of the empty sixth bed, where the knot was, the one the nurse had tied twice, that nobody was allowed to untie.

Jab stood between the beds.

"Tonk," he said.

His brother did not turn round. His breathing was not a sleeping breathing. Jab knew every kind of breathing his brother did. He had counted them on the road for eighteen days.

"I'm going on the forty-first," said Jab. "In the morning. I'll come and see you before. At the eighth hour."

Nothing.

He stood there a while longer. Then he put his hand, very lightly, on the cord where it crossed the gap between the beds, halfway, not near either end. He did not pull it. He only put his hand on it, the way you put your hand on a rope to feel what the man at the other end is doing.

It did not move.

In the fourth bed Senna lay with her hands folded and her eyes on the ceiling.

"Milo Faust," she said, to the ceiling, the way she said it every night.

Nobody said it with her.

She waited. Jab heard her wait. Then she said, quietly, to nobody, "That's all right. He's angry. You can't say a name when you're angry. It comes out wrong." She shut her eyes. "He'll say it tomorrow."

Jab took his hand off the cord and went out past Mrs Lask, who did not look up, and down the frozen street in the lit bits under three lamps that would go out at the first hour. At the loose grating between the second pole and the third he stepped wide without thinking about it.

On the step of twelve he went down, once, to measure.

A hand and a bit. A finger from the bottom, the line. Above the line, the dark, still and his and not going anywhere.

*Mine*, he thought. He did not say it out loud. He had said it on the north road into the dark with his brother behind him, and on the slab in the fog with his hand on a woman's chest. It had meant *they come at me*, both times. It meant something else tonight, and he did not know yet what, and he sat on the step in the cold and did not try to find out.
