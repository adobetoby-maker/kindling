# Chapter 25 — Callie: Two Steps

By the end of the first mile Callie knew the order was wrong.

She had walked it in the north yard a dozen times. Pell at Duchess's head, then Miss Wren, then Rook, then Jab with the frame, then Callie, then Toren last. They had walked it round the whitewash lanes with sand in the jars and the cart creaking empty, and it had looked like a line. It had looked like something that would hold.

The old north road was not the north yard.

It ran north out of the Sag in two ruts with a hump of frozen grass between them, and the ruts were full of ice that had set in ridges overnight. Duchess did not care about the ridges. She walked the way a sound horse walks on a cold morning when she has been kept in a stable for six weeks. She walked as if the road had been built for her and she meant to see the end of it by noon. Pell walked at her head with the lead rope loose in his hand, and the cart came after her with the wheels cracking the ice, and the gap between the tailboard and Rook opened a pace, and then another.

Callie watched it open. She could not help watching it. It was a thing that was happening on the ground.

Jab was two paces in front of her. He had the frame high on the bone of his hips the way Wyck had shown him, with the chest band under the yoke. The eight jars stood in their rack where Tonk's seat had been, grey to the shoulder, and the curved rail at the top had the top row leaning on it. He walked well. He had walked four hundred miles with a frame on his back. But he had walked them with his brother in it, and his brother had weighed less than eight Flasks of ash and the jars they were in. Callie could see the difference in his stride. It was shorter than it had been at the gate. He was taking the ruts carefully, one foot and then the other, like a man going down a stair with a tray.

Rook was beside him on his left, walking a careful straight walk with his hands at his sides. He did not look back.

Behind her, Toren said nothing at all. She knew he was counting.

"Pell," said Miss Wren, at the second milestone.

Pell stopped. He did it without looking round, the way he did most things, with one word to Duchess and a hand on her neck, and the cart creaked to a halt forty paces ahead of Rook.

---

They came up to it in ones and twos. Nobody said anything about the forty paces. Miss Wren stood by the tailboard with her book under her arm and watched them come, and when Toren had arrived last and stopped, she turned the brass glass over on the flat of the tailboard.

"Ten turns," she said. "Then we'll talk about what just happened. Sit down if you can sit."

Jab could not sit.

Callie saw him work that out. He looked at the frost-white verge, and at the jars on his back, and at the ground. Nothing clean went on the ground. It was the first thing Miss Wren had said about the jars in the shed, and she had said it every day since. Clean on a rack or a bench or a shelf, dirty on the floor, and the road was a floor. If he took the frame off, eight jars of lent ash would sit on the frozen grass of the old north road, and every time he did it, somebody would have to lift the whole of it back onto him. So he stood in the ruts with the weight on his hips and his thumbs hooked in the chest band and did not say anything.

Pell looked at him for a moment. Then he went round to the back of the cart and dropped the tailboard.

It came down on its chains and hung level, at about the height of a man's belt. Pell tested it with his hand. He moved two rolls of canvas to one side of it and made a gap.

"Back up," he said.

Jab looked at him.

"Back up to it," said Pell. "Slowly. Let the rack come down on the board. Don't take the belt off." He put his hand flat on the tailboard. "She'll take the weight. The frame stays on you. The jars don't touch the road."

Jab backed up to the cart. The bottom of the rack met the edge of the tailboard, and he let his knees go a little, and the board took the frame. Callie saw the weight go off his hips. She saw his shoulders come down half an inch and his face change, very slightly, the way her mother's face had changed at the weighbridge the first time she sat on the stool.

"Oh," said Jab.

"Every halt," said Pell. "You come to the tailboard. Nobody else uses it." He went back to Duchess.

Callie looked at the tailboard, and the chains, and the frame resting on the edge of it.

It was a thing her grandfather would have done. It was the kind of thing that had been done at the scale house at Thistle Ford a hundred times a day by people who did not think about it. Get the weight onto something that is not you. Don't put down what you'll have to pick up again. She had thought of the cart as the thing that carried the lead box and the home jars and the camp. She had not thought of it as a thing a person could lean on.

---

"It was forty paces," said Miss Wren.

She said it at the eighth turn of the glass, with all of them round the tailboard. She did not say it to anybody in particular.

"It'll be sixty by noon," said Pell. "The horse walks at the horse. She's been in a box six weeks."

"Then the horse is wrong," said Rook.

"The horse is never wrong," said Pell. "The horse is a horse. You don't argue with her. You put her where she can't be wrong." He looked at Jab resting against the tailboard. "A column walks at its slowest. That's him, with eight on his back. Everybody walks at him. The horse walks at him too."

"And me," said Rook.

Nobody answered that.

"And you," said Miss Wren, after a moment. She did not look at him. "I watched you. You were keeping up with him and you were paying for it. You kept your shoulders square for a mile so nobody would see the back." She opened her book. "You'll walk beside him. Not in front. Beside."

Rook looked at her. Then he looked down the road and did not argue, which Callie had never seen him do.

"And Callie in front," said Pell.

Callie looked up.

"You read the road," said Pell. "That's on the paper. The floor inside the fence and the road outside it. You can't read the road from behind a frame." He put his hand on Duchess's neck. "Twenty paces in front of her. Look at what the wheels will go over, not what you'll go over. You'll know the difference."

"I'll know it," said Callie.

So they made a new order, standing in the ruts at the second milestone with the glass running. Callie out in front, twenty paces ahead of Duchess. Pell at Duchess's head. The cart. Jab at the tailboard, so that every halt was already there when it came. Rook on Jab's left. Miss Wren behind them with the glass and the book, where she could see every one of them without turning her head. Toren last, as runner.

Pell wanted halts by the milestones. Every second stone, he said, and ten turns of the glass. Miss Wren wanted halts by the glass. Rook wanted to stop when the frame said so. They argued about it for two turns, not loudly, while Jab rested his rack on the tailboard and watched them.

"Two stones," said Toren, from behind.

They looked at him.

"Two stones and ten turns," he said. "And if Jab wants a halt before two stones, he says *board*, and we stop, and nobody asks him why." He was not looking at anybody. He was looking down the road. "That way it's a number and it's him. Both."

Miss Wren looked at him for a moment. Then she wrote it down.

"Say it," she said to Callie. "Before we go."

Callie did not ask what. She had known since the gate that it would be her. It was the only kind of counting she was allowed now, and she was good at it.

"Road," she said. "One Flask. Eight Handfuls. On the cart. None gone." She looked at Jab's back. "Inside, eight Flasks. Sixty-four. In the frame. None gone. Home, three Flasks. Twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." She stopped. "Settled. A Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in you."

"And the counter."

Callie took the counter out of its canvas bag and turned the switch on the side. It ticked. One, and nothing, and two close together. She held it at the level of her chest and counted ten breaths.

"Seven," she said. "It's the room."

"It's the road," said Miss Wren, and wrote it. "Every halt. The same words. Out loud."

Callie put the counter back in the bag. She knew, putting it back, whose words they were. They were her mother's, on the floor by the stove with the store book open on her knee when she could not sleep. *Thirty-one Flasks and a Handful. Four of them thin.* The same words every night, read out so they were said somewhere and not only in her mother's head.

She went out twenty paces in front of Duchess and began to read the road.

---

It was not hard reading, on the first day. That was the strange thing.

The old north road was a road people had used. Callie could see it in the ruts, which were old and deep and full of ice, and in the stones that had been put into the worst holes at some time and pressed down by wheels until they were part of the road. Somebody had cleared a fallen branch off it and dragged it into the ditch. The branch was grey and soft with a winter or two of rot. Nobody had been this way since, not with a cart.

She read it for the wheels. That was what Pell had said, and he was right, and it changed what she saw. For herself she would have seen a road. For the wheels she saw where the ruts went off at an angle round a frost heave. She saw where the hump between them was higher than the axle, and where the verge was soft under the white.

At the first bridge she stopped.

It was not a bridge, really. It was a culvert under the road with a low stone parapet on each side. The road went over it on a hump. She walked out onto the hump and stood on it, and looked down at the stones on the downstream side, and at the water coming out under them.

The water was brown. It came out fast and noisily, too fast for the size of the ditch, and there was a fan of bright gravel spread out below the mouth of the culvert that had not been there long enough to grow anything.

She walked back off the hump.

"Pell," she said, when Duchess came up. "Go on the left of it. The high side. The right-hand wheel wants the parapet."

Pell looked at the culvert and then at her.

"Why?"

"There's been a lot of water through it, lately, and fast. There's new gravel below it. The stones on the downstream face are standing proud of each other, the way they do when something's come out from behind them." She pointed with the hook's handle. "It'll take the cart. I think it'll take the cart. But if it's going to go, it'll go on the right."

Pell looked at the stones for a long moment. Then he took Duchess over the hump on the left, with the right-hand wheel a hand's width from the parapet, and it took the cart. Nothing moved.

"Right," said Pell, and walked on.

That was all. Callie went out in front again. She found that her face was hot, and she did not know why, and then she did. It was the first time since the plain that she had read a piece of ground for somebody who had simply done what she said. He had not argued or asked her to be sure. He had only asked *why*, which was different. At the Sag there had always been Katori, and Katori had always looked at the ground herself first.

She thought about her mother on the stool at the weighbridge, reading the beam before the old man could.

It was the ninth hour of the morning, near enough. At the long building the doors would be open, and Mrs Lask would be at her list. The fourth bed on the left, where her mother came every morning at the eighth hour for her pills, would be made up tight again and empty. Her mother had walked into the ward on her stick this morning, and let go of Callie's hand first. Then she would have walked back out again past Mrs Lask, with Katori on one side of her and Ines on the other, not holding. And now it was an hour past the pills and Callie did not know how the hand was. She did not know if the right leg had held on the step of fourteen.

She did not know.

She found, walking out in front of the cart on the old north road, that she was holding the strap of the counter bag. She had it in her left fist where it crossed her chest, tight, the way she had held the conduit on the hill with the wagon on it. She had not known she was doing it.

She opened her hand.

The strap fell back against her coat. Her hand hung at her side, open, with the cold on the palm.

It was harder here. She had not expected that. She had thought, on the step of fourteen the night before with her mother asleep behind her, that the hard part was the letting go, and that once it was done it would be done. It was not like that. On the stool at the weighbridge she could have seen the step of fourteen. On the old north road there was only the next culvert, and the next, and the whole of the road between her and a bed she could not see, and she had to let go of it again every few hundred paces. Every time, it was a little further off.

She read the road. It was the one thing she could do about any of it.

---

At noon Miss Wren measured their floors.

She did it at the tailboard with Jab's rack resting on it and the glass running, two fingers on the inside of each wrist in turn, the way she had at the gate. Callie held her hand out without thinking.

She held it out palm up.

It was her mother's way. It was how her mother held her hand out on the ledge of the hut window, or on the blanket, when she wanted Callie's hand in it. Callie had held her hand out like that at the stove every night for a month, and on the road for four hundred miles before that. She did it now for Miss Wren at the tailboard without deciding to. Then she saw what she had done and felt her face go hot again.

Miss Wren looked at the hand. She did not say anything about it. She put two fingers on the inside of the wrist, where she would have put them anyway, and looked away down the road while she counted.

"Where it was," she said. "Your own and the stone." She let go. "You've been holding something."

"The strap."

"I saw," said Miss Wren. She wrote something in her book. "Carry it in your pocket if you have to hold something. Not across your chest. If you go down with a strap across your chest, you go down on the counter." She moved on to Toren.

They ate at the tailboard. It was bread and cold meat from the long building's kitchen and a twist of salt, and water from the skins, and it was good. Duchess ate out of a nosebag with her eyes half shut. Rook sat on the edge of the tailboard beside Jab's rack, very carefully, and got up again very carefully when it was time. It took him two movements. Nobody counted them out loud. Callie counted them in her head and was ashamed of herself, and then was not, because Toren was counting them too. She could see him doing it.

---

In the afternoon the wind got up from the west.

It came across a long field on the left of the road that had been ploughed at some time and never sown, and had gone back to dirt and thistle. The wind took the top off the dirt and brought it across the road in a low brown haze. It got into the eyes and the teeth and the folds of the coat. Duchess put her head down and walked into it. Callie turned her face half away and read the road out of the corner of her eye.

Rook came up beside her.

She had not heard him come. He had left Jab at the tailboard and come up past the cart and past Duchess at his careful straight walk, and he was beside her now, twenty paces in front of the horse, with his collar turned up against the grit.

"Miss Wren said beside Jab," said Callie.

"Miss Wren's watching me do it," said Rook. "She'll tell me when I'm to go back. I wanted to see what you see." He looked at the brown haze going across the road. "That's what it'll be like up there."

"Dust?"

"Dust off a place that's worse than a field." He put his thumb to the corner of his eye and took it away grey. "The kind you don't get off with spit. Senna's crew washed at the river every night because of it. It's what's on the ground that gets onto you. That, and what you breathe." He was quiet for a few steps. "Your Ward stops a hound. Does it stop that?"

Callie looked at the haze.

"I don't know," she said. "I've never tried it on dust."

"Try it."

"Miss Wren says every hold's paid for."

"She does," said Rook. "And she's right. And we've got six days of road to learn on, and no yard at the end of them." He looked at her. "Ask her."

Callie stopped and let Duchess come up to her, and let the cart come up to her, and walked back along it to Miss Wren.

Miss Wren had heard every word. Callie could see she had. She walked behind the cart with the glass in one hand and the book under her arm, and she did not stop walking.

"Off the stone," she said. "Not your own. A little, and then stop, and you'll say how much, out loud, before you do anything else. Tonight you'll settle back what you spent, out of the road jar, and Callie will say that too." She looked at Callie. "That's the price of learning on a road. It's paid in the same ash you'll want at the fence. Every hold you practise here is a hold you haven't got at the stair. I'd like you to know that before you decide."

Callie thought about it for the length of the cart.

"I'll try it," she said.

---

She did it on herself first, because that was the one she knew.

She walked beside Jab at the tailboard, on his right, on the windward side where the grit came across. She put the hook over her shoulder out of the way, and she reached for the stone at the bottom of the well and did not shut her hand over it. She let the Ward come up out of the stone and into her. Not into the steel. Into her.

It came up through her heels, the way it always came, and over her.

The grit stopped.

It stopped hitting her face. She could see it going across the road in its low brown haze and not getting to her. It met something a hand's width from her skin and went round it, the way water goes round a stone in a stream. She could hear it. It made a small dry hiss against the Ward, like sleet on a window.

She took one step.

The Ward held. She felt it hold, the whole shape of it round her, with the weight of her coming down through her front foot into the road.

She took another.

And as her back foot came up off the road, the Ward tore.

It did not go out. It tore, low down at the back, the way a sheet tears when you pull it off a line one-handed and it catches on the peg. She felt it go ragged behind her heel and then all up the back of her, and the grit came in through the tear and hit her in the back of the neck. The whole shape of it went out like a lamp.

She stood in the road with the grit in her eyes.

"Two steps," said Toren, from the back.

"I know it was two steps."

"It was two steps in the yard," said Toren. "On the twentieth. Dessa wrote it."

"I *know*, Toren."

Jab, at the tailboard, made a small sound that might have been a cough.

Callie did it again. This time she did not lift her back foot. She kept both feet flat on the road and slid them forward, one and then the other, like somebody going across a frozen pond who does not trust the ice. The Ward came up. The grit went round her. She shuffled one foot forward, flat. It held. She shuffled the other. It held. She shuffled the first.

It held for three shuffles. On the fourth her heel came up a fraction of an inch off a ridge of ice, and it tore.

Rook laughed.

He did not laugh unkindly. He laughed the way he had laughed once at the gate, low and careful, the way a man laughs with a hole in his back. He was walking beside Jab with his collar up and his hands at his sides, and he laughed at the sight of her shuffling up the old north road like an old woman on a wet floor. After a moment Callie laughed too, because she could see it. She could see exactly what it looked like.

"It wants ground," said Rook.

She stopped laughing.

"It's always wanted ground," said Rook. "Everything you've ever held had ground under it. The hook on the scarp. The rail at the jam. The pipe. The rack. Your own feet in the lane, standing. The minute there's no ground under a bit of it, that bit goes." He looked at her. "You're standing in it, and then you're lifting half of what it's standing on."

"The wagon was on a hill," said Callie.

"The hill was ground."

Callie walked for a while with the grit in her teeth.

"How much?" said Miss Wren, from behind.

Callie went down to look. She did not like the answer, and she said it anyway.

"A quarter," she said. "Out of the stone. For about five steps, all told. On me." She heard it. It was more than a turn and a half of the glass in the hook, for five steps. "Settled, three quarters."

"Three quarters," said Miss Wren, and wrote it. "It's three times the price on you. You knew that."

"I knew it."

"Now you know it on a road," said Miss Wren. "That's different. That's enough for today."

"I want to try it in the hook."

"Tomorrow," said Miss Wren. "One thing a day. You've learned the thing today. It wants ground. Sleep on it." She turned the glass. "And tonight you'll settle a quarter out of the road jar, and you'll say it, and the road'll be seven and three quarters. That's the day's price."

Callie walked in front of the cart for the rest of the afternoon and did not try anything. She read the road for the wheels. She did not hold the strap. She thought about her own two feet on the road, one of them always coming up, and about the Ward, which did not like it.

---

At dusk they came to the place where Senna had said to camp.

Pell had it on his route sheet. He had read it out at the gate in the north yard and at the noon halt, in the flat voice he used for a distance. Senna had dictated it on the thirty-second day, and Toren had written it in the book as she said it, not tidied. *The twentieth stone. A drovers' fold on the left, stone, with a spring above it. Good water. We slept there the first night going up and the last night coming down, and nothing came.*

The twentieth stone was there. It was a short grey post at the side of the road, leaning, with *20* cut in it and moss in the cuts.

The fold was there too. It was on the left, fifty paces off the road, a square of stone wall about the height of a man's chest, with the dark of a wood behind it on the rising ground. It was the right shape and the right size and in the right place.

It was not a place anybody could sleep.

The wall on the road side had come down. It had not been knocked down. It had slumped outward all along its length, the way a wall goes when the ground under it has gone soft, and lay in a long low spill of stone. The spring above the fold had burst at some time in the last twenty years and come down through it. Callie could see the water standing in the fold in the last of the light, flat and black, with the tops of old nettles sticking up out of it.

And along the ditch below it, between the fold and the road, there was a line of grey.

Callie stopped.

She did not have to go closer. She had seen that grey every morning of her life on the plain from the age of eleven. It lay in a thin crooked line along the bottom of the ditch, in the silt, the way it lay where something had come up out of the wet and moved along a bottom and come apart there, or gone on. A husk line. And another beside it, fainter, older. And beyond the fold, where the ditch went under the road in a culvert, there was a darker place in the ditch where the silt had been turned over and not settled.

Duchess stopped.

Nobody had asked her to. Pell had not said a word. She stopped in the road twenty paces short of the fold with her head up, and put her ears back flat against her skull, and would not go on.

Pell put his hand on her neck. He did not pull on the lead.

"She doesn't like dogs," he said quietly. "She'll hate hounds. If she stops and puts her ears back and won't go on, it's not her being difficult." He looked at the ditch. "It's something in the ditch."

Callie heard the weighbridge girl say it. She heard it word for word, standing by the gate of the north yard in the frost that morning with her plaster held against her chest. *Bring her back.*

"Husk lines," said Callie. "Two. One of them from the last day or so, one older. They come up out of the culvert, there, and go along the ditch past the fold. There's been something in the fold." She looked at the water. "It's a place where they come up. Like the Sag. A little one."

Nobody said anything for a while. The light was going. The dark of the wood above the fold had gone from green to black while they stood there.

Pell took the route sheet out of his coat and looked at it. He did not need to. He knew what it said. He looked at it anyway, at Senna's line in Toren's careful hand, and at the fold in the dusk with the water standing in it.

"Twenty years," he said.

"We don't sleep here," said Miss Wren.

"No," said Rook. He was standing at the tailboard beside Jab with his hands at his sides, looking at the ditch. "We don't sleep anywhere we haven't made ourselves. Not on this road. Not with what's on that cart." He looked up the road, past the fold, to the north.

Callie looked where he was looking.

A quarter of a mile on, the road went up over a low rise. On the top of the rise, against the last grey in the sky, there were two black lines standing up at an angle to each other. They were the corner of a building that was not there any more. It was two walls meeting, with nothing on the other two sides.

"Where, then?" said Callie.

Nobody answered her at once. Behind her, at the back of the cart, she heard Toren begin to count them under his breath. He did it the way he had at the north lip of the Sag. *Pell. Dee. Rook. Jab. Callie.* And after a moment, *Duchess*, as if the horse were one of them, which she was.

"Six," he said. "And a horse."

Pell turned Duchess's head toward the rise, and she went, with her ears still back, walking faster than she had walked all afternoon. Callie went out twenty paces in front of her into the dusk and read the road for the wheels.
