# Chapter 27 — Jab: Choosing

The old line was good walking. That was the first thing Jab learned about it, and he learned it with his hips.

Somebody a long time ago had wanted it to stay where it was put. The bed of it was raised a man's height above the fields in most places, and it was made of broken stone, sharp-edged and grey, packed down so hard that seventy years of weather had not moved it. Grass grew through it in tufts. Thistles grew through it. But under the grass and the thistles the stone was still there, and the cart went along it without a lurch, and a man with eight jars on his back could put his feet down without looking.

The iron posts went along the north edge of it, one every twenty paces. They were as high as his chest, square, rusted to the colour of old blood, with a bent arm at the top where something had once been hung. Some of them leaned. Some had fallen and lay in the grass beside the bed. Most of them stood.

He counted them. He did not mean to. It was what the road did to people. Toren counted the party and Callie counted the ash, and Jab found by the middle of the morning that he was counting iron posts. Twenty paces, a post. Twenty paces, a post. At every hundredth post, Pell said, there had been a number, and there was, sometimes, on a little plate that had mostly rusted away.

At the second halt he backed up to the tailboard and let the rack come down on it, and put his thumbs in the chest band, and felt the weight go off his hips.

His hand went to his coat.

It went there on its own, the way it had gone to the cord on the road in, a hundred times a day, to feel whether it was taut. There was no cord now. There was a small hard shape under the cloth on the left side, over the place under his breastbone. He put his fingers on it through the coat and felt the shape of it. The body. The tail. The place where one fin had broken off before Tonk was born.

He took his hand away.

It was not a charm. He knew that. Tonk had not given it to him so that nothing would happen. Tonk had given it to him so that he would bring it back. It was the other end of the cord, the end Tonk had kept on his own fist, and it did not make him braver. If anything it made him more careful, which was, he thought, what Tonk had meant.

*Don't eat inside.*

He would not eat inside.

---

At the ninth hour Toren stopped walking.

It was not a halt. Nobody had said *board*, and it was not two stones since the last. Toren simply stopped, at the back, the way a man stops who has heard his name called a long way off. He stood on the stone bed of the old line with his left arm in its cuff against his chest and his face turned back east, the way they had come.

Jab saw it because he was at the tailboard and could see back past Dee.

Nobody said anything. Dee looked round and saw Toren standing and did not say *walk*. Rook looked round. Callie, out in front, did not look round, because she could not see him from there. But she slowed, and Jab saw her slow. After a while Toren started walking again, and caught up, and took his place at the back, and nobody said anything about it at all.

It was the forty-third day. At the ninth hour two orderlies would be lifting Senna from the bed into the chair.

Jab thought about the ward.

He thought about the fifth bed on the right with the chair at the foot of it, and the sixth bed, empty, with the rail bare where the knot had been for sixteen days. He thought about Tonk in the fifth bed at the ninth hour, watching the orderlies take Senna. Watching her go down the ward in the chair, and the fourth bed empty beside him for two hours. And nobody in the chair between the beds.

Tonk would be all right. He had the nurse, and the bar, and Mrs Lask, and his cord wound round his own fist. He would say *Milo Faust* with Senna tonight when she came back, if he was not still angry. He had done the chair in nine minutes without Jab. He was, Jab knew, perfectly all right.

He put his hand over the fish again and did not take it away for a while.

---

At noon the old line went into water.

They saw it from half a mile off, because the line was raised and the land round it was flat. The land went down, very gently, into a long wide bottom, a mile across, and the bottom was not a field. It was grey water with the light on it, with dead grass sticking up out of it in pale lines and a few black thorn trees standing in it up to their knees. The old line went down into it on its raised bed, and the bed went under. The iron posts went on. They stood in the water in their line, one every twenty paces, getting shorter as the water got deeper. In the middle of the bottom only the bent tops of them showed. Then they came up again on the far side, a mile off, getting taller, and went on west.

Pell stopped Duchess at the edge.

He took the atlas out of its oilcloth and opened it against her neck. Jab came up to the tailboard and rested the rack, and they all stood and looked at the water.

"It's the line," said Pell. He put his finger on the red. "Straight across. There's no water on the atlas."

"The atlas is seventy years old," said Rook.

"The atlas is seventy years old," Pell agreed. "Senna's twenty." He took out the route sheet. "*Dry to the second bridge,*" he read. "*The line's good all the way to the hill. Keep to it.*" He folded the sheet. "Twenty years ago she walked straight across this on stone."

"It's new," said Callie.

She had gone down to the edge of the water where the line went into it. She was crouching on the last of the dry bed with the hook across her knees, looking at the water and the grass and the edge of the bed where it went under.

"It's new water," she said. "The grass in it's dead, not drowned. It was growing here last summer. It's been under since the autumn. There's a line of bright dirt along the edge, where the water's come up over something and cut it." She put her hand on the stone of the bed. "And the bed's gone soft where it goes in. Look. The stones at the edge are sitting in mud, not on each other." She looked out across the water to where the post-tops showed. "It'll be deep in the middle. Deeper than the posts are short. And if the bed's gone soft here, it'll have gone soft out there, under the water, where you can't see it."

"The cart won't do it," said Pell.

"The cart won't do it," said Callie. "Not if the bed's gone. It'd go in up to the boards, and we'd not get it out."

They stood on the edge of the water.

"North," said Pell, after a while. He put his finger on the atlas. "The bottom's a valley. It goes up north into the high ground. If we go north along the edge of it we come to the head of it, where there's no water, and round, and down the other side to the line." He moved his finger. "It's not on the atlas as a road. It's fields. And the high ground."

"How long?" said Toren.

"A day," said Pell. "Near enough. Maybe more, with the cart on fields."

Jab watched Toren. He watched him do the sum. It was not a long one. He had seen Toren do it on the step of eleven with the paper on his knee, and in the long room with the Director's fingers going down one at a time. He had heard it in the dark by the fire on the rise, every armful. *Six. And a horse.*

"A day," said Toren. "That's the fence on the forty-seventh, not the forty-sixth. Back on the fifty-fourth at the earliest, not the fifty-third." He did not look at anybody. "The fifty-fourth's the next cycle. The man with the white hand said he'd be guessing by the fifty-fourth."

Nobody said anything.

Jab was not listening to the sum any more.

He had felt something. He did not know when he had started to feel it. It had been there, he thought, since they stopped at the edge of the water, and he had not noticed it the way you do not notice a sound until it stops. It was off to the left. South and west, down the edge of the bottom, where the water ran into a line of low trees. It was not near. It was further than the day holder had been in the fog.

It was a lean.

It was not his. It was not anybody he knew. It was not Tonk, or Rook, or any of the five people standing on the old line round him. It was somebody, off there, a long way off, and the somebody was bad. Not dying. Bad. The way the fever girl on Mrs Lask's list had been bad, from the way her mother's voice had sounded at the desk. Something wrong in somebody that was pulling at him, a little, the way a rope pulls when the man at the other end of it leans.

"There's somebody," he said.

They looked at him.

"Off there." He pointed with his chin, south and west, down the edge of the water to the trees. "A long way. Somebody's bad."

---

"Bad how?" said Dee.

"I don't know. I can't tell from here." He did not take his eyes off the trees. "It's a lean. Like the holder in the fog. Further than her. It's somebody who's bad."

"How far?"

"Further than forty yards." He thought about it. "Much further. A mile. Two."

Dee was looking at him the way she looked at a face at the long table, reading it.

"You've never felt anybody at a mile," she said.

"I've never tried." He heard himself. "I'm not trying now. It's just there."

Pell had the atlas open again. He put his finger on the line where it went into the water and moved it south, down the edge of the page, along a thin line Jab had not seen. It was not red. It was a fine black line that came off the old line at an angle, just before the bottom, and went south-west round the edge of the water, and then west, and then came back up to join the old line again on the far side. Eight miles, Pell said, or a bit more.

"A siding," he said. "A spur. It went to something." He peered at the atlas. "There's a word. I can't read it. A depot, or a pit. It's on its own bed. It goes round the south end of the water." He looked up. "If the bed's there, it's shorter than going north. Half a day, maybe less."

"If the bed's there," said Rook.

"I can see it," said Callie.

She had stood up on the edge of the water and was looking south, the way Jab was looking, down the edge of the bottom to the trees.

"There's a line," she said. "Along the edge of the trees. Raised. Straight. It's the same stone as this. It's above the water." She shaded her eyes. "It goes into the trees. I can't see it after that."

"So there's a way," said Toren.

"There's a way," said Callie. "I don't know what's on it."

"There's somebody on it," said Jab. "Somebody bad."

Rook looked at him for a moment.

"Something that hurt somebody is on that road," he said. "That's what that means. Somebody doesn't go bad a mile off the old line for nothing."

"Or somebody who knows the road," said Dee.

---

They all looked at Toren then.

Jab watched it happen. He had seen it happen in the long room and at the long table, and on the rise by the fire in the dark. He had not known, at the beginning, that it was happening. He knew now. It was not that Toren was in charge. Dee was in charge, on the road. It was the Director's word in her mouth. But when the evidence was all on the table and nobody's piece of it was the whole, they looked at Toren, because he was the one who would say all of it back to them in order.

He said it.

"The line's under water," said Toren. "Pell's map says straight across, and it's seventy years old. Gran's sheet says straight across, and it's twenty. Callie says the water's new and the bed's gone soft, and the cart won't do it. That's the map and the ground." He looked at the water. "North round the head is a day. That's the fence on the forty-seventh and home on the fifty-fourth. That's my count." He looked south. "The siding's half a day, if the bed's there. Callie can see the bed. And Jab can feel somebody bad on it." He stopped. "Which is a reason to go and a reason not to."

"Say which," said Dee.

Toren looked at the trees for a while.

"The siding," he said. "Because it's shorter, and because Callie can see the bed. If it goes wrong we can come back to here and go north, and we've lost half a day, not a day." He looked at Jab. "And because if somebody's bad on it, they're bad whether we go or not. I'd rather know what did it before we're at the hill."

Dee looked at him a moment longer. Then she opened her book and wrote it.

"The siding," she said.

---

They found them at the fourth hour of the afternoon.

The siding's bed was there. It was lower than the old line, and narrower, with no posts along it. It went south round the edge of the water on a low bank and into the trees, which were thorn and alder and stood close on both sides, and then out again onto open ground beyond. It was good enough. The cart went along it with the grass brushing the hubs. Callie walked twenty paces in front and read it, and twice she stopped Pell for a place where the bank had slumped. Pell took Duchess round each one with the near wheel on the edge and the cart leaning. Nobody said anything.

The lean got nearer.

Jab felt it get nearer the way you feel a fire get nearer in the dark. It was on the left, then in front. He walked at the tailboard with his hand not quite on the fish and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that he had not said.

Then Callie stopped in the road and put her hand up, flat, the way Katori did.

There was a handcart in the ditch.

It was on the left of the bed, tipped half over, with one wheel off the ground. It was a small flat thing with two wheels and two long handles, the kind a person pulls, and it was piled high with coils of something that shone dull red in the grey light. Wire. Copper. Coil on coil of it, stripped off something and wound round and round and tied with rag.

There was a man sitting against the wheel.

He was sitting in the ditch with his back to the handcart's wheel and his legs out in front of him, and his head was down. He was grey. Jab could see it from thirty paces. It was not the grey of dust. It was the grey of Senna's face coming out of the machine, the grey of somebody whose whole body is busy with something. There was a mess on the grass beside him where he had been sick. His hands were in his lap, and they were wrapped in rag, and the rag was dark.

And standing over him, between him and the road, with an iron bar in both hands held up like a club, there was a girl.

She was about twelve. She was thin and dirty and her hair was cut short with a knife. She held the bar up at them and did not say anything, and her whole body was shaking, and she did not put the bar down.

---

"Nobody move," said Dee quietly.

Nobody moved.

"We're not going to hurt you," said Dee to the girl, not loudly. "We're on the road. We're going west. We've got a horse and a cart and six people, and none of us is going to come near you unless you say."

The girl did not say anything. The bar did not come down.

"Is that your father?"

The girl looked at the man in the ditch, very quickly, and back.

"He's sick," she said. Her voice was hoarse, as if she had not used it for a day. "He's been sick since the day before yesterday. He can't walk. He can't pull the cart." She held the bar tighter. "It's our cart. It's our wire. We cut it. It's ours."

"Nobody wants your wire," said Dee.

"Everybody wants the wire."

"We don't," said Dee. "Callie. The counter. From where you are. Don't go any nearer."

Callie took the counter out of its bag. Jab heard it start: one, and nothing, and two together. The room. She held it out in front of her toward the ditch, at the end of her arm, and waited ten breaths.

"Nine," she said. "From here. That's hardly anything." She took a step. Not toward the man. Toward the handcart's end, where the coils were piled. The counter changed. It went from rain starting to rain falling.

"Twenty-two," said Callie. "At the wire. From ten paces." She stepped back. "It's the wire, Miss Wren. It's warm. Not all of it. Some of it."

Dee looked at the girl.

"Where did he cut it?" she said.

The girl's face did something.

"Off the line," she said. "The big line. With the posts. West." She stopped. "Near the hill. Where nobody's been. Where the good wire still is, because nobody goes near the hill." She did not put the bar down. "He went down along the line where it crosses the wash, and there's posts there with all the wire still on. I stayed on the line. He said I had to stay on the line. He went down to the wash for a whole day and came back with the cart full." She looked at the man. "And the day after he was sick."

"How long ago?"

"Four days. He was sick on the second. He's been sick since." Her voice went up. "He's been sitting there since yesterday and he won't eat. He can't hold anything. His hands have gone." She stopped. "My grandfather went to that hill once. He said nobody comes back right. He said never go near it. And he went for the wire anyway, because we've nothing else."

The man in the ditch lifted his head.

He looked at them. His eyes were red at the rims and very tired.

"Don't," he said to the girl. His voice was a croak. "Don't tell them. Don't tell them anything." He looked at Dee. "We've nothing you want. We'll not be in your road. Go on past."

---

Jab was already kneeling.

He had not decided to. He had come away from the tailboard and round the end of the cart, and he was kneeling on the edge of the siding bed, a pace from the lip of the ditch. That was as near as Dee had said and no nearer. He had the frame on his back with the eight jars. He did not put his hands out.

He could feel it from there.

He could feel it the way he had felt it in Dee's arm at the window, without touching. It was not a lean now. A lean was far off and pulling. This was close. This was in front of him, in the man sitting in the ditch, in his chest and his belly and his arms. It was a warmth that was not his warmth. It was fine and gritty, like sand that has been lying in the sun. It was busy.

It was the thing. The busy thing that isn't them.

It was not like the technician. The technician had been full of it all at once, everywhere, thick in the arm, from a seed spilled in his lap. This was thinner, and it was lower. It was in the belly more than the chest, where a man had swallowed dust with his bread. It was in the arms. And in the hands, under the rag, there was something else. It was the other thing, the old leaning wall. It was done, and it was not busy, and it was the most of what was in the hands. There was only a little busy under it, like warm water standing in the bottom of a broken pot.

"It's in him," said Jab. "Belly most. Chest. The arms." He stopped. "The hands are mostly done."

Nobody said anything.

The girl was staring at him. The bar had come down a little, not all the way.

"What's he doing?" she said. "What are you doing?"

"He's feeling," said Rook.

He had come up behind Jab and stood there at his shoulder, not kneeling, with his hands at his sides. He did not say anything else. He did not tell Jab what to do. Jab felt him not tell him.

"Are you a doctor?" the girl said to Dee.

"No," said Dee.

"Then what—"

"I'm the one who says what it costs," said Dee.

---

She said it to Jab, not to the girl.

"It's lent ash," she said. "The Director's. Every jar's against a name on the board, and there's a man on a stool at the weighbridge who'll want them all back or a reason. If you work on him, it comes out of the road jar. Not the frame. The road." She looked at the jar in the rack on the tailboard, the one with the paper on its neck that said *ROAD*. "There's seven and a half in it. I'd say two, near enough, for what's in him that's worth drawing. Two Handfuls. That's two Handfuls you'll not have for the road when there's hounds at a culvert on the fifth day, and there will be." She did not look away from him. "It's your terms. You go to who you choose. I'm not choosing for you. I'm telling you what it costs, because that's what I'm for."

Jab looked at the man in the ditch.

The man was looking at the frame on Jab's back. He was looking at the jars in it, grey to the shoulder, and he knew what they were. Jab could see him know it. Everybody on the frontier knew what grey in a jar was.

"We've nothing to pay," the man said.

"Nobody's asked you to," said Dee.

Jab looked round at Toren.

Toren was standing at the back of the cart with his arm against his chest and the paper in his hand. He had the pencil. He had not written anything. He was looking at Jab, and his face was doing the thing Jab had seen it do on the step of eleven, when he read *If J. can't find it* at the bottom of the back of the sheet. Jab could see the count in it. Seven and a half. Two for a stranger. Hounds on the fifth day. The fence. Everybody back.

Jab waited for him to say it.

"It's yours," said Toren.

He said it quietly.

"It's yours," he said again. "Your terms. The Director wrote them down. You go to who you choose." He looked at the paper in his hand, and then back at Jab. "I'll write what it costs. That's mine. The rest's yours."

He did not say *go on*. He did not say *don't*. He stood at the back of the cart with the pencil in his hand and waited.

Jab looked at him for a moment. Then he looked back at the man in the ditch, and at the girl with the bar, and at the hands in their dark rag.

He thought about the Elder in her kitchen with the lamp lit, four hundred miles north, and what she had said to him the first winter after his mother, when a man had come to the door who was not from the valley and had nothing. *You feed who's at the door. You don't ask where the door is.* He thought about Tonk on the step of the long building with his face to the wall. He thought about two Handfuls. Two hours of a person near the gate of the fence. Two hounds at a culvert.

He thought about the fish.

"Yes," he said.

---

Dee did it properly. She did it the way she had done it in the pen, with rules said out loud and every hand in its place.

"Nobody touches the wire," she said. "Nobody touches his rag. He takes his own coat off, and his own shirt, and she helps him, and nobody else." She looked at the girl. "You'll not touch your face after. You'll wash your hands in running water, not the ditch. There's a stream by the trees." She turned. "Pell. Water. A skin of it."

Pell looked at her.

"It's our water," he said.

"It's a skin of it," said Dee. "We're a mile from a stream."

Pell looked at her a moment longer. Then he went to the cart and took down a skin and brought it, and gave it to the girl, not to the man. He did not say anything else.

The man got his coat off. It took him a long time, with the girl pulling the sleeves, and his hands held out in front of him in their rag like things that did not belong to him. The shirt under it was grey. The girl poured water over his face and his neck from the skin, and he shut his eyes and let her, and she washed his face with her own sleeve. She did it very carefully, round the eyes. Somebody had taught her that, Jab thought. Or she had worked it out.

"Now," said Dee.

She knelt beside Jab on the edge of the bed. Rook sat down on the bed behind him, slowly, in two movements, close enough to put his hand on Jab's shoulder, and put it there. Callie crouched a pace off with the counter. Toren went to the tailboard and took the road jar out of its rack and pulled the stopper, and came and put it in Dee's hand, and then he took a vent jar out of the straw at the back of the cart. It was one of the six with spent ash in the bottom, pale and colourless, that rode apart from the clean. He set it on the grass by Jab's right knee.

"Clean in the left," said Dee. "Dirty out the right. I'm the watch. Elias is your reserve. His hand stays on your shoulder and does nothing. Callie, ticks at his chest, now."

Callie held the counter out toward the man, at arm's length, the width of the ditch between them.

"Sixty," she said. "Ten breaths. At his chest. From here."

"Go on," said Dee.

Jab went down into the ditch.

He knelt beside the man in the wet grass, on the side away from the wire, and put his right hand flat on the man's chest, over the breastbone. The man flinched. Then he did not.

Dee put the mouth of the jar to Jab's left palm.

---

It was not like the pen.

That was the first thing he knew. In the pen it had been a flood, a man full to the arms, a seed in his lap, and Rook's hand over his and Dee's voice and the counter buzzing. Everything had happened at once and he had learned it in the same breath he did it. This was one man sitting in a ditch with his back to a cart wheel, and a thin busy warmth in him, lower down. There was time.

He found it. He did not reach for it. He put his attention down under his palm, the way he put it on a table and took his hands off it, and let himself feel what was there. The heart, going slow and tired. The breath. The cold coming up out of the ditch. And the busy thing, not his, in the chest a little and the belly more, warm and fine.

The clean cold came into his left hand from the jar and went through him and out of his right. It paid.

The busy thing came loose, a little, near his hand. It came up into his palm like hot sand. He held it high. He did not let it into his wrist. Ten breaths.

"Vent," said Dee.

He lifted his hand and put it flat in the vent jar by his knee, on the pale dust, and felt it go out of him into nobody. Three breaths.

"Back on."

He went back on. He did it again. And again, lower, with his palm on the man's belly now, where the most of it was, and the man lay back against the wheel with his eyes shut and let him.

"Forty," said Callie, after the fourth.

He did it again. Draw, ten breaths, hold high. Vent, three. Back on. It was like lifting. It was the east track at first light with his palm on a drift, and the ash coming at the rate it came, and nobody hauling. It was like nothing he had ever done that mattered, and it was the most ordinary work he had ever done.

"Twenty-six."

He went down the right arm, from the shoulder to the elbow. It was thin there. Then the left.

"Nineteen."

He came to the hands.

---

He stopped at the wrists.

He could feel the hands under the rag, the way he could feel a fire he was not looking at. They were done. Most of them. The skin and the ends of the fingers had the old leaning wall in them, the thing that had happened and was not happening any more. And under it, a little, there was still something busy, the warm water in the bottom of the broken pot. It was not much. It was all through the done part, the way water gets into a wall.

"Go down the hands," said Dee.

Jab did not move.

"Go down the hands," said Dee again. "There's some left in them. I can see it on the counter. Callie. The hands."

"Twenty-four," said Callie. "At the hands. Higher than the chest."

"No," said Jab.

Dee looked at him.

"It's in the done part," said Jab. He did not take his hand off the man's wrist. He kept it there, feeling. "It's in the wall. There's a little busy in it, all through, and a lot done round it. If I draw it I'm drawing through what's done. I'd have to pour a jar into it to get a spoonful out." He heard himself. "It's the old wall in Dessa's leg. It's the technician's hand. It's not worth a jar. He'd not keep the hands if I emptied the road into them. He'd keep a spoonful less of what's in them." He looked at Dee. "No."

Nobody said anything.

Dee looked at him for a long moment. Then she took the counter out of Callie's hand and held it to the man's wrapped hands herself, close, and watched the needle for ten breaths. Then she gave the counter back to Callie. She put the stopper back in the jar with her thumb and held her own palm flat over the man's hands, an inch above the rag, not touching. She shut her eyes.

She opened them.

"Leave it," she said.

That was all. She did not say *good*. She did not say *you're right*. She wrote something in her book, and then she said, "Off. Vent. Give me your hands," and Jab took his own hand off the man's wrist and vented and held his hands out to her.

She took them, flat, palm to palm, and drew. He felt her find the little warm grit left in the creases of him and take it up into her own palm, and turn, and put her hand in the vent jar.

"Clean," she said. "You'd have carried that."

---

Callie read the jar.

She did it before anybody asked, the way she had in the pen. She took the road jar out of Dee's hand and weighed it on her own palm, with her head a little on one side the way her grandfather must have held a sack.

"It's light by two," she said. "Near enough. Two, and a bit for the vent and a bit for Miss Wren's draw." She looked at Toren. "Road, five and a quarter."

Toren wrote it. Jab saw him write it on the back of the paper, under something else. He did not say anything about it.

"And the man," said Dee. "His chest."

Callie held out the counter.

"Sixteen," she said. "From here. It was sixty."

The man in the ditch opened his eyes.

He looked at Jab, kneeling beside him in the wet grass. He did not say thank you. He looked at Jab's face for a long time with his red-rimmed eyes, the way you look at a thing you do not understand and are not sure you want to.

"My hands," he said.

"I can't do the hands," said Jab. "They're done. I'm sorry. They were done before we got here." He did not look away. "The rest was busy. It's not busy now. Most of it. You'll be sick again, a bit. Be glad when you are. It's your body getting the rest out."

The man looked down at his hands in their dark rag.

"I pulled a cart of wire for four days," he said, "for hands I'll not be able to use to spend what it fetches."

Nobody answered that. There was no answer.

---

The girl told them about the wash while Dee sealed the vent jar.

She told Dee, not Jab. She had put the bar down at last, on the handcart, and she stood with her arms round herself in the cold and told it the way she had told them about her father, fast and hoarse and all at once.

"Don't go over the wash," she said. "On the line. Where it crosses. There's a bridge in the bank, a stone one, where the water goes under, and it's going. The water's eaten under the far side of it. He went over it with the empty cart and it shook. It shook under a *handcart*." She looked at the cart and the horse. "It'll not take that."

"How far?"

"Two days. Less. Where the line goes down to the river, before the hill." She pulled her arms tighter. "And there's grey all over there. On the ground. In heaps, like snow that won't melt, all along the wash and up the bank of the line. That's where he went down for the wire. Into the grey." She stopped. "And things come up out of it. At dusk. We had a lamp on the line and two came up at it out of the grey, the first night, and he killed them with the bar and they went to more grey." She looked at the hill that was not there yet, west, over the trees. "And the sky's wrong over the hill. At night. It's like looking down a well."

Dee wrote all of it.

"Wash in running water," she said to the girl. "Every night. Both of you. Hands and face and hair, before you eat. Nothing goes in your mouth that's touched that wire." She looked at the handcart. "And leave the warm coils."

The man's head came up.

"No."

"Some of them," said Dee. "Callie will tell you which."

Callie went down the side of the handcart with the counter at arm's length, coil by coil, and said the numbers out loud. Twelve, and fourteen, and twenty-eight, and eleven. The three that were twenty and over she pointed at with the hook's handle without touching them.

The man looked at the three coils for a long time. Then he nodded, once, and the girl pulled them off the handcart with her sleeves over her hands and rolled them into the ditch. She did not look at them after.

They did not give their names. Dee did not ask, and the girl did not offer, and when the man opened his mouth as if he might, the girl said, "Don't," and he shut it again.

Toren and Pell got the handcart out of the ditch and onto its two wheels on the siding bed, pointing east. Dee put the sealed vent jar in the ditch by the three coils, with a stone on its lid, and Toren chalked a cross on the stone with the chalk from Pell's pocket. *Don't touch.*

Then the man got up. He got up on the girl's shoulder and the cart's handle, and stood. He stood with his hands held out in front of him, and the girl took the handles, and they went.

They went east along the siding toward the old line and the water and the north road and whatever was beyond it. Jab watched them until they were in the trees. The girl pulled. The man walked beside the handcart with one wrapped hand on the pile of wire, not holding, only there. Neither of them looked back.

---

That night they camped at the far end of the siding, where it came back up to the old line on the west side of the water.

Jab sat by the fire with his hands on his knees. They were steady. They were tired in a way he did not have a word for, not in the muscles. In the palms. As if he had been holding something hot for an hour and had put it down, and the holding was still in them.

Dee measured him.

"A hand," she said. "And less of a bit than this morning. Your floor's where it was." She let go of his wrist. "You didn't touch your own. The jar paid. You're tired because you worked."

"I know."

"I know you know." She wrote. "Two Handfuls and a quarter, with my draw. For a man we'll never see again, who'll not keep his hands." She did not look up. "I'd have said no, if it was mine to say. I'd have said it for the right reasons. I'd have said it for the fence." She shut her book. "It wasn't mine. I'm writing that as well."

She went to the cart.

Rook was sitting on the canvas with his back against the wheel, where Dee had put it double. He had not said anything all evening. He had not said anything at the ditch either, except *he's feeling*, and he had not taken his hand off Jab's shoulder the whole time, and it had not done anything but be there.

"That was yours," he said, when Jab looked at him.

"It was theirs," said Jab.

Rook thought about that.

"Yes," he said. "That too."

Jab looked at the fire. He thought about two Handfuls. He thought about a man walking east with his wrapped hand on a pile of wire he would not be able to spend, and a girl pulling the cart. He thought about hounds on the fifth day, and five and a quarter in the road jar, and eight jars on his own back that were not his, and a fence.

He did not know if he had been right. He knew that the man's chest was sixteen and had been sixty. He knew that he had said no to the hands, and that Dee had put her own palm over them to see, and then said *leave it*. He knew that Toren had stood at the back of the cart with a pencil and not said *go on* or *don't*.

He did not know what it would cost on the fifth day.

Toren came and sat by him with the paper.

"I wrote it," he said. He held it out. Jab did not take it. He read it where it was, in the firelight, in Toren's careful slow hand, under the day's count.

*43rd. The siding (the line's under water). A man and a girl, copper off the line near the wash. J. chose. Road jar 2 and a bit. His chest 60 to 16. The hands were done; J. said no to them and D. W. checked and said leave it. The girl says the wash bridge is going.*

Under it, in the same hand, smaller:

*Gran's was today.*

Jab gave it back.

"You didn't say don't," he said.

"No," said Toren. He folded the paper. "It wasn't mine." He looked at the fire. "I wanted to. I had the sum. I'd have said it if you'd asked me. You didn't ask me."

"I was waiting for you to say it."

"I know," said Toren. "I was waiting for you not to need me to."

Jab sat with that. After a while he put his hand on his coat, over the small hard shape on the left side, and left it there.

*Bring him back.*

He would. He was going to try very hard. He sat by the fire with his tired hands and the fish under one of them, and thought about the grey on the ground at the wash like snow that would not melt. And about the sky over a hill he had not seen yet, that a girl of twelve said was like looking down a well.
