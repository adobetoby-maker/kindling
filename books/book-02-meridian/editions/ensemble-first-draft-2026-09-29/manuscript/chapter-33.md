# Chapter 33 — Out: Four

Before the light, the six of them were where the night had left them.

Pell was asleep on the cart with his splinted leg out along the boards. Rook lay on his side on the canvas, where Dee had put him the night before so that his back was off the ground. Jab was asleep at last by the dead fire, curled on his side, with one hand flat over the left side of his coat. Callie was asleep with her hand on the hook. Toren lay on the gravel with his arm in its cuff against his chest and his eyes shut, and his lips were moving, because he was counting them. He did it every morning before he opened his eyes.

Dee was not asleep. She had not lain down again after her watch, and there was no gravel on her coat. She was kneeling beside Rook with two fingers on his wrist.

Toren's lips stopped moving when they came to Rook. He opened his eyes.

The sky over the river was grey, going white at the bottom. The pump house wall stood black above them. Across the river the fence ran north along the far bank, and behind it the hill went up into the last of the dark. Over its flat top there was still the place where the sky was wrong, with the light round its edge the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened, and thinned. Nobody counted it. They all knew what it came to.

"He's hot," said Dee, without looking round.

Rook's eyes were open. His face was wet, though the morning was cold enough to put frost on the cart.

"I'm warm," said Rook. "It's a warm morning."

"There's ice on the wheel," said Dee.

"Then I'm warm and the wheel's cold," said Rook. "We're both of us wrong."

Dee did not smile. She took the knot out of the canvas strip round his chest and lifted the pad off his back, and Toren got up and came and knelt on the other side of the canvas, where he could see.

The stitches ran in a short dark line between Rook's shoulders, small and neat and tight, the way Dee had put them in at the cut. The skin round them was red. The red went out a hand's width on each side of the line, swollen and shiny, and when Dee put the backs of her fingers near it, not on it, she took them away again at once.

"It's declared," she said.

"That was quick of it," said Rook.

Jab was standing at the end of the canvas. Nobody had heard him get up. He still had his hand on his coat, and he was looking down at Rook's back.

"He's been hot since the fourth hour," said Jab. "I felt it. I didn't know if I'd dreamed it."

"Put your hand over it," said Dee. "Don't touch."

Jab knelt and held his right palm an inch above the red, and shut his eyes. His face went still, the way it went at the long table. Toren watched it. So did Callie, who was up on one elbow by now with her hand still on the hook.

"There's nothing busy," said Jab after a while. "Not the grey. That's gone. We had all of that out." He did not open his eyes. "It's him. It's him, doing something. Fighting it. It's hot all through, not in bits." He took his hand away. "It's not the thing I can draw."

"No," said Dee. "It's the dog."

She said it to Toren, because Toren was the one who had written the dog down.

"The grey came out yesterday. What's left is what a hound's mouth puts in a man when it bites him. It's the same as a dog bite in a yard, or a nail in a foot." She laid the pad back on. "That's not Sustain. That's Sowerby's. And Sowerby's eighty miles off."

She opened her small bag and took out four folded papers, white, each the size of a thumb, tied together with thread. She untied one.

"Fever powder," she said. "He gave me four for the road. For a hound bite, or a wound gone bad, or a fever in the night." She looked at the paper in her fingers. "I'm to put it in boiled water and see he drinks all of it. I'm to do it once a day. I'm not to do it twice to make myself feel better."

"And if it doesn't break?" said Toren.

"Then by tomorrow night he'll be worse," said Dee. "And then we'll not be waiting for anything."

---

The water came first, then.

Dee's skin was empty. She had put the whole of it into Rook's back at the cut, a little at a time, and nobody had counted the others since. So Toren went to the cart and counted them: two skins hanging from the rail behind the driver's bench, one of them full, and the other a third full, near enough, when he hefted it.

"One and a third," he said.

"That's for drinking," said Dee. "Not for washing. Not for his back." She looked at the river. "The river's for the rest. If it's clean."

Callie was already on her feet with the counter out of its bag. She went down the gravel to the edge of the water above the pump house, where the river came down out of the north fast and dark over the stones, and she held the counter out over it at the length of her arm for ten breaths.

"Seven," she said. "The room." She walked down past the pump house and did it again, below the place where they had washed at dusk. "Nine. Our wash is in it."

"Above the pump house for drinking," said Dee. "Below it for washing. And nothing out of it goes in his back till it's boiled."

Boiling meant a fire, and a fire meant wood, and the pump house had none. It was stone, its roof was gone, and the thorn they had carried on the cart had burned the night before. So Toren and Callie went down the bank below the pump house, where the river had once run high and had left a line of wrack along the gravel a hand above the water. It was sticks and reeds and bleached old branches, dry, jammed among the stones. They picked along it for half a mile and came back with an armful each.

"That's one pot," said Callie. "Maybe two."

"Then it's one now and one tonight," said Dee.

She boiled the pot at the pump house wall. When it had cooled enough, she stirred the powder into a cup of it and held the cup while Rook drank, lying on his side with his hand round hers on the cup. He drank all of it, and he made a face at the end.

"Sowerby's," he said. "It tastes like his office."

"Drink the rest of the pot," said Dee. "Slowly. It's the last boiled water you'll see till dark."

Pell had not waited for anybody.

Duchess was tied to the near wheel by her rope halter, cropping the thin grass along the foot of the pump house wall, and she had not looked at the hill once. Above her, on the boards, Pell had got himself sitting up with his back against the rack behind the driver's bench and his splinted leg out straight in front of him. He had done it alone. His face said what it had cost him, and it also said that he did not want it mentioned, so nobody mentioned it.

Dee knelt on the tailboard beside the foot. She did not undo the splint. She pinched the end of his big toe, where it came out of the canvas strips, and watched it go white and then pink again.

"The blood's going round it," she said. "That's good. It's swelled under the strips. That's not." She loosened one knot a finger's width. "You'll not put it down."

"I've nowhere to put it down," said Pell. "I'll sit." He had the atlas open on his knee in its oilcloth. "A man on his back can't do much. A man sitting up can count."

"Count what?"

"Whatever wants counting." He looked across the river at the hill. "She said to count what comes out of that. The Director. It's on the paper." He did not look at Toren, who had the paper in his coat. "That's a thing a man can do sitting on a cart. I'll do it."

---

Dee measured their floors on the gravel by the cold fire as if the fire were the gate, and the three of them stood in a row for it without being told.

She did Jab first, the way she always did now. "A hand," she said. "Floor's a finger." She turned his palm up and held her own over it, not touching. "Cool. They've come down."

"They came down about the third hour," said Jab.

"I know. I was awake."

She did Callie. "Your stone's what you settled last night. All of it." She kept her fingers on the wrist. "Your own's come back. Not all. Three parts, near enough."

"I slept," said Callie.

"You slept like a stone in a well," said Dee. "I watched you. That's the only reason it's three parts."

She did Toren. He had been the last of them to settle. After Jab lay down, he had sat by the fire with his palm flat on a square of paper that held a Handful of jar C's ash, and he had let it come at the rate it came, which for Toren was most of an hour. It had been full dark by the time the paper was clean.

"Stone's full," said Dee. "Your own—" She waited, with her fingers on his wrist. "Two turns. Not the bit. The bit'll come back by noon, if you don't spend it before."

That was how she measured his own: in turns of the glass, as long as it would hold a strut with no stone under it.

"I'll try not to," said Toren.

"Don't try," said Dee. "Don't spend it."

She did her own last, with two fingers on her own wrist and her eyes on the hill, and she did not say it out loud. She wrote it in her book.

Then there were the coats. Senna's rules said coat off, shake it, wash at the river, and they had done all that the night before; but they had done it at dusk, in the last of the light, and now it was day. Callie went round them with the counter, and they stood still for it one at a time, the way they had stood for Dee.

Jab's coat had the long pale stain down its back where jar B had broken on the rail at the wash. Callie held the counter to it and waited.

"Eight," she said. "It's clean. It's only the jar." She looked at the stain. "It's the stair's ash. It's clean on you."

Dee's coat was twelve. Callie's own was fourteen. Pell's, up on the cart, was twenty, and Dee made him take it off and hand it down to her, and she shook it out downwind herself.

Toren's was thirty.

Everybody knew why. He had been outside the lee at the wash, killing two husks against it, with the grey going up over him in a burst each time. He stood in his shirt on the gravel with his arm in its cuff while Callie held the counter to the coat, and he was cold, and he did not say so.

"Wash it," said Dee. "In the river. Below the pump house. Then beat it on the stones till you're tired, and then some more."

He did it one-handed. He knelt at the edge of the water, pushed the coat under and held it there until his hand stopped feeling, which took ten breaths, and then he pulled it out and beat it on a flat stone and put it under again. After a while Jab came down the gravel, took the coat out of his hand without being asked, and did the beating for him. The grey went off it into the water in a thin cloud and away down the river.

"Sixteen," said Callie, when it had lain on the stones in the sun for an hour. "It's still in the seams."

"Sixteen's a coat," said Dee. "Thirty's a coat I'd not let inside."

Then the hook.

Callie held it up and read it herself. The curve read eighteen, because it had been down the loose bank at the wash twice and on the lip in the wind. Then she held the counter to the grip. The top of the conduit was wrapped in rag where her hands went, and the rag was old and grey and soft, and it had been wet with the wash and had dried on her hands.

"Thirty-four," said Callie. "It's the rag."

She took out her knife and cut it off. She did it carefully, in one long strip, without letting it touch her other hand. Then she stood with the strip hanging off the point of the knife and looked at Dee.

"Not the river," said Dee. "Not the fire."

So Toren dug a hole at the far end of the gravel, away from the water, with the heel of his boot, and Callie dropped the rag into it off the knife. He kicked the gravel back over it and laid a flat stone on top, and Callie chalked a cross on the stone with Pell's chalk. *Don't touch.* It was the second cross they had left on the road.

She washed the curve of the hook in the river with sand, scouring it the way her grandfather had scoured a scale pan, and when she read it again it said eleven. The grip was bare steel now, cold and smooth. Below where the rag had been, a paper tag was still tied round the conduit, where the grey coats had tied it in the pen. *pen, 35th.*

"You could cut that off too," said Toren.

"No," said Callie. "They'll want to write under it. When we get back."

Jab looked up from the wet coat at that. He did not say anything. He nodded, once, as if it were a thing he had been waiting for somebody to say.

---

At the third hour of the morning three of them went to look at the bridge in daylight: Callie with the counter, Dee with the glass, and Toren. Nobody went to look at anything alone any more. Jab stayed at the pump house with Rook and Pell and watched them go.

The bridge was thirty paces across. It was three planks wide, laid side by side on cross-beams, and the cross-beams sat on posts that went down into the river. The planks were grey and split along the grain and furred with old moss at the edges. On the upstream side, the north side, there was a rail, one long pole at the height of a man's hip, lashed to the posts with wire. On the downstream side there was no rail at all.

Below the bridge, downstream, there had been another crossing beside it. Two iron pipes, each as thick as a man's body, had gone over the river side by side on iron legs. The pipes came out of the river wall of the pump house on this bank, and on the far bank they went into the ground. In the middle the legs had gone, and the pipes had broken and dropped into the river, and their ends lay in the water with the current going white round them.

"That was the pump," said Dee. "River water, over to the hill."

"What for?" said Toren.

"I don't know," said Dee. "For something that needed water. Things like that always need water."

Callie walked out onto the bridge first with the counter held out in front of her. She did not look at the water. She looked at the planks.

"Seven," she said, at the first post. "Nine. In the middle." She went on, and at the far end, two planks short of the bank, she stopped and crouched.

"This one's gone," she said.

It was the middle plank. It had cracked across, right through, between two cross-beams, and the two halves sagged a finger's width in the middle. Through the crack, Toren and Dee could see the river.

"It'll take a person," said Callie. "On the outside planks. Not on this one." She put her hand flat on the plank on one side of it, and then on the plank on the other side. "Those are sound. They're old, but they're sound. This one's rotten at the beam."

"And a load?" said Toren.

"What load?"

"The carrier." He looked back across the river at the cart. "It has to come over. It can't come over on a back. It's got to come over on something."

Callie looked at the crack.

"Not on that," she said. She stood up and walked the last two planks to the bank, and the other two came after her, and the three of them stood at the gate.

The gate was a frame of iron as tall as a man and a half and as wide as a cart, with the same rusted wire as the fence stretched across it in squares as big as a hand. It hung from two hinges on an iron post on the right. On the left, where it met the other post, there was a chain. The chain went twice round the frame of the gate and the post, and its two ends had been brought together and joined. They had not been joined with a lock. They were joined with a twist of copper wire gone green, wound round and round on itself in a tight knot the size of a fist, with the ends tucked in.

Toren looked at it for a long time.

He could see the man who had done it. Somebody had stood here twenty years ago, on the last evening, and pulled the gate shut behind eleven people, and twisted a bit of copper through the chain so it would stay. He had not locked it. He had tied it, the way a man ties a gate he means to open again himself.

"His," said Toren. "The man who ran the crew."

"Maybe," said Dee.

"Gran said he wouldn't let anybody sleep inside." Toren did not take his eyes off the knot. "He'd not have left it open."

He did not touch it. Callie held the counter to it instead.

"Twelve," she said. "At the gate. It's fourteen through the wire." She held the counter out through one of the squares, into the fence, at the length of her arm. "Fourteen."

Then all three of them looked through the wire at Caul Hill.

---

Inside the fence the ground was flat for a hundred paces. It was a wide yard of old concrete gone to cracks and weeds, with gravel in the cracks. Beside the gate, on the inside, stood a squat grey hut with a door and one window, both black. Long low sheds ran along the south side of the yard with their roofs fallen in, and near them stood a tall thing on four iron legs, like a water butt on stilts, with its side rusted through in a long red wound.

The pipes came up out of the river bank on the inside and crossed the yard side by side on low concrete blocks to the foot of the hill, and then they went up the hill beside a road. The road climbed the hill in two long slants, back and forth, a great Z cut into its face. At the top of the Z, on the rim of the flat top, there was the building.

It was long. It was the longest building any of them had ever seen that was not the Sag's own berm. It ran along the rim of the hill from north to south, three rows of windows high, grey and flat-faced, and every window was black. It stood on the edge of the flat top like a wall somebody had built to keep the hill in. In the middle of it, at the top of the road, there was a door, and the door was a black square.

Behind the building, a long way up, over the flat top of the hill, there was the sky.

It was bigger this close. At dusk from the rise it had been the size of Callie's thumb at the end of her arm, and from here it was the size of a cart wheel. It did not hang over the building. It hung over something behind the building. Past the north end of the long building there was the top of a wall that was not straight: it curved, a great round wall higher than the building, broken off in jags at the top like a pot with its rim knocked off. The dark patch hung over that, and the light round its edge thickened and thinned over the broken rim.

"That's not the building," said Toren.

"No," said Dee.

"Gran never said anything about a round one."

"She said the main building," said Callie. "She said *inside the main building*. If you've got a main one, you've got others." She did not take her eyes off the yard. "Look at the ground."

They looked.

The yard was not bare. There was grey on the concrete, in the cracks and along the edges of things, but it was not heaped in drifts the way it had been in the wash. It lay in lines, thin, like hair that has been combed. Every line went the same way. The lines came across the yard from the south and the east, from the sheds and the fence, and they met at the foot of the road and went up it. The nearest one lay beside the gatehouse.

It slid a hand's width toward the hill, and stopped.

Nobody said the count out loud. Nobody needed to. Ten, and three.

"It's going up the road," said Callie. "All of it. I can see it on the first bend." She pointed with her chin. "On the top bend. And at the top—" She stopped. "It goes in the door. Some of it. Some goes round the end of the building, over the top. Toward the round thing."

"Some goes in," said Toren.

"Some goes in the door," said Callie. "Where we're going."

Dee turned her back on the fence.

She did it on purpose. She stood with her back to the gate, facing the two of them, and she took out her book and did not open it.

"Say what she said," she said. "The Director. The narrow thing."

Toren had it by heart. He had written it at the long table, and again on the back of the paper, and Dee had quoted it twice at the fire on the forty-sixth. He said it anyway, because she had asked.

"*You're going for the boxes. The live ones, with the wheel on the plate, in lead, on the cart. Two, three if you can. And you're going to look at that sky and count what comes out of it and write it down. You are not going into it. You are not going looking for whatever's under it. If it comes to you, you do what you have to do and you come home.*"

"*Into it,*" said Dee. "What's *it*?"

Toren looked back through the wire, at the round wall with its broken rim and the dark hanging over it.

"That," he said. "The round one. Whatever's in it. Under the sky."

"And the building?"

"The building's in front of it." He said it slowly and in order, the way Pell said a distance. "The door's at the top of the road, on the east side. The stair's inside it. Gran said down the stair, a door, and then a corridor, and the rooms off the corridor on the left. Eleven of them. She didn't say which way the corridor goes." He looked at the long grey face of the building. "If it goes along the building, north or south, it's under the building all the way. If it goes back into the hill, west, it's under the building for as long as the building's deep, and then it goes on under the top. Toward the round one."

"Which?"

"I don't know."

"Then we'll count doors," said Callie.

Dee looked at her.

"The building's about thirty paces deep," said Callie. "I can see its end from here, past the north corner. If the corridor goes west, and the rooms are eight paces apart, or ten, then three rooms are under the building. Four at the most. The rest are under the yard behind it." She did not look up at the sky. "The rest are nearer to that."

Dee opened the book then. She wrote in it standing up, with the book against her forearm.

"Three doors," she said. "If the boxes are in the first three rooms, they're ours. If they're not, they're under that, and they're hers, and she can decide what to do about them with a Sag held and a machine that can see." She wrote. "Nobody goes past the third door. Not to look. Not for a box. Not for anything."

"And the ash that goes in the door?" said Toren.

Dee shut the book.

"The ash that goes in the door is lying where it's lying," she said. "If it's in the first three rooms, we'll be standing in it. We knew that yesterday. It's why we've got two healers and a counter and a line." She looked at him. "It's not a thing. It's ash, going somewhere. If a thing comes to us, then it's come to us. She said what we do then."

---

They said all of it again out loud at the pump house, because Dee would not have anything decided that had not been said in front of everybody who would have to live with it.

Callie said the ash first. She stood by the cart with her hands open at her sides and said it flat, the way her mother said the store book.

"Inside, forty. Five whole, in the frame. The line's sixteen. That's two whole." She looked at Jab's frame, which stood against the pump house wall on two flat stones. "Twenty-four to work with. Three jars. Home, three. Twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." Dee was still looking at her, so she went on. "And two empty in the frame. A and C. And the road jar in the rack, empty. And five vent jars on the cart. And one socket with glass in it."

"Settled," said Dee.

"A Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in you." Callie stopped. "Jab carries none. Jab's the jar."

"Four going in," said Dee. "Not five. Say it."

"Four going in," said Callie. "Two healers." She did the sum with her lips not quite moving, and the others waited while she did it. "We priced forty-eight for five people. An hour in, two in the rooms, an hour out. The rooms were thirty." She looked at the hill. "It's not five. It's four. But it's four with two healers, not three, and one of them's got to be the jar and the watch in turns, all the time, and nobody's the reserve." She shook her head. "I can't make it come out better than it is. Twenty-four's four people for half the day, with nothing going wrong."

"Then we don't spend twenty-four today," said Toren.

They all looked at him.

"Twelve," he said. "Half. We go in, and we find the stair, and the door, and we find which way the corridor goes. We read the first room. If the boxes are in it, we get them in the carrier and we come out. If they're not, we come out anyway." His voice was steady, and he kept it that way. "Twelve spent is twenty-eight left. We come out at twenty-eight, whatever we've got. Then we've still got a day."

Dee looked at him for a long moment.

"Twenty-eight's today's line," she said. She wrote it. "Sixteen's the line. Both."

"And the rest," said Toren. "Say them."

They said them, and the four who were going in said them together. Red, and nobody trapped: everybody out, at once. Red, and a body trapped: the fewest who can do it stay, named. Two Flasks left inside: everybody out. A healer at their floor twice. A healer still warm after the watch. Jab below his floor once.

"One more," said Dee.

She said it to Jab, but she said it for all of them.

"There's two of us," she said. "You and me. If one of us goes down—hurt, or under, or anything—there's nobody to draw the one that's left. Not after. Not ever." She did not look away from him. "So if either of us is lost, for any reason, it's everybody out. That moment. Not when we've finished what we're doing."

"Everybody out," said Jab.

Toren wrote it on the back of the paper under the rest. *If a healer's lost, everybody out.* Under that he wrote the thing he had written on the step of eleven the night before they left home. *Everybody back.*

Rook had listened to all of it lying on the canvas with his eyes half shut, and nobody had asked him anything. When they had finished, he opened his eyes.

"And the outside," he said.

Dee turned.

"There's two of us out here," said Rook. "One can't walk and one's hot. We can't come in after you. We've both got that. I'd like it said, now, what we're to do if you don't come out."

"If you're not out by the time the hill's shadow's on the gate," said Pell, from the cart, "I'll know. I'll have been counting."

"And then?" said Dee.

Pell looked at Rook. Rook looked at Dee.

"Then I don't come in," said Rook.

"Say it again."

"Then I don't come in." He said it flatly, to the stones of the pump house wall. "I sit at the near end of that bridge where I can see the gate. I'm the only one of us who can get to the near end of the bridge, and I can get there lying down if I have to. If something comes out of that gate that isn't you, I shout. If it's you, I count you." He was quiet a moment. "If it's dark and it's nothing, I don't come in."

Dee knelt down beside him on the gravel.

"It's the Director's word," she said.

"It's yours," said Rook. "I know whose it is." He shut his eyes. "Go on. You've a day."

---

Then there was the carrier.

It stood in the cart behind the three home jars, on the straw where it had ridden for seven days. It was the size of a small chest, made of the depot's lead in sheets, folded and seamed, grey and dull, with a lid that sat down into a groove round the top and an iron handle at each end. Even empty it was so heavy that it had taken Pell and Toren and Jab together to set it on the cart in the north yard, and Toren had had only one arm to do it with even then.

"It doesn't go on anybody's back," said Toren.

"No," said Pell.

"And the cart can't cross the bridge."

"No," said Pell.

He was sitting on the boards with his leg out. He looked at the carrier, and at the bridge, and at the tailboard, which hung on its chains at the back of the cart with the two long pieces of its rail gone. Those two pieces were on his leg.

"Then it drags," he said.

He told them how. He told them the way he had told Toren where to cut the traces at the wash: once, slowly, with his finger on each thing.

The tailboard came off its chains. It was a single thick board as long as a man was tall, and as wide as the carrier with a hand to spare on each side, and they set it on the gravel. Jab and Callie and Toren got the carrier down onto it off the back of the cart, sliding it on two poles of wrack wood while Pell said *easy* and *easy* and *now*. Then they lashed it to the board with rope through both handles, crossed under the board and back, and pulled the rope tight.

"Two ropes," said Pell. "One at the front to pull. One at the back to hold." He looked at Toren. "On a slope, down or up, there's a rope round something that doesn't move, and you pay it out. And nobody stands below it. Not ever. Not for a breath."

"Below it," said Toren.

"Downhill of it. If it's on a slope and you're below it, and it runs, you're under a box of lead." Pell put his hand on the edge of the tailboard. "If it runs, let it run. Lead's cheaper than you. There's more lead in the world than there is of any of you."

Callie had lifted the lid off the carrier. She was looking in.

"There's something in it," she said.

She lifted it out. It was a pair of tongs, iron, a yard and a half long, with two flat jaws at one end and two ring handles at the other, wrapped in a piece of sacking with a label tied to them. The label was in a square, neat hand that Toren did not know. *For the boxes. Not your hands.*

"The shed," said Dee. "The old woman." She looked at the tongs. "She never said."

"She never said anything," said Callie.

She put the tongs back in the carrier and set the lid in its groove.

They went in at noon.

The wind had not come all morning, and the grey lay still in the yard in its combed lines. "Go while it's down," said Rook, from the canvas, and nobody argued with that either.

They ate first, at the pump house: bread, and the last of the cold meat. They drank as much as they could hold of the boiled water that was left, and then a little from the full skin, because there would be nothing to drink inside the fence. One at a time they went down to the river below the pump house to make water, because Dee said so, and because nobody sits down inside the fence.

Dee opened the first jar at the pump house, not at the gate. She lifted it out of Jab's frame by the neck, one of the five, and pulled the stopper and looked in, and then she stoppered it again loosely so that it would come out with a thumb.

"Jar one," said Callie. "Open. Eight in it. Four whole left in the frame."

"Four whole," said Dee, and wrote it.

They put two vent jars in a canvas bag, and Toren hung the bag from his good shoulder. Callie had the hook and the counter. Dee had the glass and the book and the open jar in the crook of her arm. Jab had the frame, with four full jars in it and the two empties and the broken socket, and his knife not on his fist. The cart's two lanterns hung unlit from Toren's belt and Callie's, with a twist of tallow wick in each, and Dee had the box of lights in her bag. Senna had said it went down stairs, where the walls went thick, and there would be no windows.

---

When they came down the gravel with the sledge, Rook was already at the near end of the bridge.

Nobody had seen him go. He was sitting on the ground at the foot of the first post with his back against it and his coat over his shoulders, and his face was wet again. From there he could see the whole length of the bridge and the gate at the far end of it.

"You're off," said Dee, as she passed him.

"I'm sitting," said Rook. "Sitting's allowed."

They said the rules at the bridge end, because Senna's crew had said them at the gate every morning, like a grace. Nothing to eat inside the fence. Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, coat off, shake it, wash at the river. Never carry one of the boxes.

"Pull the door," said Toren, at the end.

Pell said it after him, from the cart. So did Rook, from the post.

The sledge went onto the bridge easily. Toren had the front rope over his good shoulder and Callie had it behind him, both of them pulling, while Jab walked on the outside plank with the frame and Dee came last with the back rope loose in her hand. The tailboard slid over the old planks with a long dry sound, and the carrier sat on it and did not move.

"That's the thing about a board," Pell called from the bank. "A board doesn't care."

At the far end Callie said, "Stop."

The cracked plank was the middle one, and the sledge was as wide as all three planks together. Its front end had come up to the crack and was lying across it, over the sagging middle and onto the two sound planks on either side.

"It'll carry it on the sound ones," said Toren. "The board's wide."

"It'll carry it till the middle of the load comes over the crack," said Callie. "Then it all comes down on the middle. The middle's rotten at the beam."

She crouched at the edge of the bridge and looked down between the planks, at the post and the cross-beam and the water.

"There's stone under," she said. "The post's sitting on a big flat stone in the river. A foot under the water. Where the current's white on it."

Toren looked where she was looking and saw it, pale and square under the running water beside the post, like a step.

"Set," he said.

He put the foot of a strut down through the water onto the stone beside the post, and let it come up at its slant under the middle plank, beside the crack. It came up thin and hard through the river and met the underside of the plank. He stayed soft behind it. He did not lock.

"Pull," he said.

Callie pulled. Jab leaned the frame's weight against the rail and took the front rope in one hand, and the sledge slid. The middle of the carrier came over the crack, and the weight came down onto the plank and down the strut into the stone in the river, and it did not come up into Toren. His knees gave a little and took what they had to. His lips were moving.

"Three," he said, under his breath. "Four."

The back end of the sledge came off the crack and onto the bank.

He let the strut go. At the end. Not after.

"Sliding," called Pell, from across the river, a breath late. Somebody laughed on the far bank, and it was Rook.

Toren stood at the gate with the green knot of wire in front of him.

He untwisted it one-handed, and it took a long time. The wire was stiff with twenty years and did not want to come, so he did it a turn at a time with the ends of his fingers, while Callie held the chain for him so that it did not swing. When the last turn came loose, he held the wire in his hand for a moment. It was only a bit of copper. He put it in his pocket. Then he took it out again and hung it on the fence wire beside the gate, where it would be when they came out.

The chain came off the post.

He put his hand on the frame of the gate and pulled. It did not open. He pushed, and it went. It swung inward on its hinges, slowly, with a long scrape of iron on the concrete, and stood open.

Across the river Pell had his head bent over the paper on his knee. He was already writing.

Toren took the signed paper out of his coat, and the pencil, and wrote on the back of it under the night before. He stood at the open gate to do it, with the paper flat against the iron.

*47th. The east gate. Noon. Out: 4.*

Under it he wrote *In:*, and stopped, and looked at the word.

He was thinking of the big book at the shed at home. At the foot of the departure page Dessa had left six lines blank, for *back*. He folded the paper with nothing written after the word, and put it back in his coat beside the plate.

"Twenty-four," said Callie, beside him. "Twelve today."

"Twelve today," said Toren. He picked up the front rope. "Everybody back."

They pulled the sledge through the gate into Caul Hill.
