# Chapter 4 — Callie: The Last Sum

The line was yellow, and it was as wide as her hand.

It was painted across the floor at the bottom of a long slope under the building, in front of a door wider than the gate at the south pen. The air down here was cool, and the walls were thick and grey, with no windows at all. On the wall beside the door there was a sign.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

"It's the machine," said the man in white pushing her mother's bed. "It doesn't like metal. It pulls. Anything iron that goes in there, it'll have it off you and across the floor, and it won't ask." He looked at her mother. "Anything iron on you, ma'am? Pins? Rings?"

"Nothing," said her mother. "I sold the rings at the Reach."

He looked at Callie, and at the hook.

"You'll have to wait here, miss."

"I know."

Her mother's left hand tightened on hers. It could still tighten.

"Forty minutes," said her mother. "I've lain still on a wagon for a month. I can lie still in a room for forty minutes."

"I know."

"You'll be here."

"I'll be here."

They took her through the heavy door. It closed behind her with a sound like a lid going down on a box.

Callie sat on the bench against the wall with her grandfather's hook across her knees. There was nothing on the other side of that door she could see or hear or reach.

Then the knocking started.

---

It came through the wall in long runs, fast and steady and mechanical, like somebody hammering a pipe in another building. It stopped, and started again faster, and stopped, and none of it sounded like a thing that could look at the inside of anybody.

Callie counted the runs. She could not help it. Eleven knocks, a gap, eleven. Then a long one she lost count of. Then eleven again.

She held the hook, and then held it harder, and her fingers closed round the conduit the way they had closed round it on the hill, with the whole wagon on it, when she had not been able to open them.

She heard wheels on the slope.

Another rolling bed was coming down, with a man in white at one end and Jab walking at the other. There was a small boy on it. He was flat on his back with a tube in his hand and a padded trough under his leg, and his hair stuck up again at the back. He had a wooden fish in his fist.

Jab had a paper tray in his other hand. There was a thumb-length of dull river metal in it, on a cord.

He saw her on the bench and his face did something small.

"They're taking my mother first," said Callie.

"I know. They told us." He looked at the door. "We're next."

The boy on the bed turned his head and looked at Callie. He looked at her for a long time with great seriousness.

"You're the girl who *whistled*," he said.

"I am."

"It was really loud."

"It was meant to be."

"Jab said you said seven lamps," said Tonk. "There were *five*."

"There were seven," said Callie. "Two were behind a cart. You were lying down. You couldn't see behind the cart."

Tonk thought about this.

"You're wrong," he said.

"I'm not wrong."

"He said you'd say that," said Tonk, with great satisfaction, and Jab put his free hand over his eyes.

---

Jab sat down on the other end of the bench with the tray on his knees.

The knocking went on. Tonk lay on his bed by the yellow line and talked to the man in white about the machine, and asked whether it had teeth, and was told it did not, and did not believe it.

Callie watched Jab instead.

He was sitting with his hands open on his knees, looking at the door. Then he put his right hand flat on his own chest, over the place where the metal was not, and shut his eyes.

He did nothing else, and he kept perfectly still. His face went very quiet, as if he had put a question down on a table and taken his hand off it.

Callie knew the look. It was the look she had when she went down into herself to see what was in the well.

After a while he opened his eyes and took his hand away. Nothing had happened; she could see that, and she could see what it cost him that nothing had.

He saw her watching.

"I was asking something," he said. "Through the wall. Whether she's all right in there. Your mother." He looked at the tray in his lap. "It's no good. It doesn't reach past my own arm. And it's dry."

"Mine's dry too," said Callie.

"What's yours?"

"A ward. And an edge. Off the hook." She turned the hook over. "Two doors. I'm told it's rare. It doesn't feel rare. It feels like being empty twice."

He almost smiled.

---

"There's somebody in there by herself," said a voice from the top of the slope. "She's lying very still."

It was the girl with the bad leg. Dessa. She was pushing a chair with wheels down the slope, and there was an old woman in it with a blanket over her knees. It was Senna.

Dessa came down carefully, and Callie could see the bad leg taking the weight of the slope and not liking it. She stopped the chair by the bench. Then she sat down on the floor with her back to the wall, because the bench was full, and stretched the leg straight out. She looked at the heavy door with her head a little on one side.

"She's about twenty feet in," she said. "On her back. She hasn't moved. There's two of them at the far side, and one's sitting down."

Callie looked at her.

"How do you—"

"I know where people are. About thirty feet, all round, without looking, in the dark, all the time." Dessa said it flatly, like a thing she had said a great many times and was tired of saying. "It's not a trick. It doesn't cost me anything. It's just there."

"All the time?" said Jab.

"All the time. It doesn't go off." Dessa kept her eyes on the door. "That's the part people never ask about."

"We were on our way back from the shed," said Senna, from the chair, with her eyes shut. "They start the course today and they did the first of it. Dessa came to push me back, and she stopped at the top of that slope and said *there's somebody down there by herself.* And I said, Dessa, it's a hospital, everybody's somewhere by themselves. And she came down anyway." She opened one eye. "Dessa Cole, sit up. You're making the corridor nervous."

"I'm sitting how I sit."

---

The knocking stopped. Started again, faster.

"She still hasn't moved," said Dessa. "She's very good at it."

"She's been practising," said Callie. "On a wagon. For a month."

"It shows."

Callie found her fingers had come open on the conduit. She had not told them to.

"Would you tell me," she said. "If she moves."

"I'll tell you everything," said Dessa. "The dull bits as well. If I only say something when something happens, you'll go mad between them." She shifted the leg. "I count things. I can't help it."

"I count," said Callie.

Dessa turned her head.

"Everything," said Callie. "Husks. Seconds. Days to Meridian. The ash in the jar. My mother's breathing." She stopped. "I put it on my card. At the bottom. *I count.* I don't know why."

Dessa looked at her for a long time with that hard straight look.

"Good," she said at last. "Somebody in this place should."

"So do I," said Jab.

Dessa did not even turn her head.

"I know," she said. "It's in the family. Your brother counted the lamps last night. Out loud. All the way down the dip."

"He got five," said Callie. "There were seven."

"Then you're all as bad as each other," said Dessa, "and I'm the only one in this corridor who counts what's actually there." She looked at the door. "Somebody's stood up. Two of them are going to her. She's still not moving."

---

Jab was looking at Dessa.

"You can do that through a wall," he said.

"Through a wall, through a road, through the ground. It doesn't care what's in the way."

"Mine won't go past my own arm." He said it low. "It comes when it likes. I've had it since the road, and I don't know what it is. I've been sitting on this bench asking it about a stranger through a foot of concrete like an absolute fool."

Dessa looked at the door for a while.

"I was Kindled at thirteen and nothing happened," she said. "Nothing at all, for two years. Where I come from there's a phrase for it. *Kindled to nothing.* People say it in front of you." She moved her hand on her knee. "Then one night a man told me something about an empty mind and an open soul. And something came on in me, and it's been on ever since. And if you asked me to make it bigger, I couldn't. I couldn't tell you how.

"So no. I don't think you're a fool. I think you've got one that only comes when it's needed. Everybody would tell you that's worse." She paused. "It came twice this week. Both times it was needed. Add that up."

Jab did not answer. Callie saw his hand go to his collar and find nothing, and stay there.

"She's still," said Dessa. "The knocking's stopped. They're bringing her."

The heavy door opened.

---

Her mother came out on the rolling bed with her eyes shut and her hair every which way. She opened her eyes as they crossed the yellow line, and found Callie.

"It knocked," she said. Her voice was very tired. "Like a man mending a roof inside my head. I didn't care for it."

"You stayed still."

"I'm very good at staying still." Her mother turned her head and saw the chair, and Senna in it, and Dessa on the floor, and a boy on a bed with a wooden fish. "Oh. We've got company."

"You didn't move once," said Dessa. "The whole forty minutes. I was counting."

Callie's mother looked at her.

"I know," she said. "I could tell there was somebody counting." She shut her eyes. "There usually is."

Then it was Tonk's turn. The man in white asked him if there was anything on him, and Tonk said no, and asked Jab if there was anything on *him*, and Jab held up the paper tray.

"It's a rule," said Jab. "No metal past the line. So I stop here."

Tonk looked at the yellow line on the floor.

"Do *they* keep it?" he said.

"Everybody keeps it. That's the only reason it's any good."

Tonk looked at the line a moment longer. Then he let go of his brother's hand.

Nobody had asked him to. Callie saw it happen. She saw Jab's hand stay in the air for a second after, open, and then come down to the tray.

Tonk went in with the fish. The door shut like a lid.

The knocking started again. Jab sat very still.

Callie laid the hook across her knees with her hands loose on it. She sat on the bench beside him, saying nothing, and let the knocking count itself.

---

The man in the long coat came to the door of hut fourteen at dusk.

Callie knew him. He had stood on top of the bank at the south gate at fourteen minutes and looked down at all of them, and at her longest. He was tall and thin, about fifty, with a close grey beard and a quiet face. He had a folded paper in one hand and did not offer it.

"I'm from the yard office," he said. He did not give his name. "I've read your card. Starting with the first line. Mr Hallet said you'd asked."

"It's the true one."

"I think they're all true. That's why it came to me." He stayed on the step. "We'd like you to come to the north yard. Not now. When you're not dry. We'd like to see what it says on the card. The hook, and the thing you can put in it. Nobody here's seen that. Nobody I've ever met has."

Callie stood in the doorway. She felt Katori behind her, not moving.

"My mother's got a doctor at the ninth hour tomorrow," she said. "I'm not doing anything till after that."

"No. Of course not." He put the paper back in his coat. "There's no hurry. It's only a request. You can say no to it." He looked at her a moment longer. "Most people don't."

He nodded to Katori and went away down the row.

"Most people don't," said Pell, behind her.

"I heard."

"That," said Pell, "is the sort of thing I'd want a column for."

---

There were five chairs round the bed in the morning. Callie counted them before she had taken three steps into the ward.

Somebody had written *all of them* at the desk. Somebody else had read it and fetched five chairs and set them in a half circle and gone away. Callie looked at them and did not like them, and knew exactly why this time. She had seen Senna's four.

Her mother was sitting up against three pillows with her hair brushed.

"There you all are," she said. "Sit down. They've counted you."

They sat. Katori and Pell and Ines and Otto, and Callie in the chair nearest the bed. Across the ward Senna sat up with a cup in both hands, watching her fingers round it. A nurse came to pull the curtain.

"Leave it," said her mother. "She'll hear anyway. I'd rather she heard it straight."

Across the ward, Senna kept her eyes on her cup. But the corner of her mouth moved.

Doctor Sowerby came in at the ninth hour exactly, small and bald and in a hurry. He stopped at the foot of the bed and looked at each of them the way Ines looked at a range.

"Right," he said. "The bad thing first. Then the good thing. Then the bill. Stop me."

Nobody stopped him.

"There's a growth in her head." He put two fingers against the side of his own head, on the left, above and behind the ear. "Here. Deep. It's in the part that makes words, and the part that tells the right hand and the right leg what to do. It's been growing since before the winter. Probably well before. We can't take it out. There's no way in that wouldn't take more of her than it took of it. And there's nothing we have that makes it smaller. I've looked. I looked again this morning, because I didn't want to be wrong."

He looked at her mother and nobody else.

"We can't treat it," he said. "I want that said with you sitting up. In a fortnight somebody in a corridor will say *treated* to your daughter, and she'll hear something it isn't."

"Thank you," said her mother.

---

Callie heard the next part and did not hear it.

The words went into her the way the cold had gone into her on the causeway, a long way down and all at once. She was in a chair by a bed. She was also in a hayfield in the spring with her grandfather in the grass. She was also on the tailboard at three in the morning, watching her mother try to braid her own hair with a hand that would not take instruction.

And she was counting, though she did not know what.

"—the good thing," Sowerby was saying. She made herself hear it.

"There's swelling round it. A lot of what you've been living with is the swelling, not the growth. The headaches, mostly. Some of the hand, maybe. Some of the words. That we can bring down. There's a medicine. Pills, morning and night. Some people get a bit of the hand back. Some get words back. Nearly everybody gets their mornings back without the head.

"The fits. There's a second medicine for those. They'll come less. Maybe not at all, for a while." He put his hand down. "It'll make your days better. I can promise that. I've got the numbers in this folder. I can't promise it makes more of them. I won't say it does."

"No," said her mother. "Don't."

"And then there's the part everybody asks me for. So I'll—"

"Stop," said her mother.

He stopped.

"I know what the next part is," said her mother. "You're going to tell me how long."

"If you want it."

"I don't."

Nobody in the ward moved.

"That's your right," said Sowerby at once. He said it at once, and without any weight at all. "It's in the folder. It'll stay in the folder. Whoever you say can have it, whenever you say. Including you. You can change your mind any day you like, and I won't ask you why."

"I want it," said Callie.

---

It came out of her too loud for the ward. Across the room Senna looked up.

Her mother turned her head on the pillow.

"I want the number," said Callie. Her voice was doing something. She made it stop. "I'm not asking to be frightened. I'm asking to know. I'm the household. The man at the window said so. For the window, it's me."

"Then the window can have it," said her mother. "You can't."

Callie stared at her.

"It isn't yours," said her mother. She said it very gently and very clearly, and every word was in the right place. "It's mine. It's in my head. It's the one thing in there that's still mine to decide about."

"I have to know how many—"

"I know you do. That's why." Her mother's left hand came across the blanket and found Callie's wrist. "I've watched you do a sum every morning since Gaunt's Reach. Fifteen days. Nineteen. Nine pounds of flour. Seven matches. A hundred and fifty-eight seconds. You do it before you've got your boots on. You'd do it every morning with this, and you'd get it right, you always do. And you'd take one off every day before you'd said good morning to me." She held on. "I'm not giving you that one. I'm not giving you the last sum, love. You can hate me for it. I'd rather you hated me for that than did it."

Callie could not say anything at all.

"And I'm not having it myself either," said her mother, to the room now. "I've spent a month on the back of a wagon counting miles. I'm not going to spend whatever I've got counting days." She let go. "That's all. You can go on to the bill."

Doctor Sowerby looked at her for a long moment. Then he took the folder from under his arm and put it on the end of the bed, closed, with his hand flat on it.

"The bill," he said.

---

The bill was short. Her care was clinical. It had gone through at the gate. It was owed to nobody. If anybody said different, they could come and hear him explain it slowly.

"And where she sleeps," said Katori quietly.

"Where she sleeps is a different window," said Sowerby. "I don't sit at it."

"I'm not sleeping in a ward," said Callie's mother.

He looked back at her.

"I've nothing against it. It's very clean. The woman opposite is the best company I've had since the spring. But I'm not living in a bed with strings up the back of my gown, being asked my name eleven times, while my daughter sits on a bench outside waiting for the afternoon." She looked at him steadily. "Can I come in for the pills? Mornings? Like going to market?"

"You can. Most people don't want to. You'll need somebody with you who knows the fits. Who knows what to do when one comes."

"I know the fits," said Callie. It came out flat and small.

"She counts them," said her mother.

Sowerby looked at Callie, and back.

"Then I'll see you in the mornings," he said. "Eighth hour. With the girl. You can go tomorrow."

He went away down the ward as fast as he had come.

---

"Well," said Senna, into the silence.

Everybody looked at her. She was not looking at any of them. She was looking at the cup in her hands, watching her fingers on it.

"You heard me yesterday," she said. "I know you did. I'll only say it's still a good rule." She drank, looking at the cup, and put it down. "That's all. I'll go back to my tea."

Callie's mother lay against her pillows. She looked at the ceiling. Then she looked at the five of them in their five counted chairs.

"All right," she said. "Once. And then nobody does it for me.

"I've got a thing in my head. They can't get it out and they can't make it smaller. They can make it easier. They're going to, and I'm going to let them, and I'll take their pills like a good woman.

"I'm not having a number. There's one in that little man's folder and it can stay there. Nobody in this room goes and asks him for it behind my back. If I find out anybody has, I'll be extremely rude, and I've been practising.

"And I'm not living in a ward. I'm going to live in a hut with a stove, with my daughter, and with whichever of you will have me. And I'm going to walk in here every morning for pills, like somebody going to buy bread."

She stopped and got her breath. Some of the words had come slower than others. None of them had come wrong.

"That's it," she said. "Nobody says it for me again."

Otto was crying. He did it without any sound, sitting very straight with his stitched arm in its sling, and he did not wipe his face.

Pell had the atlas on his knee. He left it shut. After a while he put his hand flat on the cover and left it there.

"Not everything goes in the book," he said, to nobody.

"No," said Callie's mother. "Not everything."

---

Callie went out.

She had not decided to; she got up and went down the ward and past the desk and out through the doors, and she kept going until she was round the back of the long building, where there was nobody. There was a strip of wet grass between the wall and a drain, and a stack of old crates, and grey sky.

She sat on the crates with her arms round her ribs and her head down on her knees.

She did not cry. It was too far down to reach, the way the well had been on the plain when it was dry to the stone. And in the dark behind her eyes she was counting. She did not know what. She could not stop.

Katori found her there.

She said nothing. She sat down on the next crate, a little apart, with her cap pulled down, and waited.

"She won't let me have it," said Callie, into her knees.

"No."

"It's the only thing I can do. I count. I put it on my card. It's the one thing I know how to do for her."

"What would you do with it?" said Katori.

Callie lifted her head.

"The number," said Katori. "Say he'd told you. Say it was whatever it was. What would you do with it?"

Callie opened her mouth. She shut it.

"Count," she said.

"Yes," said Katori. "That's why."

---

They sat a long time.

"She's not asking you to stop holding her," said Katori at last. "She's asking you to stop looking at the number. That's not the same thing. You can hold on to her with both hands for as long as there is. You just can't count it."

"I don't know how."

"No. You'll find out. Badly and slowly. You'll hate it." Katori stood up. Then she did not go. She stood looking at the long wall of the building. "There's a boy on a corridor floor in there. His brother went in at the ninth hour, the same as your mother's doctor. I said I'd be there when it came out."

Callie looked up at her.

"Go," she said.

"You'll be all right?"

"No," said Callie. "Go anyway. He hasn't got anybody. He said so at the window. *Two.*"

Katori looked at her a moment longer. Something moved behind her face, the way wind goes over a field.

Then she went.

---

In the middle of the day Callie found herself at the south pen, in front of the hatch.

She had not planned to go. Ivor Hallet looked up from his book, and saw her face, and put down his pen.

"Miss."

"My mother's care is clinical," said Callie. "She's going to live in the hut. Fourteen. What does it take to stay in the hut? After fourteen days."

Hallet told her. He told it plainly and in order, the way he told everything. Two gates, kept apart on purpose. Medicine, which was not withheld from anybody and never had been. And residence, which was reviewed. A household was reviewed on what the station reckoned it contributed.

"Not on what it needs," said Callie.

"Not on what it needs. I won't dress that up for you."

"What do we contribute?"

Hallet turned back some pages.

"On paper? Three Handfuls, lifted, less station. A sponsor line in a name that came in heavy. And you, miss. A red thread. A card that's gone upstairs." He looked up. "There's work in the north yard for red threads. Most of it's ordinary and counts ordinary. There's one kind that counts heavier than the rest." He picked up the pen and did not write. "I'd rather you heard about it from somebody who's doing it. You're in Yard C. You've got a neighbour who signed for it yesterday. He'll tell you the truth, because he doesn't seem able to tell anybody anything else."

---

Her mother already knew. Of course she did.

"Senna told me," she said, when Callie came back into the ward. "While you were at the window. Don't look like that. There was nobody else to tell me, and she wanted to, and I wanted to know."

Across the ward Senna sat with a cup in both hands.

"Four seasons," said Senna, not looking up. "The worst work there is. Holding a road shut while people behind you sack what comes through. He signed yesterday for my address. Not my medicine. My address."

"And you let him," said Callie's mother.

"I let him. Because he'd decided, and it was his to decide. If I'd said no, he'd have done it anyway, without me saying yes." Senna drank, watching the cup. "Yours is thirteen. Mine was a grown man who'd already decided. It's a different sum."

Callie stood at the foot of her mother's bed. She could not sit.

"You don't get to spend yourself on me," said her mother.

"I haven't—"

"Not seasons. Not for an address. Not for a window." Her mother's voice was very steady. "I told you at the bottom of the scarp you don't get to spend me. It goes both ways. I didn't say that half then, because I didn't know I'd need to."

"Then where do you live?" Callie heard her own voice crack and did not care. "After fourteen days. If there's nothing on the paper—"

"I don't know," said her mother. "I've got eleven days before the fourteenth. I'm not going to spend them on the fourteenth. And neither are you." She looked up at Callie from the pillow. "You promised me. On the plain. You don't sign one thing for me without me. This is me, saying don't."

Callie stood at the foot of the bed with her hands empty.

She did not say *I promise*. She had promised already. She only nodded, once, and her mother saw it and shut her eyes.

---

At the fourth hour a boy of about ten came down the row with three buff cards in his hand.

Callie watched him from the step of fourteen. He stopped at eleven and knocked and waited. He stopped at twelve, where nobody answered, and slid a card under the door. Then he came to fourteen.

"Miss Callie?" he said. He said the rest of her name, the one off the scale-house door, carefully, as if he had practised it.

He held out the card and waited to be told there was no reply.

She took it. It was the size of her hand.

*Requested at the Director's office, second floor, yard office, concerning the north road incident. Attendance is not compulsory. Please bring your band.*

The date under it was eleven days away. It was the day of her mother's review.

Callie read it twice. Then she looked down the row at the door of eleven, and the door of twelve with a card under it, and the step of fourteen where she was sitting. Three doors. One boy with three cards.

"There's no reply," she said.

The boy nodded and went away up the row.
