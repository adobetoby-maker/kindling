# Chapter 37 — Callie: The Flap

The pump was still in the pump house.

Callie had not looked at it the night before. She had been too cold, and too tired, and the wall had been something to put a fire against and nothing else. Now she went in through the gap where the door had been, with the lantern, because she could not sit by the fire and think about a rope any more.

It stood in the middle of the stone floor under the open sky. It was iron, and it was big, bigger than the cart. There was a long iron drum lying on its side on a stone bed, and at one end of it a wheel as high as she was, with spokes, and a rod going from the wheel into the drum. A pipe as thick as her body came up out of the floor on the river side and went into the bottom of the drum. Two more came out of the top of it and went through the wall toward the river, toward the broken pipes in the water.

It was all rust. It had not moved in seventy years. She put her hand on the wheel and it did not move now.

"It's a pump," said Pell, from outside.

He was on the cart, drawn up against the outside of the wall. The cart was so close that its rack was a pace from the gap. Callie held the lantern up and he could see in. He was sitting with his back to the rack and his leg out along the boards, the way he had sat all day.

"I know it's a pump," said Callie.

"I know you know," said Pell. "Ask me what you came in to ask me."

She stood by the wheel with the lantern.

"How does it work?" she said.

---

Pell told her.

He told her the way he had told her about the wheel, sitting back with his hands on his knee, not looking at her, looking at the thing.

"It lifts," he said. "And it pushes. That's all a pump does. Two strokes." He nodded at the drum. "There's a plunger in that. The wheel goes round and the rod goes in and out. When it comes out, it lifts. It pulls the water up out of the river through that pipe in the floor, into the drum. When it goes in, it pushes. It pushes the water out of the drum, out through the top, up those pipes, over the river, up the hill. Wherever they wanted it."

"It lifts and it pushes."

"Lift, push. Lift, push." He moved his hand. "Only there's a thing in between. There's a flap."

Callie waited.

"In the bottom of the drum, where the river pipe comes in," said Pell. "A flap. Leather, on a hinge, like a door. When it lifts, the flap opens and the water comes up past it into the drum. When it pushes, the flap shuts. It shuts because the water's pushing on it the wrong way. So what it lifted can't go back down the pipe to the river. It's got nowhere to go but out the top." He looked at the drum. "My father had a little one, on the farm, for the trough. A hand one. The flap went every spring. When the flap goes, you can hear it."

"What does it sound like?"

"Like it's working," said Pell. "That's the thing. It lifts, and it pushes, and it sounds like it's working all day. But it pushes the water straight back down the pipe it came up. It lifts it up and it pushes it down. Up and down, the same water, all day. Nothing comes out of the top." He shook his head. "You'd not know. You'd pump for an hour and go to the trough and it'd be dry."

Callie stood very still with the lantern.

She was thinking about the heap in the corner. The ten, and the heap drawn out into threads and gone into the rope. The three, and the corner swelling. She was thinking about Jab with his hand on the rope by the door, saying *nothing, it's going past*. And the three, and the push, and his face.

"If you wanted to stop it," she said. "The pump. If you were in the drum."

"I'd not be in the drum."

"If you were. If you wanted it to stop pushing water up the hill."

Pell thought about it. She saw him think about it, the way he thought about a distance.

"You'd not do it on the lift," he said slowly. "On the lift it's got the whole river behind it. You'd stand in front of that pipe in the floor and it'd lift the river at you. You'd not stop the river." He looked at the pipe going down into the stone. "You'd do it on the push. On the push it's only got what's in the drum. What it's just lifted. That's all it's got. A drum's worth."

"And if you took the drum's worth," said Callie. "On the push. Every time. So it pushed and nothing got up the pipe."

"Then it'd lift the river again," said Pell. "And push it at you again. And you'd take that." He was quiet. "Till the river ran out. Or you did."

"And if somebody shut the river?"

Pell looked at her properly then.

"Then it'd have a drum's worth," he said. "And no more. And it'd push it at you, and you'd take it, and it'd lift nothing, because there's nothing to lift. And push again. Less." He was quiet for a long time. "It'd pump itself dry. It doesn't know how to do anything else. A pump doesn't stop because the water's gone. It stops when somebody stops the wheel."

Callie put the lantern down on the stone bed of the pump.

"Nobody stops its wheel," she said.

"Then it'd go on," said Pell. "Pushing nothing. Into whatever was taking it."

---

She went out to the fire.

They were all there. Miss Wren had changed Rook's pad and was sitting with her book. Jab was sitting with his right hand palm up on his knee. Toren had the paper. Rook was on the canvas with his eyes open. Nobody was talking. They had been not talking since *tomorrow we decide*.

"Pell," said Callie. "Say it again. The pump."

Pell said it again, from the cart, while she stood by the fire with her hands open. Lift and push, and the flap, and the river. On the lift it's got the whole river. On the push it's only got what it's lifted.

Jab had looked up at *the push*. He did not look away from Pell until Pell had finished.

"On the push," said Jab.

"That's what I did wrong," he said. He said it to his hand. "I put it on the rope in the ten. On the lift. And it went past, because it had the whole hill behind it and it was going somewhere. And then the three came, and it pushed, and I was a hole." He turned the hand over. "And there was nothing shut. The whole hill was behind the push. So it kept pushing."

"If somebody shut the river," said Callie.

"The lee," said Toren.

He had put the pencil down.

"The lee stopped the floor," he said. "At the door. It stopped everything crawling in. It didn't stop the rope." He was looking at the fire, not at her. "But the rope's a thing. It's a rope. It's lying there. If the rope's cut—"

"It mends in the three," said Jab.

"It mends in the three if it can reach." Toren looked up. "If it's cut at the door, and Callie's lee is in the door, in the cut, it can't reach. It can grow up to the lee. It can't grow through it." He stopped. "Then what's in the room's all there is. That's the drum. The river's on the other side of Callie."

Nobody said anything.

Miss Wren had opened her book. She was not writing. She was looking at Toren.

"Say it in order," she said. "All of it. From the stair."

---

He said it in order.

He said it the way he had said everything since the forty-third, the way he had said the siding and the wash. He did not have the answers. He only had to ask for them in the right order, and put them where everybody could see them, and see if they stood.

It would be in the room. Everybody agreed with that. Rook said it, from the canvas: "It goes where the grey is." The grey had been lying in the first room all afternoon, and the heap in the corner, and the husks killed and killed again. And the carrier was there. They did not think it wanted the carrier. But it would be there.

Miss Wren said the rest of it, so that it was said.

"It's in the first room," she said. "The room she sent us to, and the box she sent us for. It came at us there." She did not look at the hill. "So we go to the first door and no further. Not the second, not to look. Nowhere near the round one. We're not going hunting for anything. If it's not in the first room in the morning, we don't go looking for it. We come out."

There were two ways into the room from the corridor for the rope. The floor, through the door, where the rope would lie across the threshold. And the ceiling, through the hole above the door, and down the wall.

"I cut both," said Toren. "The ceiling first, outside, before it goes in the hole. Then the floor at the threshold. The heel. In a ten. Before it pushes."

"And me in the door," said Callie. "On the threshold. In your cut." She put her hand out flat and tipped it back. "The lee off the curve, standing up into the corridor. And leaned back. Up the wall over the door. Over the hole. Like a lean-to, the wrong way up."

"Can you?"

"It's a door," said Callie. "And a bit. It's still. It's on concrete." She thought about it. "A door's the cheapest thing I know. A door and a bit's more. And if there's husks in the corridor, and there will be, they'll come at it, and that's more again." She looked at Miss Wren. "I want two. Settled. Tonight."

Miss Wren did not answer at once.

"Two's more than you've ever carried," she said.

"I settle well. You said. It's the only thing I do better than Toren."

"It's not the only thing," said Toren.

"It's the only thing tonight," said Callie.

Miss Wren looked at her for a long time. Then she wrote it.

"Two," she said. "You'll settle it slowly, and I'll sit with you, and if it slops I'll stop you at one and a half." She looked up. "And you'll be in the room."

"I'll be in the door."

"You'll be in the room," said Miss Wren. "With your back to it. You'll be facing the corridor with the lee in front of you, and everything that's in that room will be behind you. You'll not see it. You'll not see any of it."

"I know," said Callie.

She did know. She had known since she said *in the door*. She had known it the way she knew a husk line.

"Jab," said Toren.

"On the push," said Jab. "Not the lift. When you cut it, it'll mend. That's all it does. It'll push into the cut at the three. I put my hand on the cut, on the room side, at the three, and it pushes into me. And I take a drum's worth. And I put it in a vent jar, and stopper it. And I take my hand off." He looked at Miss Wren. "Before the ten. Every time. Off before the ten."

"I'll say *off*," said Miss Wren. "At the end of every three. Every one. You'll not wait for me to say it twice."

"I'll not wait."

"And what's in the room is all it's got," said Toren. "And every three it pushes some of it into Jab, and Jab puts it in a jar. And every ten it lifts what's lying in the room. And it's less." He stopped. "Pell said. It'll pump itself dry."

"It'll not stand there and let you," said Rook.

He said it from the canvas. He had not moved, but his voice was clear.

"It had a husk on your strut all afternoon," said Rook. "It'll make more. It'll put them in your way. It'll throw what it's got at whatever's worked, and Jab's hand is worked, and so is Callie's back. And so is you." He looked at Toren. "You'll not be cutting. You'll be keeping it off them."

"Then I'll keep it off them," said Toren. "And cut when I can." He looked at the paper. "Every rope it's got in the room is a way it moves. Every one's a way it throws. I cut them, one at a time. Every cut's a hole. Every hole, it pushes, at the three. And Jab's there."

"And when it's got nothing left to push with?" said Miss Wren.

"It opens," said Jab. "At the three. In the middle. The light." He did not look at anybody. "I saw it open. It opens every time it pushes. That's where the push comes from."

"Then that's where I'll put the point," said Toren.

---

"Say what it costs," said Miss Wren.

Callie said it.

"Twenty-nine," she said. "Inside. One jar open, with five in it. Three whole." She did the sum out loud, because Miss Wren would want it out loud. "Settling tonight. Two in me, that's a Handful and three quarters more. A Handful and a half in Toren. Half in you, Miss Wren, to make you one. That's three and three quarters." She stopped. "Twenty-five and a quarter. The line's sixteen. Nine and a quarter to work with."

"Nine and a quarter," said Toren.

"It's not twelve," said Callie.

"It's not twelve," said Toren. "It's nine and a quarter. That's what I'll pay." He wrote it. "If it's not done at the line, we come out, finished or not."

"And if it's nearly done?" said Miss Wren. "At the line. If it's lying there with nothing left and you've got the point up."

Toren did not answer at once.

"Then I'll say it," he said. "Then. Out loud. What we're doing and what it costs and how long. And you'll write it. And it'll be mine."

"It'll be mine as well," said Miss Wren. "It's my line." She wrote. "And the rule's still the rule, after. Two Flasks left inside, everybody out. If you say it, it's once. It goes in front of her at home with both our names on it."

"And the home three don't cross that bridge," said Toren. "Whatever happens. Whatever it's lying there with. They're for the road." He looked at the cart, at the rack behind the driver's bench, where the three jars stood grey to the shoulder in the dark. "Say it, somebody who's not going in."

"The home three don't cross the bridge," said Pell. "I'm sitting on them."

---

She settled by the fire with Miss Wren beside her.

It was the most she had ever taken. Miss Wren weighed it out of the open jar onto the square of paper on her knee, a Handful and three quarters, grey and fine. Callie put her palm flat on it and let it come.

It came. It always came well, for her. It came up into her palm slow and cold, and went down, and lay at the bottom of the well on the quarter that was there, and she felt the stone get bigger. She did not haul. She let it come at the rate it came, and she thought about nothing, the way Miss Wren had taught them at the long table.

She did not think about nothing.

She thought about the door. She thought about standing in it with the hook on the threshold and the lee up, facing the corridor, and the room behind her. Toren behind her, and Jab, and Miss Wren, and the lifter, and she would not see any of them.

She had always held what she could see. That was what Rook had taught her on the forty-fourth. *Watch who's inside. Put your eyes on the people and hold them there.* She had held Jab's shoulders on the old line, and Jab and Pell under the lee at the wash, and taken Toren in and out of it with her eyes. The lee went where she looked. It had always gone where she looked.

Tomorrow she would be looking at a corridor.

The lee would not be over anybody. It would not be shaped like where they were. It would be a wall across a door, standing between a corridor and a room, keeping the corridor out. Nobody would be inside it. Everybody would be behind it.

*You can't have all of us,* Miss Wren had said on the forty-fourth. *You'll have to decide who's worth the lee.*

She understood it now with her palm on the paper. It was not about who was worth it. Tomorrow the lee was not for anybody. It was for the thing that was not them. It was for keeping the rest of the hill off, so that the three people behind her could do what only they could do, where she could not see them do it.

She would not be carrying them. She would be holding the door so they could carry it.

*Stop holding so hard. Let what's been built hold.*

Her mother had said that with her words half gone, on the edge of the bed, with Callie's fingers round her wrist. She had opened Callie's fingers off her wrist, one at a time.

"One and a half," said Miss Wren quietly. "It's lying still. Go on."

Callie went on. The last quarter came up into her palm and went down and lay there.

"Two," said Miss Wren. "Is it still?"

Callie went down to look. The stone was big at the bottom of the well, bigger than it had ever been, and it was still. It was not slopping. It was not in the hall. It was lying where she had put it.

"It's still," she said.

"Then it's yours," said Miss Wren. She wrote it. "Road, none. Inside, twenty-five and a quarter. One open, with a Handful and a quarter in it. Three whole." She looked up. "Say it back."

Callie said it back.

---

She had the first watch with Pell, because Pell was awake.

He sat on the cart with his back to the rack and the atlas shut on his knee, and she stood by the cart wheel with the hook and the counter. The fire was down. Toren had settled his Handful and a half at the fire for most of two hours with his palm flat and his face shut, and then lain down by Jab and counted them. Jab had slept at once. Miss Wren had not.

Across the river the fence stood black against the dark. The hill went up beyond it. Over the flat top of it there were no stars, and round the place with no stars there was the light the colour of the bottom of a pond. It thickened and thinned.

"I'm counting it," said Pell. "In case anybody asks."

"Can you see the falls?"

"No. It's too dark. I can see the edge." He was quiet. "I'd like to see the falls. I'd like to know if it's more at night."

They watched it.

"I said what I said at the fire," said Pell, after a while. "About a box and a half short. About the woman's table."

"I know."

"I'd say it again."

"I know you would."

"I said it so it had been said," said Pell. "By somebody who wasn't going in. Somebody has to say it, on a road, or you go in without anybody having said it, and then you don't know you chose." He looked across the river at the hill. "It's said now. It's written down. So now you can go in and do it properly."

Callie looked at him.

"That's all," said Pell. "That's what saying it's for."

She stood by the wheel and did not say anything for a long time.

"Pell," she said. "Your father. With the pump."

"What about him?"

"Did he mend the flap? Every spring?"

"Every spring," said Pell. "Cut a new one out of an old boot and put it in. Took him ten minutes." He was quiet. "Took me a year to learn how, and he let me do it wrong for most of that year. He'd stand there and watch me pump water down the pipe for an hour and not say anything. Then he'd say *now go and look at the trough.*"

"That's cruel."

"It's a good way to learn about flaps," said Pell. "You never forget which way they go."

---

In the morning Rook was cooler.

Not cool. Miss Wren had her fingers on his wrist at first light and her face said so. But the sweat had stopped, and his eyes were clear, and when she lifted the pad the red round the stitches had not gone any further out in the night. It had not come in either.

"It's holding," she said.

"Like the post," said Rook.

"The post was in lead," said Miss Wren. She gave him the second of the four papers in boiled water, and he drank it and made the same face.

Then he got up.

He did it in three movements, with a hand on the cart wheel and a hand on Pell's good knee, and Pell said "Mind the leg" and Rook said "I'm minding it." He stood with his coat over his shoulders and walked, slowly, down the gravel to the near end of the bridge, and sat down at the foot of the first post with his back against it. He had not asked anybody.

"Sitting's allowed," he said, when Miss Wren looked at him.

She measured the rest of them at the cold fire. Toren's own had come back in the night to two turns, near enough, under the stone he had settled. Callie's own was three parts. Miss Wren did her own last and wrote it without saying it. Then she took the last three vent jars out of the straw on the cart and put them in the canvas bag.

They ate. They drank the boiled water and a little of the full skin. They said the rules at the bridge end, and *pull the door* at the end of them.

Rook said the rules with them from the post. At the end he looked up at Callie.

"You'll have your back to them," he said.

"I know."

"When I shut the road at the Sag," said Rook, "I never once looked round at the lifters behind me. Not once, in six weeks. I looked at what was coming up out of the bottom." He was looking at the hill. "If I'd looked round, I'd have dropped it. You can't hold a thing and look behind it. You hold what's in front. They'll hold what's behind." He looked back at her. "You'll want to look. Don't."

"I'll not look," said Callie.

"You will," said Rook. "Once. Everybody does. Then you'll not do it again."

---

They went in faster the second time, at the end of the morning.

There was nothing to drag. The sledge was in the first room, under a hill. They went along the pipes on the north side of the blocks, and up the Z, and round the heap at the first bend. Miss Wren drew them at the top of the road at the twentieth turn, off the open jar, and Callie weighed it. A Handful and a quarter had been in it at the gate.

"A half," she said. "Three quarters left in it."

At the top of the road she counted the sky. Ten, and three. At the ninth count a fall came out of the bottom of it into the drum.

"One in twelve," she said. "The same as yesterday going in."

Toren wrote it.

The lanterns in the hall. The yellow rail. The stair. The long grey slide of the husk's ash down the steps from the top, where Toren had killed it against the hook, lying on the stair in a streak and going down it in threads. They went down beside it. At the landing the counter said fifty. At the bottom it said sixty.

The door was open.

It stood against the concrete wall of the flat place where it had swung the day before, wide, as far as it went. Toren looked at it as he came off the last step. He looked at the place on the floor two paces from it where his strut had stood.

"Open," he said.

"Good," said Miss Wren. "That's thirty breaths we don't have to spend."

---

The corridor had changed.

The tray on the right-hand wall was bowed all along. The rope in it was as thick as a leg, grey, heavy, crawling. It had bent two of the brackets so that the tray hung down at a slant between them. And where the small trough went across the ceiling toward the first door, the rope in it was as thick as an arm.

And on the floor, along the foot of the left-hand wall, there was another rope.

It had not been there yesterday. It came out of the dark from the west along the floor, as thick as Toren's thigh. It lay along the foot of the wall on the room side, heavy and grey, and it turned in at the first door and went over the threshold into the room.

"It laid that," said Jab quietly. "Coming along. Behind it."

Callie read the floor. The threads came along it from the stair, the way they had all day yesterday, and went into the big rope on the floor and the one in the tray. Everything went west. But the big rope on the floor went east, into the first room. It was going in. It was feeding the room.

"A hundred and ten," she said. "At the first door. From here."

They went along the corridor to it. They went on the right-hand side this time, under the tray, because the rope on the floor was on the left. Nobody touched either one. At the doorway Callie stood two paces off and held the lantern out, and looked in.

---

It was lying on the carrier.

She had thought it would be a heap. It was a heap. It lay in the middle of the room against the boxes, grey, as high as her waist and as long as two of her, with its back up in a long hump. It lay over the sledge and the carrier, and the lead lid of the carrier was under it. She could see one iron handle sticking out of its side. It lay there the way a dog lies over a bone in a yard, not eating it, lying on it. It was lying on what they had come for.

The big rope came in over the threshold and went across the floor into it. The rope from the hole came down the wall beside the door and went across the floor into it. And out of it, out of the hump, three more ropes came, each as thick as an arm, and lay across the heap of boxes and the floor like arms flung out in sleep.

It was not asleep. It had nothing in it to sleep. She could see the grey on it crawl, all of it, toward the middle, ten breaths. Then it stopped.

At the three, the line of light opened along the top of the hump. The colour of the bottom of a pond. For three breaths.

It shut.

"Two heaps," said Toren. He had the lantern on his belt and the spike dark on his fist. "The corner. And by the cabinets."

"And one out here," said Miss Wren behind them. "By the stair door. Where you killed the one on the ground yesterday."

"They'll stand up when we start," said Jab.

"They'll stand up when we start," said Miss Wren.

She stood with her back to the right-hand wall of the corridor with the glass in her hand and the open jar in the crook of her arm and the canvas bag of vent jars on her shoulder. Three of them. She looked at the four of them, one at a time. At Toren with the spike. At Jab with the frame, and the lantern hanging from it, and his right hand held away from his side. At Callie with the hook.

"On a ten," she said. "Mine. I'll count it."

She counted it. They all counted it with her. Callie heard Toren do it under his breath. The line of light shut on the top of the hump.

"Ten," said Miss Wren. "Go."

---

Toren lit the heel.

He did not go in over the threshold. He stopped in the corridor in front of the door and reached up first, over his head, to the small trough in the ceiling where it went into the wall above the doorframe. The rope in it was as thick as an arm. He put the heel into it a hand from the hole and dragged it through.

It came apart. Half of it hung out of the trough over the door, spilling.

Then he put the heel down across the big rope where it lay on the threshold, low, and dragged it through. The rope was as thick as his thigh. It did not come apart like a strand. It came apart like a sack of flour opened with a hook, all along the cut, and the grey spilled out of both ends onto the concrete. He went through it and into the room, and turned, and let the heel go dark.

"Cut," he said. "Both."

Miss Wren went in over the threshold after him, and Jab with the frame, close, the way they had said at the fire. Callie went last.

She stepped into the doorway.

She stepped over the spilled grey at the threshold, into the room, and turned round. She stood with her back to the room and her face to the corridor. She put the back of the hook's curve down on the threshold, on the concrete, in the cut. Still.

She reached for the stone at the bottom of the well. It was big, and it was still. She let it go into the curve, where it touched.

The lee stood up.

It stood up off the outside of the curve, into the corridor, the width of the door frame, pale. She did not let it stand straight. She let it lean. She leaned it back from the threshold, up, and up, along the corridor side of the wall over the door. She leaned it over the top of the doorframe and on up the wall to the ceiling, over the square hole with the cut rope in it, like a lean-to roof leaned the wrong way. It was a door and a bit. She felt the stone go into it harder than a door.

The cut end of the big rope lay on the corridor side of the curve, a foot from her boot, spilling. The cut end of the ceiling rope hung out of its trough above her, on the corridor side of the lee, a hand from the hole it could not get to.

"Holding," said Callie.

Behind her, in the room, she heard Miss Wren set the bag of vent jars down on something that rang like steel. She heard Jab's frame scrape against the cabinets.

"Seven," said Miss Wren, counting. "Eight. Nine."

Callie looked at the corridor. The lantern on the floor where Miss Wren had put it lit the concrete in a yellow circle. It lit the big rope going away west along the foot of the left-hand wall, and the heavy rope in the tray on the right, and the cut ends in front of her, spilling. At the far edge of the light, by the stair door, there was the heap.

"Three," said Miss Wren.

The push came.

She felt it come against the lee from the corridor side. It was not a weight. It was the cut end of the big rope at her boot, pushing. It pushed out from its cut toward the threshold, to mend, the way the strands had pushed toward each other on the floor of the room. It came up against the pale foot of the lee where it stood on the curve, and could not go through, and heaped. She felt it heap. It was like somebody leaning a sack against the outside of a door she was holding shut.

Over her head, the cut end of the ceiling rope pushed out of its trough toward the hole, and met the lean of the lee, and stopped, and heaped against it there.

And at the far edge of the light, by the stair door, the heap swelled, and cracked, and something stood up out of it the colour of an old wasp nest.

It turned toward her.

Behind her, in the room, something moved.

She heard it. She did not see it. She heard a long heavy dry sound, like a wet rope lifted off a floor, and Toren say "It's up," and Jab say "Busy," in the same breath. She heard the air move behind her back. She felt it on her neck.

Then there was a sound behind her like a sack hitting a wall.

It was close. It was very close, a pace behind her, at the height of her shoulders. She felt it through the floor under her boots. She heard Toren make a noise through his teeth, and say "Holding," tight.

Something had come at her back. At the Ward. At the worked thing in the doorway. And Toren's strut had been there, and had taken it.

She did not turn round.

She had wanted to. She had felt her whole body start to, the way Toren's arm had started to go out when he fell in the stream, and she had stopped it. She stood in the doorway with the curve on the threshold and her hands open on the conduit and her eyes on the corridor, on the husk coming along the floor toward her from the stair door, and she held the lee.

"That's the three," said Miss Wren, behind her. "Jab. *Off.*"

Callie did not look round. She held the door.
