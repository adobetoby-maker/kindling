# Chapter 46 — Callie: The Wheel in the Machine

They hosed them at the gate like arrivals.

Callie had known they would. She had thought about it on the road, on the nights when she could not lie down, and she had not minded thinking about it. It was the pen, and the gate, and the grey coats with the hose. It was the first thing Meridian had ever done to her. It was the thing it did to everybody who came in from outside, whoever they were, and she found she wanted it done.

The water was cold. It came out of the hose in a hard grey rope and went over her hair and her face and down the back of her collar, and her ribs took every shiver of it. She stood with her arms out from her sides the way the grey coat told her, the right one out and the left one only a little. She did not make a sound.

"Hook," said the grey coat.

She gave it to him.

He held it up under the lamp by the gate. He read the counter against the curve, and then against the grip, and then he looked at the paper tag tied round the conduit below where the rag had been. *pen, 35th.*

"Fourth time, miss," he said.

"Third was the pen," said Callie.

"Third was the pen." He took a stub of pencil from behind his ear and wrote on the tag, under the old line, holding it flat against the steel. Then he gave the hook to the other grey coat for the washing.

"When do I get it back?"

"Morning," said the grey coat. "Same as always."

She stood in the lamplight dripping and watched them take it away. She had not been without it since the forty-first. Her right hand did not know what to do.

Behind her, Hallet was at his window with his pen on its string. He was saying things to a young man about the time of night and the distance from the Sag to the north gate. He said them quietly and at length. He had his ledger open, and he was writing them in.

*Six.* She saw him write it. And then, after a long moment in which he looked at the cart, *and a horse.*

---

Doctor Sowerby had Pell off the cart before the cart had stopped.

He did not say anything to Pell. He said a great deal to the two orderlies with the stretcher, and to the lamp, and to the air in general. Most of it was about people who put broken ankles on carts for ten days. Some of it was about the splint, which he looked at without touching.

"Who did this?" he said at last.

"Miss Wren," said Pell.

"With what?"

"The tailboard rail," said Pell. "And the canvas off the cart."

Sowerby looked at the splint a while longer.

"It's not bad," he said, in a voice that meant it was very good and he would not say so. "Take him."

Then he got to Rook.

Rook was lying on the canvas on his side with his eyes open. Sowerby stood over him with his arms folded and his face like a thundercloud.

"Well," said Rook.

"Don't," said Sowerby.

"I've a hole in my back," said Rook. "And a dog in it. And she gave me all four of your papers. One a day. Not twice."

Sowerby knelt on the wet gravel of the gate yard and lifted the pad off Rook's back with two fingers. He looked. He did not say anything. He put the back of his hand against Rook's neck.

"How long?" he said. To Miss Wren.

"Since the forty-seventh," said Miss Wren. "It declared at dawn. The worst was the forty-seventh and the fifty-first. It's not spread since the fifty-first. It's yellow at the foot."

Sowerby put the pad back.

"Take him," he said to the orderlies. And then, to Rook, not looking at him: "You'll lie on your face for a week. You'll eat what I give you. You'll not get up without somebody counting."

"I've been counted for a week," said Rook. "It's the only thing I'm good at now."

The orderlies took him.

Callie looked for Jab.

He was not in the gate yard. The frame was leaning against the cart wheel with its jars in it, and Toren was standing beside it with his hand on the rack, the way you put your hand on a thing so it will not walk off. Jab was not there.

She saw him then. He was halfway across the north yard in the dark, walking. Not running. Nobody ran in the north yard at night, on the cinders, with the whitewash gone slick. He was walking very fast toward the long building, with his right hand held out a little from his side and his left hand flat on his coat. He was walking the way he had walked out of the room under the hill with the frame on his back, the lantern swinging, when Miss Wren had said *out*.

She did not call after him.

---

Her mother was at the weighbridge.

Callie had not expected that. She had thought her mother would be in the hut, at this hour, or in the ward. She came round the end of the berm into the south yard in the dark, wet through, with no hook and her arm across her side, and the weighbridge hut had a lamp in its window.

Her mother was on the stool.

She was sitting at the window of the hut on the high stool with the tally board on her knee and her stick against the wall behind her. The weighman sat on a crate beside her, in his leather apron. There were three jars on the plate. They were the watch's, from the lip, the dirty ones. Otto must have brought them up before the push. The weighman had not weighed them. He was sitting on the crate with his hands on his knees, not weighing them.

Her mother saw her come round the berm.

She did not get up. Callie saw her not get up. She saw her mother's right hand move on the board, and the board stay where it was, pinned against her knee by the heel of that hand, and her left hand lift off it and go up, flat, the way you lift your hand at a window to somebody across a yard.

Callie went to the window.

"Two days," said her mother.

"I know."

"I counted them. Don't look like that." Her mother looked at her through the window. "I'm allowed. You weren't here."

Her voice was slower than it had been. Callie heard it. She heard the words come one at a time, each one with a little space in front of it. They came, though. Every one of them came.

"Your ribs," said her mother.

"Two. The same two. Cracked this time."

"Show me."

Callie leaned in at the window. Her mother put her left hand out through it and laid it flat against Callie's side, over the wet coat, over the strapping. She did not press. She held it there.

"You've been holding something," her mother said.

"A door," said Callie. "And a road. And the top of the Sag."

"On those."

"On the steel," said Callie. "Not on me. I'd seen it on the hill and not known it." She did not move away from the hand. "I know it now. I don't have to be behind it."

Her mother looked at her for a long time through the window of the weighbridge hut.

"No," she said. "You don't." She took her hand back. "What's in the frame?"

Callie told her.

She told it the way she had told it at every halt, flat, the whole of it. The fence's, eleven. Three in the fourth and eight in the fifth. Dirty, four. The lifter's, none. It had gone on the wash and the watch. Home, twenty-four.

"Not touched," said Callie.

Her mother looked at the three jars on the plate. Then she looked at the tally board on her knee, where she had written the watch's weights for fourteen days in big careful figures.

"I'll weigh your three when they let them out of the shed," she said. "At the plate, with the test weights on first. I tied the stoppers." She looked at Callie. "My knots. I'll know."

"They're your knots."

"Then I'll know," said her mother. She looked at the weighman beside her. "And these. In the morning. He's not weighing tonight."

"I'm not weighing tonight," said the weighman.

"He sat down at the fourth hour when the fog came up," said her mother, "and he's been sitting since." She looked out through the window at the dark yard, toward the Sag. "So have I."

---

In the ward, Keel looked at her first. He put his ear to her back, left side and right, and made her breathe, and then cough, which she did not want to do. Then he held the lamp up and looked in her nose.

"Nose," he said. "Burst at the floor. That's your blood. The lung's clear." He wiped her lip and chin with a wet cloth, and that was the end of the blood.

Then he strapped her ribs again, properly, with a long bandage and his mouth shut. Her mother sat on the edge of the bed in the fourth on the left and watched him do it. Then Callie lay on the bed in hut fourteen on her back with her arm across her side, and could not lie on either side, and did not sleep.

She lay and listened to her mother breathe.

She did not count it.

She noticed that she was not counting it. She noticed it the way you notice that a sound has stopped. Her mother's breath went in and out in the dark on the next bed, slow, with a little catch on the out-breath. Callie lay and let it go in and out, and did not put a number on it. After a while she was asleep.

---

The technician was waiting at the yellow line in the morning.

He was standing on the far side of it, in the treatment shed, with his left hand at his side and his right hand in a sling. The hand in the sling was bandaged to the wrist, with the fingers curled in a way that fingers do not curl on their own. Callie had last seen that hand on the thirty-fifth, in the pen, with the glove still on the concrete.

The carrier was on the near side of the line. Otto had brought it in on the sledge the night before, with Duchess, and left it where the man told him. The lid was in its groove.

"Sowerby says tomorrow," said the technician. He meant the hand. He did not look at it. "I know already. It's left-handed from here." He looked at Callie. "You read them. On the hill. The boxes. With the counter."

"I read about fifteen boxes," said Callie. "At a hand's width."

"Then read them again," said the man. "For me. Here. I'd like to hear the numbers in the same voice."

Callie took the counter out of its bag. Her hook was in the gate washhouse still, but they had given her the counter back. Toren lifted the lid out of its groove with his good hand. There were the two boxes, in the lead bottom, grey steel loaves with the little dark windows and the plates beside them.

She held the counter to the first at a hand's width and waited ten breaths.

"A hundred and ten," she said. "Shut."

The second.

"A hundred and six. Shut."

The technician stood on his side of the yellow line with his good hand shut.

"That's what you wrote," he said. "The boy read me the paper last night. Out of his coat." He looked at Toren. "He had it in his hand before the gate had shut behind him."

"You'd asked," said Toren.

"I'd asked," said the man. He looked at the boxes again. "Two," he said. "I asked for two, and three if you could. I didn't think I'd get one."

"There's a third," said Toren. "On the hill. It's open."

"Then it's the hill's," said the technician.

---

It took all of the morning.

He had been getting ready for it. Callie could see that. There were papers on the bench along the wall, the pages of a book copied out in his hand, in pencil, dozens of them. And on the steel of the rack, beside each box, there were numbers chalked in small figures. He had been writing down what every box ought to say, one by one, while he still knew.

"I did a month's work," he said, when he saw her looking. "Waiting. I didn't sleep much." He looked at the rack. "Now it's a night, maybe two, not a month. It learns the new one off the ones that still tell the truth. It doesn't need me to know, after that."

The rack was the one she had first seen on the tenth day. It was rows of grey boxes behind glass, each one with a small light under it. The lights were green. Most of them. Four along in the third row, one was yellow. The one beside it was a paler green than the rest.

"That's the liar," said the technician. "The fourth. And the fifth, catching it off him."

He had two assistants. They were the two who had made the carrier out of depot sheet, and they had never said a word to Callie in forty days, and they did not say one now. They did what he told them. He told them with his left hand and his voice.

They opened the glass. They took the fourth box out of the third row with the shed's own tongs, long and iron, *not your hands*. They carried it on the tongs to the bench, where it lay dead and lying. Then they came across the yellow line to the carrier, and the technician came with them, and stood over it.

"The hundred and ten," he said.

The assistant closed the tongs on it and lifted it out of the lead. He carried it on the tongs across the yellow line, slowly, the way you carry a pan too full. They set it in the third row, in the place the liar had been.

It did not go in at once. There were screws, and a plate that went across the back, and the technician did not have the hand for the screws. He stood and told the assistant which, and in what order, and the assistant turned them one at a time. Callie counted them. Eleven.

"There," said the technician. "Now it's in."

The light under it came on yellow.

"Yellow's right," he said, before anybody could ask. "Yellow's *learning*. It's got to be told what it is by the others. A night. Two." He looked along the row. "If the others agree with it, it goes green. If they don't, it's the hill's box and not ours, and I'll try the other."

"And the other?" said Toren.

"The other goes in that," said the technician. He nodded at a steel cupboard against the back wall, with a lock on it. "In its lead. The Director's said. It's the spare. And it's what she'll show anybody who asks whether six people went to a hill and came back with what they said." He looked at Toren. "It's the proof. You don't spend the proof."

---

The rack was still open.

Callie was standing at the glass, because the technician had asked her to read the new box once more through it, in its place. She held the counter at a hand and waited ten breaths and said the number. A hundred and ten. The same.

She looked along the rows while she waited.

The first row was at the top. At the end of it, nine along, there was an empty place. That was where the dead box had lived, the one Toren had carried on the eleventh day, the one that had gone into the pen with Miss Wren's arm at its window and come apart. It had gone to the depot after. There was a square of bare steel where it had been.

And beside the empty place, set into the steel of the rack's frame at the end of the row, there was a round thing.

It was a socket. It was a shallow round seat in the steel, not deep, as wide as two of her thumbs laid side by side. It had a rim. And from the middle of it out to the rim there were ribs, fine, raised a little from the steel, like the spokes of a wheel with no wheel on them.

She counted them without deciding to.

Nine.

It was empty. It was clean. It was the only place on the rack that did not have the grey film of seventy years on it. The steel of it was bright, as if nothing had ever sat in it long enough to leave a mark.

"What goes in that?" she said.

The technician looked where she was looking.

"Nothing," he said. "Not in my time. Not in my father's. It came empty."

"Then what's it for?"

"I don't know," said the man. "It's in the book." He went to the bench with his left hand and took down a book from the shelf over it. It was old, thick, the cover gone soft, with the wheel mark stamped into it and worn nearly flat. He opened it at the first page and held it out to her across the yellow line. He did not step over. "The fittings. When it came. The list of everything that was in the rack."

Callie looked.

The page was printed, in the small square letters the plate on the boxes had. A long list, line after line, with numbers. Most of it meant nothing to her.

One line had a pencil line through it.

It was near the bottom. It had a number, and a word she did not know, and after the word the figure *9*. The pencil line went through all of it. And beside it, in the margin, in the same pencil, in a small upright hand that was not the technician's and not anybody's she had ever seen, there were three words.

*released to bearer*

"Released," said the technician. "That's all I know. It was taken out before the machine ever came here. Out of the rack. Whatever it was." He shut the book. "To who, it doesn't say. *Bearer.* Whoever had it in his hand, that's all that means."

Callie did not say anything.

She had looked up from the book, because that was what she did. She looked at people when a number came, to see what the number did to them.

Toren was standing beside her at the yellow line.

He was not looking at the book. He was looking at the socket in the steel at the end of the first row, the round seat with its nine fine ribs and nothing in it. His face was doing nothing at all. It was so still that Callie looked at it twice.

And his right hand had come up and was lying flat on his shirt. Not his coat. The shirt, under it, where the coat was open. It lay over his breastbone, flat, the way Jab's hand lay over the fish.

He did not seem to know it was there.

"When?" said Toren.

His voice was quite ordinary.

"It doesn't say when," said the technician. "It doesn't say anything. It's a line in a book." He put the book back on the shelf. "There's a lot of lines like that. In a thing this old."

"Yes," said Toren.

He took his hand off his shirt. He did it the way you put a thing down that you did not know you had picked up.

Callie did not ask him. She did not know what she would have asked. She had read ground for fifty-five days and floors for twenty, and she had read that face on a stair and at a door and at the top of a dark lane. She had never seen it do that.

She put it where she put the numbers she did not understand yet. She kept it.

---

On the fifty-seventh the light under the new box went green.

The technician came to the ward at first light to say so. He stood in the door of the long room with his arm in its sling and did not come in, and said it to the whole ward.

"It's green."

Senna was lying on the fourth bed on the right. She turned her head on the pillow.

"All of it?" she said.

"All of it," said the technician. "It asked itself this morning. Row by row, every one. They all agreed." He was quiet. "I've not seen that since I was twenty-six."

"Then I'll go down," said Senna.

"It's not your day."

"It's not my day," said Senna. "My day was the fifty-fourth, and Sowerby took it out of my arm with a needle and a lot of language." She did not raise her voice. "I'd like the machine to have a look at me now, if it's honest. I'd like to know what it thinks of his needle."

Sowerby was at the end of the ward. He looked at the technician, and the technician looked at him.

"This afternoon," said Sowerby.

Callie was there because her mother was. Her mother sat on the edge of the fourth bed on the left with her stick against her knee. The bruises were still on Senna's arms from the needle on the fifty-fourth, yellow and green down the inside of both elbows. She had had a fever on the fifty-fifth, the night they came back, and had not said so to Toren when he came in.

After noon the two orderlies came and lifted Senna off the bed into the chair.

It took both of them. Callie watched. Senna could not take the step. She could not put her feet where the floor was, because she could not feel the floor. They lifted her the way you lift something you do not want to wake, and set her in the chair, and put her feet on the board, one and then the other. One of them took the handles.

"Mind the camber," said Senna.

"There's no camber in the ward, missus."

"There's camber on the slope," said Senna. "And you never mind it." She turned her head. "Tor."

Toren was at the foot of the bed. He had been there since the gate. Callie did not think he had gone back to eleven at all.

"Walk with me," said Senna.

They went down the long slope to the shed, the orderly pushing, Toren walking at the side of the chair, Senna saying *camber* at the place where the slope tipped. Callie walked behind with her mother. Her mother's right leg lagged. Callie did not take her arm. She walked beside her and did not hold anything.

At the yellow line they lifted Senna onto the narrow bed.

The technician sat at the screen with his left hand. He warmed the machine.

Callie had heard about it for fifty days. The warming, over and over. Seven times on the thirty-second. The sound it made, that Senna told the ceiling was flat. She had never heard it herself.

She heard it now.

It came up out of the machine low and long, a sound like a finger drawn round the rim of a glass. It rose and held. It did not wander. It held.

The lights along the rack went green, row by row, left to right, like somebody going along a row of windows with a candle.

"Once," said the technician.

Senna lay on the narrow bed with her eyes shut.

"It's in tune," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"Seventy years," said Senna, to the ceiling. "Seventy years it's been singing that song, and it's been flat for all the years I've been lying under it. And now it's not." She opened her eyes. "Is it looking at me?"

"It's looking at you," said the technician.

"Tell me what it thinks of his needle."

The technician looked at the screen for a long time. Sowerby came and stood behind him and looked too.

"It thinks it was a needle," said Sowerby. "It did what a needle does. Badly. It's taken you a step back. Not more." He was quiet. "The course is where it was on the forty-third, near enough. The next one's on time. On the sixty-fifth. With this." He looked at the rack. "And every one after."

"And my hands?"

"Your hands are where they were," said Sowerby. "It can see them. It can't do anything about them. Nothing can." He did not look away from her. "It's not that kind of machine. It never was."

"I know it wasn't," said Senna. "I wanted it said by somebody who wasn't me." She shut her eyes again. "Tor."

"Here."

"Did it pull?"

Toren did not answer at once.

"Thirty breaths," he said. "The three of us. Jab and Callie and me. It stuck, and then it came all at once, with a noise that went up the stair and round and round. Thirty breaths from *pull it*."

Senna lay very still.

"We did a quarter of an hour," she said. "Eleven of us. In the dark." Her mouth moved. "Pushing."

"I know," said Toren.

"Write it down," said Senna. "Somewhere better than your wrist."

---

After Senna, Sowerby looked at Callie's mother.

He did not do it at once. He stood by the machine with his hands in his coat pockets and looked at her across the shed. She was standing at the yellow line on her stick, where she had stood to watch Senna go under.

"I'd like to look at your head," he said. "Now the thing's honest. I've not had a picture I believed since the thirteenth."

Her mother looked at him.

"What for?"

"To see what the pills are doing," said Sowerby. "The second one. I raised it on the fortieth. I've been watching your face for seventeen days instead of a picture, and your face says it's working. I'd like to see if the picture says the same."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll know what to change."

Her mother was quiet for a long moment. Callie did not look at her. She looked at the yellow line on the floor, and at the machine, and at the rack with every light green.

"Look," said her mother. "Don't tell me the number."

"I'll not."

"Tell me what the pills are doing," said her mother. "That's all."

So they put her on the narrow bed.

Callie stood at the yellow line and watched. She watched her mother lie on the narrow bed where Senna had lain, with her stick on the floor beside it and her right hand on her chest, the fingers not quite closed, and her battered hat in her left. The machine sang its one long note. The lights went along the rows.

Sowerby looked at the screen.

"The swelling's down," he said.

He said it to her mother. He did not say it to Callie. He came round the machine and stood by the narrow bed and said it down to her.

"That's the second medicine. The swelling round the thing is less than it was on the thirteenth. That's why the words come. That's why there's been no fit since the fortieth." He was quiet. "Seventeen days."

"And the thing?" said her mother.

"The thing's where it was," said Sowerby. "It's the size it was. It's not smaller." He did not look away. "It won't be. The pills take the swelling off it. They can't take it."

"That's what I thought," said her mother.

"I know it's what you thought."

"I wanted it said by somebody who wasn't me," said her mother.

It was what Senna had said. Callie heard it. She saw Sowerby hear it too.

Her mother sat up on the narrow bed, slowly, with her left hand, and swung her legs over. The right one came after the left, late. She reached for the cup of water the assistant had put on the edge of the machine. She reached for it with her right hand, on purpose. Callie watched her do it.

The fingers went round the cup. They closed on it, most of the way. They held it. She lifted it an inch, and it shook, and she held it, and drank, and put it down.

The fingers did not open again on their own. She had to open them with her left hand, one at a time.

"There," said her mother.

Sowerby wrote something on the folder on his knee. Then he shut it and held it out to her. He did not open it. She put her left hand on it, flat, on the shut cover.

She held it there.

Callie did not ask. She had not asked on the fortieth, at Sowerby's door, and she did not ask now. The number was in the folder under her mother's hand, and it was her mother's. It had always been her mother's.

---

In the evening they gave her back the hook.

The grey coat brought it to the door of hut fourteen himself, washed, the curve bright. He held it out.

"Eleven, miss," he said. "At the grip. Best it's been."

She took it. Her right hand knew at once what to do with it.

There were two lines on the paper tag now. *pen, 35th.* And under it, in pencil, in the grey coat's square hand:

*Caul Hill, 55th.*

She stood on the step of fourteen and read it. She read it the way she read everything, twice. She thought about the pen, and the rag cut off at the pump house and buried under a stone with a cross on it. She thought about the steel lying on a threshold in a room under a hill with a thing leaning on it. She had knelt beside it in the bottom of the Sag with her hand on the tail, not behind it.

"There's room under it," said the grey coat. "For the next."

"There'll be a next," said Callie.

"There always is, miss," said the grey coat, and went away across the yard.

Down the row, on the step of eleven, Toren was sitting with the paper on his knee, not writing. On the step of twelve there was nobody. The door of twelve was shut. Jab had not been back to it. He had slept, Otto said, in the chair by the fifth bed on the right, both nights.

Her mother came out behind her and sat down on the step. She did it slowly, with her stick, the right leg last. Callie did not help her down. She stood beside her with the hook.

"Sit," said her mother.

Callie sat. It hurt. She sat anyway, on the cold step, beside her mother, with the hook across her knees.

The lamp came on at the end of the row, flat and white.

Neither of them said anything for a long time. Across the yard, in the long building, the windows of the ward were lit. Somewhere behind them an old woman was lying on the fourth bed on the right in a machine's good opinion, and a boy was asleep in a chair.

"You're not counting me," said her mother.

"No," said Callie.

"Good," said her mother.

She put her left hand on Callie's knee, over the hook, and left it there.
