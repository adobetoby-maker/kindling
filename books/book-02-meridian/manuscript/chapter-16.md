# Chapter 16 — Callie: Something Left

She woke with a finger of water in the well, and knew it before she opened her eyes.

It was the first thing she did every morning now. Before the stove, before her boots, before she looked at her mother. She went down into herself the way her grandfather had gone down a ladder into a grain bin, carefully, one rung at a time, and looked. This morning there was a finger of cold clear water lying in the bottom, above the place where the floor was, and it had not been there when she lay down. It had come back in the night while she was asleep, slowly, on its own, the way it always did.

She had kept it. She had stood in a ditch with her feet in black water and a pale hook across the mouth of a pipe, with nothing on her at all, and counted to her floor, and stopped.

She lay on the floor by the stove and let herself know that for a while.

Her ribs hurt. They hurt the way they had hurt on the hill, deep, all down the left side, where the pipe had tried to go out through her hands when the Ward left the hook at the jam and again yesterday when the first hound hit it from inside. Her nose had bled twice in the night. There was a crust of it on her lip.

"Something left?" said her mother, from the bed.

Callie had not known she was awake.

"A finger," said Callie.

Her mother held out her left hand, palm up, over the edge of the bed, and Callie reached up from the floor and put her own hand in it. They stayed like that for a while, with the stove ticking, and the blanket over the window, and Katori asleep on her face in the next bed with her coat on.

"Good," said her mother. "Now tell me the rest. I've had it from four people and they all tell it wrong."

---

So Callie told it. She told it lying on the floor with her hand in her mother's, once, in order, the way she had told the Director's office.

She told the warm iron at post five, and the two shadows, and Toren saying *I've seen that before*, and the slab going. She told the fog sitting up. She told Katori ten paces above them with her grey blade going, and the husks coming up between, and Katori seeing she could not get down, and saying *West*, and going up with the rest of the crew because that was her job. She told the bottom of the Sag like a lake of milk, and the weighbridge girl going down and falling right, and Toren hauling her up by the arm with the heel of his ugly spike. She told Jab on the open slope with his whole knife lit, calling the husks off them like water to a drain.

She told the water stopping.

"I knew they were in the pipe," she said. "Because the water wasn't coming out. It had been coming out for six days, since the rain. You could hear it from the rim. And it stopped. Something big enough was in there to stop it." She looked at the stove. "And they hadn't come at the light. The husks came at the light. Whatever came up after them didn't. So it had gone to the dark. And the only dark between the east ditch and the door was the pipe."

"So you put your hook in it."

"Across it. Like the rail at the jam. With the Ward in it." Callie was quiet a moment. "And then I had nothing on me."

Her mother's fingers tightened.

"It hit the hook," said Callie. "From inside. It sounded like somebody throwing a sack of stones at a gate. The hook didn't move. Nothing moves the hook when it's in there. So everything moved round it." She looked at the ceiling. "Toren came down and stood beside me with his light, low, across the mouth. They went back from the light. And then Jab called him up to the door, and he went, and it was me and the hook and Ines on the rim with one arrow."

"And you counted."

"I counted." Callie shut her eyes. "At about thirty they went quiet in the pipe. Dessa says the thing that goes quiet is the thing that isn't coming. I didn't believe her. I kept the hook in. At fifty I could feel the floor coming up. At fifty-eight I was on it."

"And every part of you wanted to go on."

Callie opened her eyes.

"Yes," she said.

"Because what if they came back."

"Yes."

"And you didn't."

"Katori came down the rim," said Callie. "She said *out*. I'd have said it myself. I think. I'd got as far as opening my mouth." She let her breath go. "I took it out. The hook went dark. They'd gone. They'd gone east, back through the pipe, into the fog, to the dark. To where Jab was. I didn't know that. Nobody knew that."

Her mother did not say anything for a long time.

"I'm very proud of you," she said at last, "and I'm very angry about all of it, and I'd like you to hold my hand a bit longer while I decide which one I'm going to be for the rest of the morning."

Callie held it a bit longer.

---

The lift was lost.

She found that out at the weighbridge, at the middle of the morning, when she walked her mother over for the tally. The drift below post five, the one the night crew had called a Flask and three Handfuls, had gone down with the slab into black water at the bottom of the dip and was there still, a smear with the light crawling through it under four feet of ditch. The canvas bag Toren had dropped on the east track had been picked up by the night crew in the afternoon: eleven jars, five broken, the ash from the broken ones spread through the frost and the grit and the glass, dirty past lifting.

The board on the wall of the weighbridge hut had the twenty-fifth day painted along the top in white, and under it, against every name, nothing at all.

"Nobody gets paid for a lift that went in the ditch," said the old man in the apron. He said it without any feeling, the way he said a weight. "That's the rule. It's always been the rule."

"It's a bad rule," said Callie's mother, climbing onto her stool.

"It's a rule," said the old man. He put the test weights on the plate, one and then the other, and went back into the hut and looked at the beam. Then he came out again and stood beside the stool. "I'd have paid it," he said. "If it were mine. It isn't."

Callie looked at the board. Her own name was there, painted in white, with nothing after it. There would be nothing after it on the sheet at the review. A lost day, and the day before that when she could not kneel. Two days out of the last five.

She thought of her mother's rule, and of a floor kept with the blood running out of her nose, and did not know whether she had spent the day or saved it. She thought that was probably the question Miss Wren had been trying to get at all along.

---

They brought Jab across to the shed in Senna's chair in the afternoon.

Toren pushed. He pushed one-handed, with his right palm freshly stitched where the seam had opened again round the spike, and a clean white wrapping over it, and he leaned his weight on the handle to steer, and Senna had lent the chair on the condition, relayed by Toren word for word, that it was Tuesday for somebody even if it was not Tuesday for her.

Jab sat in it wrapped in two blankets with his hands in his lap. Callie had seen people come off the road looking like that. She had looked like that, on the tailboard, the first night on the causeway, with the Ward on her for thirteen hours and nothing to eat. Grey, and very still, and cold all the way through in a way that did not come from outside. His hands shook in his lap. He did not hide them either. He had learned that off Rook, Callie thought, or off the day holder, or off everybody in this yard at once.

He looked at her when the chair came in through the door.

"You stopped," he said.

"I stopped."

He looked at her a long time. There was nothing in his face she could read; she had tried, the way she read ground, and found only the fog. Then he nodded, once.

"Good," he said.

---

Miss Wren had the brass scale out on the table, and the horn spoon, and one of the three jars from the rack by the gate, grey to the shoulder.

Rook was sitting on the cot. His hands were shaking so hard that he had put them flat on his knees to keep them still, and they would not keep still. He had held the whole bottom of the Sag alone for thirty-one minutes, and then sat in a chair at the foot of a bed all night, and Sowerby had come to the shed at noon and said a number of things to him in front of everybody in a voice Callie had never heard Sowerby use, and Rook had sat there and let him.

"Everybody who can see, look," said the woman. "This is the thing I should have shown you on the first morning in this yard. I didn't, because I thought there was time." She did not look at Jab. "That's mine. I'll write it down."

She weighed out a quarter of a Handful. She did it slowly, with the spoon, tipping grey powder from the jar into the little pan until the beam came level, and then she tipped the pan out onto a square of paper, and it was a heap Callie could not have hidden under her thumb. A heap the size of the top of a thumb.

"Three," she said. "Take in a little that you've measured."

She put her palm flat on it. Callie watched the grey go up into her hand, not fast and not slow, at the rate it came. The paper was clean when she lifted her palm.

"Four," said the woman. "Let it settle. Don't touch it." She sat down on the edge of the table with her hands in her lap. "Callie. Count for me. My breaths. I'll need sixty."

Callie counted.

It was the strangest thing she had ever counted. She counted the woman's breathing, in and out, slow and even, the way she had counted her mother's on the plain every night for a month and had stopped counting, on the floor by the stove, because it did not need her. This did. The woman had asked for it. It was the kind of counting that was for somebody else.

At forty the woman shut her eyes. At fifty she opened them.

"Sixty," said Callie.

"It's settled," said the woman. "It's in me. It isn't me. I know which is which, because I've done this ten thousand times since I was fourteen, in a hut with a very small old man called Orrin Vell standing over me with a stick, and I can tell you exactly where it's lying and exactly where I start." She looked at Jab. "You can tell too. You told me on the first morning. You put your hand on a dish and said *mine's got somebody in it*. Remember that."

Jab said nothing.

The woman got up and went to the cot and knelt in front of Rook, and put both her hands flat over the tops of his, where they lay shaking on his knees.

"Don't," said Rook.

"Too late," said the woman.

---

Callie watched Rook's hands stop.

They did not stop all at once. They slowed, the way the woman had slowed them on the north lip on the twelfth day, like a bell slowing after it is struck, the long fine tremor going longer and finer until she had to look for it, and then it was not there. Rook's shoulders came down half an inch. He shut his eyes.

The woman knelt there with her hands on his for as long as it took Callie to count thirty. Then she took them away and stood up, and went back to the table, and picked up the little brass glass with the sand in it.

"I measured my floor at first light," she said. "Before any of this. Two turns and a half, Ward, standing. I'm going to measure it again."

She stood in the middle of the shed with her feet planted and a shine came over her like old glass, a skin's width off her grey coat, and Dessa turned the glass. The sand ran. Callie watched it go, a thin grey thread through the waist of the glass, and pile up, and the woman stood in her Ward with her face calm and her eyes on the stove, and Dessa turned the glass again, and the sand ran through again, and the woman stood.

At two turns and a half, the shine went off her.

"Floor," she said. "Same as this morning. I've spent a quarter of a Handful through Sustain into a man who can't get the angle on himself, and I'm standing exactly where I was standing at first light." She put the glass down. "That's what the jar's for."

---

Nobody said anything for a while. The bucket at the far end of the shed went *tick*.

"Yesterday you had nothing to spend but you," said the woman to Jab.

She said it gently, for her. Callie had not heard her say anything gently before.

"You went across the bottom of the Sag in the fog because something in you told you a woman was dying there, and it was right, and nobody else knew. And you got to her, and she was dying, and you held her up. For nine minutes. On nothing. On you." She pulled a chair round with her foot and sat on it, in front of him, so that their faces were level. "I had a Handful already in me when I came down that road. Settled. I'd taken it in at the gate before first light, because that's what you do on a lift day, because Vell made us do it before every lift from the first week, and I've never once not done it since. So when I got to you I had something to spend that wasn't me. You didn't. And you didn't because I hadn't taught you how." She did not look away from him. "That's not yours. That's mine."

Jab looked at his hands.

"You'd have spent the same," she said. "I know. You'd give her the same again tomorrow. I'm not asking you not to. I'm asking you to have a jar when you do it. And somebody standing behind you whose job it is, written down, to take your wrist and lift your hand off when you get to your floor. And somebody next to you who can take over when they do." She sat back. "That's all it is. Skill, so you know which is which. Something in your hands besides you. Somebody to take your place. And a line, and somebody who'll pull you back from it. Four things."

"You pulled my hand off," said Jab.

"Somebody has to," said the woman. "Next time we'll write down who, before we go."

"Not so I'll care less," said Jab slowly. Callie could see him turning it over, the way she had seen him turn over a jam from the bottom up.

"No," said the woman. "So you're still standing for the next one."

---

Rook opened his eyes.

"She's right," he said.

He said it to the room, not to her. He sat on the cot with his hands flat on his knees, still for the first time since the Sag, and looked at the stove.

"I found my floor on a riverbank four nights running and didn't know it was there. I found it on the Level, and on the north road, and yesterday for thirty-one minutes with a road shut under me and nobody to take the other end. I've never once known where it was till I was under it." He was quiet. "She's right. I'd have taught him the doing. I'd never have taught him to come back from it. I don't know how."

"He found her," said Miss Wren.

Rook looked at her.

"I was at the gate," she said. "Where the Director put me. Two hundred paces. When the three blasts went, I came up the road with the bag and the jars and two men carrying a stretcher, which isn't the same thing as running. At the lip there was the weighbridge girl with her wrist broken, and I stopped and did her wrist, because she was there and she was hurt and nobody on the lip had anybody else on their count." She did not look at anybody. "Dessa had the lifters. Dessa had Callie and Toren and the girl. Nobody had the holder. The holder was the line, and nobody counts the line." She was quiet a moment. "If Wyck hadn't said *where's the holder* while I was splinting, I'd have been on that lip two minutes longer, with a Handful settled in me and the jars at my feet, and she'd have been dead on that slab. He found her. He knew. With nothing."

"You'd have been in time for the next one," said Rook.

"That's the point of me," said the woman.

They looked at each other across the shed, a man of thirty on a cot with his hands still and a woman of twenty-five on a chair with a crooked wrist, and Callie thought that the argument that had started on some road she had never seen was not finished. It had not been won. It had changed shape, the way a jam changes shape when you take the key out of it and everything that was held comes loose at once and goes somewhere new.

"I saw her," said Toren.

He was standing behind the wheeled chair with his stitched hand on the handle. He had not said anything since he pushed Jab in.

"When it folded," he said. "She shouted *weight*, and I was looking up the track at her, and I saw her leave the hollow and go down into the fog on her own. Then the husks came up, and I had Callie's sleeve, and I didn't call it." He looked at the stove, not at any of them. "I was the runner. I was meant to know where everybody was. I knew where she was, and I didn't say."

Nobody answered him. Callie looked at his hand on the handle of the chair. It had closed, and a small red spot was coming through the clean wrapping at the seam.

"Then you saw her," said Miss Wren, "and I was two hundred paces off with a stretcher, and we both got to her after Jab did." She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way she said a floor.

Toren nodded, once. In the chair in front of him Jab turned his head and looked up at him, and did not say anything, and did not look away.

"Write it down," said Senna's voice, from nowhere.

Everybody turned. Senna was not there. She was in the fourth bed on the right in the long building, four hundred yards away, flat on her back with her hands folded. It was Dessa who had said it, in Senna's voice, without meaning to, sitting at her table with the pencil in her hand.

Dessa went red.

"She'd have said it," said Dessa. "If she was here. So I've said it."

"Then write it," said Miss Wren.

Dessa wrote it.

---

"Could it hold the hook longer?" said Callie.

They looked at her.

"The jar," said Callie. "What you did. Settled. Could it go into the hook? Instead of me? So I could keep a Ward in the steel past my floor, without going under it?"

The woman looked at her for a long moment.

"Any door," she said. "It's the same fuel. It doesn't care whose door it goes through. Edge, Ward, Sustain, his struts, Wyck's standing still." She turned the brass glass over in her fingers, and did not set it running. "Not yet. You can't spend what you haven't learned to settle, and you've settled once, a tenth, sitting down in a shed. You'd be the one holding the hook with a Handful sloshing about in you on a road with things coming at you." She put the glass down. "Not yet."

"But yes."

"Not yet," said the woman, "is a kind of yes. It's the kind that keeps people alive long enough to hear the rest of it."

Callie filed it. She put it away in the same place she kept the grade on a sack and the gap on the handoff and the eleven seconds with Wyck, the way you put a tool in a bag you mean to carry.

---

The next morning somebody had chalked a word on the board at the weighbridge.

Callie saw it when she walked her mother over for the tally. The painted names ran down one side of the board in white, and the chalked weights down the other, and the twenty-fifth day was still blank all the way down. But against the line for hut twelve, where Jab's name was painted and had a Flask of hound ash chalked after it from the north road night, somebody had written a word in the space for the weight, in big, careful, uneven capitals, the way a person writes who does not write very often.

*SUSSTAIN.*

"That wasn't me," said her mother, climbing onto her stool.

"I know."

"It wasn't him either," said her mother, nodding at the old man in the apron. "He'd have spelt it right. There's only one *s* in the middle and he knows it."

The old man looked at the board for a long time.

"Night crew," he said at last. "They came through at the fifth hour for the test weights. They were talking about it all the way across the yard." He did not rub it out. "The whole Sag knows. The lad from twelve went into the fog in the dark and found her. Nobody else did."

A woman came to the window of the hut at the middle of the morning. She was not a lifter. She was a Meridian woman in a knitted shawl, about her mother's age, with her hands twisted together, and she did not look at the plate or the beam or the jars. She looked at Callie, because Callie was standing nearest.

"Which hut's the boy?" she said. "The one who kept the holder. They said at the pump he's in the second row." She twisted her hands. "My girl's had a fever three days. It won't break. They said he'd know."

Callie looked at her.

"He's in the long building," she said. "In a bed. He can't do anything. He can't find it." She did not know how else to say it. "Go to Mrs Lask at the desk. She'll send you to Doctor Sowerby. He's better than Jab at fevers. Jab would tell you so himself."

"They said he'd know," said the woman again. She said it as if it were the only thing she had come with, and she could not put it down.

"He'd know she was worse," said Callie. "He wouldn't be able to do anything about it. Not today." She thought about the bucket Jab had told her about on the steps, that he went down to look at every night, and could not find. "Go to the desk."

The woman went. Callie watched her go across the yard toward the long building with her shawl pulled round her, walking fast, and thought: *there'll be more of them.* She did not know how she knew. She knew it the way she knew a husk line: not a track so much as a direction. Once a thing like that got onto a board in chalk, it did not come off.

---

The Director came to the shed at noon.

She came across the yard on foot with the man in the long coat a pace behind her, small and square in a grey coat with her spectacles on their cord, and people in the yard stopped what they were doing and watched her come, because nobody had ever seen her come down the outside stair in the middle of a working day. She came in at the shed door without knocking and stood inside it, looking at the stove, and the cot, and the bucket under the drip, and the table with the brass scale, and Jab, in Senna's chair again, with his two blankets.

"You're the one," she said to him.

Jab looked at her.

"Sowerby says she'd have been dead before the stretcher got to the lip," said the Director. "Without you. He put it in writing, which he never does, and sent it up the stair, which he never does either." She took her spectacles off. "So I'll say it to you once, in person, because it should be said to your face and not on a card. Thank you. The station owes you a woman who's held a road for it for two years with nobody watching. It knows it does."

"I'd like her coat given back," said Jab.

The Director looked at him.

"She's asked for it," said Jab. "Rook says they won't give it to her."

The Director looked at him a moment longer. Then she turned her head a quarter inch toward the man in the long coat, and the man in the long coat wrote something.

"Done," said the Director.

---

Then she took a buff card out of her coat.

It was not like the cards that had come under the doors. It was bigger, and there was more written on it, in a neat hand that was not the Director's, and there was a line at the bottom for a name.

"I'll tell you what I want," said the Director. "And then what I'll take."

She did not put the card down. She held it.

"I want you on the medical register. Not the long line with one name on it. The roll of the long building, with Sowerby and Keel and Miss Wren. I want your household settled for four years, not twenty-eight days. A hut by the long building, not in Yard C, four hundred yards from your brother. Training under Miss Wren, every day, on whatever terms she sets." She paused. "And in return, I want you on call to the long building. When a thing comes through the gate that's worse, and there's nobody else. That's what I want."

The shed was very quiet.

Callie watched her. She watched her hold the card without putting it down, and she thought of her grandfather at the horse fair at Thistle Ford again, walking round a horse he had already decided to buy. She had thought, on the second floor, that the Director looked at Jab like a buyer. She had been wrong. That had been a buyer looking. This was a buyer who had seen the horse run.

"Not while I'm like this," said Jab.

"No," said the Director. "I know. I've got eyes." She put the card back in her coat. "I'll ask again."

"And if I say no when you do?"

"Then you'll have said no," said the Director. "And it'll be on your sheet that you did, in the margin, the way you like it." She put her spectacles on. "I'll not pretend it goes away, Mr—" She stopped. "Jab. I'll not pretend. Yesterday this station found out what it had in hut twelve. The whole Sag knows. There's a word in chalk on my weighbridge board that I didn't put there and can't have rubbed off without every crew in the north yard asking why. I'd be lying if I told you the station was going to forget. It never forgets anything. That's the trouble with it."

"That's a column now," said Pell, from the doorway, very quietly, to nobody.

The Director turned her head and looked at him.

"Yes," she said. "It is."

---

Dessa showed her the book that evening, when the yard was empty.

She did not show anybody else. She waited until the others had gone, and Callie was the last, putting the hook over her shoulder at the door, and Dessa said "Callie," in her flat voice, and turned the book round on the table so that it faced her.

It was the page for the twenty-fifth day.

It went across both sides of the book, the way the map had, and Dessa had ruled it into columns in pencil. *What we did.* *What went wrong.* *Who.* *How long.* *What it cost.* *Recovery.* *Second attempt.* *What to change.* She had filled in every column except one.

*Supervised lift. Minute nought to minute eighteen: lift as planned.* Under it: *Minute eighteen: warm iron, post 5. Two shadows — T. Slab at 6 going — C. Lifters moved back ten paces — K. Minute twenty: change of holders called — P. Holder kept the line with six moving; R. kept his hands down. No new minute set. Minute twenty-one: slab dropped. Line folded, east corner. Husks up. Lifters below post 5 cut off: C., T., the girl. Across the bottom to the door. Girl down, wrist; fell right — Wyck's lane. Hounds went to the dark, into the pipe. C.: hook across the west mouth, Ward in it, fifty-eight seconds, to her floor, stopped. T.: light at the pipe; then the door; light held through a fall, first time. J.: lit to call the husks off; then left the door to T.; went across the bottom dark.*

And under that, in capitals, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly gone through:

*THE HOLDER WAS HIT AT THE FOLD, IN THE FOG. NOBODY COUNTED HER.*

*T. saw her leave the hollow at the fold. Did not call it.*

*J. felt her. Forty yards. Before he could see her. Held her nine minutes. Below his floor. Floor: a finger.*

*R.: the whole bottom, alone, thirty-one minutes.*

*D. W.: at the gate. Came at three blasts, with kit and stretcher. Did the girl's wrist first; nobody had the holder on the count. Came down at Wyck's word. Took over from J. on a settled Handful. Stayed at her floor.*

In the column for what it cost, Dessa had written: *A Flask and three Handfuls, in the ditch. Eleven jars, five broken. The girl's wrist. T.'s palm. R.: flat. J.: can't find it. C.: a finger. The holder: her spleen, and the winter.*

In the column for *second attempt*, there was nothing.

In the column for *what to change*, there were a great many things, in several hands. Some of them were Pell's, small and neat. Some were Miss Wren's, square. One was in letters big and bad, from shaking hands. Callie read them all.

*Somebody at the bottom who can feel where people are. Not at the lip. Thirty feet isn't the bottom of a dip.* That was Dessa's own hand. *The medic at the lip. Argue it again.* *Both ends of the pipe.* *If the change can't be made on the minute, somebody says out loud who's holding and when we try again.* *The runner calls anybody who leaves their place.* *Floors checked at the twentieth minute, not only at the gate.* *A jar for every healer. Settled before they go down.* *Somebody whose job it is to lift the hand off. Written down, by name, before.*

And the last one, in the big bad letters: *COUNT THE HOLDER.*

---

Callie read it twice, and then she looked up.

"There's nothing in *second attempt*," she said.

"No," said Dessa. "There hasn't been one."

"There'll be one."

Dessa looked at the empty column for a long time.

"That's what the book's for," she said. "So the second one's got something to go on." She turned the pages back, carefully, past the map, past *the strut stood up* and *Toren, can't tell* and *Jab, unprompted, on a person*, past *Everybody inside it has to stand like me*, all the way back to the very front, to the first page, which Callie had seen her write on before anybody came, on the first morning, and cover with her hand.

She took her hand away.

There was one line on it. It was in Dessa's writing, very neat, in the middle of the page, with a great deal of white round it.

*Say it out loud. Even when it's awful.*

And under it, smaller: *M. F. On the wall.*

Callie looked at it for a long time. She did not know Milo. She knew his name, which Senna said every night at the tenth hour to a ward full of strangers, and which a four-year-old had started saying with her. She knew he had had a lamp, and that it had been terrible, and that it had got them somewhere. She knew there was a slab of bread every night at hut eleven folded in a cloth at the end of the row.

"He said it on the wall," said Dessa. "On the Level. When he was—" She stopped. She did not go on. She put her hand flat on the page, beside the line, not over it. "It's the only rule there is for a book like this. So it's the first thing in it."

"It's a good rule," said Callie.

"Senna says it's the best rule anybody ever gave her," said Dessa. "She says a boy on a wall made it up." She shut the book. "She's right. He did."

---

At the bottom of the page for the twenty-fifth day, below all the columns, there was one more line. Callie had not seen it until Dessa shut the book and she saw it upside down, for an instant, through the gap of the pages closing. It was not in Dessa's hand. It was Toren's, careful and slow, the way a boy writes with a stitched palm.

*Why (S. V., dictated): Because the ones who hold the line are the ones nobody watches.*

---

Doctor Sowerby told her mother the next morning, at the eighth hour, with his folder shut under his arm.

"I wanted a picture of your head," he said. "At four weeks. To see what the pills are doing to the swelling. I'd have liked it. I'd have learned something from it, about you and about the next person." He looked at the folder. "It won't happen. The machine's on cycles only, from today. Pictures only if somebody's dying, and then only if I think the picture would change what I do. Yours wouldn't. So you're not getting one."

"Is that the bad thing?" said her mother.

"That's the bad thing."

"What's the good thing?"

Sowerby looked at her for a moment.

"I'll look at your face instead," he said. "Every morning. And listen to her." He did not look at Callie. "She tells me exactly the right amount."

"Then look at it," said her mother, and put her chin up, and let him.

---

Toren told them on the steps that night.

He told them the grey-haired man had come up the slope to find him in the afternoon, which the grey-haired man never did, and had stood at the door of the shed with ink up the side of his hand and said that the box beside the fourth box, third row down, fifth along, had started to wake slow as well. Not as bad. A hair. But the same way.

"He said that's how it goes," said Toren. "One goes, and the ones either side work harder to agree with it, and then they go too." He was quiet. "He said four weeks was a week ago. It's less now. He wouldn't say how much less."

Nobody said anything.

The lamp at the end of the row was lit, flat and white, putting its circle down on the frozen concrete the colour of bone. Jab was on the step of twelve. He had made Otto bring him back from the long building at the fourth hour, against Sowerby, against Keel, against the woman with the good boots, who had said in front of everybody that he was an idiot and then helped Otto with the blankets. He sat on his step wrapped in both of them with his hands in his lap. They were still shaking. The long cord was not on his wrist. It was on Tonk's, in the fifth bed on the right, and the other end was still tied to the rail of the empty sixth bed, in a knot a four-year-old had made a nurse do twice, and Tonk had said nobody was to untie it.

Toren was on the step of eleven with his stitched hand on his knee.

Callie was on the step of fourteen with the hook across her knees and a finger of water in the well.

---

"Gran says Caul Hill," said Toren.

He said it to the lamp, not to either of them.

"She told me on the twenty-first. In the ward, at night. I wrote it down in Dessa's book because she asked me to." He did not look at them. "Six days north and west, on the old line. A hill with the top taken off and eleven miles of fence round it, and inside the fence, rooms of racks. With the same boxes in them. The same mark on the plates." He stopped. "She pulled the backs off them for the copper when she was fifty-three, and threw the boxes on the floor, because they were heavy and they were warm and the man who ran the crew said never to carry one. Hundreds. On the floor. Where they dropped them."

Callie did not say anything.

"She was in there eleven days," said Toren. "That's where her hands went. And her feet. That's eleven days."

"The Director said the old plant," said Callie. "On the second floor. The sky's gone wrong over it. Something opened there in the summer. There's ash lying in drifts on the ground that nobody's lifting, because nobody goes near."

"Same place," said Toren.

"Same place," said Callie.

---

Jab had not said anything. He sat on his step with his hands shaking in his lap and looked at the lamp.

"Dee had a Handful," he said, at last. "Settled. When she put her hand on the holder, beside mine. I felt it go into her. It wasn't Dee. It came out of her like it came out of nobody." He was quiet for a while. "It held the holder up while they got her on the stretcher and up the road to the lip. That's a quarter of a mile, near enough. And the day before yesterday she did a quarter of a Handful into Rook's hands and stood exactly where she'd stood." He moved one of his hands, the way he moved them when he was doing a sum and running out of fingers. "A Handful for a quarter mile of holding somebody up. That's what it costs. I don't know what it costs for eleven days."

Callie looked at him.

"Nobody could go in there," she said. "If it took her hands in eleven days."

"No," said Jab. "Not on their own."

He did not say anything else. He did not need to. Callie heard the rest of it, the way she heard a husk line, not a track so much as a direction. Not on their own. Not without somebody holding them up the whole time they were in there. And not without something besides themselves to hold them up with: jars, a lot of jars.

And ash in drifts on the ground up there, that nobody was lifting.

She looked at Toren. He was looking at the lamp. His face was doing nothing at all, which was how she knew.

---

None of them said it.

Callie heard all three of them not say it. She heard it the way she had heard the scan door not open, on the tenth day, and read a four-year-old lying still off the fact that nobody came out to fetch his brother. It was in what did not happen. Toren did not say *I'm going*. Jab did not say *you'd need somebody to hold you up*. She did not say *you'd need somebody to hold the line while you did it*. They sat on their three steps in the flat white light and did not say any of it, and it sat there between the three steps anyway, the size of a hill with the top taken off.

A part in a room full of racks. A tear in the sky above it. Ash on the ground there that nobody had lifted. A poison that took a woman's hands in eleven days. A machine with less than four weeks left in it. A south lip at the end of the road with a hollow in the gravel the size of a saucer, and nobody standing in it tomorrow.

It was all one place.

She had not known that, this morning. She knew it now the way you know a thing about ground: not because anybody told you, but because you had been standing on it for a while and it had shown you.

---

The lamp went out at the first hour.

It did not flicker. It went, all at once, for its written reason, and the dark came down over the three steps and the row and the whole of Yard C.

Callie sat in it.

She could not see Toren now, or Jab, but she knew where they were. She could hear the stove tick through the door of eleven, and Jab's blankets move on twelve, and somebody's breathing, slow, which might have been either of them. She did not count it.

She went down into herself instead, the way she did every morning, and every night now, and looked. There was a finger of cold clear water in the bottom of the well, above the floor. She had kept it. She had stood in a ditch with nothing on her and kept it, and her mother had held her hand on the blanket and said *good*, and it was hers, and tomorrow there would be a little more.

Somewhere at the north end of the station, under a long low building with a turf roof, there was a pale ring standing on its edge in the dark, and a rack of little windows behind it, and two of them were yellow now.

Somewhere at the other end of a road there was a hollow in the gravel at the south lip of the Sag, filling up with frost, with nobody standing in it.

And somewhere six days north and west, on the old line, there was a hill with the top taken off, and a sky gone wrong above it, and rooms of racks going down under it in the dark with the backs pulled off, and on the floors of those rooms, where a crew had thrown them twenty years ago because they were heavy and warm and nobody should carry one, hundreds of grey boxes, lying where they fell.

Waiting, Callie thought, for somebody to count them.
