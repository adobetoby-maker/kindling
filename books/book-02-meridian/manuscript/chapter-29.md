# Chapter 29 — Toren: Set and Step

On the forty-fifth morning Dee made them say the rules at the start of the day, as if the old line were a gate.

She did it at first light by the fire, with the frost on everything and the hill already there in the west, flat and dark against a sky that was only beginning to be a colour. Nobody looked at the hill. Everybody knew it was there.

"Two days," said Dee. "Say them now. I want them in your mouths before we're anywhere near a fence. I want them said so often you'd say them in your sleep."

They said them. Nothing to eat inside the fence. Nothing to drink inside the fence. Nobody sits down inside the fence. Nobody touches their face. At the gate going out, every time, coat off, shake it, wash at the river below the pump house, the hands and the face and the hair, before you eat. Never carry one of the boxes. Not on your back. Not in your arms.

"And the rest," said Dee.

"Red, and nobody trapped," said Callie. "Everybody out, at once."

"Red, and a body trapped," said Toren. "The fewest who can do it stay. The watch says their names. They stay till the person's out and the source is covered. Then out. Nobody back in till the counter's under the red."

"Two Flasks left inside," said Jab. "Everybody out. A healer at their floor twice. A healer still warm after the watch. And me below my floor, once." He did not look at Dee. "And you turn us round."

"And me," said Rook. "Half a working turn. Ten minutes by the glass. Every time."

"And me," said Pell. "Outside. At the cart. Writing down who went in." He looked at the hill. "And the three Flasks don't go inside for anything."

Dee wrote nothing. She had written it all before.

"Good," she said. "Now the road."

---

The siding had brought them back to the old line on the forty-third, and the forty-fourth had been good stone all day. On the forty-fifth day the old line went on west across the flat toward the hill, and it began to break.

It did not break all at once. It broke the way ice breaks on a pond in a thaw, a crack here, a tilted slab there. The bed of the old line had been packed stone for three days, hard and grey and good for the wheels. Now the frost had got into it. It had heaved it up in places into long humps, and dropped it in others into dips with water standing in them. In one place it had split it clean across, so that the near half of the bed was a hand's width higher than the far half, with a gap between them the width of a boot.

Pell stopped Duchess at the first of the gaps and looked at it.

"She'll take it," he said. "The wheels won't. Not loaded. The near wheel drops in the gap and the whole cart comes down on the axle." He looked along the line. There were more gaps. There were more humps and dips, as far as he could see. "It'll be like this all day."

"I'll set under it," said Toren.

Pell looked at him.

"Like the washout," said Toren. "Only more times. Under the axle when the wheel's over the gap, and hold, and let go when it's past. Then walk up and do the other wheel." He heard himself say it. It sounded simpler than it was going to be. "I'll do it every time."

"There'll be forty of them," said Pell.

"Then I'll do it forty times."

"You'll not," said Dee, from the tailboard. "You'll do it as many times as there's ash for. Your stone's a Handful. A tenth's four holds, if you're soft." She looked at the line. "Forty gaps is eighty wheels."

Toren thought about that.

"Then I'll learn to do it for less," he said.

---

It did not go well at first. He had known it would not.

The first time he set too far ahead. He stood on the far side of the gap and put the foot of the strut down on the far slab and let it come up toward where the axle was going to be. The wheel came to the gap and dropped into it, a hand's width, and the axle came down short of the top of the strut and hit the near end of it. It knocked the strut sideways. It went out. The wheel banged down onto the far slab and the cart jolted, and the four jars in the cart's rack rang against each other in their straw.

"*Easy*," said Jab.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry to me," said Jab. "Be sorry to them. They're lent."

The second time he set right under the axle and locked. The wheel came into the gap and the weight came onto the strut, more of it than he had expected, and everything in him went hard at once, the way it had under the sack at seven paces on the thirty-second. The weight came up through the strut into his knee and his hip and his arm. He felt it go into the arm. Not a break. A jar, up from the wrist into the elbow inside the leather cuff, deep and dull, like a door slammed in the next room.

He let go. He stood on the slab with the arm against his chest and breathed through his teeth.

"Where?" said Dee.

"Same place. Not as bad."

"Soft," said Pell, from Duchess's head. "You locked."

"I know I locked."

He did it a third time. And this time, before he set, he did not look at the axle. He looked at the ground.

He looked at the slab on the far side of the gap. It was grey and flat and heavy, sitting on the bed with its edge a hand's width lower than the near one. He looked at where the foot of the strut would go. Not the middle, which was cracked. The near edge of it, where it sat on the stone of the bed underneath, solid, the way the stone shelf had sat under the gravel in the washout.

"Callie," he said.

She was twenty paces in front of Duchess. She came back.

"Tell me the ground," he said. "Where the foot goes. Tell me as I'm doing it."

Callie looked at the slab.

"Near edge," she said. "Left of the crack. It's sitting on stone. The middle's sitting on water." She crouched and looked. "The right-hand end's hollow. Don't go there."

"Near edge," said Toren. "Left of the crack."

He put the foot down there. He let the strut come up from the ground at its slant, soft behind. It came up under where the axle would be when the wheel was in the gap. It did not come up under where the axle was now. He stood there and waited with it, soft, the way you wait for a sack you can hear coming down the lane.

"Set," he said.

"Rolling," said Pell, and Duchess leaned.

The wheel came to the gap. It dropped. The axle came down onto the top of the strut and the weight went into it. It went down the slant into the near edge of the slab where Callie had said. It did not come up into him, because he was soft, and his knees gave a little and took the last of it. The wheel rolled across the gap on the strut the way it had rolled up the bank in the washout. The axle went along the top of the strut toward the end.

He let it go at the end. Not after. At the end.

The wheel came down on the far slab without a jolt.

"Step," said Toren, and walked up the side of the cart to the other wheel.

"Stone," said Callie, already crouching at the gap. "Same place. Near edge."

"Set," said Toren.

"Rolling," said Pell.

It went across.

---

By the middle of the morning they were not saying anything else.

It had become the thing they said. Toren had not meant it to. He had said *set* in the washout because it was what came out, and he said it now because it was what came out, and Pell had said *rolling*. Callie had begun saying *stone* and *soft* and *hollow* before Toren had even asked her, crouching at every gap before the cart came to it. By the eighth gap they were not deciding who said what. It was only a thing that happened on the old line: *stone*, *set*, *rolling*, *step*, and the cart going over.

On the fifteenth gap Callie was not there, because she had gone ahead to look at a hump in the bed. Toren looked at the slab himself, and found the near edge sitting on stone, and set, and Pell said *rolling*. It went over.

He stood on the far side of it with the strut gone. He had not even thought about it until it was done.

"That was without her," said Pell.

"I know."

"You looked at the ground yourself." Pell looked at him over Duchess's neck. "You'll not always have her. Up there she'll have the stair."

"I know," said Toren.

At the second halt Dee made him go down and measure.

"Half," he said. He heard himself say it, and it was less than he had thought. "A half of the stone. For twenty-two gaps."

"Twenty-two gaps is forty-four wheels," said Dee.

"Some of them I didn't set. Some of the gaps were small." He thought about it. "About thirty sets. Half a Handful."

"That's fifteen to a quarter," said Dee. "At the washout it was two to a quarter. At six paces in the yard it was ten." She wrote it. "You're not holding them as long. You're letting go at the end."

"Pell said."

"Pell was right," said Dee. "When the wheel's gone by it's gone by."

"It's the holding," said Toren. "Not the setting. Every breath there's a wheel on it, I pay for."

"Then that's your price," said Dee. "Breaths under a wheel. Two or three a gap, today." She wrote it. "Ninety breaths of wheel for a half. Keep them short." She shut the book. "Settle a half tonight. Out of the road jar. That's four left in it, then." She looked at him. "And don't do it forty times tomorrow. Tomorrow you'll do it once, and it'll be the one that matters."

---

In the early afternoon they came to the first drift.

It lay in a hollow on the north side of the old line. The line ran along the edge of a long shallow dip in the flat, and the wind off the hill had filled the bottom of the dip with grey. It lay against the north side of the bed, the side the wind came from, in a long soft heap like a snowdrift, grey to the colour of the jars. The top of it had a crust on it, darker, where the frost and the wind had set it. Along the edge of it, where it met the stone of the bed, there was a thin line of grey going up over the lip of the bed and onto the stones, blown there.

Callie stopped twenty paces short of it.

She did not go closer. She took the counter out of its bag and switched it on and held it out in front of her, and the ticking went from rain starting to rain falling while Toren watched her face.

"Twenty," she said. "From here. Ten breaths." She took two steps. "Twenty-six." Two more. "Thirty-one. At the edge of the bed." She stopped. "It's warm. Not like the wire. It's thinner than the wire. But it's all of it warm." She looked at the drift. "It's off the hill. It's the grey the girl said. Like snow that won't melt."

They all looked at it.

"It's loose," said Pell. "Nobody's lifted it. It's lying where it fell." He looked along the line. "And there's the wind."

The wind was coming off the hill from the west-north-west, across the flat, as it had for two days. Toren saw it take the top off the drift. It did not take much. It lifted a thin grey haze off the crust, very fine, and brought it across the bed of the old line in a low smoke. The smoke crossed the stones and went on over the south side into the flat.

"That's what the girl's father walked into," said Dee. "Going down to the wash for the wire. That, only more."

"Where's Rook?" said Callie.

---

Rook was on the drift.

Not on it. At the edge of it. He had come round the front of the cart while they were all looking at Callie, and gone along the north edge of the bed at his careful straight walk, and down off the bed onto the flat at the foot of the drift. He was standing there with the grey to his shins, with his head on one side, looking at the crust. He crouched, slowly, in two movements, and put his hand out flat over it, not touching.

"Elias," said Dee.

Her voice did not go up. It went very quiet.

Rook stood up.

He came back up onto the bed. He did it slowly, and when he got to the top of the lip Toren saw his boots and his trousers to the shins. They were grey. It was not much grey. It was the fine grey of a man who has walked through ash that has been blown and is lying loose. He stood on the bed with his hands at his sides and looked at Dee.

"I wanted to see how it lies," he said. "Whether it's lying like a drift or lying like something's been through it. Whether it's been lifted, or turned—"

"Callie," said Dee. "His shins."

Callie held the counter out toward Rook's legs. She did not go nearer than her arm's length.

"Forty," she said. "Ten breaths. At his shins."

"You went to look," said Dee.

"I'm the one who looks," said Rook. "I've always been the one who looks. That's what I'm for."

"Not with my ash," said Dee.

It was very quiet on the old line. Duchess shifted her feet. The wind brought the thin grey smoke across the bed and past them.

"I'm not your ash," said Rook.

"On this road you are," said Dee. "Every bit of you. You're ten minutes, half a working turn, in my book, signed. And every grain that's on your legs now is a grain Jab has to draw off you before you've done anything at all, and that's out of a jar that was for the road." She did not look away from him. "You went to look alone. At a drift. Two days from the fence. Because you wanted to see."

Rook looked at her for a long moment.

Then he looked down at his own grey shins.

"Yes," he said. "I did."

"Then we'll do it properly," said Dee. "Now. Here. As if it was the fence." She turned. "Jab. You've got a patient."

---

It was the first time they did it all together.

It was not planned. Toren understood that afterward, sitting by the fire. Nobody had meant it to happen on the forty-fifth day on the old line by a drift nobody needed to go near. It happened because Rook had gone to look, and Dee had decided to turn it into something instead of just being angry. She stood at the tailboard with the glass and gave them their places the way she had in the pen.

"Rook stands where he is," she said. "On the bed, at the edge, by the drift. He's the patient. The wind's bringing that across him and onto whoever's working on him." She looked at Callie. "You. The lee. Between the drift and them. On the stone."

Callie had the hook already.

"Jab. You draw his legs. Off the road jar. Toren brings it." She looked at Toren. "And the cart's got to go past him on the bed while you're doing it, because we're not stopping on this road for the afternoon. There's a gap in the bed there by the drift." She pointed. There was, a hand's width, where the bed had split. "So you set under the wheel while they work."

"All at once?" said Toren.

"All at once," said Dee. "That's what it'll be."

So they did it all at once, and the first time it did not work.

---

Callie went to the north edge of the bed between the drift and Rook. She put the back of the hook's curve on the stone, and rocked it, and the lee stood up off the bottom of the curve, pale, with the grey smoke hissing against it. It stood between the drift and Rook's legs. She walked it along the edge toward him, a step, two, with her eyes on his shoulders the way Rook had told her.

Jab came off the tailboard with the frame on his back and knelt in front of Rook on the bed in the lee, and put his right hand flat on Rook's shin. Toren went up the cart to the rack behind the driver's bench for the road jar.

And Pell brought the cart up the bed toward the gap by the drift, with Duchess walking slowly. Toren came round the side of the cart with the road jar in his good hand, and saw where the gap was, and saw where Callie was. They were in the same place.

The gap in the bed was on the north side, by the drift. The north wheel would drop into it. And Callie was walking the lee along the north edge of the bed toward Rook with the curve of the hook rocking on the stone, right across the place where Toren had to put the foot of his strut.

"Callie—"

"I'm here—"

He put the jar in Dee's hand, and went to set, and the foot of his strut came down on the stone at its slant exactly where the back of Callie's curve was coming down to rock forward. It did not hit it. It came down next to it, and the strut and the curve were in the same hand's width of ground. The curve came down on the foot of the strut instead of the stone. It skidded on it.

The lee went out.

The grey smoke came across Rook and Jab and Jab's hand on Rook's shin, and Jab coughed, and Dee said "Sleeves," and everybody put their sleeves over their mouths. The north wheel came to the gap with Toren's strut standing in it, and he held it, and it rolled over. And Callie stood on the north edge of the bed with the hook in her hands and no lee at all.

"Stop," said Dee. "Everybody stop."

They stopped.

"That's what it'll be," said Dee, into her sleeve. "That's exactly what it'll be. Two of you wanting the same ground." She took her sleeve away. "Sort it out. Now. Before we do it again. Out loud."

---

They sorted it out standing on the old line with the grey smoke going past.

"You're on the outside," said Toren. "Always. The windward edge. Where the wind comes from and the drift is."

"And you're under," said Callie. "Under the cart. On the downhill side, or the inside. Never the edge." She looked at the gap. "If the gap's on the edge, you set from the inside, slanting out. Your foot's never where my curve is."

"And if it has to be?"

"Then you say *ground*," said Callie. "And I lift for a breath. And you say *set*. And I put it down again past you."

"And Jab's inside," said Toren. "Always. Behind you. In the lee."

"Behind me, in the lee, with whoever he's working on," said Callie. "They're who I'm looking at."

Jab, kneeling on the stone with his hand on Rook's shin and his sleeve over his mouth, said nothing for a moment.

"And I say when it's busy," he said. "When I've got it. And when it's clean." He looked up. "So you know how long you've got to hold it. So you're not holding the lee over nothing."

"*Busy*," said Toren. "*Clean*."

"And *jar*," said Jab. "Before it's dry. Not after."

Dee wrote it all down on her knee.

---

The second time it worked.

Callie went out to the north edge on the windward side with the hook and said "Lane." It was the first time she had said it. She did not seem to have decided to. The lee stood up off the curve between the drift and Rook, pale, and she walked it along with her eyes on Rook's shoulders. It leaned back over him and Jab, the way it had over Jab on the forty-fourth, the shape of where they were.

Jab put his hand on Rook's shin. Dee held the road jar to his left palm.

"Busy," said Jab.

Pell brought the cart up. The north wheel came toward the gap. Toren came round on the inside, the south side of the wheel, and did not go near the edge. He looked at the ground on the inside of the gap and found stone, and put the foot of the strut there, slanting outward under the axle.

"Set," he said.

"Rolling," said Pell.

The wheel came into the gap and onto the strut and rolled across it, and Toren let it go at the end. The curve of Callie's hook rocked along the stone of the edge a pace outside where his strut had been, and nothing touched anything, and the lee held.

"Vent," said Dee.

Jab lifted his palm off Rook's shin and put it flat on the stone of the bed on the downwind side, where the ground was already grey. He did not put it in a jar. Dee had told him on the first day on the old line that on a road where the ground was already dirty, you could vent into the ground, and only there. *Not into my floor. Into the road's.* He held it there for three breaths.

"Back on," said Dee.

"Busy," said Jab. And then, after two more draws: "Clean."

"Down," said Callie, and let the lee go.

It had taken eleven turns of the glass. Callie held the counter to Rook's shins at arm's length.

"Nine," she said. "The room."

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

Rook stood on the bed of the old line with his clean shins and his hands at his sides and looked at the three of them. He did not look at Dee. He looked at Callie with the hook, and Toren by the wheel, and Jab still kneeling on the stone with his hand flat on the bed and his face quiet.

"Well," he said.

That was all. He did not say anything else. But Toren saw his face, and it was the face he had had in the first lane on the thirty-fourth day, when the rack came down onto the cinders where nobody was. It was the face of a man who has watched something happen that he did not do.

"Callie," said Dee.

Callie read the jar.

"A half," she said. "Out of the road, for Rook. With the vent." She went down to look. "A quarter of mine, for the lee. Eleven turns." She looked at Toren.

"Two sets," said Toren. "Hardly anything."

"And mine," said Dee. "I'll draw Jab's hands and his, and that's a quarter of mine I'll settle back tonight." She wrote. "So tonight the road jar gives a half for Toren and a quarter for Callie and a quarter for me, and a half's gone out of it on Rook already." She looked up at Rook. "That's a Handful off the road because you went to look. Toren's half is the gaps."

"I'll not go to look again," said Rook.

"You will," said Dee. "You'll go to look again, because it's what you're for. You'll take Callie with the counter when you do, and you'll stand on the line and let her read it from there. And you'll not put a foot off the bed." She shut the book. "That's the rule now. Nobody goes to look alone."

Rook looked at her.

"That's better than mine," he said.

"It's better than both," said Dee.

---

The line came down off the flat in the late afternoon onto the last long slope, and at the bottom of the slope there was the wash.

Toren saw it from the top. The old line went down the slope on its raised bed, straight, with its posts along the north side. At the bottom of the slope the flat opened out into a wide shallow bottom, grey from one side to the other. The line ran out across it on a long embankment, higher than it had been anywhere, a man's height and a half above the bottom. At the far side the embankment went into a cut through a low ridge, a notch with steep walls. And beyond the ridge, a mile off, there was a river, low and dark in its bed. Beside the river there was a square stone building with no roof.

The pump house.

And beyond the river, the fence, running away north along the far bank, a line of posts with wire on them. And the hill, near now, so near that it filled half the west, flat-topped, dark. And over the flat top of it, the place in the sky that was wrong.

"The wash," said Pell.

They camped at the top of the slope, on the bed of the old line, a mile and a half from the embankment, with the wind in their faces and the sun going down behind the hill. Dee would not go nearer to sleep. And in the last of the light, Toren and Callie and Pell went down to look.

They did not go alone. Dee had said so. They went together, down the bed of the old line to the start of the embankment, with Callie in front with the counter held out at the end of her arm, reading it out every ten breaths. Toren walked behind her and counted. Pell walked behind him with his hands in his coat, looking at the embankment the way he looked at a road.

"Fourteen," said Callie, at the foot of the slope where the embankment began.

They stopped there. Dee had said not a step onto the embankment. They stood on the last of the old line where it came off the slope and went out onto the embankment, and looked.

---

The wash came down from the north-west, from the direction of the hill.

It was a wide shallow channel across the bottom, dry in the middle now except for a thin dark trickle, with banks of grey on either side of the trickle. The grey was the drifts. They were not like the drift by the old line in the afternoon. They were twenty drifts, a hundred. They lay all along the bed of the wash and up its banks, long and soft and crusted, grey to the colour of the jars. They were deepest against the north side of the embankment, where the wash came to it. There the wind and the water had piled them up against the foot of the bank in a long grey heap, half as high as the bank itself.

The embankment crossed the wash from east to west. It was about two hundred and fifty paces long, from where they stood to the mouth of the cut in the ridge. The bed of the old line went along the top of it, four paces wide, packed stone, with the iron posts along the north edge of it every twenty paces. On the south side the bank went down steeply into reeds, a marsh of them, grey-brown and dead, standing in black water, going away south to the river. On the north side the bank went down steeply into the drifts.

The wash went under the embankment through a culvert.

Toren could see it from where he stood. It was about three quarters of the way along, a hundred and eighty paces out, where the embankment dipped a little over the middle of the wash. There was a stone arch in the bank there, low, with the dark trickle of the wash going into it on the north side under the drifts. On the south side the arch was not there any more. It had fallen out of the face of the bank into the reeds, and there was a raw place in the south side of the embankment, like a bite. The bed of the old line ran over the top of the arch. There was an iron post on the north parapet of it, set in stone, leaning a little outward over the wash.

Across the top of the embankment, over the arch, there was a line in the stone of the bed. A dark line, crooked, from the north edge nearly to the south.

"That's the crack," said Pell quietly. "The girl was right." He looked at it for a long time. "The south face is old. It fell out years ago, and the arch has settled down onto what fell and packed it. That half's sitting on stone." He pointed with his chin at the north side, where the trickle went in under the drifts. "It's the north side that's going. The wash has been cutting in under it from the drift side all year. The crown's hanging on that side. That's why it's cracked."

"Will it take the cart?"

"It'll take the horse. It'll take people." Pell did not look away from it. "It might take the cart. I'd want both wheels on the south half, over the good stone. And the bed's four paces and the cart's two. With the south wheel on the south edge, the north wheel's still over the crack."

Callie had the counter out in front of her, pointed along the embankment.

"From here," she said. "Fourteen at our feet. I can hear it go up in the gusts." She waited. The wind came across the drifts from the hill and brought the thin grey smoke up off them and across the embankment in a long low haze. The counter went from rain falling to rain on a tin roof, and down again. "Forty, in that. Out there, halfway, it'll be more. On the arch, more. It's the drifts. They're against the north side, and the wind's coming off them across the top." She was quiet. "And at the bottom of the bank on the north side, by the arch, where they're deepest. I don't know. More than I want to say."

"Say it," said Toren.

"A hundred," said Callie. "Maybe. More, if you were in it. If you were down there." She put the counter away. "Nobody goes down there."

They stood and looked at the wash.

"The other ways," said Toren.

Pell told him. North up the wash, round the head of the drifts, toward the hill. It was nearer the fence and hotter all the way, and there was no bed for the cart. South down the side of the embankment into the marsh, and round through the reeds to the river, and up the river to the pump house. There was no bed for the cart there either, and the marsh was water to the waist. Or back.

"Or over," said Pell. "Once. Fast as the frame goes. Two hundred and fifty paces. And the arch."

Toren counted it. He could not help it. He did it the way his father had done a road, with the ifs.

*If the arch takes the cart. If the wind's down. If nothing comes up out of the grey at the lane, the way the girl said things came up at the lamp. If nothing comes out of the reeds.*

"Over," he said.

---

They made the plan at the fire. Dee made it. She said every part of it out loud and Toren wrote it on the back of the paper as she said it, under the day's count. Afterward he read it all back.

Two hundred and fifty paces. One pass. At the frame's pace, Dee said, it was four turns of the glass, five with the arch. At forty ticks, fifty, a hundred in a gust, for five turns, it was a little in everybody. A quarter each, maybe, drawn out at the far end, in the cut, where the ridge was between them and the drifts and the counter would be low again. A Handful and a half, near enough, out of the road jar, for all six of them and the horse, if it went right.

"And if it doesn't?" said Rook.

"Then it's more," said Dee. "And we'll know how much when we're there, and not before."

The order.

Callie in front on the north edge, the windward side, on the stone of the bed, with the hook. She would not hold the lane the whole way. She could not. She would put it up in the gusts, when the grey came across, and let it down between them, and say *lane* and *down* so everybody knew. Behind the lane, in the lee, Jab at the cart's tail, with the frame on his back, and Dee beside him, on the north side of the cart. Dee would have the glass and the road jar out of its rack, in her arms, so it was to hand.

Pell at Duchess's head, on the south side, with his knife on his belt where he had shown Toren.

The cart in the middle of the bed. The four Flasks in the rack behind the driver's bench. The road jar was coming out of it into Dee's arms, so it would be three, the home ones. The lead box behind them, empty, on its straw. The vent jars in the straw at the tail, apart from everything, five of them now.

Rook at the tail on the south side. His job was the reeds. If anything came out of the marsh, it would come on the dark side, away from the lane and the light, and he would light his blade and fence it off the cart. Half a turn. Ten minutes. No more, and Dee would count it.

And Toren on the south side of the cart by the north wheel's axle, under, on the inside. At the arch, he would set under the north axle from the good stone on the south half of the crown, slanting across, and hold the cart while the north wheel went over the crack. Then *step*, to the south wheel, and set again if it wanted it.

"Callie's got the counter," said Dee. "She'll say the ticks every ten breaths while she can. When she can't, I'll take it off her hip."

"What's red?" said Pell.

"The line on the window," said Callie. "A hundred and fifty, near enough. When it's past that it doesn't tick. It buzzes." She touched the bag. "The needle sticks at the top of the red. He said. Tap it once. Not twice."

"Once," said Toren, and wrote it.

"Exits," said Dee.

"Back," said Toren. He had it on the paper. "East. Wherever we are, back is back the way we came, to the foot of the slope, where it's fourteen." He looked at the plan. "Until the arch. After the arch, forward's shorter. Seventy paces to the cut."

"Not the reeds," said Pell. "Nobody goes in the reeds. There's water in there to your chest."

"And not the drifts," said Callie.

"Nobody goes in the drifts," said Dee.

Toren read it all back to them, from the top. They listened. When he had finished, nobody said anything for a while. Duchess shifted at the wheel.

"And the ash," said Toren. "Before we go. Say it."

Callie said it.

"Road, three. After tonight's settling. In Miss Wren's arms tomorrow." She looked at Jab. "Inside, sixty-four. On the frame. Not touched." She looked at the cart. "Home, twenty-four. On the cart. Not to be touched." She was quiet a moment. "Settled, a Handful in me. A Handful in Toren. A Handful in Miss Wren." And then: "We've spent five of the road in five days. Two and a quarter on the man in the ditch. A Handful today because of the drift. The rest on learning." She did not look at Rook. "Not a grain on a hound."

"Not a grain on a hound," said Dee. "Write that, Toren. I'd like it written somewhere that we got to the wash with three in the road jar and never spent a grain on a hound."

He wrote it.

---

He settled his half at the fire after the others had lain down.

It took him a long time. Longer than the washout's quarter. He sat with his palm flat on the square of paper on his knee and did not haul, and the ash came up into his palm at the rate it came, cold and slow, and went down. He sat with it. He felt it lie at the bottom of him and warm, and join the half that was there, until the stone was a whole Handful again. Warm and faint, a long way down.

The first one full. The night before.

He looked at the paper when he had finished. At the plan, and under it the count.

*45th. The top of the slope above the wash. Out: 6. In: 6. The drift by the line: R. went to look. First time all together: lane, set, draw. It worked the second time. Road, 3.*

He wanted to write something else. He did not know what. He sat with the pencil over the paper and thought about the arch, and the crack in the crown, and the grey heaped against the north side of the bank half as high as the bank itself.

He thought about his father, who had never once priced the way home. Behind them, east, was a road they had come along for five days. It was the only way home they knew. In the morning they would go out along the embankment, over the arch, and it would be behind them.

He did not write anything else. He folded the paper and put it in his coat with the plate. He lay down by Jab and counted them.

Pell. Dee. Rook, longest. Jab. Callie, with the hook on the ground beside her and her hand on it. Duchess at the wheel with her ears forward, not eating, looking west.

---

In the morning the wind had dropped.

That was the first thing Toren knew. He woke at the fourth hour when Dee woke, because she woke, and lay on the canvas and listened, and there was nothing. No wind. The frost had come down hard in the stillness, and the grass on the bed of the old line was white and stiff, and the smoke from the fire went straight up.

"That's luck," said Pell, at the fire. "Don't anybody say it out loud."

They said the rules at first light, on the bed of the old line at the top of the slope, with the hill in front of them. It was very near now, dark against a sky going grey. The place over the flat top of it was darker than the rest of the sky, and round its edge was that light the colour of the bottom of a pond. Nobody looked at it for long.

Dee measured their floors. Callie's own and the stone, where they were. Toren's own, back from the night on the rise, and the stone full. Jab's hand and a bit, his floor a finger. Rook's.

She held Rook's wrist longer than the others.

"Half a turn," she said.

"Half a turn," said Rook.

Then they went down the slope in the order Dee had said, and at the foot of it, where the embankment began, Callie held out the counter.

"Twelve," she said. "No wind. It's lower than last night."

"Go now," said Dee. "While it's down."

And they went out onto the embankment.

---

Toren walked on the south side of the cart with his left arm in its cuff against his chest and his eyes on the north wheel.

He knew where everybody was. He knew it without looking. Callie in front on the north edge with the hook, not rocking it yet, walking with the curve an inch off the stone. Pell at Duchess's head on the south side, a pace in front of him, with his hand on her neck. Duchess walking. The cart. Dee and Jab behind it on the north side, at the tailboard, Dee with the road jar in the crook of her arm and the glass in her other hand. Rook at the tail on the south side, behind Toren, walking his careful straight walk with his eyes on the reeds.

Six. And a horse.

The drifts lay against the north side of the bank below Callie, grey and crusted and still. The reeds stood on the south side below him, grey-brown, dead, still, in black water. Nothing moved anywhere. There was no wind. The smoke that had come off the drifts all yesterday was not coming.

"Twenty," said Callie. "Ten breaths."

Fifty paces. A post. Another.

"Twenty-six."

A hundred paces. The arch was ahead, eighty paces on, with the dark line of the crack across the bed over it and the iron post on the north parapet leaning out over the wash.

"Thirty."

And the wind came.

It came off the hill all at once, the way wind does after a still night. It came across the wash from the west-north-west, low, and it took the crust off the drifts below Callie as if somebody had blown on a plate of flour. The grey came up off them in a long low smoke and across the top of the embankment. Across Callie, across Duchess's head, across the cart.

"Lane," said Callie.

She put the back of the hook's curve down on the stone of the north edge and rocked it forward, and the lee stood up off it. It was pale, shoulder high. The grey smoke hit it and hissed and went along it and round the end of it. Toren saw it go round the end, in front of Duchess, and across Pell.

"Sixty," said Callie. "Ten breaths. In the lee. Ninety outside it."

"Keep walking," said Dee.

They kept walking.

A hundred and twenty paces. The arch was sixty paces on. The grey was coming across in waves, and Callie was walking the lee along the north edge at the frame's pace with her eyes on Jab and Dee behind her. It leaned back over them. Toren could see it lean. He was on the other side of the cart from it, and the grey was coming over the top of the cart onto him, fine, and he put his sleeve over his mouth.

Then Callie said, "Something," and her voice was different.

Toren looked past Duchess's head.

The drift at the foot of the bank below Callie was moving.

It was not the wind. The wind was taking the top off it in a smoke. This was underneath. The crust of the drift heaved, twenty paces ahead of Callie and below her, at the foot of the bank. It heaved up in a long hump the way the old line's bed had heaved in the frost. The crust cracked along the top of the hump. Something came up out of it.

It was the colour of an old wasp nest. It was the colour of the grey, because it was covered in it, and it came up out of the drift the way husks came up out of the ditch water at the Sag. All at once, as if it had been lying there waiting, it stood. It had no face. It turned toward the lee.

"Husk," said Callie.

Another came up behind it. And a third, further along, by the arch.

"They're coming at the lane," said Callie. Her voice was flat and loud. "At the Ward. It's worked. They go for anything worked. Rook—"

They came up the bank.

---

Rook came forward.

He came up the south side of the cart past Toren, at more than his careful walk. He went round Duchess's head and across the bed to the north edge in front of Callie. He was lighting his blade as he went. Toren saw it come on his fist, short and plain, and the light come along the edge of it, white. He met the first husk at the top of the bank, where it came up over the lip onto the stones of the bed, and put the blade through it.

It came apart.

It came apart the way husks did, all at once, into grey. And the grey did not fall. The wind had it. It went up off the lip of the bank in a burst, the whole of a husk's worth of loose grey, and the wind took it and brought it across the top of the embankment in a cloud, over Rook and Callie and Duchess's head, and over the cart. Over Toren.

"*Don't*—" said Dee, from the tailboard. "Not in the wind—"

The counter on Callie's hip went from rain on a tin roof to something faster.

"Ninety," said Callie. "In the lee. A hundred and twenty outside." She did not stop walking. She did not let the lee down. "Rook, don't kill them on the lip. The wind's bringing it over—"

The second husk came up over the lip. Rook met it, because there was nothing else to do with it, and it came apart, and the wind brought it over them.

The cart came to the arch.

---

Toren saw the north wheel go onto the crown. He saw the dark line of the crack in the stone of the bed under it. He was already moving.

He came round under the cart on the inside, on the south half of the crown, the good half, where Pell had said the stone was sitting on the part that was still there. He did not look at the husks or the grey. He looked at the ground, the way Pell had said on the forty-fifth. *You'll not always have her.* The south half of the crown. The dressed stone of the arch under the bed, sitting on its springing. A flat grey slab with its near edge sitting on stone.

He put the foot of the strut down on it, slanting outward under the cart, toward the north axle.

"Set," he said.

The north wheel came to the crack.

And the crown went.

It did not go all at once. It went the way the Sag had gone on the twenty-fifth day, a sound first, under his feet, a deep grinding like a mill wheel turning over. Then the north half of the bed over the arch dropped. It dropped a hand's width, and then another, away from the crack. The north wheel went down with it, into the gap, and the whole weight of the cart came over onto the north side.

It came onto the strut.

He was soft. He was soft behind it the way he had been at seven paces, and at the washout, and on thirty gaps on the old line. He felt the weight arrive. It was more than any sack. It was more than the rack or the box in the pen. It was a loaded cart going over into a hole, with three Flasks and a lead box and everything on it. It came down the strut into the slab on the good half of the crown, and into the stone under it. It did not come up into him. His knees gave, and took the last of it, and the cart stopped.

It stopped with the north wheel down in the broken crown to its hub, and the cart leaning out north over the lip of the bank, over the wash, over the drifts, on a line of light that ran from the south half of the arch to Toren's knee.

The wheel had gone down onto what was left in the hole, and that took some of it. What came down the strut was the lean, the part of the cart that wanted to go over the lip. It was less than a wheel on the gaps, not much less, and it did not roll past and go. It stayed.

Breaths under a wheel. He began to count them.

"Holding," said Toren.

In front of him, Duchess screamed.
