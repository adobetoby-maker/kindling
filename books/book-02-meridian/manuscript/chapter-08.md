# Chapter 8 — Jab: Calibration

It woke him at the second hour.

At first he could not tell what had woken him. Hut twelve was dark, the stove had gone down to a red line, and Otto was asleep in the bed by the door, on his back, breathing like a big dog.

Jab lay still and listened. There was nothing to hear.

Then he felt it again. It was not a sound but a lean under his breastbone, where it lived, neither up nor down, the way a floor leans in an old house. Everything in him was sliding one way, very slightly, toward the north end of the station.

*Worse*, it said. That was all it ever said.

He knew what it was worth. Four times in five it was right. When it was wrong, it was wrong in the direction he wanted. So he had made a rule. He did not ask it when he wanted an answer.

He had not asked. It had come on its own.

He got up and got his boots on in the dark without waking Otto, and went out of the door and up the row, past the dark step of eleven, and turned onto the main street.

The lamps were off. They had gone off at the first hour. He walked in the dark down the middle of the street, counting paces, and stepped wide of the loose grating between the second pole and the third without thinking about it.

---

Mrs Lask was at the desk. She looked up.

"I haven't sent for you," she said.

"I know," said Jab. "I'm going in anyway."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she looked down at her list.

"Fifth on the right," she said, as if he did not know. "The nurse is already with him. She came on at the first hour and went straight there. I didn't send for you, and neither did she. So who did?"

"Nobody," said Jab.

Mrs Lask wrote something on her list. He did not wait to see what.

---

The ward was dark except for a small lamp at the far end and another by the fifth bed on the right. The young nurse was there, the one who had stitched his arm on the bench. She had the blanket off Tonk's leg.

Tonk was awake. His face was red and wet. His eyes were too bright.

"Jab," he said. "The nails are *hot*."

Jab sat down in the chair.

"I know," he said. He did not know. He looked at the nurse.

"Fever," she said, low. "Came up about the twelfth hour. It's high. And this one's angry." She pointed without touching. The pin nearest the knee went in through a red place in the skin. The red had spread. There was a wet shine round the pin where there should not have been. "It might be only the pin. That happens. The skin gets in a temper round the metal and it'll clean up with medicine. Or it might be deeper."

"How do you tell?"

"Keel tells," said the nurse. "I've sent for her."

---

Tonk's hand found the long cord. It was still knotted round his wrist, and the other end was tied to the bed rail, the way Jab left it every night when the ward turned him out. Eight nights now. The bell on its string hung from the same rail, and Tonk had never once rung it.

Jab untied the rail end and knotted it round his own left wrist.

Tonk pulled the cord. It was a small pull. Jab was already there.

"I *pulled* it," said Tonk.

"I know. I'm here."

"You were *already* here."

"I came early," said Jab. "I had a feeling."

Tonk looked at him with his too-bright eyes.

"Is it the *thing*?" he said. "Your thing?"

Jab did not answer for a moment. Tonk had never asked about it before. He had not known Tonk knew there was a thing to ask about.

"Yes," he said. "It told me you were worse."

"I *am* worse," said Tonk, with the air of a man confirming a report. "It's right."

"It's right this time."

"Is it going to *fix* it?"

---

Jab looked at his brother's leg in its padded trough, with the four pins through it and the bar along the outside, and the red place at the top pin.

He had written it on the card. He had pressed hard with the pencil. *It does not touch his leg. It has never gone within a hand's breadth of the bone and it never will.*

He had written it because he was afraid. He knew that now. He had watched a woman with her sleeves rolled up take a dead piece of bone out of his brother, and set the leg straight, and bolt it. He had watched her do it with instruments and pictures and nine children's worth of knowing. He did not know anything. He had a thing that came when it liked and went out like a bucket going dry. If he put it into that leg and it did the wrong thing, he would not know it had done the wrong thing until it was too late to undo.

"No," he said. "It's not going to fix it. That's Keel's."

"Why?"

"Because I don't know what it does," said Jab. "And she does."

Tonk thought about that. His face was very hot.

"Can it do the *other*?" he said. "The warm one. From the wall."

Jab went still.

He had not known Tonk remembered the wall. Tonk had been going grey. He had not been looking at anything.

"I can try," said Jab. "It might not come. It doesn't come when I call it."

"Try *anyway*."

---

So he put his hand flat on his brother's chest.

Not the leg. Nowhere near the leg. The chest, the way he had at the culvert, over the small fast heart.

He did not push; he had learned that much. He did not reach, or lean on it, or ask it to run uphill. He sat there in the lamplight with his hand on his brother and let go of wanting it to do anything.

It was very hard. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything since the wall.

Nothing came.

Not nothing. He could feel the bottom of the bucket, and there was something in it. A little. More than there had been on the bench on the first night. It had come back while he was busy with other things. But it lay at the bottom without moving, and it would not come up, and he did not make it.

After a while he took his hand away.

"It didn't come," he said.

"No," said Tonk. He was not surprised. "It was warm, though. Your *hand* was."

"That's just a hand."

"It was warm," said Tonk, and shut his eyes. "That's allowed."

---

Doctor Keel came at the third hour with her sleeves already rolled and her hair flat on one side from a pillow.

She looked at the pin for eleven seconds. She put two fingers on Tonk's foot and watched the toes. She put the back of her hand on his forehead. Then she turned round.

"It might be the pin," she said. "It might be deeper. I'm not guessing, and I'm not opening him again on a guess." She said it fast and flat. "I want a picture at first light. Before I decide anything. If it's the pin, I clean it and change his medicine and we carry on. If it's the bone, I'm going in again, and it'll be today."

"And the two weeks?" said Jab.

"The two weeks still stand. This isn't the answer to that. This is a bad night." She looked at him. "Everybody gets one. I told you he'd hate us all at some point in the middle. It's started early. That's all."

"Are you going to do it *inside* again," said Tonk, with his eyes shut.

"Maybe," said Doctor Keel. "I'll tell you before I do. I won't sneak up on you."

"You *did* last time."

"I told you last time," said Keel. "You weren't listening. You were talking about a fish."

---

At first light they took him down the slope on the rolling bed.

Jab walked beside it with the long cord still running from his wrist to Tonk's, and the paper tray in his free hand. The metal lay in the tray on its short neck cord, pale and clean from its second washing. He kept his eyes off it.

At the bottom of the slope was the wide door and the yellow line.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

At the line he untied the long cord from his own wrist. Every night before this he had moved that end to the bed rail when they turned him out, so that Tonk was never tied to nothing. This time he held the loose end out.

Tonk looked at it.

"You keep it," said Jab. "It's not metal. It can go in."

Tonk took the loose end and closed his hot fist on it. He did not let go of Jab's hand straight away. Last time he had. This time he held on first, hard, for about three seconds.

Then he let go.

The door shut like a lid.

---

Jab sat on the bench against the wall with the tray on his knees.

He heard wheels on the slope. He knew the sound of these wheels. It was the chair.

Toren was pushing it. His arm was still in the sling, and he was pushing one-handed, leaning his weight on the handle to steer. Senna was in it with a blanket over her knees and her hands in her lap. She was watching her hands.

"It's the eleventh day," said Senna, before anybody could say anything. "They told me first light. Why is it always first light? Nothing good has ever happened to anybody at first light."

"The little lad's in first," said Jab. "They brought him down early. He had a bad night."

Senna looked at him. Then she looked at the door.

"Then I'll wait," she said. "I've a great deal of time, and nothing to be rude about till they call me."

Toren stopped the chair by the bench and sat down on the other end of it. He asked nothing. He looked at the door, and at the tray on Jab's knees, and at Jab's empty wrist where the cord had been.

"The pin," said Jab. "Nearest the knee. It might be only the pin."

Toren nodded. He put his hand in his coat pocket and left it there.

---

Callie came down the slope a quarter of an hour after, with the hook on her shoulder.

"Otto came and banged on fourteen," she said. "He said you'd gone out at the second hour and not come back, and he'd gone up to the long building and Mrs Lask said *fifth on the right, and the picture at first light*." She looked at the door. "Mum's pills aren't till the eighth hour. Katori's got her."

She sat down on the floor with her back to the wall, because the bench was full, and laid the hook across her knees. Her hands were loose on it.

Behind the wall, the knocking started.

It came in its long runs, fast and steady and mechanical. Jab had heard it twice before through this wall, once for Tilda and once for Tonk. He had no need to count it; he could see Callie's lips moving on the floor beside him.

*Eleven. Gap. Eleven.*

Then her lips stopped.

"That's ten," she said.

---

Nobody said anything.

The knocking went on. Callie's lips moved again.

"Eleven," she said quietly. "Eleven. Ten. Eleven." She looked up. "It's dropping one. Not every time. Every third run. Maybe every fourth."

"When your mother was in," said Jab, "was it—"

"Eleven every time," said Callie. "I counted all of it. I couldn't help it." She looked at the door. "It's not the same."

The knocking stopped.

It stopped in the middle of a run. There was no gap after it and no next run. There was only the wall, and the cool grey air, and nothing.

Toren had taken his hand out of his pocket. He was sitting forward with his elbow on his knee.

"Third row down," he said, very low, to nobody. "Fourth box along."

Jab turned his head.

"He told me," said Toren. "The man who runs it. A week ago. He ran the rack twice before he'd let it look at her. He said that box wakes up slow. He said it did it in the summer, and it was fine for a month after."

The knocking started again.

It stopped again.

---

Somebody came down the slope fast.

It was Doctor Sowerby. He came down it at something that was very nearly a run, with no folder under his arm, and he went past them to the heavy door without looking at any of them, and knocked on it with his fist.

"Nobody runs in here," said Senna, from the chair, to nobody. "I'd like that understood."

The door opened a hand's width. Jab saw the grey-haired man with the ink on his hand. The two of them spoke through the gap, too low to hear. Then Sowerby went in and the door shut.

Jab sat with the tray on his knees.

His brother was on the other side of that door, lying very still on a narrow bed through the middle of a pale ring, with a cord in his fist. The machine that was supposed to be looking through him had stopped looking. He could not see him. He could not hear him. He could not get to him.

He put his hand on his chest, over the place where the metal was not.

He did not ask. He only put it there.

"He's still," said Callie, from the floor.

Jab looked at her.

"I can't do what Dessa does," she said. "I don't know where he is. But he's four and he's frightened and he's in a loud room with nobody he knows. And nobody's run out. And nobody's shouted." She kept her eyes on the door. "If he'd moved, they'd have come out to tell you. They'd need you to make him lie still. They haven't come."

"You read that off the door."

"I read it off the not-coming," said Callie. "It's the same as grass. It's what didn't happen."

---

The knocking came back.

It came in long runs, and Callie's lips moved, and she did not say *ten*. It went on for a long time. Then it stopped at the end of a run, the way it was meant to.

The heavy door opened.

Tonk came out on the rolling bed with his eyes screwed shut and his face red and furious, and the cord in his fist. He opened his eyes when he crossed the yellow line and found Jab.

"It *broke*," he said.

"I heard."

"It stopped knocking and a man *swore* and then a different man came in and *they* swore." Tonk's voice was high with fever and outrage. "And then it went again. And then it stopped again. And they did it *three times*."

"You stayed still."

"I stayed *very* still," said Tonk. "I stayed still for *three* of them."

Doctor Keel was at the top of the slope. She must have been in the room at the back, behind the desk, where the picture came out. She came down to the line and looked at Tonk and then at Jab.

"It's the pin," she said. "Not the bone. I think."

"You think."

"*I think* is not a phrase I like," said Doctor Keel. "It's what I've got. The first two pictures were no use to anybody. The third one's a picture of a leg if you squint and you know what you're looking for, and I do." She put her hands in her pockets. "There's no collection in the bone that I can see. There's a temper round the top pin. I'll clean it this morning and change his medicine. He'll be hot for two days and cross for three."

"And the two weeks?"

"Six days from now," said Keel. "I'll need a picture for that. A proper one." She looked at the heavy door, and said nothing else.

---

They could not take Senna in straight away.

The grey-haired man came out and stood in the doorway and looked at the old woman in the chair. His face had the look of somebody who has been awake since the first hour doing arithmetic.

"Give me a little while," he said to her. "I need to talk to the doctor. And then I'll need to warm it up twice. Maybe three times."

"I've had eleven days," said Senna. "I can have a little while."

He went back in. Sowerby came out after him and stood just inside the door with him, on the far side of the yellow line. Neither of them seemed to remember that the corridor had people in it. Or they remembered, and had decided it did not matter, because there was no curtain in the building worth the name.

Jab heard all of it.

"How long," said Sowerby.

"It's been drifting a month. It's been worse this week. Today I had to walk the rack four times to get one clean read." The grey-haired man rubbed the ink on his hand with his thumb. "I can correct it by hand. I've been doing that since Tuesday. Every run takes longer. Every run I'm less sure."

"How long can you correct it by hand?"

"I don't know. Weeks. Maybe less. It's not one box. It's one box that's worst. When one goes, the ones either side of it start working harder to agree with it."

"Is there a spare?"

The grey-haired man laughed. It was not a laugh.

"There's never been a spare," he said. "There's never been a second one of anything in that room. I've been through the depot list twice this month. Things go into that depot. Nothing comes out that fits."

Sowerby was quiet.

"Then we ration it," he said at last. "Cycles first. Every cycle, on the day. Pictures after. Only the pictures we can't do without."

"And the lad's two-week?"

"Is a picture we can't do without," said Sowerby. "So is the woman with the growth, if she fits badly. So is the next child who comes through that gate." He put his hand flat on the door frame. "Warm it up. She's waited long enough."

---

Toren had heard it too. Jab could see it in his face. *Cycles first.* That was Senna. *Pictures after.* That was Tonk, and Callie's mother, and whoever came next.

Toren was not looking at any of them. He was looking through the wide door at the back of the long room, where Jab could just see the edge of a steel rack with little green lights in it. One of them was not green.

Senna was looking at it too.

She had turned her chair a little, with her useless hands, by leaning. She was looking through the door at the rack with a face Jab had not seen on her. It was not frightened. It was the face of somebody recognising an old road.

"The last time I stood this close to one of those racks," she said, "I was fifty-three. And I'd come for the copper."

Toren's head came round.

"Gran?"

"It's nothing," said Senna. "It's an old woman in a chair saying things." She looked down at her hands in her lap, watching them. "There were rooms full of them. All in rows. With the little windows. We pulled the backs off and took the wire." She was quiet. "We didn't know what they were for. We only knew what they were made of."

Nobody said anything.

Then the grey-haired man came to the door and said, "Mrs Voss," and Toren got up and pushed her to the yellow line, and stopped there, because that was the rule.

---

She was in there two hours.

The three of them sat outside. Nobody sent them away. Tonk had gone up the slope with Keel and the nurse and his cord, to have his pin cleaned. He had told Jab to stay and *mind the old lady*, because she had been rude to the machine and it might be rude back.

So Jab stayed.

They did not talk much. Callie sat on the floor with the hook on her knees and did not count out loud. Jab could see her not counting. He could see it in her mouth, the way it stayed shut when the knocking came in its runs. He could see how much it cost her. He thought she was counting anyway, somewhere inside. He thought she was not letting it out, because out loud it would be for Toren to hear, and it was not hers to give him.

Toren sat with his hand in his pocket, and it stayed there; he never checked. Jab had watched him do the checking at the culvert, on the road, in the ward. He had watched him stop. He did not know what had happened to make him stop, and he did not ask.

And Jab did the arithmetic, because his head would not do anything else.

Eleven days to Senna's next cycle, six to Tonk's picture, three to Callie's review and the Director's room on the second floor of the yard office, and four to his own review. Four seasons of Rook's debt, starting now. Thirteen years of Tonk coming back through those doors to be measured.

And one machine.

He had known that. Sowerby had told him on the first morning, in a corridor. *There's one machine in a shed at the north end of this station that takes the picture. I don't know of another within four hundred miles.* He had heard it as a fact about Meridian. Like the lamps. Like the two gates.

He had not heard it as a thing that could stop.

---

Senna came out at the end of the second hour on the rolling bed, grey and small, with her eyes open.

"It sang wrong," she said. "It sang flat. Somebody ought to know that."

"Did it work?" said Toren.

"He says so." She looked at the ceiling of the corridor. "He looked at his screen for a very long time before he said so. And then he said *yes* to me, and *I think* to the doctor, when he thought I couldn't hear." She shut her eyes. "I've been lied to by better men. He's not good at it. I like him for that."

Toren took the chair handles. He did not push yet.

"Eleven days," he said.

"Eleven days," said Senna. "And I'll be here, and so will it." She did not open her eyes. "I've no intention of going anywhere, Tor. I've told everybody."

---

Tonk's fever came down a little by the evening.

Not all the way. Keel had said two days. But the red round the top pin had been cleaned and dressed, and the new medicine was going into him through the tube. He lay flat with the cord in his fist and the fish on his pillow and looked at the ceiling.

"Did the machine get cross?" he said.

"It got tired," said Jab.

"Machines don't get *tired*."

"This one does. It's very old."

Tonk thought about this.

"Like Rook," he said.

Jab opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"Yes," he said. "A bit like Rook."

"Rook's *got* a hole in him," said Tonk. "Wyck told me. Has the machine got a hole?"

"Sort of," said Jab. "There's a bit in the back that's gone wrong. And they haven't got another bit."

"Then they should *get* one," said Tonk, and went to sleep before Jab could tell him that there was nowhere to get one from.

---

He sat on the step of twelve that night.

He had not decided to. He had come back from the ward at the tenth hour, when they turned the visitors out and he had tied the long cord's end to the bed rail, and he could not go in. Otto was inside, asleep, breathing like a dog. The stove was banked.

One door down, on the step of eleven, Toren was sitting with his back against the door frame.

On the step of fourteen, on the other side, Callie was sitting with her knees up.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The lamp at the end of the row put its flat white circle down on the wet concrete, the colour of bone.

"Third row down," said Toren at last. "Fourth box along. I looked at it through the door. The light in it wasn't green."

"What colour?" said Callie.

"Yellow. Like the line on the floor."

Callie was quiet.

"It dropped one every third run," she said. "At the end, when it was working. Before that, every other." She did not count anything else out loud. Jab heard her not do it.

"He can fix it by hand," said Jab. "For weeks, he said. Maybe less."

"Which," said Toren.

"He didn't know."

---

They sat on their three steps.

Jab thought about the card in hut twelve. *Requested at the Director's office.* All three of them, the same hour. He had thought it was about the north road. About the hounds, and the light, and people going out through a gate they had been told not to. Maybe it was.

He thought about the man in the long coat. *There's a line on the register for it. It's a long line with very little written on it.* He thought about Rook on this same row, in the dusk, saying *I've done a thing that rhymes with it*.

He thought about Callie on the floor of the corridor, reading a door by what did not come out of it. About Toren sitting outside a room he could not go into, learning the rack by heart. About himself on a bench with a hand on his chest, not asking.

None of it could make a box in a rack turn green.

"There's no other one," said Callie. "Is there. Anywhere."

"Four hundred miles," said Jab. "Sowerby said. He's looked."

"Four hundred miles is only as far as he's looked," said Toren.

Neither of the others answered that. Jab did not think Toren had meant it to be answered. He thought Toren had said it the way you say a thing to find out whether it is true, and then heard it out loud, and did not know yet.

---

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour.

It did not flicker. It went, all at once, the way it did every night, for its written reason. The dark came down over the three steps and the row and the whole of Yard C.

Jab sat in it.

He could not see Toren now, or Callie, but he knew they were there; he could hear the stove tick through the door of eleven, and Callie shift on her step and settle again.

He thought about a squirrel, four hundred miles north, and a heap he could not get back to.

He had told Tonk he would find a different one, somewhere in this place, for somebody, and he had thought it would be a person when he found it, and that he would know it when he saw it.

He had not thought it might be a room.

He sat in the dark with his hand on his knee. Somewhere at the north end of the station, under a long low building with a turf roof, there was a pale ring standing on its edge in the dark, and a rack of little windows behind it. One of them was yellow. All three of them were held up by it, one way or another. Senna's marrow. Tonk's picture. Whatever Callie's mother would need, the next time a fit ran long.

It was older than anybody who ran it. It was the only one.

And it was starting to wake up slow.
