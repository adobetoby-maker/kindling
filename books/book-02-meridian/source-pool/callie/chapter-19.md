# Chapter Nineteen

There were five chairs by the bed when they came in, and Callie counted them before she had taken three steps into the ward.

Five. Set out in a half circle round the fourth bed on the left, under the window, by somebody who had been told how many there would be. Somebody had written down *all of them* at the desk yesterday, and somebody else had read it this morning and gone and got five chairs, and put them out, and gone away. Callie looked at them and did not like it, and could not have said why.

Her mother was sitting up. Somebody had helped her. She was propped against three pillows with her hair brushed and her left hand on the blanket and her right hand lying beside it, and her eyes were open and clear. She looked at the five of them coming down the ward, Katori and Pell and Ines and Otto and Callie, and her face did the thing it had done on the wagon when Callie came back from a husk line with the tin in her coat.

"There you all are," she said. "Sit down. They've counted you."

They sat down. Callie took the chair nearest the bed. Pell sat at the end with the atlas shut on his knee, and did not open it.

Across the ward, Senna was sitting up in the opposite bed with a cup of something in both hands, looking at it. She did not look over. She did not need to. The ward was one long room, and there was nowhere in it you could not hear the bed across from you.

A nurse came to pull the curtain round.

"Leave it," said Callie's mother. "She'll hear anyway. I'd rather she heard it straight."

Across the ward, Senna did not look up from her cup. But Callie saw her mouth move at the corner.

---

Doctor Sowerby came in at the ninth hour exactly, with a folder under his arm.

He was small and bald and in a hurry, just as her mother had said. He came down the ward fast and stopped at the foot of the bed and looked at all of them, one after another, the way Ines looked at a range. Then he looked at Callie's mother, and nodded, as if something had been confirmed.

"Right," he said. "I'll do this the way I always do it. The bad thing first, then the good thing, then the bill. I've been told I do it too fast. Stop me."

Nobody stopped him.

"The bad thing is that there's a growth in her head," said Doctor Sowerby. He did not look at the folder. He put two fingers against the side of his own head, on the left, above and behind the ear. "Here. Deep. It's in the part that makes words, and the part that tells the right hand what to do, and the right leg. It's been growing since before the winter. Probably a good while before." He took his hand down. "We can't take it out. There's no way to get at it that wouldn't take more of her than it takes of it. And there's nothing we have here that makes it smaller. I've looked. I looked again this morning, because I didn't want to be wrong about it."

He stopped. He looked at Callie's mother, not at any of the rest of them.

"We can't treat it," he said. "I want that said in this room with you sitting up in it. Because in a fortnight somebody in a corridor is going to say the word *treated* to your daughter, and she'll hear it as something it isn't. And I'd rather be got out of the way now."

Callie's mother looked back at him.

"Thank you," she said.

---

Callie did not hear the next part properly.

She heard it. The words went into her. But they went into her the way the cold had gone into her on the causeway, a long way down and all at once, and she was sitting in a chair by a bed in a long white room, and she was also standing on the lip of a husk line in a hayfield in the spring with her grandfather lying in the grass. And she was also on the tailgate in the dark at three in the morning, watching her mother try to braid her hair with a hand that would not take instruction. And she was also counting. She was counting something. She did not know what.

"— the good thing," Doctor Sowerby was saying.

Callie made herself hear it.

"There's swelling round it. A lot of what you've been living with is the swelling, not the growth. The headaches, mostly. Some of the hand, maybe. Some of the words." He held up one finger. "That we can bring down. There's a medicine for it. Pills, morning and night. Some people get a bit of the hand back. Some get words back. Nearly everybody gets their mornings back, without the head."

He held up a second finger.

"The fits. There's a second medicine for those. They'll come less. Maybe not at all, for a while." He put his hand down. "It will make your days better. I can promise you that, and I've got the numbers for it in this folder, and you can read them. I can't promise it makes more of them. I won't say it does."

"No," said Callie's mother. "Don't."

"And then there's the part," said Doctor Sowerby, "that everybody asks me for. So I'll — "

"Stop," said Callie's mother.

---

He stopped.

He stood at the foot of the bed with the folder under his arm and looked at her, and waited. Callie saw that he had been stopped at this exact place before, and knew what came next, and was not going to say it for her.

"I know what the next part is," said Callie's mother. "You're going to tell me how long."

"If you want it."

"I don't."

Nobody in the ward moved.

"That's your right," said Doctor Sowerby. He said it at once, without any weight on it, the way you'd say the door was on the left. "Most people want it. Some don't. It's in the folder. It'll stay in the folder. It's written down, and whoever you say can have it, whenever you say." He paused. "Including you. Next week, next month. You can change your mind about it any day you like, and I won't ask you why."

"Thank you."

"I want it," said Callie.

She had not known she was going to say it. It came out of her all at once, too loud for the ward, and across the room Senna looked up from her cup.

Her mother turned her head on the pillow and looked at her.

"I want the number," said Callie. Her voice was doing something. She made it stop. "I'm not asking to be frightened. I'm asking to know. I'm the household. The man at the window said so. For the window, it's me."

"Then the window can have it," said her mother. "You can't."

---

Callie stared at her.

"It isn't yours," said her mother. She said it very gently, and very clearly, and every word of it was in the right place. "It's mine. It's in my head. It's the one thing in there that's still mine to decide about."

"I have to know. I have to know how many — "

"I know you do. That's why." Her mother's left hand came across the blanket and found Callie's wrist. "I've watched you do a sum every morning of your life since Gaunt's Reach. Fifteen days. Nineteen days. Nine pounds of flour. Seven matches. A hundred and fifty-eight seconds. You do it before you've got your boots on. You did it on the causeway with your arms up over your face. You'd do it every morning with this, and you'd get it right, you always get them right, and you'd take one off every day before you'd said good morning to me." She held on. "I'm not giving you that one. I'm not giving you the last sum, love. You can hate me for it. I'd rather you hated me for that than did it."

Callie could not say anything at all.

"And I'm not having it myself either," said her mother, to the room now, to Doctor Sowerby. "I've spent a month on the back of a wagon counting miles. I'm not going to spend whatever I've got counting days. I'd rather spend them on something else. I haven't decided what." She let go of Callie's wrist. "That's all. You can go on to the bill."

Doctor Sowerby looked at her for a long moment.

"Right," he said. His voice had not changed at all. But he took the folder out from under his arm and put it on the end of the bed and put his hand flat on it, closed. "The bill."

---

The bill was the shortest part.

"Her care is a clinical matter," said Doctor Sowerby. "It went through at the gate, and it's owed to nobody. The pills. The bed. Me. For as long as she needs it, which is as long as she needs it, and nobody at any window in this station can tell you different. If they do, you send them up to me, and I'll explain it to them slowly." He looked round at them all. "I'm told I'm very tiresome when I explain things slowly."

"And where she sleeps," said Katori quietly.

Doctor Sowerby turned his head.

"Where she sleeps is a different window," he said. "I don't sit at it."

"I'm not sleeping in a ward," said Callie's mother.

He looked back at her.

"I've nothing against the ward," said her mother. "It's very clean. The woman opposite is the best company I've had since the spring. But I'm not living in a bed with strings up the back of my gown, being asked my name eleven times, while my daughter sits on a bench outside waiting for the afternoon." She looked at him steadily. "Can I come in for the pills? Morning and night? Like going to market?"

"You can," said Doctor Sowerby. "Most people don't want to. You'll need somebody with you who knows the fits. Who knows what to do when one comes."

"I know the fits," said Callie. It came out of her flat and small.

"She counts them," said her mother.

Doctor Sowerby looked at Callie for a moment, and then back at her mother.

"Then I'll see you in the mornings," he said. "At the eighth hour. With the girl." He picked up the folder. "You can go when you like. Today, if the nurse says. I'd rather tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," said Callie's mother. "I'll give you tomorrow."

He nodded once. Then he went away down the ward as fast as he had come, and did not look back.

---

"Well," said Senna, from the opposite bed, into the silence.

Everybody looked at her.

"There's a rule in my family," said Senna. She was not looking at any of them. She was looking at the cup in her hands, which she was holding very carefully, watching her own fingers round it. "It wasn't mine. Somebody made it in a lane, about four months ago, and it stuck. The rule is that you say the whole of it once. Properly. With everybody there. So that nobody finds out second, and nobody has to carry a piece of it round on their own." She lifted the cup and drank, looking at it, and put it down. "And then you don't let anybody say it for you again. Not in any room, to anybody. If they ask, you send them to you."

Callie's mother looked across the ward at her for a long moment.

"That's a good rule," she said.

"It's the best rule I've ever been given," said Senna. "I recommend it. That's all. I'll go back to my tea."

Callie's mother lay against her pillows. She looked at the ceiling, and then at the window over the bed, where there was grey sky and the edge of a turf roof. Then she looked at the five of them in their five counted chairs.

"All right," she said. "Once. And then nobody does it for me.

"I've got a thing in my head, and they can't get it out, and they can't make it smaller. They can make it easier. They're going to, and I'm going to let them, and I'm going to take their pills like a good woman, morning and night.

"I'm not having a number. There's a number in that little man's folder and it can stay there. Nobody in this room is to go and ask him for it behind my back, and if I find out anybody has, I'll be extremely rude, and I've been practising.

"And I'm not living in a ward. I'm going to live in a hut, with a stove, with my daughter, and with whichever of you will have me. And I'm going to walk in here every morning to be given pills, like somebody going to buy bread." She stopped and got her breath. Some of the words had come slower than others. None of them had come wrong. "That's it. That's all of it. I'd like nobody to say it for me again."

Nobody said anything.

Otto was crying. He was doing it without any sound at all, sitting very straight in his chair with his stitched arm in Callie's old shirt and the tears running down his face, and he was not wiping them.

Pell had the atlas on his knee. He did not open it. After a while he put his hand flat on the cover and left it there.

"Not everything goes in the book," he said quietly, to nobody.

"No," said Callie's mother. "Not everything."

---

Callie went out.

She did not decide to. She got up out of the chair and went down the ward and past the woman at the desk and out through the doors, and she did not stop walking until she was round the back of the long building, where there was nobody. There was a strip of wet grass between the wall and a drain, and a stack of old crates, and the grey sky.

She sat down on the crates. She put her arms round her ribs and her head down on her knees.

She did not cry. She wanted to and she could not. It was down in her somewhere too far to reach, the way the well had been on the plain when it was dry to the stone. She sat on the crates with her head on her knees and her eyes shut, and in the dark behind her eyes she was counting. She did not know what she was counting. She could not stop.

Katori found her there.

She did not say anything. She sat down on the crate next to Callie, a little apart, with her knees up and her cap pulled down, the way she had sat down on the gravel behind the pump house at dusk. She waited.

"She won't let me have it," said Callie, into her knees.

"No."

"It's the only thing I can do. It's the only thing I've got. I count. I put it on my card. It's the one thing I know how to do for her, and she won't let me."

"What would you do with it?" said Katori.

Callie lifted her head.

"The number," said Katori. "Say you had it. Say he'd told you, and it was — whatever it was. What would you do with it?"

Callie opened her mouth. She shut it.

"Count," she said.

"Yes," said Katori. "That's why."

---

They sat on the crates for a long time.

"On the hill," said Callie at last. Her voice was very small. "I gripped the hook. The first pull. I couldn't stop. I knew it was draining me and I couldn't open my fingers, because the whole wagon was on it. And it went. It went out in twenty-six. And then she said, *don't look at it. Look at me.*" She stopped. "And I didn't look at it. And it held. Ninety-one."

Katori said nothing.

"I told a boy that. Yesterday. On a bench. That you can stop holding a thing and it goes on being held. I told it to him like I knew it." Callie looked at her hands. "I don't know it. I only know it about a hook."

"Nobody knows it about anything else," said Katori. "Not the first time." She was looking at the drain. "You found it out about a hook in the middle of a hill with a wagon on it. It took you one pull. I've been trying to find it out about something else for eleven years, and I haven't got it yet." She was quiet. "Your mother's not a hook."

"I know."

"She's not asking you to stop holding her. She's asking you to stop looking at the number." Katori turned her head. "That's not the same thing. You can hold on to her with both hands for as long as there is. You just can't count it."

Callie sat with that. She sat with it for a long time, the way you sit with a thing that is too heavy to pick up yet, and you know you are going to have to, and you are not ready.

"I don't know how," she said.

"No," said Katori. "Neither did I, with the ward. You'll find out. You'll find out badly, and slowly, and you'll hate it for a long time." She stood up. "Come on. Your mother's going to want to know you haven't run off to the plain."

---

In the middle of the day Callie went to the window.

She had not planned to. She found herself in the reception yard, in front of the hatch, with Ivor Hallet behind it writing in his book. He looked up and saw her, and saw her face, and put the pen down.

"Miss," he said.

"My mother's care is clinical," said Callie. "The doctor said. It went through at the gate."

"It did."

"She's going to live in the hut. Fourteen. And come in for pills." Callie held on to the ledge. "What does it take? To stay in the hut. After fourteen days."

Hallet looked at her for a long moment through the glass. Then he told her.

He told her plainly and in order, the way he told everything. There were two gates at Meridian and they were kept apart on purpose. There was medicine, and there was residence. Medicine was not withheld from anybody, and it never had been, and the station was telling the truth when it said so. Residence was the other gate. Residence was reviewed. And a household was reviewed on what the station reckoned it contributed.

"Not on what it needs," said Callie.

"Not on what it needs," said Ivor Hallet. "I'm not going to dress that up for you."

"What do we contribute?"

Hallet looked down at his book. He turned back some pages, to where her name was.

"On paper," he said. "Three Handfuls, lifted, less station. A sponsor line in a name that came in heavy." He looked up. "And you, miss. A red thread. And a card that's gone upstairs."

"The man in the long coat."

Hallet's face did not change very much. But it changed.

"There's work in the north yard for red threads," he said. "All kinds. Most of it's ordinary and it's paid ordinary and it counts ordinary on a review. And there's one kind that counts heavier than the rest." He picked up the pen and did not write with it. "I'd rather you heard about that one from somebody who's doing it than from me."

"Who?"

"I don't give out names through this window, miss." He put the pen down again. "But you're in Yard C. You've got neighbours. And there's one of them who's signed for it, and who'll tell you the truth about it if you ask, because he doesn't seem able to tell anybody anything else."

---

Her mother already knew. Of course she did.

"Senna told me," said her mother, when Callie came back to the ward in the afternoon. "While you were at the window. Don't look like that. There was nobody else to tell me, and she wanted to, and I wanted to know." She was sitting up with a cup of tea in her left hand, and across the ward Senna was sitting up with a cup of tea in both of hers, and they had plainly been talking for some time.

"The man in eleven," said Senna. "The one who can't sit down and stand up in one evening. He's signed for four seasons on the worst work in the north yard. It's lifting ash off a stretch of road where things come through, and you have to be able to hold the road shut the whole time while people behind you sack it. Hardly anybody here can do that. So they pay it like nobody can." She looked at her tea. "He signed for my address. Not my medicine. My medicine was never the station's to sell. My address. So that I can live inside a mile of the shed."

"And you let him," said Callie's mother.

"I let him." Senna did not look up. "It's the hardest thing I've done, and I've done hard things. I came into this station on a board, and a man with a hole in his back stood up in a hut and told three children and me that he'd put his name to four years of the worst work there is, and I sat in a chair and said *I'll need somebody to push this on Tuesdays*." She drank. "Because he'd decided. And because it was his to decide. And because if I'd said no, he'd have done it anyway, and then he'd have done it without me saying yes."

Callie's mother was quiet for a long time.

"I won't let her," she said.

"No," said Senna. "You won't." She looked across at Callie at last, with those pale eyes. "Yours is thirteen. Mine was a grown man who'd already decided. That's a different sum. I'd not let a thirteen-year-old do it either, and he'd not let me let him."

---

"You don't get to spend yourself on me," said her mother.

Callie was standing at the foot of the bed. She could not sit down.

"Not seasons. Not for an address. Not for a window." Her mother's voice was very steady. "I told you at the bottom of the scarp you don't get to spend me. It goes both ways. I didn't say that part then, because I didn't know I'd need to. I'm saying it now."

"Then where do you live?" Callie heard her own voice crack and did not care. "After fourteen days. If there's nothing on the paper. If I don't — "

"I don't know," said her mother.

"You have to know."

"I don't have to know anything." Her mother put the tea down on the table by the bed, carefully, looking at it. "I've got fourteen days. I've got thirteen of them before the fourteenth. I'm not going to spend thirteen days on the fourteenth." She looked up. "When it's the fourteenth, I'll find out. Maybe there'll be something. Maybe Katori's line's bigger than the man thinks. Maybe they'll give a sick woman a hut for nothing because it's winter and nobody comes in in the autumn." Her mouth moved. "Maybe I'll sleep in the ward after all and be rude to the nurses. I don't know. And neither do you. And you're going to have to let neither of us know, for thirteen days, which you're going to find very hard, because you've never not known anything in your life."

Callie stood at the foot of the bed.

"You promised me," said her mother. "On the plain. You don't sign one thing for me without me."

"I know."

"This is me," said her mother. "Saying don't."

---

At the fourth hour a boy came down the row.

Callie saw him from the step of hut fourteen, where she had gone to sit because she could not be inside. He was about ten, in a coat too big for him, and he had a buff card in his hand the size of a hand. He went past fourteen and past twelve and stopped at the door of eleven, and knocked. When it opened he held out the card and said something, and then he stood there on the step, waiting.

Somebody inside took the card. The boy waited a moment longer. Then somebody said something to him, and he nodded and went away up the row again, the way he had come.

The door of eleven stayed open. Callie could hear voices inside, low. One of them was the dark girl's, Dessa's, flat and hard and very clear, saying something about the card. She could not hear what. She did not try to.

She did not know what the card was. She only knew that it was buff, and the size of a hand, and that the boy had waited to be told there was no reply. And that she had seen a card like that before, in a bin at a gate, with her own writing on it.

---

The man in eleven came out at dusk and sat on the step.

He did it in three movements. Callie watched him. He put one hand on the door frame and lowered himself, and stopped, and lowered himself again, and stopped, and sat. He sat with his coat over his knees and his back very straight, as if bending it were a thing he had decided not to do for a while. He was not old. He was not young either. He had a long plain face, not much hair, and the look of somebody who had been ill for some time and was being very patient about it.

He turned his head and looked at her, two doors down, sitting on her own step.

"You're the girl who reads ground," he said.

"Senna's been talking."

"Senna talks about everything. It's the only thing she's got left that nobody can take off her, and she's making the most of it." He looked at her for a moment longer. "And Toren. Toren said you read the south pasture off the grass. The whole of it. From the road."

"It's what I do."

"So I gather." He was quiet. "I'm Rook."

"Callie."

"I know," said Rook. "Everybody in Yard C knows. You and your mother came through that gate yesterday morning, and she walked, and you gave the window a card, and the window sent it upstairs." He looked at the lamp at the end of the row, which was not lit yet. "Which is about as fast as I've ever heard of anybody getting famous in this place, and I got famous here very fast myself."

---

"You signed," said Callie.

"I did."

"For four seasons."

"For four seasons. On the Sag line. It's a stretch of road at the edge of the north yard that never settled, and things come through there more than anywhere else near here." He did not look at her. "You hold it shut for a shift. The whole shift. And people come behind you and sack up what comes through, before it scatters or something else comes for it. And if you let go for three seconds, there's something between you and the people behind you." He said it flatly, like Pell reading a distance. "I haven't started. I start when I can stand up straight. Which is about a fortnight on the wrong side of when they'd like me."

"What's it like?"

"I don't know yet. I know what it pays. It pays like a joke. And the station knows it pays like a joke, and I know, and it's still the only line I had to put on a review." He turned his head and looked at her properly. "Your mother's told you not to."

"How do you know?"

"Because Senna's told me your mother's told you not to. And because I'd have told you not to, if you'd asked me first, and I'm glad I don't have to." He looked back at the lamp. "Don't sign anything because I did. That's all. I'm not a reason. I'm a man with a hole in his back who made a choice about somebody who'd already been carried three hundred miles. It was mine. It won't be yours."

"She says the same."

"She's right," said Rook.

"I know," said Callie. "Everybody keeps telling me she's right. It doesn't help."

"No," Rook agreed. "It never has. Not once in the whole history of anybody."

---

"What did you put on your card?" he said, after a while.

Callie looked at him.

"You don't have to say," said Rook. "I'm only asking because Hallet looked at me through that window a week ago with the exact face I imagine he looked at you with, and said he'd have to have somebody else look at it. And I wondered what it takes."

"*I read ground*," said Callie. "That's the first line. I told him to read them that first."

Rook made a sound. It took her a moment to understand it was a laugh, and that laughing hurt him, and that he was doing it anyway.

"That's the first honest first line that window's had in a month," he said. "Mine had a great deal in it, and none of it was that." He got his breath back. "A man came?"

"Last night. From the yard office. In a long coat. He wants me to go to the north yard when I'm not dry and show them."

"He'll have said you can say no."

"He said most people don't."

"Most people," said Rook, "haven't been told they can by somebody who isn't asking." He looked at her. "You have now. That's all. I'm not telling you to say no. I'm telling you it's allowed. It's a different thing, and people get them muddled."

Callie sat on her step with her arms round her knees.

He was turning something over in his fingers.

She had not noticed at first, because he did it the way you breathe, without looking. It was small and flat and grey, a slab of stone about the size of the palm of a small hand, rounded at the corners. He turned it over, and over, between his thumb and his first finger, the way a man turns a coin he has carried so long he has forgotten he is carrying it. He held it oddly. Not wrong exactly. As if his hand were too big for it, and had learned to go round it.

She had seen somebody do that before. Somebody sitting on a rock at the bottom of a scarp, turning a flat grey slab over and over in her fingers while Callie tried to walk in a ward.

Katori's was bigger.

Down the row, a girl with a bad limp came out of the dusk pushing a chair on wheels, with an old woman in it under a blanket, and a big boy walking beside it complaining. Rook looked up and saw them coming, and put the little grey stone away in his coat, and got himself up off the step in three movements with his hand on the door frame.

"That's the rest of my evening," he said. "Goodnight, Callie."

"Goodnight."

The lamp at the end of the row came on, flat and white and steady. Callie sat on her step and watched the chair go in at the door of eleven, and the door shut. She sat there a long time after, looking at the door, and thinking about a small grey stone that was too small for the hand that held it.
