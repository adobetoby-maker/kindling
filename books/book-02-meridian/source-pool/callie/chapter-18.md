# Chapter Eighteen

They gave her the hook back at the hatch in the morning, and it was clean.

Callie stood at the window in the reception yard and held it in both hands and did not know what to do with it. The grey coats had washed it. They had washed it the way they had washed the party in the road, all over, with warm water and something that smelled sharp. The black smear of old husk down the inside of the curve was gone. It had been there since the culvert on the first night, and since long before that, since the hayfield. It had been there so long she had stopped seeing it. Now it was gone and the steel underneath was grey and bright, and the edge she had put on it with a wet stone at Gaunt's Reach shone along the inside of the curve like a line of water.

The tag was still on the handle. It had her name on it in somebody's neat hand, and a number.

"It's yours," said the man at the hatch. It was not Hallet. It was a younger man with a cough. "Sign there."

She signed. She took the tag off the handle and put it in her coat, and she did not know why she kept it.

---

The board was in the yard office, on the wall of a long room with a queue of nobody in it. It was a sheet of paper as big as a door, ruled in columns, with names down the side in a clerk's hand and numbers across.

Katori's name had the biggest number on it that Callie had ever seen written next to a person. Pell stood in front of it for a long time with his hands in his pockets.

"Well," he said at last. "That's a winter."

"That's a winter for five," said Katori. She did not sound pleased. She sounded like somebody reading a distance. "With the stable fee, and the huts, and whatever they'll want for the horse. And nothing for the road back."

"There's nothing for the road back anyway," said Pell. "The man at the stable says nobody goes out on the high road after the first frost. He says it like a law."

"Is it a law?"

"He didn't say it was a law. He said it like one." Pell looked at the board. "I asked about a team, for the bridge. He laughed. It wasn't unkind. He just laughed."

Katori said nothing. Callie watched her look at the number beside her name, and knew she was thinking about eleven crates under a net under a concrete pier, a long way south on a plain with hounds on it.

Callie's own name was lower down. There was her tenth of the four hound Flasks, which had been more than she expected. And there were three Handfuls, lifted, clean, in her mother's name and hers. Beside that there was a small number in another column, marked *less station*, and beside that a smaller number that was what was left.

"That's the jar," she said.

"That's the jar," said Pell. "Less what they take for weighing it. They always take something for weighing it."

Callie looked at it. Four hundred miles, and a spring, and a culvert on the first night. It was not a number that would fill a column. It was hardly a number at all.

"It's in the book," said Pell, beside her. "Theirs. And mine. It's not nothing."

"It's nearly nothing."

"Nearly nothing's a different thing from nothing," said Pell. "I've been saying that to Otto for three years as well."

---

Visiting at the long building was in the afternoon. Callie went in the morning anyway.

The long building was at the north end of the station, past the main street with its three lamps on poles, all dark now in the grey daylight. It was long and low like everything else here, with a turf roof and small deep windows, and it went on for a long way. There were doors at one end, and a woman at a desk inside them.

"Visiting's in the afternoons," the woman said. She said it without any malice whatever. "Your mother came in yesterday morning. She's had a good night. Doctor Sowerby saw her last thing, and he'll see her again, and there's a picture being taken today. He'll speak to the family tomorrow."

"What picture?"

"Of the inside of her head," said the woman. "It's a machine. It doesn't hurt. It's loud." She looked at Callie. "You can wait outside if you want. There's a bench."

So Callie went out and sat on the bench.

It was against the long wall of the building in a strip of pale sun. She sat on it with the hook across her knees and her left arm against her ribs and looked at the yard in front of her, which was poured concrete, with a drain.

She had not been sitting there long when somebody came along the wall and stopped.

It was the boy from the step. He was taller than she had thought in the dark, about her height, with his left arm in a sling across his chest and his right hand wrapped in white, and he stood a little crooked, as if the back of one leg did not like being straightened. He looked at her, and at the bench, and at the hook across her knees.

"They told you it's afternoons," he said.

"Yes."

"They told me that the first night. I sat out here till the first hour." He looked at the bench. "Can I — "

She moved the hook. He sat down on the other end of the bench, slowly, in two movements, the way somebody sits who has learned where everything hurts.

"Toren," he said.

"Callie."

"You're in fourteen."

"You're in eleven."

That seemed to be all either of them had. They sat on the bench in the strip of sun and looked at the drain.

---

"You read that off the grass," said Toren, after a while. "Last night. The mule. You knew it was a mule from the size of the — "

"From the size of the mound." Callie looked at the drain. "And I read the other thing too. Before that. On the big road."

He went very still.

She had not meant to say it. She said it anyway, because it was the thing between them on the bench and she did not know how to sit beside it and not say it.

"Something came across the pasture from the east," she said. "Heavy. Three legs to a stride, not four. Wide, the way a thing stands that's made for pushing. And a fourth one that wasn't a leg. It cut the grass off level, like mowing, and it cut a line in the turf a man long, clean as a knife through a loaf." She kept her eyes on the drain. "It hit the rail on the east side and bent it. It went on up the grass to the ditch. And somebody was in the ditch. Under it. There's a hollow at the edge the size of somebody lying down." She stopped. "And it's dead, and it's been lifted. Somebody killed it."

Toren did not say anything for a long time.

"You got all of that," he said at last, "off *grass*."

"It's what I do. It's the only thing I'm good at." She turned the hook over on her knees. "I'm not asking who. I'm not asking anything. I just read it, and you were sitting here, and I didn't want to sit here knowing it and you not knowing I knew."

He looked down at his strapped hand.

"Yes," he said. That was all.

She did not ask. She knew about not asking. Katori had taught her, and Pell had taught her, and her mother had been teaching her since she was four. She sat on the bench and let the *yes* be the whole of it, and after a while she felt him let his breath out beside her, very slowly, as if he had been holding it since the gate.

---

He put his right hand in his coat pocket. Then he took it out again.

He did it the way you do a thing you have done so many times that your hand has stopped asking you. It went into the pocket and stopped there, and then, as if it had remembered something, it came out again and lay on his knee, and he looked at the drain.

Callie watched him do it. She knew that movement. She knew it in her own hands.

"I did that," she said. "With my hook. On a hill. I had my hand on it and I kept looking at it, to see if it was still there. And every time I looked, it went."

Toren turned his head and looked at her.

"Somebody told me that," he said slowly. "On a towpath. That checking on it's what puts it out."

"Somebody told me something like it. After." Callie looked at her own hands on the hook. "She said you can stop holding a thing, and it goes on being held. She said she didn't know that. She said she'd been holding things very hard for a long time."

He was quiet.

"It's out," he said. It came out of him low and flat, the way a thing comes out that has been sitting in a person for a long time with nobody to say it to. "Mine. My Ember. It's been out since the ditch. It's a stone. I don't know when it's coming back."

Callie thought about the pump house, and the gravel behind it at dusk, and Katori sitting down beside her.

"It's not gone," she said. "It's empty. Those are different."

"How do you know?"

"Somebody told me. When mine was." She looked at him. "I'm dry now. Nearly dry. I did too much on the plain. And it's coming back, slowly, on its own. I can feel it at the bottom. You can't use it for anything. You just know it's there." She thought about it. "Hers was nine days, once. The second time she emptied herself. She said she was sure it was gone. Absolutely sure."

Toren looked at his coat pocket for a long moment. He did not put his hand in it.

"Nine days," he said.

"That was her. I don't know about you. I don't know anything about yours." She stood up, slowly, because the door of the long building had opened and the woman from the desk was looking out at them. "I'm only telling you what somebody told me. Because it helped."

---

The ward was a long room with beds down both sides and a window over every bed, and it smelled of soap and something sharp, and there was a light in the ceiling that did not flicker.

Her mother was in the fourth bed on the left, under the window.

She was lying on her back in a white bed with white sheets folded down over a grey blanket, and her hair was loose on the pillow, out of Ines's braid. Somebody had washed it. Somebody had put her in a white gown with strings at the back. She looked smaller in the bed than she had ever looked on the wagon, and cleaner, and much more tired, and when Callie came down the ward she turned her head on the pillow and looked at her and her whole face changed.

"There you are," she said.

"Here I am."

"They washed my hair." Her mother's voice was thick and slow, the way it had been the morning after the hill. The good day was spent. It had gone out of her some time in the night, in this bed. "They washed all of me. They put a needle in my arm and a bag on a hook. And they asked me my name eleven times, and what year it was, and who was in charge of the station. I didn't know who was in charge of the station. I told them I'd find out and let them know."

"Did they laugh?"

"One of them did. The young one." Her mother's left hand moved on the blanket, and Callie sat down on the chair by the bed and took it. "The doctor came last night. Small man. Bald. In a hurry. He said he'd do it in order, the bad thing first, and then he didn't do anything at all, because he said he didn't know what the bad thing was yet. He said he'd need a picture." She looked at the ceiling. "I liked him. I liked him for not guessing."

"They're taking the picture today."

"So I'm told. Of the inside of my head." Her mother's mouth moved. "I'd be interested to see it. I've been wondering what's going on in there since the spring."

---

"You're the daughter," said a voice from across the ward.

Callie turned.

In the bed opposite, under the window on the other side, there was an old woman sitting up against her pillows. She was very thin and very straight, with white hair cut short and a long face that had been out in a great deal of weather. She had a tray across her lap with a cup on it and a plate, and on the plate there was a piece of bread. She was looking at Callie with a pair of pale eyes that did not seem to miss anything.

"Your mother walked in," the old woman said. "Through the south gate. On her own feet, with the gate nurse at her elbow. They were talking about it at the desk this morning, which they never do. Nobody walks in. They come in on a board." She looked at Callie's mother. "I came in on a board. I'd have walked if I could. I'd like that noted."

"It's noted," said Callie's mother, from her pillow, without opening her eyes.

"Senna," said the old woman. "I'd get up, but I'm enjoying myself."

Callie's mother opened her eyes.

She turned her head on the pillow and looked across the ward at the old woman in the opposite bed, for a long moment. Callie watched something go across her mother's face that she had not seen there since Thistle Ford. It was not a smile. It was the look her mother got when she met somebody at a wall or a market who was going to be worth talking to.

"Tilda," said her mother. "I said that on the first morning. To a stranger. By a fire."

"Did you."

"Almost exactly."

"Then we've got that settled," said Senna, "and we can get on to the interesting part."

---

The interesting part, it turned out, was the bread.

Senna picked it up. She did it slowly, looking at her own hand the whole time. She watched her fingers go round the bread the way you watch somebody else's fingers do a thing you have told them to do. When she had it, she lifted it, still looking, and took a bite, and put it down again, and let go of it, looking.

Callie's mother watched her do it.

"Your hands," said her mother.

"They've started going," said Senna. She said it the way Pell said a number. "It's the treatment. It takes the hands and the feet, and it doesn't give them back. It's started early. I can feel the weight of a thing. I can't feel the thing." She looked across at Callie's mother's right hand, lying on the blanket the way a glove lies on a table. "And yours?"

"It's stopped taking instruction. Since the winter."

"Can you feel it?"

"I can feel it. I just can't tell it anything." Callie's mother looked at her own hand. "It's like shouting at somebody in the next field."

Senna was quiet for a moment. Then something happened to her long weathered face that made her look about thirty years younger.

"Well," she said. "Which of us is going to cut the other's bread?"

"Neither," said Callie's mother at once. "We'll look at it."

Senna laughed. It was a short dry sound like a stick breaking. "I've been saying that to four people and a man for a week," she said, "and not one of them has laughed."

"They're frightened of you," said Callie's mother.

"They are. I've worked very hard at it."

"I'll need to know how you do it," said Callie's mother. "I've got five of my own and none of them are frightened of me at all. The horse is. The horse is the only one."

---

Toren came in at the start of the afternoon, when the woman at the desk let the visitors through. He came down the ward and stopped at the foot of Senna's bed, and then he saw Callie, sitting by the bed across from it, and stopped again.

Senna looked from one of them to the other. She did not seem to need anything explained to her.

"You two have met," she said.

"On a step," said Toren.

"On a step," Senna agreed, as if this were the only proper place for people to meet. "Tilda, this is my grandson. He is thirteen and he has a broken arm, and I'm going to live, and he's not going to cry about it in front of anybody, are you, Tor."

"No, Gran."

"Good." Senna turned her head on the pillow. "Tilda has a daughter who reads ground. That's what she said at the desk, apparently. The girl who came in with her. She reads ground. I thought you'd want to know that before she read yours."

"She already did," said Toren.

Senna looked at him. Then she looked at Callie, for a long time, with those pale eyes that did not miss anything.

"Did she," she said.

---

They came for Callie's mother in the middle of the afternoon, two people in white with a bed on wheels.

"The picture," one of them said. "It takes about forty minutes. She has to lie very still. You can come as far as the line."

Callie did not know what the line was until she got to it. She walked beside the rolling bed, holding her mother's left hand, down the ward and out through a door and along a corridor that went down, a long slope under the building, where the air was cooler and the walls were thick and grey and there were no windows at all. At the bottom of the slope there was a heavy door, wider than the gate in the reception yard, and in front of the door there was a line painted across the floor. It was bright yellow, as wide as her hand.

On the wall beside it there was a sign.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

Callie looked down at the hook in her other hand.

"It's the machine," said the one in white, seeing her look. "It doesn't like metal. It pulls. Anything iron that goes in that room, it'll have it off you and across the floor at it, and it won't ask." He looked at her mother. "Anything iron on you, ma'am? Pins? Rings?"

"Nothing," said her mother. "I sold the rings at the Reach."

He looked at Callie. "You'll have to wait here, miss. With that."

"I know."

Her mother's hand tightened on hers. It was the left one. It could still tighten.

"Forty minutes," said her mother. "I've lain still on a wagon for a month. I'll lie still in a room for forty minutes."

"I know."

"You'll be here."

"I'll be here."

They took her through the heavy door, and it closed behind her with a sound like a vault, and Callie stood at the yellow line with her grandfather's hook in her hand and nothing on the other side of the door that she could see or hear or reach.

There was a bench against the wall. She sat on it. She laid the hook across her knees.

Then, from somewhere behind the thick grey wall, very far off, the knocking started.

---

It was a loud sound, and a strange one. It came through the wall in long runs, like somebody hammering on a pipe in another building, fast and steady and mechanical. It stopped, and started again, faster, and stopped. It went on and on. It did not sound like anything that could look at the inside of a person.

Callie sat on the bench and listened to it. She could not see her mother. She could not hear her. She could not count her breaths through a wall as thick as a bank.

She held the hook.

She held it harder. She did not mean to. Her fingers closed round the conduit, the way they had closed round the curve at the top of the pitch, and she sat there gripping four feet of steel pipe as if it could do anything at all, as if holding it tight enough would put her on the other side of the door.

"There's somebody in there by herself," said a voice. "She's lying very still."

Callie looked up.

---

A girl was standing in the corridor at the top of the slope, pushing a chair with wheels on it. There was an old woman in the chair with a blanket over her knees. It was Senna.

The girl was about fifteen, dark, with a hard straight look to her. She stood with her weight all on one leg, the way somebody stands who has learned not to trust the other one. She was looking not at Callie but at the heavy door, with her head a little on one side.

"Dessa," said Senna, from the chair. "Don't frighten the child."

"I'm not frightening her. I'm telling her." The girl looked at Callie at last. "She's about twenty feet in. On her back. She hasn't moved. She's lying very still." She paused. "Is that your mother?"

"How do you — "

"I know where people are. About thirty feet, all round, without looking. It's not a trick." The girl said it flatly, like a thing she had said many times and was tired of having to say. "It doesn't cost me anything. It's just there." She looked at the door again. "She still hasn't moved. I can tell you if she does."

Callie sat on the bench with the hook across her knees and looked at her.

Her fingers had come open on the conduit. She had not told them to.

"Would you?" she said.

---

The girl came down the slope, pushing the chair, and stopped it beside the bench. She did it carefully. Callie could see the bad leg taking the weight of the slope and not liking it. Then she sat down on the other end of the bench, and stretched the leg out straight in front of her, and looked at the door.

"Dessa Cole," she said.

"Callie."

"I know. Senna told me. You read ground." Dessa did not look away from the door. "She's still not moving. She's very good at it."

"She's been practising," said Callie. "On a wagon. For a month."

"It shows."

Senna, in the chair, had shut her eyes. "We were on our way back from the shed," she said, without opening them. "They do things to me in the shed, and they did them today, and Dessa came to push me back, and then she stopped at the top of the slope and said *somebody's down there by herself*. And I said, Dessa, it's a hospital, everybody's somewhere by themselves. And she came down anyway." She opened her eyes. "Dessa Cole, sit up straight, you're making the corridor nervous."

"I'm sitting how I sit."

"You're sitting like somebody who counted ten kinds of paper between the hut and the ward."

"Eleven," said Dessa. "There's a new one on the ward door. It's green."

Callie looked at her.

"You count," she said.

Dessa turned her head.

"I count too," said Callie. "Everything. Husks. Seconds. The days to Meridian. The ash in the jar. My mother's breathing." She stopped. "I put it on my card. At the bottom. *I count.* I don't know why."

Dessa looked at her for a long time with that hard straight look.

"Good," she said at last. "Somebody in this place should." She turned back to the door. "Still hasn't moved. The knocking's stopped. Somebody's walking in there now. Two people. They're going to her."

The heavy door opened.

---

Her mother came out on the rolling bed with her eyes shut and her hair every which way on the pillow, and opened her eyes when they crossed the yellow line, and found Callie.

"It knocked," she said. Her voice was very tired. "Like a man mending a roof inside my head. I didn't care for it."

"You stayed still."

"I'm very good at staying still." Her mother turned her head and saw the chair, and the old woman in it, and the dark girl on the bench with her leg stuck out. "Oh. We've got company."

"You didn't move once," said Dessa. "The whole forty minutes. I was counting."

Callie's mother looked at her.

"I know," she said. "I could tell there was somebody counting." She shut her eyes again. "There usually is."

---

Doctor Sowerby would speak to the family in the morning. The woman at the desk told them so when they brought Callie's mother back up to the ward, and she said it the way you'd say a train was coming.

"At the ninth hour," said Callie's mother, from the pillow.

"At the ninth hour."

"I'd like chairs," said her mother. "Enough for all of them. There's five. And me."

The woman at the desk wrote it down.

When visiting was over, Callie stood by the bed and did not want to go. Her mother lay with her eyes shut and her left hand on the blanket. The bag on its hook dripped. Across the ward Senna was sitting up looking at a new piece of bread, and Toren was gone.

"Callie," said her mother, without opening her eyes.

"Yes."

"Tomorrow. Whatever he says." Her mother's hand moved on the blanket and found Callie's wrist. "You'll let me hear it first. Not you first and then you tell me, the way you tell Pell a road. Me first. It's my head."

"I promise."

"You've promised me a lot of things this week."

"I've kept all of them."

"You have," said her mother, and let go of her wrist. "Go on. Go and eat something. And somebody tell Pell to stop reading his book in the corridor. They've asked him twice."

---

The man in the long coat came to the door of hut fourteen at dusk.

Callie knew him at once. He was the man who had come up onto the top of the bank at fourteen minutes and looked down at all of them, and at her longest. He was tall and thin, about fifty, with a close-cut grey beard and a quiet face, and he had nothing in his hands but a folded paper. He stood on the step and knocked, and when Otto opened the door he asked for Miss Callie, by her whole name, the one off the scale-house door.

Callie came to the door. Katori came and stood behind her without being asked. So did Pell.

"I'm from the yard office," the man said. He did not give his name. "I've read your card."

"All of it?"

"All of it." He looked at her. "Starting with the first line. Mr Hallet said you'd asked him to tell whoever read it to start there. So I did." He paused. "*I read ground.* It's a good first line."

"It's the true one."

"I think they're all true," said the man. "That's why it came to me." He held up the folded paper, but did not give it to her. "We'd like you to come to the north yard. Not now. When you're not dry. You say on your card you're dry at present, and I believe you. When you've got something back in you. We'd like to see."

"See what?"

"What it says on the card." His face did not change. "The hook. The thing you can put in it. Nobody here's seen that, miss. Nobody I've ever met has seen that. We'd like to understand what we've got on the register."

Callie stood in the doorway. She felt Katori behind her not moving at all.

"My mother's got a doctor at the ninth hour tomorrow," she said. "I'm not doing anything till after that. Not anything."

"No," the man agreed at once. "Of course not." He put the folded paper back in his coat. "There's no hurry. Nobody's asking you to do anything tomorrow, or the day after. It's only a request. You can say no to it." He looked at her for a moment longer. "Most people don't."

He nodded to Katori, and to Pell, and went away down the row in the dusk, past the door of hut eleven, where a boy with an iron bar was sitting on the step turning it over in his left hand and did not look up.

Callie stood in the doorway until he was gone.

"Most people don't," said Pell quietly, behind her.

"I heard."

"That's the sort of thing," said Pell, "that I'd want a column for."

Callie went back in and sat down on the floor by the stove, with her grandfather's clean hook across her knees, and went down into herself to look. There was a finger and a half of water at the bottom of the well, cold and clear. It had come back on its own, slowly, since the plain. It would go on coming back whether anybody wanted to see it or not.

She did not touch it. There was nothing here to use it on.
