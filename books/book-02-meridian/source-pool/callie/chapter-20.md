# Chapter Twenty

Her mother woke on the last morning in the ward without a headache, and lay very still for a long time, as if she did not trust it.

"It's gone," she said, when Callie came down the ward at the eighth hour. She was sitting up against the pillows with her eyes very wide. "The head. The one behind the eye. I woke up and I waited for it, the way you wait for somebody who's always late. And it didn't come." She put her left hand up and touched her own temple, carefully, as if it might be somebody else's. "I haven't woken up without it since before Gaunt's Reach."

"It's the pills," said Callie.

"I know it's the pills. The little man said it would be. I'm not stupid." Her mother looked at the window. "I just didn't believe him. I thought he was being kind." She was quiet a moment. "He isn't kind. He's something better. He's right."

Her right hand still lay on the blanket like a glove. Her right leg still would not hold her. When the nurse came to help her dress, it took both of them and Callie, and her mother said a word that Callie had not known she knew. But she said it clearly, and it was the right word, and she laughed after.

Across the ward, Senna was watching over the top of her cup.

"You're going, then," said Senna.

"I'm going. I'll be back at the eighth hour tomorrow for my pills, like a woman going to buy bread."

"I'm here every morning," said Senna. "And every night, and every afternoon, and all the bits between. I've a great deal of time and nobody to be rude to properly."

"I'll come and be rude to you."

"Good," said Senna, and looked back down at her cup. "That's settled."

---

They took her home in the barrow.

It was Otto's idea. He had it the night before and would not let it go. He had gone to the south stable at dawn and got the barrow down off the wagon bed, where it had been lashed with its legs in the air since the high plain, and wheeled it the whole length of the station on its two taped bicycle wheels. Now it stood outside the doors of the long building with a folded blanket on the boards and the two lengths of cargo strapping lying across the handles.

Callie looked at it for a long time.

"I can't pull it," she said.

"No," said Otto. "I can."

He backed into it the way she had backed into it every morning of the spring, and lifted the strapping, and brought it over his shoulders. He crossed it over his chest, over her old grey shirt with his stitched arm in it, and reached round behind him for the buckle. He could not reach it one-handed. Callie did it for him. It clicked.

He took the handles, one of them in his good hand, the other one braced against his hip.

"There," he said. He went red. "How's that?"

"The left strap's twisted."

He looked down. It was. She untwisted it. He stood there in her yoke, twice the size of her, with the two lengths of webbing lying across his chest where the two dark lines were worn into the shirt, and waited.

They brought her mother out between them, the nurse and Ines, and sat her on the blanket on the boards with her back against the front of the barrow and her legs out. She put her hat on with her left hand and pulled it down.

"Well," she said, looking round from the boards at the concrete yard and the long building and the turf roofs. "This is where I came in."

---

It was four hundred yards from the long building to the second row of Yard C, and Otto pulled it at a walk. He did it carefully, with his tongue between his teeth, the way he had lifted the hound ash. The barrow creaked on its rubber tyres over the poured concrete. Callie walked beside it with her hand on the rim, not holding, only there.

She had pulled that barrow four hundred miles. She had never once seen it from the side.

It looked smaller. That was the first thing. It looked smaller and older and more mended than she had known, with the tape on the rims gone grey and the boot-sole strip on the left tyre worn nearly through. It went along at the pace of a person walking, and her mother rode on it with her hat pulled down, looking at everything.

People looked at them. There were people in the station, going about the business of a place where people lived, and they looked at a very large boy with one arm in a sling pulling a woman in a hat on a two-wheeled barrow, and a girl walking beside it with a hook. Most of them looked away again. One woman with a basket stopped and watched them all the way past.

"She's wondering what I cost," said her mother, from the boards, not quietly.

"Mum."

"I'm only saying. I'd wonder too."

---

At the corner of the row, a big boy was sitting on the step of hut eleven with a short iron bar in his left hand.

He had his right arm strapped across his chest, the same as Otto's, and his shoulder sat oddly under his coat, as if something in it was the wrong shape. He was turning the bar over in his left hand. He dropped it on the step as they came along the row. He picked it up.

He watched Otto come with the barrow. He watched the whole length of it, the strapping and the one-handed grip and the handle braced against the hip.

"You've got it on your hip," he said, when Otto was level with the step. "The other handle. You'll have a bruise the size of a plate by tonight."

Otto stopped. He looked at the big boy on the step.

"I've got one arm," he said.

"So've I," said the big boy. He held up the bar in his left hand, as if that settled it, and then dropped it again, and did not pick it up this time. "Put the strap under the handle instead of over it. It'll take the weight off your hip and put it on your chest. Your chest doesn't mind."

Otto looked down at the strap. He moved it. He took the handle again and walked two steps, and stopped.

"That's better," he said, in surprise.

"I know," said the big boy. He picked up the bar. "I've been carrying things with one arm for longer than I'd like. I've had a lot of time to think about hips."

"Thank you."

"Wyck," said the big boy, not looking up from the bar.

"Otto," said Otto, and went red, and walked on.

---

Toren was sitting on the step next to him, a little apart, with his right hand in his coat pocket.

He did not take it out. Callie saw that as the barrow went past. He was sitting on the step with his back against the door frame, looking at nothing, and his hand was in his pocket, and it stayed there. It did not come out and go back. It stayed where it was, the way a hand stays on something it has decided not to let go of and not to look at.

His face was different. She could not have said how. It was the same face as on the bench two mornings ago, tired and careful. But something in it had come down a little, the way Ines's shoulders came down half an inch when she counted six arrows.

He looked up and saw her. He nodded.

She nodded back. She did not say anything, and neither did he. She did not know what had happened, and she knew she was not going to ask, and she knew somehow that it was not bad.

---

Hut fourteen had four beds and five of them, and now six. Nobody said anything about it. Pell gave his bed up before anybody could stop him. He said he had slept on a wagon board for eleven years and a bed made him nervous, and moved his blanket to the floor by the door, next to Ines.

They put her mother in the bed by the stove. Katori took the blanket off the end of her own bed and hung it over the small deep window, the way Hallet had said to, and did not say anything about pride. The stove ticked. The room was warm.

Her mother lay in a real bed, in a hut with a stove, with a blanket over the window. She lay there looking up at the poured concrete ceiling for a long time.

"There," she said at last. "A ceiling. A real one."

"You called the sky a ceiling on the plain," said Ines.

"I did." Her mother shut her eyes. "I was wrong. This is a ceiling. The sky's the other thing. I'll remember that now."

She was asleep before anybody could answer.

---

They held the meeting round the stove in whispers, so as not to wake her.

"We're staying the winter," said Katori.

Nobody argued. Pell had the atlas open on his knee on the floor by the door. He did not write it down yet.

"The crates stay where they are till the spring," said Katori. "Nobody goes out on the high road after the frost. The man at the stable says so, and I believe him, and I'm not going to be the one who proves him wrong." She said it flatly. "Duchess mends. We find her a way to earn her keep that isn't hills. And we find five of us a winter's work."

"Six," said Callie.

Katori looked at her across the stove.

"Six," Katori agreed. "Pell."

"The ash is a winter," said Pell. "Katori's ash. For all of us. Without the stable, and without whatever they'll want for the horse, and without the review." He looked at the page. "With those, it's most of a winter. And a winter's no good to a review. A review wants to see something coming in."

"The north yard," said Otto.

"Red threads only," said Ines. "That's two of us."

"Not for everything," said Callie. "Hallet said there's all kinds of work. Most of it's ordinary." She stopped. "And there's one kind that counts heavier. He wouldn't tell me what. He said to ask a neighbour."

"The man in eleven," said Pell.

Katori said nothing. She was turning her Ember over in her fingers, the flat grey slab, the way she always did when she was thinking. She had done it on a rock at the bottom of the scarp and on a gravel lay-by and on the tailboard of a wagon a hundred times.

"Then I'll go and talk to the man in eleven," she said.

---

She did not go that afternoon. The man in eleven was not there. The door was shut and the step was empty, and Pell, who had asked at the yard office because Pell asked at everything, said he had gone to the long building to sit with the old woman.

So Katori sat on the step of fourteen instead, at dusk, with her back against the door frame and her cap pulled down, turning the slab over in her fingers. Callie sat in the doorway behind her with her knees up, because her mother was asleep inside and she wanted to be near and she could not sit still in a room.

The lamp at the end of the row came on. It put its flat white circle down on the wet concrete, the colour of bone.

The man in eleven came back along the row slowly, with one hand on the wall. He did not look at them. He got to his own step, two doors down, and put his hand on the door frame, and lowered himself onto it in three movements, and sat.

Then he took something out of his coat and began to turn it over in his fingers.

Callie watched him do it. She watched him the way she watched a ditch. She did not say anything. She had not said anything last night either, or this morning, or all day. It was not hers to say. She knew that the way she had known on the ninth day, at the tailgate before dawn, that she must not ask.

Two doors apart, on two steps, in the edge of the lamplight, a man and a woman sat turning two flat grey stones over and over in their fingers, the same way, without looking.

Katori's hand stopped.

---

Callie saw it stop. She saw Katori's head come up, very slowly, and turn, and look down the row at the man on the step of eleven.

He had the stone flat on his palm, now, the way you'd hold a thing up to the light to see it. The lamp was behind him. The stone was small, smaller than Katori's, water-grey, rounded at the corners. And on its two long edges there were two places worn smooth and a little darker than the rest, where a thumb and a first finger had gone, for years, and years, and years.

His hand was too big for it. It had learned to go round it.

Katori stood up.

She did not say anything. She put her own slab away inside her coat and buttoned the coat over it. Then she walked down the row, past the dark door of twelve, and stopped on the wet concrete a pace in front of the step of eleven, in the edge of the lamplight, with her hands at her sides.

The man looked up.

Callie did not move from the doorway. She could hear. The row was very quiet, and the stove in fourteen ticked, and somewhere down the row somebody shut a door, and then it was quieter still.

Katori was looking at the stone in his hand.

"Where did you get that," she said.

---

He did not close his hand over it. Callie saw that. He did not put it away, or move at all. He sat on his step with the stone flat on his palm and looked up at the small woman standing in front of him, a head shorter than most people, standing the way a fence post stands. He looked at her face for a long time.

Something went across his own face. It was not surprise. It was the look of somebody who has heard a voice in the next room that he had stopped expecting to hear anywhere.

"Off a floor," he said at last. His voice was very quiet. "A long time ago."

Katori said a name.

It was one word. Callie had heard it once before, in the grey before dawn at the tailgate of a wagon on the ninth day, when a woman had stood with her hands on the boards and her head down and said it to nobody. She had not caught the sound of it well enough to keep. She had known that she was never going to hear it again, and that she must not ask.

She knew it now. She knew it the way you know a bird you have only ever heard once, the moment it calls again.

"Satori," said Katori.

The man on the step shut his eyes.

"Yes," he said.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The lamp stood at the end of the row. The stove ticked. Callie sat in the doorway of fourteen with her arms round her knees and did not breathe, the way she had not breathed on the first morning, watching a grey blade come up out of a closed fist.

"She had small hands," said Katori.

"She did." He opened his eyes. "I've had to hold it wrong."

Katori looked at the stone on his palm.

"May I," she said.

He did not answer. He only held his hand out a little further, flat, with the stone on it, the way you hold out something to a horse that you are not sure will take it.

Katori took it.

She took it between her thumb and her first finger. And her thumb went to one of the two worn places on the long edge, and her finger went to the other. They went there by themselves, the way a foot goes into its own print in the mud. The stone lay in her small hand as if it had been cut for it.

The man on the step watched her do it. He did not move at all. He watched the stone sit in a hand that fitted it, for the first time in eleven years, and his face did something that Callie would not have known how to describe, and would not have tried to, and would not ever tell anybody she had seen.

Katori held it. She did not look at it. She held it the way her mother held her wrist on the tailgate, for about three seconds, which was as long as either of them could stand.

---

"She was my sister," said Katori.

"I'm Rook," said the man.

That was all he said, for a while. Then: "She talked about her people. She said they'd kept words. Their own words, for things, that nobody else had." He was looking at the stone in Katori's hand. "She never said she had a sister."

"She wouldn't have," said Katori. "She was the best of us. She'd not have wasted a word on the rest." Her voice did not change at all. "She was unbearable about it."

Rook made a sound. It was not a laugh. It was the place where a laugh would have been.

"She was," he said. "She was completely unbearable."

They were quiet. Then Rook said, very carefully, as if he were putting down something heavy on a floor that might not take it:

"Her people gave the stone and the thing it's for the same name. The same word, on purpose. She told me so. And I've never in eleven years met anybody who could tell me why."

Katori looked at him for a long moment.

"I can tell you why," she said.

He waited.

"Not tonight," said Katori.

"No," said Rook. He let out a long breath. "No. Not tonight."

---

She gave it back.

She did it slowly. She held it out on her own palm, the way he had held it out to her, and he took it, and his hand closed round it the way it had learned to, too big, going round.

"You carried it," said Katori.

"Every day."

"Keep carrying it."

He looked up at her.

"I'm going to want to talk to you," said Katori. "I don't know when. Not soon. I've been holding this very hard for a long time, and I don't know how to put it down yet, and I'm not going to learn tonight on a step." She stood with her hands at her sides. "But I'm going to want to."

"I'm here four seasons," said Rook. "I signed."

Katori made a small sound. It was almost a laugh too.

"So I've heard," she said.

Then she turned and walked away. She did not walk back to fourteen. She walked past it, past Callie in the doorway, without looking at her, and round the end of the hut into the dark between the rows, where the lamp did not reach.

---

Rook sat on his step with the stone in his closed hand.

After a while he looked down the row at Callie, in the doorway of fourteen with her knees up. He did not seem surprised to see her there.

"You heard," he said.

"Yes."

"She'll want somebody," said Rook. "Not me. Not tonight. Somebody who doesn't need anything from her." He looked at the dark where Katori had gone. "Go on. I'll sit here. I'm not going anywhere at speed."

Callie got up.

---

Katori was sitting on the ground behind hut fourteen with her back against the concrete wall, in the dark, with her knees up and her cap pulled down over her eyes.

Callie sat down beside her, a little apart. She did it slowly, the way she did everything now. She put her back against the wall, and her arms round her ribs, and did not say anything.

They sat there for a long time.

"You heard me say it," said Katori at last. She did not lift her cap. "At the tailgate. On the ninth day. Before dawn."

"Yes."

"I thought you had. I saw you sitting against the wheel." Katori was quiet. "You never asked."

"You didn't want me to."

"No," said Katori. "I didn't."

Somewhere on the far side of the hut the stove ticked. Callie could hear her mother breathing through the wall, very faintly, in and out.

"She went away when she was young," said Katori. "To a place that paid for things we needed. And she didn't come back. Nobody ever came to tell us how." She said it the way Pell said a distance, as if it were a thing so flat and true it was almost boring. "Eleven years. That's all. That's the whole of it that I'm going to say tonight."

"All right."

"I've never said even that much to anybody. Not Pell. Not Ines." Katori's hand moved on her knee. "I've been carrying it very hard."

"I know," said Callie. "You said. On the hill."

"I did." Katori was quiet for a moment. "And you said you can stop holding a thing and it goes on being held."

"I said it about a hook."

"You did." Katori lifted her cap at last and looked at the dark. "He's been holding it. For eleven years. Every day. Wrong, because his hand's too big." Her voice did not change. "It fitted, Callie. It went into my hand like it was cut for it."

"I saw."

"I know you saw," said Katori. "I'm glad it was you."

---

They sat a while longer.

"I'm not leaving this station while that man's in it," said Katori. "And I can't say one more sentence to him. Not yet. Maybe not for a long time." She let out a breath. "That's a stupid place to be."

"Then stay till you can," said Callie.

"That's the winter."

"It was the winter anyway. Pell says nobody goes out on the high road after the frost. He says it like a law."

Katori laughed. It was a small tired sound in the dark, and it was the first time Callie had ever heard her do it all the way through.

"He needs lifters," said Katori, after a while. She said it slowly, the way she said things she was working out as she went. "Behind that line of his. When he can stand up straight. That's what the work is. Somebody holds the road shut, and people behind him lift what comes through before it scatters. People who know how. People who don't put grit in it." She was looking at the dark. "I've got a crew that lifts. Ines can count anything. Pell can write it down so nobody at any window can say it isn't so. Otto learned from the best there is, on the high plain, by starlight."

Callie said nothing.

"And the best lifter I've ever seen," said Katori, "is thirteen. And it's her call."

---

"My mother says I don't sign anything," said Callie.

"Lifting's not signing," said Katori. "It's wages. It's a day's work and a day's pay, and you go home at the end of it to a hut with a stove. It's what you did every day since you were eleven." She turned her head. "I'm not asking you to hold any road. I'm not asking you to put anything in that hook for anybody. That's yours, and that man in the long coat can ask for it, and you can tell him what you like."

"He says most people don't say no."

"He does."

"I haven't decided if I'm most people," said Callie.

Katori looked at her in the dark for a long moment.

"No," she said. "You haven't. That's the best thing I've heard said at this station."

Callie sat with her back against the wall and her arms round her ribs, and thought about it. She thought about a stretch of road at the edge of the north yard where things came through, and a man with a hole in his back holding it shut, and people behind him on their knees in the ash, two fingers and the flat of the hand, like cream off milk. She thought about ash lying in a seam, going nowhere, calling. She thought about her mother, asleep on the other side of this wall, who had said *don't*.

"I'll look," she said. "When he can stand up straight. I'll go with you and look at his road. And I'll read it." She stopped. "That's all. I'll read the ground. That's the first line."

"That's the first line," Katori agreed.

---

When Callie went back in, her mother was awake.

She was lying in the bed by the stove with her eyes open, looking at the ceiling, with the blanket over the window and the stove light on her face. She turned her head on the pillow when the door opened.

"Katori?" she said.

"She's all right. She's sitting outside."

"Is she." Her mother looked at her for a long moment. "Something's happened."

"Yes."

"Is it yours to tell?"

Callie thought about it.

"No," she said.

"Then don't," said her mother, and shut her eyes again. "Come and sit down. My feet are cold and there's a girl in this hut who's got warm hands and nothing to do with them."

Callie sat down on the floor by the bed with her back against the frame. She put her hand under the blanket and found her mother's foot, the right one, the one that would not take instruction, and held it. It was cold. She held it until it was not.

The others came in, one by one. Pell to the floor by the door. Ines to the bed nearest it, with her bow. Otto, who looked at Callie's hand under the blanket and went red and said nothing. Katori, last, very quietly, who took her cap off and lay down on her bed with her face to the wall and her coat still buttoned.

The stove ticked down. The room went dark except for the red line of it through the grate.

---

Her mother's breathing changed as she went to sleep. It went slower and deeper, the way it had always gone, on the barrow in the road and on the wagon bed on the plain and in the white bed in the long room with the bag on the hook. In and out.

Callie heard it.

And she began to count. She did not decide to. It began in her the way it had always begun, under everything, the way her heart began. *One.* In and out. *Two.*

She stopped.

She sat on the floor in the dark with her hand round her mother's foot, and she did not count the third one. She let it go past. And the fourth. It was the hardest thing she had ever done. It was harder than the causeway, or the span, or the hill, or taking her hand off a hook with a wagon on it and looking at her mother on the rim instead. Every part of her wanted to count. It was the only thing she knew how to do.

She did not count.

Her mother went on breathing. In and out, slow and deep, in a real bed under a real ceiling, the way she had been breathing before Callie started counting and would go on breathing without her. It did not need her to count it. It had never once needed her to count it.

Callie held on to her foot with both hands, and listened, and let it be held.

Under the edge of the blanket over the window there was a thin line of flat white light from the lamp at the end of the row. She watched it. At the first hour, the way it did every night, the way it had done every night for nine nights on the plain and seventy years before that, it went out.

Callie did not count the seconds after. She went down into herself instead, one last time, the way she always did, to see.

There were two fingers of water in the bottom of the well, cold and clear. She had not drunk anything to put them there. Nobody had. They had come back on their own, slowly, the way Katori had said they would, while she was busy with other things.

She did not use them. She did not need to. She sat in the dark with her mother's foot warm in her hands, and let the well fill.
