# Chapter 26 — Toren

The fire had been out since before midnight, and nobody had put it back on, so the ninth morning north of Hobb's Wall began in the cold, which Toren decided later was the point.

Rook came down the bridge at grey light and said one word, and the word was "Up," and there was nothing in his voice about the night before. He had sat against the cart wheel for seven hours. He looked exactly as he had at dusk. That was somehow the worst part of it — Toren had spent most of the night working out what he was going to say about the disk, and the man had gone ahead and made the whole subject not exist.

They were moving inside twenty minutes. No fire, no kettle, cold corn cake out of the sack, Milo chewing with his eyes shut and walking into the tailgate twice.

Dessa went in the cart. There was no argument about it, which was how Toren knew how bad the leg was, because Dessa argued about everything.

"Well," said Senna, shifting over to make room. "Look at this. Two of us."

"Don't."

"I've been in this cart alone for a hundred and ten miles," Senna said. "I've been talking to a mule. The mule's not much." She got the blanket over both their knees with her good hand. "What's your name again, properly? Cole?"

"Dessa."

"I know it's Dessa, I've known you since you were four, I'm asking what your mother called you when she was furious."

"Dessalyn."

"That's a terrible name."

"I know."

"Wonderful," said Senna, and lay back, satisfied, like a woman who had been handed a book.

---

Rook changed the drilling that day, and it took Toren until the afternoon to work out what the change was, because it wasn't more.

It was less. That was the thing. For eight days Rook had been running them — shoving, shouting, setting lines, making Milo call, walking down the row and putting people on the ground. Now he stood twenty feet back with his arms folded and made them run it themselves.

"Set a line."

"Where?"

"You tell me."

They stood in the road looking at each other.

That was the entire first hour of the ninth day: four young people arguing on an open road about where a line should go, while a man watched them do it and said nothing at all. They got it wrong three times. They put their backs to a hedge that turned out to have a gap in it, and Rook let them stand there for ten minutes before he walked over, put his hand through the gap up to the shoulder, and walked away again. They put Milo out in front once, for reasons that had made sense when Wyck said them. They argued about whether the cart counted as a wall.

"It counts as a thing that can be knocked over onto your grandmother," said Dessa, from inside it.

"Nobody's knocking over the cart."

"Something knocked over a whole *bridge* parapet with my leg."

Toren said, "Line across the mouth of the lane, cart behind us, Milo up on the load."

"Why the lane?" said Wyck.

"Because it's the only place they have to come through single."

"That's the same as the bridge."

"Yes."

"The bridge went great," said Wyck.

It was not said hotly. It was said in the flat, even, reasonable voice he had been using since the parapet, and it went into Toren like a splinter going in sideways. There was nothing at all to take hold of in it, because it was true.

Milo looked at the ground. Dessa said, "Wyck," and Wyck said, "What? It did," and shrugged, and went and stood on the left of the lane mouth without being told, which was where he was supposed to be.

Rook watched all of it from twenty feet back and did not say one word about any of it.

---

They set the line eleven more times before noon.

By the eighth Toren had stopped hearing his own voice as an announcement. That was Dessa's doing, from the cart, on the second attempt, when he had called the shape of it, and Milo had opened his mouth at the same time and shut it again.

"Milo first," Dessa said. "Every time. He says it, you say it after him. That's the arrangement."

"He knows where the lane is as well as I do—"

"Then it costs you nothing," said Dessa, "and it costs him everything, and you'd have to be stupid to argue, and you're not stupid, you're just used to going first."

So they did it that way. Milo called; Toren repeated it louder; the line went where Milo had said. By the fifth time Milo had stopped sounding like a boy asking permission. By the tenth he was correcting Toren, sharply, twice, and Toren had said "Right — belt of trees, not the fence, sorry, *trees*," and gone where he was sent.

Rook, who had not appeared to be listening, said: "Do that again."

"Do what?"

"That."

He walked off up the road.

---

The country north of the creek changed that afternoon. The stubble went out, and low hills came in, and with the hills came old field systems — stone walls two feet high running for half a mile in perfectly straight lines across country that had nothing to do with them any more, and the shells of steadings at the corners where the walls met. Somebody had farmed all of this with machines, once. Now there were thorn trees standing in the middle of the fields in the exact pattern of a thing that had been planted and then forgotten about for sixty years.

It was good country to drill in and bad country to walk in, and Rook drilled them in it until dark.

The walls became the lesson. He put them on one side of a wall and sent them over it and told them nothing, and it took four goes and a barked shin before Wyck worked out that the way to get over a two-foot wall at speed with a weapon in your hand is to not get over it at all — to fight across it and let it do the work of a line of men. Then Rook made them do that until it was boring. Then he made them do it tired. Then he made them do it in the dark, and then he made them do it in the dark with Milo's light, which was the first time in nine days that anybody had asked Milo to do the only thing Milo could actually do.

"Light," Rook said.

Milo blinked. "It's not—"

"Light."

Milo put his hands together and opened them, and there it was: a soft, unsteady, greenish glow about the size and brightness of a jar of fireflies, sitting in his cupped palms and going up onto everybody's faces from underneath. It was not enough to see by. It had never been enough to see by. In Sallow Creek, it had been the thing Milo Faust could do, and it had been worth exactly one round of laughter at every gathering for four years, and Milo had learned to do the laughing first.

"Higher," said Rook.

"It doesn't go—"

"Your arms go higher. Put it over your head and hold it there and keep calling. That's your job at night. That's your whole job at night, and if you drop it because your arms hurt, people who can't see get killed, so your arms are going to hurt."

Milo held it over his head for eleven minutes, shaking by the end, and they fought across the wall in green half-light with their shadows going enormous behind them, and afterward Milo sat down in the grass very suddenly and said, in a voice full of wonder, "I hate this."

"You're going to be good at it," said Dessa, from the cart.

"I *hate* it."

---

"He was Fire, your grandfather," Senna said that night, apropos of nothing, to the general area of the fire.

Toren looked up.

"Oren. Thirty years married to him. He got to Fire when he was about forty, and he was insufferable about it for the rest of his life." She poked at the corn mush in her bowl. "There's a way men have of standing when they've gone up a rung. He had it. His brother never got past Ember and pretended not to care, and the two of them had one argument about it in 2109 and then didn't have it again for twenty-two years, which is its own kind of having it."

"What's Fire?" said Milo.

"What do they teach you?"

"Nothing," said Milo. "Nobody teaches anybody anything."

"Kindled's the door. Ember's the first thing that stays lit when you stop looking at it. Flame's when it holds itself up. Fire's when it does work, real work, the sort other people can warm their hands at." She sniffed. "Then Torch, which almost nobody, and then two more that are stories."

"Which two?"

"Ask someone who believes in them."

Across the fire, Rook was cleaning ash out of the seams of the flask cap with the point of his knife, and did not look up, and did not stop, and Toren happened to be watching his hands and saw them go on doing exactly what they had been doing at exactly the same speed, which was a thing hands only do on purpose.

Wyck said, "What was Toren's dad?"

"Torch," said Senna.

"He was Torch?"

"Thirty-one years," Senna said. "He was as good as anybody I ever saw with that stone, and he never went up past it once, and he knew by the time he was thirty that he wasn't going to, and he never once said a word about it to anybody including me." She scraped the bowl. "So don't let anyone tell you the rungs are the whole story. He held a bend for two minutes with a brush hook. That's not a rung. That's a man."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Wyck said, "So what's he, then," with his chin at Rook, and the whole fire went to a different temperature.

Rook screwed the cap down on the flask.

"Go to sleep," he said.

---

The drill on the tenth morning was the one Toren had been waiting nine days for without knowing it.

Rook made him stand out in the middle of a mown field with the Ember in his fist and nothing to do.

"Bring it out."

Toren brought out the spike. It came first try. It had been coming first try for four days.

"Now stand there."

"And do what?"

"Nothing."

He stood there. It held for three minutes and then went out, and he swore, and got it back, and it went out again in under two, and by the fourth attempt it was going out faster than it had on the first morning.

"It won't hold when I'm thinking about it," he said, furious.

Rook walked over.

He stopped six feet away and stood looking at Toren with an expression that Toren had absolutely no way of reading, and then he said: "You said that at the river."

"What?"

"On the gravel bar, the first night out of the wall. You said those exact words to me, and I said *remember you said that*." He tipped his head very slightly. "Have you remembered it?"

"I —"

"No. You've been using it as an excuse for nine days." He held out his hand. "Give it here."

Toren put the grey slab in his palm.

Rook closed his fist round it and held it out at arm's length between them, and nothing happened, because nothing was going to happen; it was a stone in a stranger's hand, and everybody in that field knew it. He held it up anyway for a good five seconds so that they all had to look at it.

Then he opened his hand and gave it back.

"Nothing," he said. "That's what a woman with a book told you in a weigh-yard, and she was right about everything she knew. Take it."

Toren took it. It was cool, the way it had been cool his whole life.

"Now tell me where it went," said Rook.

"Where what went?"

"The thing that isn't there when I'm holding it and is there when you are. Where does it go while I'm holding it? Does it wait in the stone? Does it follow you around the field?" He watched Toren's face. "Don't answer. You'll say something stupid, and then you'll defend it for a week. Just carry the question."

He walked away four paces and turned around.

"Out," he said. "And stand there. And this time when it goes out, notice what you were doing an instant before, and don't tell me, and don't tell them, and don't write it down. Notice it."

It went out eleven times.

The twelfth time it held for a quarter of an hour, and Toren could not have said what he had done differently, and he stood in the middle of a mown field in the cold with an ugly crooked grey spike in his fist for fifteen straight minutes feeling like somebody who had been handed something he had not been told the price of.

---

Dessa's leg was worse in the evenings. That was the pattern. It was fine by lunch, and then the afternoon in the cart stiffened it, and by dusk she could not put weight through the calf at all without her whole face changing. She had worked out a way of going up onto her toe on that side and pushing off the stick that let her cross a camp without it showing, provided the light was bad, and nobody watched her do it twice.

Toren watched her do it twice.

"Say one word," she said.

"I wasn't—"

"You were about to do the face." She got herself down onto the cart step and got the leg out in front of her and let out a breath she had clearly been holding since about four o'clock. "Everybody's doing the face. Milo does it and then goes and finds me something to sit on, which is worse. Wyck won't hand me anything heavier than a cup."

"That's just—"

"I *know* what it is. That's not the part." She pulled her sleeve down over the bad forearm. "The part is that I've been in that cart for two days now, and in two more days I'll have been in it four, and this morning I watched all three of you learn a thing about a wall, and by the time I can stand on this leg you'll all know eleven things I don't, and you won't have done it to me on purpose, and I'll still be the one at the back."

"That's not —"

"It absolutely is. That's exactly what it is, it's arithmetic, don't be nice about it." Her voice had gone tight. "I was the one who got Pitch through the cut. I was on the bad side because he gave it to me by name, and I stayed on it. I'm not *frightened* of it. I'm frightened of getting back on my feet in a month and finding out that the month happened without me."

"He knows that."

"He doesn't know anything about me."

"He gave you the bad side," Toren said.

Dessa opened her mouth and shut it again.

---

Rook came over an hour later.

He did not say anything for a while. He stood at the tailgate with his hands in his coat looking north up the black line of the field wall, and Toren, who had been about to leave and had then found himself not leaving, sat where he was on the cart step with a strap and an awl and pretended to be working.

"That leg," Rook said at last, "is three weeks from carrying you and about eight from carrying you in a hurry. I've told you that, and you've decided I'm wrong. You're not wrong to decide that, everybody your age does, and it doesn't change the leg."

"I know how long it is."

"You know the number. You've been spending the number since the bridge." He glanced at her. "You've been getting up in the night and putting weight on it in the dark to see if the number's changed. It hasn't. It won't. And if you tear that calf open again where it's knitting, it won't be three weeks, it'll be a winter in a chair, and I'll be able to do exactly as much about that as I could about your mother's flask."

Dessa said nothing.

"So," said Rook.

He crouched down on his heels in front of the cart step so that his face came level with hers, which was not a thing he had ever done with any of them, and Toren stopped even pretending to work the awl.

"Tomorrow morning, after they go out, you're going to sit down in the grass on that bank," he said, "and you're going to do nothing. On purpose. For an hour."

There was a pause.

"That's — what?"

"Sit. Hands empty. Eyes open or shut, I don't care. Don't sleep, don't plan, don't listen to them drilling and keep a tally of what you're missing. Don't want anything." He stood back up. "When you've had enough, come and tell me you've had enough and how long it took."

"That's not a *drill*."

"No."

"That's sitting in a field."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because it's the only thing on this road," said Rook, "that I've never asked any of the rest of them to do."

He went back to the fire, and Dessa Cole sat on the cart step in the dark with her bad leg out straight in front of her and her mouth slightly open, and Toren watched her file it away, because Dessa filed everything.
