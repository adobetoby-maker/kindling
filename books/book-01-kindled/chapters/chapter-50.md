# Chapter 50 — Toren

Rook told him the other thing at first light, sitting on the ground with his back against the sixth block, in about two minutes, and did not look at him once while he said it.

"You asked me at a fire," he said. "Thirteen days old, with a bowl in your hands. You said *you did both*, and I said everybody's mostly right about most things, and I told you to eat your food. I've thought about that four hundred times since and I've never once been sorry, because you'd have spent the whole summer trying to do it, and you'd have put it out, and you'd have decided it wasn't for you, and it *is* for you, and I'd have taken it off you with one sentence on a riverbank in the dark."

"You've never—"

"Don't talk. I'm going to say it poorly anyway and I'd like it to be short.

"Dessa asked me on a bridge what it was called and I said it isn't called anything and then I was rude to her, and the first part was true and the second part was mine. It isn't called anything. There's nothing to name. It is not a third thing you do instead of the two. *It's what's there when you stop doing the two.*

"So. Four sentences, and none of them will help you until they do.

"One. The changeover isn't a distance. It's a question. Every single time you put one down and reach for the other, what you're actually doing is stopping to ask which one you want. The second and a half is the asking. The asking is the only part of it that takes any time at all.

"Two. You have never once, since I have known you, had to ask where you wanted to be. Not in the cut. Not in the orchard. Not on the bank at the arch. You are bad at nearly everything and you have never been bad at that.

"Three. So don't ask. Have somewhere to be, and go there, and don't stop halfway to find out which half of you is doing the carrying."

He stopped and got his breath, which took him longer than it used to.

"Four," he said. "It'll cost. I don't know how much. I watched a girl do it for the first time in a valley with instruments on her, and she was fifteen, and she'd had four years of the best instruction there has ever been in this world, and it put her on a floor for two days, and when she got up she said it had been worth it, and she was lying, and I knew she was lying and I didn't say so because she'd have hit me."

"Rook—"

"And the last thing, and it's the only one I'd go back and die for getting wrong." He turned his head then, finally, and looked at him. "*Don't do it.*

"Don't go out on that pasture planning it. Don't carry it out there with you like a stone in your pocket and wait for a good moment. If you plan it you'll be checking on it, and you have known since a towpath in the spring what checking on it does, and I have counted every single time you've done it and the number was eleven and it will be twelve this morning if you're clever about this.

"Go and get your grandmother to a wall. That's all you've got to do. That's all anybody's ever had to do."

---

They went out onto the pasture at the first hour with the light coming up grey and flat behind them.

It was exactly what Rook had said it was. The old six-lane ran out of the Sag and across three miles of drained pasture as straight as a ruled line, with a rusted crash rail down the near side of it and a drainage grip three feet deep along the foot of the rail, and a wire fence a hundred yards out to the east on rotted posts, and after that nothing at all: no hedge, no tree, no fold in the ground, no wall, nothing for a thing to stop behind and nothing for a man to get his back against, all the way to a low green berm on the horizon with a line of poles along the top of it.

Meridian did not look like anything. That was the thing Toren was not prepared for. Nineteen days out of Sallow Creek his father had written a number on a piece of paper and crossed it out and written a smaller one under it, and it had stopped at eighteen, and it had been in Toren's coat for four months, and the place at the bottom of that column of numbers turned out to be a green bank with poles on it, three miles away, in the rain.

Rook had them formed up before they left the blocks and said the whole of it out loud, once.

"Wyck at the mule's head, because Dessa can't run and he can, and because if Pitch goes we need somebody at that head who won't let go. Dessa at the tailgate with her hands on the boards and her eyes shut, and she calls, and nobody argues with a call, and nobody asks her to explain one. Toren out on the east flank at forty yards, because forty yards is far enough that it has to choose.

"I'm in the bed with her and I am going to keep my hand on these boards and I am going to do nothing whatever unless it is inside twelve feet of this cart, and then I am going to do it once, and after that I am nothing on this field but a large slow parcel.

"Here's the condition and then I'll shut up. *If it gets to the cart, that's the end of it.* Not the end of the cart. The end. There is nobody on this pasture who can stop that thing twice, and I am the only one who can stop it once, and once is what there is."

Senna said, from under the blankets, "And if it comes at the cart, I should like everybody to keep walking."

"Nobody's going to keep walking, Gran."

"No," said Senna Voss. "I didn't think you would. But it'll be said now, won't it, and nobody can pretend afterward that it wasn't."

---

They made a mile and three quarters, which Toren thought afterward was more than anybody had expected and more than any of them had said.

Then Dessa said, "Thirty feet," in an absolutely ordinary voice, and then, without any change at all in the voice, "*it's under the road.*"

The grip along the foot of the rail was three feet deep and full of dead grass and it ran the whole length of the six-lane, and a thing that travels in a dead straight line and goes from standing to full speed with nothing in between had lain down in it a mile and a half ahead of them at some point in the night and waited.

It came out of the grip forty feet in front of the mule.

It did not go for the people. It went for Pitch.

Wyck had the head. Wyck was one-armed and the arm was the broken one, and he was fifteen, and he got both his boots into the road and hauled the mule's head round toward the rail with the whole of his weight on the cheek strap, and Wyck Tanner did not run, and it made absolutely no difference at all.

The blade came round at the height of a horse's chest.

It was over in a second and a half. The mule went down in the traces and took the near shaft with him and the cart went over onto the rail with its off wheel in the air, and the tailgate came off its pins, and Dessa went down under the boards, and Senna Voss and four months of blankets and the whole contents of a family's road came out of the bed and across the wet surface of a six-lane in the open with no wall anywhere on any of it.

Wyck was flat on his back nine feet away with the cheek strap still in his hand.

And Rook — who had come out of that bed with the rest of it and was lying half across the old woman with his face in the road — got his left hand down flat on the surface of the six-lane and shut it.

Twelve feet of nothing stood up across the road between Senna Voss and the thing that had killed the mule.

The thing hit it and stopped.

---

Toren was forty yards east in the grass, and everything from there on happened in a sequence exact enough to describe and too fast for any description of it to sound true.

He saw Rook's hand go down. He saw the thing check, and turn, and put its head against twelve feet of held air, and push with its three legs the way a bullock pushes a gate, and he saw, past it, Rook's arm shaking from the wrist to the shoulder in the road with an old woman under him.

And he understood the trap in the whole of his body before any of it reached his head, which was this: that the wall was the only thing on that pasture keeping any of them alive, and that the wall was a bonfire on a hill, and that it would go on being a bonfire on a hill until Rook was finished, and that Rook would be finished in about four minutes, and that the thing had no reason in the world ever to go anywhere else.

"Four minutes," Rook said, into the road, not loudly, to nobody.

Toren started running.

He got the spike out as he went. He did it on purpose and he did it in the open and he lit it as high and as hard as he had ever lit anything in four months, holding nothing back and auditing nothing, running east away from the road with an ugly crooked grey spike blazing out of the bottom of his fist like the worst-kept secret in the world, and he shouted — he never afterward knew what, and Wyck swore it had not been words.

The thing came off the wall as if it had been called.

"*TOREN*—"

"Get her up," he shouted back, over his shoulder, already forty yards out and going. "Get her up and get her to the berm — *Elias!* — let it go, let it *go*, I've got it—"

He heard it go out behind him. He felt it, the way you feel a window shut in another room.

---

The first pass he read correctly and it cost him anyway.

He was out past the wire by then with the fence line on his left and open grass everywhere else, and it came at him across a hundred yards in something under two seconds, and forty feet out its blade-limb went slack and dropped and trailed, and Toren thought *the legs, it's the legs, it's already going* and put himself two paces to the right of where he was standing, and it went past him at full speed and took the fence and forty feet of wire with it.

*The thing that goes quiet is the thing that isn't coming.* It worked. It had worked yesterday in a gap eleven feet wide and it worked now in the open, and that was the entire problem, because in the open it did not have to come back the same way.

It stopped — standing, instantly, with no slowing in it — and its back half went loose and soft and quiet, and its blade came up and flat and hard, and it was fourteen feet away and Toren read that correctly too.

He did exactly what he had done at the revetments. He stepped along the cut instead of back from it.

But the grass was wet and it was not eleven feet of cropped turf between two concrete blocks, and his heel went, and he was an inch and a half slower than he had been the day before, and the edge went through the back of his right thigh on its way past.

He did not feel it. He went down on his face in the grass and got up and did not know why his leg had stopped taking instructions, and then he looked down and knew, and made himself stop looking.

Behind him, four hundred yards off, there was a knot of people on a road moving very slowly toward a green bank, dragging something with a person on it.

---

It came the third time and it came for his back.

He got to the crash rail. There was no reason for it except that the rail was the only thing on three miles of pasture that was not grass, and a body that has stopped being able to think will go toward the one thing that is not the same as everything else, and afterward he decided that was probably the whole of why he was alive.

He got his hand on the rail and turned round with the wet iron at the back of his legs and the grip behind that, and the thing was stopped at twenty feet with its head down.

Its back half went loose.

*Blade.*

And Toren Voss understood, with perfect clarity, standing on one leg against a rusted crash rail three miles from a wall, exactly what was going to happen: that it would commit to the cut, and that he would read the cut, and that he would step along it the way he had been taught and the way he had proved twice, and that the leg would not take it, and that he would be inside the arc with nothing in his hands but a spike he could not bring to bear from where the step would put him — and that what he actually needed, in the quarter second after the step, was not the spike.

What he needed was to not be standing there any more.

And the question came up out of him the way it had come up ten thousand times on a verge in the wrong light with a man counting halves at him from a cart bed — *which one, put one down, pick the other up, which one, which* —

He did not answer it.

That was all it was. He had somewhere to be. He wanted to be past that edge and inside it and he wanted it with the whole of himself and he did not have the time or the interest to stop and find out which half of him was going to do the carrying, so he did not stop, and nothing was put down, and nothing was picked up.

The struts came up the outside of both legs in thin uneven lines of light, the left one late the way it always was, and the spike was in his fist, and neither of them had waited for the other.

It went to read him.

He saw it do it. He was four feet from its head by then and he saw the whole thing check for a fraction of an instant in the middle of its own commitment and go looking, the way it had gone looking at Wyck and at Rook and at him twice already, for the half that dropped.

Nothing dropped.

There was nothing to read, and it had never in its existence met a thing with nothing to read, and it came on anyway because coming on was all it had, and Toren went past the edge on the inside with the struts firing off the crash rail and the wet iron, and he was behind its shoulder with the root of that limb extended and open and four inches from his hand.

---

He did not put the spike in.

He had it. That was the thing he never told anybody except Rook, and Rook already knew, because Rook had been lying in a road four hundred yards away watching the whole of it with an old woman's head on his arm. Toren Voss had the root of that limb open in front of him with a lit Ember in his fist and every part of him in the right place at once for the first time in his life, and he did not put the spike in, because from where he was standing he could see, past the shoulder, straight up the line the thing was travelling.

It was not travelling at him. It had not been travelling at him. It had come round the rail with all that speed already in it and it was going to carry, the way a cart carries, and the line it was going to carry along went four hundred yards up the wet grass of a drained pasture and through a knot of people dragging a tailgate toward a green bank.

He had about a quarter of a second, and afterward he could account for every part of it, and there was no version of it in which he did anything else.

He let go of the shoulder and got in front of it.

---

The rest of it was not skill and he never once pretended it was.

It hit him and he went up and over the crash rail and into the grip, and his left forearm broke on the rail on the way, and he came down in three feet of dead grass and rusty water with everything in him disconnected and the spike gone out of his hand somewhere and his ears full of a sound like a flyover being hit.

It came into the grip after him. It could not get properly into a three-foot cut with three heavy legs and one it could not fold, and it did not care, and it came down into the ditch on top of him half sideways with its blade-limb extended straight out over him at full stretch because that was the only way it fitted.

And the root of the limb was six inches from Toren Voss's face.

He had no struts. He had no light. He had one arm that worked and about as much left in him as a wet sack has, and he got his right hand up out of the water with the spike in it — it had gone into the grass beside his head, which was luck, and he had always said so — and he put it in with the whole of his shoulder behind it, once, ugly and crooked and poorly, exactly the way his father had told him to on the second of the month in a road cut, keeping the ugly shape and not trying to be good at it.

Something in there let go.

The whole of it came apart at once. It did not fall. It stopped being a thing that was holding itself in a shape, all at the same instant, the way a knot comes out when you pull the right end, and four hundred pounds of Riftspawn went to a fine drifting grey that did not blow away, and it came down into the grip and over Toren and into the rusty water and filled the ditch from one side to the other, and pooled, and ran along the camber of the road bed above him, and found the low places, and lay there with a shine on it.

He lay in it with his head back in three feet of it and his broken arm across his chest and looked up at the sky.

The grey was in his mouth and in his eyes and in his ears and it made his back teeth hum, and he understood, distantly and with no interest at all, that he was lying in more money than his family had ever had.

---

Dessa got to him first, on a bad leg, over three hundred yards of wet pasture, and she was the one who went down into the grip with him and got his head up out of the ash, and she did not say anything for a while and neither did he.

Then she said, "You did both."

"Yes."

"You did both and you did it going *at* it—"

"Dessa."

"I had my eyes shut," she said. "I had my eyes shut the whole way because he told me to, and I felt you at the edge of thirty feet doing it, and it went — I don't know what it did, I'll never be able to say what it did." She wiped his face with her sleeve, which made it worse. "Don't do it again."

"All right."

"I mean it. Not this year."

Wyck came up and stood on the rail and looked down into the ditch with his one usable arm hanging and his collarbone the wrong shape and grey ash to the ankles, and said, "Right," and sat down heavily on the rail, and put his head down, and stayed like that.

Rook did not get there for eleven minutes, because eleven minutes was how long it took him to cross three hundred yards, and Toren watched him do the whole of it from the bottom of a ditch.

He got down onto the edge of the grip and sat with his boots in the ash.

"Say it," said Toren.

"Later."

"Say it now. I want it now."

Rook looked out at the pasture, where there was a mule and a cart on its side and four hundred pounds of grey lying about in a road, and then down into a ditch at a boy with a broken arm and his leg opened and both hands full of a thing nobody had a word for.

"It isn't called anything," he said.

Then he put two fingers on the inside of Toren's wrist, the way he had done on a towpath in the spring and at the road cut before that, and held them there for three seconds, and then for another three, and then took them away and did not say anything at all.

"What," said Toren.

"Where's your stone."

It was in the ash beside his hip. Dessa found it and put it in his hand and he closed his fingers round it, and it was cold.

Not cool. Not the low unbothered coolness it had carried through four months of road, the way the inside of a well is cool. It was cold the way a stone is cold, the way any stone in that ditch was cold, the way it had been in a fire at seven in the morning on the second of the month before it ever opened, and there was nothing in it, and when he asked it for the shape it had given him ten thousand times without being asked twice, it did not answer, and he could not feel the place where the asking went.

He lay in three feet of another creature's ash with his father's Ember in his fist and could not find the door.

"Rook."

"I know."

"It's *out*—"

"I know it is." Rook's hand came down on the back of his neck, hard, and stayed there. "Listen to me and then don't ask me again today, because I'll only say the same thing and I'd rather say it once well. I don't know. I have seen one person in my life do what you did this morning and she was not thirteen and she was not empty when she started, and she was on a floor for two days and she got up. That's the whole of the evidence there is in the world on this. One girl, eleven years ago, in a valley that isn't there any more."

"That's not—"

"No," said Rook. "It isn't. It's what I've got, and I'm not going to improve it by being confident at you."

---

They got about eleven pounds of it.

Marta Faust's flask took what was left in it after Senna's measure, which was not much; Rook's own flask had been empty since the arch and took a good deal; Dessa filled the cooking pot and tied the cloth over it and then filled both her boots, and walked the last mile and a half of her life's worst week in wet socks and did not mention it.

They left the rest of it in a road. There was nothing on that pasture to carry it in, and there was a boy who could not walk and an old woman on a tailgate and a man who could not lift either of them, and Dessa stood at the edge of the grip for a moment looking at three or four hundred pounds of lifted ash going slowly silver in the low places and did the arithmetic out loud, because she was Dessa Cole.

"That's a year," she said. "That's a year for six people at Hobb's Wall prices and it's lying in a ditch."

"It'll keep," said Rook. "Ash doesn't go off. Ash brings more, which is the other half of that sentence, and I'd rather be behind a wall by then." He was sitting on the road with his back against the overturned cart and had been for some minutes. "And somebody up on that bank has been watching this field through a glass for about two hours, and I promise you they can see it from there, and I promise you they know exactly what it is."

---

They came the last four hundred yards at about the fourth hour.

Wyck and Dessa had the tailgate with Senna on it, one-armed and bad-legged between them, and it took them eleven rests. Toren came behind on Rook's shoulder, which was the wrong shoulder, and the two of them moved at about the speed of one very old man and fell over twice.

Meridian's south gate was a slab of plate steel in a green berm with a lit lamp over it, and the lamp was on in daylight, and there were four people standing on the top of the berm above it looking down at them, and one of them had a glass, and one of them had a long gun on a sling, and none of them were moving.

The gate did not open.

They got to within a hundred feet of it and stopped, because there was nowhere further to go, and stood in the road in the rain — three of them standing and two of them not — and looked up at four people on a bank, and the four people looked back.

Somebody up there said something to somebody else. Toren could not hear it. He could see the shape of a man turning his head and speaking, and another man writing something down.

"Don't say anything," said Rook, very quietly, without moving his mouth. "Not one of you. Not a word about the field, not a word about me, not a word about what he's just done out there, until somebody asks a question with a name attached to it."

"Why," said Wyck.

"Because I have stood in front of this gate once before," said Rook, "and they wrote my name in a book, and I have spent eleven years being extremely glad that nobody at that wall ever once asked me what I could do."
