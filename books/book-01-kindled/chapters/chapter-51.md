# Chapter 51 — Callie

The gate did not open.

Callie counted, because she always counted. She stood in the road forty paces from it with the hook on her shoulder and her left arm held against her ribs, and she counted the seconds, and then the minutes, out loud under her breath where only she could hear.

It was a slab of plate steel set into the green bank, taller than two men and wider than the wagon, with a lamp over it that was lit in broad daylight. The bank went away on both sides further than she could see. It was built of earth, grassed over, and it was higher than any wall she had ever seen. On top of it, above the gate, there were four people standing still and looking down at them. One of them had a glass. One of them had a long gun on a sling. None of them moved.

At three minutes, one of the four went away.

At eight minutes, Pell said, "Well, they've seen us," to nobody.

At eleven minutes, Callie's mother sat down on the tailboard. She did it carefully, without any fuss, and she did not say anything about it. She sat with her hands in her lap and her hat pulled down and looked at the gate. Callie watched her do it and understood. Her mother was not tired yet. She was saving her feet.

At fourteen minutes, a man came up onto the top of the bank who had not been there before. He was in a long coat, with nothing in his hands. He looked down at them for a long time. He looked at the wagon and at the horse standing on three legs, and at Katori, and at the woman on the tailboard. Then he looked at Callie, standing in the road with a hook on her shoulder, and he looked at her longest. Then he went away again.

At nineteen minutes the gate opened.

---

Four people came out of it in grey coats sealed up to the throat, with hoods up, and in front of their faces were flat clear panels, like windows. They came out at a fast walk in a line, side by side, and they did not look at the horse or the old man or the woman on the tailboard. They went straight past all of them to the wagon bed. To the tins.

"Ma'am," one of them said to Katori, "I need you to step away from that."

Katori looked at the panel in front of the face. Callie could see her own face reflected in it, small.

"It's ash," said Katori.

"Yes, ma'am. And I need you to step away from it."

Katori stepped away from it. She did it without any expression at all.

They took everything. They took the causeway sacks, all of them, the soft heavy sacks that had been Katori's whole road. They took the four tins of hound ash that Otto had lifted by starlight. They took the jar out of the bottom of the barrow, the screw-top jar with three Handfuls in it, and Callie made a small noise that she did not mean to make, and one of the grey coats looked round at her through the panel and then went on. They put it all into a big lidded bin on two wheels that another of them had pushed out through the gate, and closed the lid on it, and clamped the clamp.

"It'll be weighed," said the grey coat, to nobody in particular. "Inside. Everything that goes in the bin gets weighed and written against the name it came in under. You'll see it on the board."

"That's four hundred miles in there," said Callie. She did not know why she said it.

"Yes, miss," said the grey coat. "Most of it is." Then, after a moment: "I need your boots."

---

They took their boots. They took Callie's hook.

She had not thought they would. She stood in the road in her socks with the hook on her shoulder, and one of the grey coats held out a hand for it, and she looked at the hand.

"It's a hook," she said.

"It's got ash on it, miss. On the inside of the curve." The grey coat pointed with one gloved finger at the black smear of old husk down the sharpened inside, where it had always been. "It'll go in its own bin. It'll be washed and tagged and you'll have it back at the hatch. Nobody's keeping it."

Callie stood there. She could put it down. She knew that. It was dark, and when it was dark it was only a hook. She had set it down a hundred times since the causeway. She had left it standing in a tar seam on the plain two nights ago and run round the tailboard of a wagon. She had not ever handed it to somebody and watched them walk away with it.

"Callie," said Katori quietly.

She gave it to him. He took it by the conduit, not the curve, which she noticed. He carried it to a second, smaller bin and laid it in, and closed the lid, and tied a paper tag to the handle and wrote on the tag.

Then they hosed them down.

They did it where they stood, in the road, with a long canvas hose that came out through the gate. The water was warm. Nobody had told them it would be warm. They did all of them, one after another, and they did Callie's mother sitting on the tailboard. She lifted her face into it and shut her eyes, and when it was done she said, "Well, that was the first hot water I've had since the spring. Thank you." The grey coat holding the hose said, "You're welcome, ma'am," in a completely different voice from the one he had used about the ash.

---

Then, and only then, a woman came out through the gate without a bin, and went to Callie's mother.

She was in a grey coat like the others, but her hood was down and there was no panel in front of her face. She was about sixty, with short white hair and a lined brown face and small quick hands. She did not kneel, because Callie's mother was not lying down. She stood in front of the tailboard and looked at her the way Callie's mother had looked at Otto's arm in the pump house, all of her, from the top, taking her time.

"All right, my love," the woman said. "What's happened to you?"

And Callie's mother told her.

She told her in order. She told her the way Callie told Pell a road, the way she had clearly been telling it to herself, for days, on the wagon bed with her hat over her eyes.

"My right hand," she said. "Since the end of the winter. It's got worse since the spring. It won't take instruction. Headaches, mornings, behind the left eye. Sick with them, sometimes. Fits. Three that I know of. The first one the morning of a causeway, a fortnight back. The last one five nights ago. A hundred and fifty-eight seconds. My daughter counted." She did not look at Callie. "My right leg, since the ninth day on the road, and worse since the last fit. I couldn't stand on it yesterday. And the words. I call things by the wrong names. I'm told. I don't always hear myself do it." She stopped. "I think that's all of it. It's all I've got written down."

The woman had a flat board with paper clipped to it, and she had written the whole of it down while Callie's mother talked, without once asking her to go slower.

"And today?" the woman said. "You're sitting up very straight for somebody who couldn't stand yesterday."

"Today I've borrowed," said Callie's mother. "A Handful. At first light. It'll run out by dark, and I'd like to be lying down somewhere when it does."

The woman looked at her for a moment. Something went across her face that Callie could not read.

"You drank ash," she said.

"I did. It's the second time. I'd do it again."

The woman wrote that down too. Then she turned and said over her shoulder, not loudly, "Stretcher."

"No," said Callie's mother. "Thank you."

The woman turned back.

"I'll walk," said Callie's mother. "Thank you. I paid for it."

---

She walked.

She got down off the tailboard by herself, slowly, with her left hand on the boards, and stood up on both feet in the wet road in her stockings. She swayed once, and then did not sway. The woman in the grey coat did not take her arm. She put her hand under Callie's mother's elbow, very lightly, not holding, only there. And the two of them walked to the gate together, slowly, a step and then a step, across forty paces of wet concrete.

Callie did not follow. Nobody had told her not to. She knew anyway, the way she had known on the first morning not to walk the last thirty paces to four strangers by a fire. This was her mother's forty paces. She had paid for them.

At the gate her mother stopped and turned round.

She did not wave. She lifted her left arm, flat, and held it up, the way you hold something up to be seen. The way somebody had held an arm up on a rail grade at dusk on the first night of all this, and the way her mother had held it up from the rim of a cut at the top of a scarp.

Callie lifted hers.

Then her mother turned and went in through the gate with the woman's hand under her elbow, and the gate shut behind her.

Callie stood in the road in her socks with her arm still up.

"That's it," said Pell, beside her, very quietly. "That's the whole of it. She's in."

"She's in," said Callie. She put her arm down. "And I'm out here."

"For about an hour," said Pell. "They'll have us in the next door along, and then they'll have us at a window, and then they'll give us a bed. You'll see her tomorrow." He paused. "She walked in, Callie. On her own feet, through that gate. That's in the book."

"You haven't got the book. They took the book."

Pell looked at her. Then he opened his coat and showed her the atlas, flat against his shirt, where the grey coats had not asked about it and he had not offered it.

"They took the ash," he said. "Nobody said anything about the book."

---

The door along was a smaller gate in the bank, a hundred paces west, and behind it was a pen.

It was a yard of poured concrete about forty feet long and twenty wide, with walls as high as the bank on three sides and a roof at one end. There was a drain in the middle of the floor, and a bench along one wall, and at the far end, under the roof, a hatch in the wall with a window in it. They were put in it and the gate was shut behind them, and nobody said anything about how long they would be there.

It was warm.

It was warm because there were pipes running along the wall behind the bench, and the pipes were hot. Otto found this out first. He sat down on the bench and leaned back and his shoulders touched the pipes, and he said, "*Oh,*" in a voice Callie had never heard him use, and did not say anything else for a long time.

Callie did not sit down. She stood in the middle of the yard in her wet socks and looked up.

There was a light on the wall under the roof. It was a real one, in a fitting, behind a cage of wire. It was small and flat and white, and it did not move and it did not flicker and there was no flame in it anywhere. She looked at it for a long time. It was the same light she had watched from the plain every night for nine nights, from the top of a scarp and the side of a road and the bed of a wagon in the rain. It was the light that went out at the same time every night.

It was the size of her hand.

"Callie," said Katori, from the bench. "Sit down."

Callie sat down. The pipes were hot against her back, through her wet coat, and her ribs did not like it and the rest of her did not care.

---

At the far end, behind the hatch, there was a room with more light in it, and in the room there was a man at a desk with a book.

He was about forty-five. He had a grey cardigan on under an oilskin, and a pen on a string round his neck, and a face that looked as if it had been told a great many things through that window and had decided long ago not to be surprised by any of them.

"Ivor Hallet," he said, through the window, the moment they came up to it. He said it the way you give a thing that is going to be written on a paper anyway. "I'm going to do this in the order it's got to be done in, and I'd ask you to let me, because if I do it out of order I have to start again. Persons in party."

"Six," said Callie.

Hallet looked up. He looked past her at the yard behind, where there were five of them.

"I count five, miss."

"Six. One went in through the gate. My mother." Callie did not look away from him. "She's in the party. She came three hundred miles in the party. She's six."

Hallet looked at her for a moment. Then he wrote something down.

"Six," he said. "One clinical, admitted at the south gate this morning." He went on writing. "That's correct, and I'd rather have it that way than the other. Names, starting at your left, as you'd want them written."

Katori gave her name. When Hallet asked if there was more of it she gave the rest, quietly, a word Callie did not quite hear. Pell gave his, and it was longer than Callie had ever imagined Pell's name could be, and he said it without looking at anybody. Ines gave hers flatly, all of it. Otto gave his and went red.

"Callie," said Callie. And then she gave the rest of it, the name that had been painted over the door of the scale house at Thistle Ford, which she had not said out loud since the spring.

"Thirteen?"

"Thirteen."

"Household?"

"My mother. And me."

"Head of household?"

"My mother."

"Your mother's a clinical admission," said Hallet. "For this window, that makes it you." He looked up. "I'm sorry, miss. I know how that sounds. It's only a column."

"I'm thirteen," said Callie.

"Yes, miss," said Ivor Hallet. "I've got a column for that as well."

---

"I stand for them," said Katori. "The girl and her mother. I said I would, at this gate. I'm saying it."

Hallet wrote. "Noted."

"Noted?"

"It goes in the household column. Sponsor. Your name against theirs." He did not look up. "It's a line on a review. I'll tell you honestly, it's worth something. It's not a key."

Katori was quiet for a moment.

"I was told it was a key," she said.

"You were told by somebody who came through this gate before there was a review," said Hallet, "or somebody who'd heard it from somebody who had. There's a lot of that on the south road." He looked up at last. "I'm not saying it to be hard. I'm saying it so that in a fortnight you're not stood in front of a board wondering why it didn't open a door. It's worth something. It's on the paper. It's just not a key."

Katori said nothing. Callie watched her face, and it did not change, and something behind it did. It went through the way the wind goes across a field of wheat, the way it had gone through on the first morning when Callie said *I want the wagon*.

Then Katori said, "Then put it down as a line. A big one."

"I'll put it down as you said it," said Ivor Hallet, and did.

---

"Declaration of capability," he said, and laid four buff cards on the ledge inside the window. He did not push them through.

He told them what it was. He told them the way you tell a thing you say every week and have stopped hearing yourself say. Meridian kept a register of Kindled persons inside the line. It was not a secret register. There were about eleven hundred names on it. If you were Kindled and inside the berm, you were expected to be on it. On the card went your name, your rank if you had a word for it, and a plain description of what you could actually do. Not what you would like to do. What you would do at four in the morning if a wall came down.

For it you got a band with a red thread in it, which got you through the north yard, where the work was. What it cost was that you told this window things. If you went up a rank. If you left. If you were going to do anything in a public street that somebody who wasn't Kindled would find remarkable.

"And if we decline," said Katori, "we're what?"

"Transient. Fourteen days, a grey band, no north yard. And a review, and I don't sit on the review." Hallet looked at her. "I've been told I say that part with a bad face. I've been trying not to."

Ines looked at the cards on the ledge.

"I've no door," she said. "So I've no card."

"No, miss."

"There's a column for that."

"There is," said Hallet. "It's the biggest one."

"I know," said Ines. "I've been in it all my life." She stepped back from the window and went and sat on the bench against the hot pipes, and put her quiver across her knees, and did not look at the cards again.

Katori took one. Callie took one.

The others had their own business with the window, and Callie did not watch it. She went back to the bench and sat down, with the card on her knee and a pencil Hallet had put through the hatch after it, and looked at the card for a long time.

It was buff. It was the size of her hand. It had lines printed on it, and at the top in small printed letters it said *NAME* and *RANK (if known)* and then *CAPABILITY*, and under that there was a great deal of empty space.

---

Katori wrote hers in about the time it took Callie to read the top of her own. Then she turned it face down on her knee and put her hand flat on it, and did not look at it again.

Callie did not ask. She knew better than to ask.

She wrote her name at the top. Her whole name, the one off the scale-house door. Under *RANK* she wrote *Kindled*, because it was the only word she had and it was true.

Then she sat with the pencil over the empty space.

She had had nine days. She had put it together one line at a time, on a wagon board, and on the bed beside her mother in the dark, and in the rain. She knew every word. She had known every word since the night before the rain. And now that she was sitting on a bench against a hot pipe with the card on her knee, every word looked much smaller than it had in her head, and much more permanent.

*The man knows what he's buying now*, her grandfather had said, about a horse. *He'll never try to make it into something it isn't.*

She wrote it.

She wrote slowly, because her hand was not steady, and because she wanted it to be neat. When she had finished she read it back once, the whole of it, and then she read it again.

*Callie. Thirteen. Hunter. I read ground. That's the thing I'm best at.*

*Two doors. Since the Fenner causeway.*

*I can make an edge come off my hook. One shape. It's ugly and I can't change it. About a minute and I'm half empty.*

*I can stand in a ward. I can't walk in it. If I lift my foot it comes off.*

*I can put the ward in my hook instead of on me. Then nothing can move the hook. Then I've got nothing on me at all.*

*Ninety-one seconds was everything I had.*

*Not both on me at once. Not since the first night.*

*I can't do any of it at present. I'm dry.*

She looked at the last line for a long time. Then, at the very bottom, in smaller letters, she added one more.

*I count. Out loud, if you want.*

She did not know why she added it. It was not a capability. But it was the truest thing on the card, and she was not going to cross it out.

---

She took it up to the window.

Hallet took it through the hatch and read it. He read it all the way down, and then he stopped, and went back to the top, and read it again. He did not look up while he did it. When he got to the bottom the second time, he stayed looking at it for a while.

Then he looked up at her, through the glass. He looked at her for a long time, a girl of thirteen standing in wet socks at his window, with her arm held against her side and a line of bald pink skin over her left ear.

"Miss," said Ivor Hallet.

"Yes."

"I'm going to have to have somebody else look at this."

"I know," said Callie. She had known since the top of the scarp. Katori had told her. "Read them the first line first."

Hallet looked at her.

"The first line," said Callie. "*I read ground.* Read them that first. Before the rest. That's the thing I'm best at, and it's the thing they'll want least, and I put it first so they'd have to read it." She held his eyes. "Tell them I asked."

Hallet was quiet for a moment.

"I'll tell them you asked," he said. He laid the card down on the desk in front of him, face up, square to the edge. "It's a true card. That's the second one I've had this month that says *at present*. I'd rather have one card like that than ten good ones." He wrote something in the book. "And I'll tell you something for nothing, which I'm not supposed to. Nobody reads the first line first. They read the one they're looking for. But I'll tell them you asked."

---

Katori's card went through. Hallet read it and wrote in his book and said nothing about it at all, and Callie did not see it and did not try to.

Then there were bands. They were paper, and they went round the wrist and fastened, and two of them had a red thread run through the paper. Hallet pushed them through the hatch one at a time and said each name as he did. Callie's had a red thread in it. She put it on her right wrist. It was the lightest thing she had ever been given that she knew was heavy.

"Conditional admittance," said Hallet. "Fourteen days, reviewable. The review for a household with a clinical admission comes up faster than most, because the station likes to know where it stands. Yard C, second row, hut fourteen. There's four beds in it and a stove, and the stove works." He looked at Pell. "The horse and the wagon go to the south stable. There's a man there who does legs. It's a fee against the ash."

"Is there a number for the ash yet?" said Katori.

"Tonight. It'll be on the board in the morning, against the names it came in under." He paused. "It came in heavy. I heard from the gate. You'll be all right for the fee."

"And my mother," said Callie.

"The long building at the north end. Visiting's in the afternoons. She'll have been seen by now, and she'll be seen again tomorrow, and somebody will talk to the family." He looked at her. "That's you, miss. And whoever you say."

"All of them," said Callie. "It's all of them."

"Then it's all of them." Hallet wrote it down. "Bathhouse is open till the tenth hour and there's no queue this time of year. Nobody comes in in the autumn." He stopped, and seemed to hear himself. "I say that every autumn. I've had two parties through this window in ten days, and I said it to both of them."

"The lights," said Callie. "On the plain. They go out every night at the same time."

Hallet looked up at her sharply.

"They do," he said. "At the first hour. How did you — "

"I watched them," said Callie. "Nine nights. From the other side."

Ivor Hallet looked at her through the glass for a long moment.

"There's a written reason," he said at last. "I've never read it. There's three lights on the main street and they're on all night till then, and people find them hard to sleep next to at first. Put the blanket over the window and don't be proud about it."

---

The bathhouse had hot water in it that came out of a pipe in the wall when you turned a handle, and Callie stood under it until Ines banged on the door and said other people were waiting, which was not true, and then stood under it for a while longer.

Hut fourteen was a low building of poured concrete with a turf roof and a door and a small deep window. There were four beds in it along the walls, and a stove, and the stove worked. Otto lit it. Pell sat down on the nearest bed and put the atlas on his knee and opened it and did not write anything, and after a while Callie saw that he had gone to sleep sitting up. Katori stood at the window looking out at nothing. Ines took the bed nearest the door without asking anybody and lay down on it with her bow beside her and her eyes shut, which did not mean anything.

There were four beds and five of them. Nobody said anything about it. Callie took the floor by the stove, because she had slept on worse and because she did not think she was going to sleep.

She did not sleep.

At some time late in the night she got up and went out and sat on the step.

---

The row was long and dark, a line of low huts all the same with their doors on the same side, and at the far end of it there was a lamp on a pole. It put down a circle of flat white light on the wet ground, the colour of bone, and between it and her the dark was complete.

She sat on the step with her arms round her knees and her left side against the door frame and looked at it. It was not like the lamp at a wall, or a fire. It did not move. It had no life in it at all. It was just there.

Two doors down, somebody else was sitting on a step.

She had not seen him at first. He was sitting very still in the dark of his own doorway, the way she was, with his back against the frame. He was a boy. She could see that much by the edge of the lamplight. He had his left arm strapped across his chest in a sling, and his right hand was wrapped in something white, and he was sitting with his right hand in his coat pocket. Every so often he took it out. Then, after a while, he put it back.

Earlier, when they had first come along the row with their bands, there had been others at that door. A bigger boy with a short iron bar in his left hand, turning it over, dropping it on the step, picking it up. A girl who came to the door with a bad limp and said something about a drain, and went in again. Callie had not looked at them long. She had been looking for fourteen.

Now there was just the boy, on the step, in the dark.

She did not say anything. Neither did he. They sat on their two steps, two doors apart, and looked at the lamp at the end of the row.

Then the lamp went out.

It did not flicker. It went out the way it had gone out every night on the plain, all at once, and the dark came down over the whole row, and there was nothing to look at any more.

"It does that every night," said the boy, across the dark. His voice was quiet and very tired. "At the first hour. They've got a written reason."

"I know," said Callie. "I watched it do it from the plain. For nine nights."

He was quiet for a while.

"What is it?" she said. "The reason."

"Nobody's told me," said the boy. "I've asked twice."

They sat in the dark. Somewhere down the row a stove ticked. The wet was coming up through the step into her, and she did not move.

"There's a mule," said Callie. "Out on the big road. By the rail. Somebody buried it. There's a board on a stake." She did not know why she was telling him. It came out, the way things came out of her in silences, the way her mother had always said they did. "It says *Pitch*."

He did not say anything for such a long time that she thought he had gone in.

"He pulled a cart three hundred miles," said the boy at last. His voice was very level, in the way a voice is when somebody is holding it there. "He was nine hundred pounds of extremely disagreeable animal. I asked somebody to get him off the road."

"Somebody did," said Callie. "Somebody dug it square. And they painted the board."

The boy did not answer. After a while she heard him stand up, slowly, the way a person stands up who has something broken and something else strapped. She heard the door of his hut open and not close.

"Thank you," he said, from the doorway. "For telling me."

Then the door closed, and Callie sat alone on her step in the dark, in a place that had written everything down, with her mother in a long building somewhere at the north end of it and her hook in a bin with a paper tag on the handle, and her card on a desk somewhere, face up, square to the edge, waiting for somebody else to look at it.
