# Chapter 37 — Callie

It stopped at a hundred and fifty-eight.

Callie would carry that number for the rest of her life. She did not decide to. It simply went into her, the way the number of husks on the causeway had gone into her, and the ninety-one seconds on the hill. A hundred and fifty-eight. Her mother's right hand stopped plucking at the blanket, and lay still, and her mother took a long breath like somebody coming up from under water, and did not say *what*.

She did not say anything. Her eyes were open and they were looking at the canvas, and they did not come round to Callie's face.

"Mum," said Callie.

Nothing.

"*Mum.*"

Her mother's eyes moved. They came round slowly and found her, and looked at her for a long time as if she were a word on a page that her mother had once known how to read.

Then her mother's mouth moved, and nothing came out of it. It moved again.

"Cal," she said. It was thick and slow and wrong at the edges. "Cal. Lie."

"I'm here."

"Lie down." Her mother's left hand found Callie's wrist on the blanket. "You're. Awake."

"So are you."

"Hm," said her mother, and shut her eyes, and was asleep before Callie could say anything else, all at once, the way the lamp goes out when the oil is gone.

Callie did not lie down. She sat on the wagon bed with her mother's hand round her wrist until the light on the edge of the world went out at the same time it always went out, and then for a long time after that, in the dark.

---

In the morning her mother could not stand.

She tried. She woke at dawn, slow and heavy, and it was an hour before her words came back to her properly. When they had, she said she wanted to get down off the wagon, because there were certain things a woman did not do on a wagon bed in front of five people and a horse. Katori and Ines lifted her down between them. Her left leg took her weight. Her right leg did not take anything at all. It folded under her the way it had folded getting off the tailgate on the ninth day, and Ines caught her, and they got her to the side of the road and back again.

When they had her on the bedding against the crates again, her mother lay very still for a while.

"Well," she said at last. "That's new."

Nobody laughed.

"It's all right," said her mother. "You're allowed to. I'd laugh if it was anybody else." She looked down the length of herself at the leg under the blanket. "I can feel it. I just can't tell it anything. It's like shouting at somebody in the next field."

"It'll come back," said Callie. "Like after the others. It came back after the others."

"Maybe." Her mother turned her head and looked at her. "Don't promise me things about my own leg, love. I'll tell you if it comes back. It's my leg. I'll be the first to know."

---

Duchess could put her foot down.

That was the good news, and it was all the good news there was. The mare had stood on three legs all night at the end of the picket line. At first light Pell went to her and ran his hand down the near hind, from the big muscle at the top all the way to the hoof, slowly, and she let him. Then he stood back, and after a long time she put the hoof down on the concrete and put a little of her weight on it, and then a little more.

"She'll walk," Pell said. He did not turn round. "She'll walk because she's a horse and a horse will walk on anything short of a broken leg, and it's not broken. I'd know." He was quiet. "She won't pull."

"Nothing?" said Katori.

"She'll pull an empty wagon at a walk, on the flat, if everybody who can walk walks beside it. For an hour. Then she stands." He turned round at last. His face was very tired and very old in the grey light. "She won't pull the crates. Not today, not tomorrow. Not for a week. Not with what she did last night on that leg."

Nobody said anything.

"Say the number," said Katori.

"Four days, with the crates, at the pace she'd go if I made her go. Which I won't. So it's not four days with the crates. It's a week of standing here while she mends, or it's the crates off." He looked at the wagon bed. At the crates stacked two high along the near side, under the net. At Callie's mother lying against them. "Without the crates, three days. Maybe less, if the road stays like this."

Callie looked at her mother.

Her mother was looking at the sky. She had heard every word.

---

"The barrow," said Callie.

They all turned.

"My barrow. It's on the bed. It's light and it's got two wheels, and it goes eleven miles a day. Twelve on a good road." She had worked it out in the dark with her mother's hand round her wrist. She had worked it out six times. "Somebody pulls my mother on it. Otto can. The strapping goes across his chest, not his arms. He can pull it with one hand on the handle. And me, and Ines, we go ahead with it. And you come behind with the wagon and the crates at Duchess's pace. And we meet you at the gate."

"No," said Katori.

"You didn't — "

"I heard all of it. No." Katori did not raise her voice. "You saw the fourth one last night. You saw where it came from. It came from the side nobody was standing on. And you want to split six people into two lots of three, on a plain with hounds on it, with a girl with two ribs gone and a boy with one arm and a woman who can't stand in one lot, and a lame horse and an old man in the other." She looked at Callie steadily. "That's two sides nobody's standing on. It's the worst plan you've made since I've known you, and you've only made it because it's your mother."

Callie opened her mouth and shut it again.

"You're allowed," said Katori, more quietly. "I'd have made it too. It's still no."

---

She went and stood by the wagon bed.

She did not say anything for a while. She stood at the tailboard with her hands flat on the boards, the way she had stood in the grey before dawn on the ninth day, when Callie had been sitting against the wheel and had not been supposed to see. She looked at the crates. There were eleven of them, stacked two high along the near side of the bed under the net, square and heavy and roped, the size of small trunks. Callie had lived beside them for nearly four weeks. She had slept against them and leaned on them and caught one of them coming off the tailboard at the bottom of the scarp. She had never once been told what was in them, and she had never once asked.

Katori put her hand flat on the top of the nearest one. She held it there. Her head went down.

Then she lifted it.

"There's a bridge on the atlas," she said. "Pell. North. The old kind, where one road goes over another one."

Pell opened the book. He did not need to look for it. "Four miles. A flyover. There's nothing under it but another road that goes nowhere."

"Is there shade under it?"

"There's a pier at each end. Concrete." He looked up. "There'll be shade."

"Then we'll take them to the bridge," said Katori, "and we'll put them under the north pier, where it's dry. And we'll mark it in the book. And we'll go on without them, and we'll come back for them." She took her hand off the crate. "They'll keep. She won't."

---

It took them all morning to get four miles, with Duchess walking and everybody else walking beside her except Callie's mother, and Callie, who walked for an hour and then was made to ride.

The flyover came up out of the plain like something left behind by a giant. It was a long grey concrete bridge on two thick piers, standing across the old road, with a second road going up a ramp onto it from the west and down another ramp to the east. Neither of the other roads went anywhere. They went up onto the bridge and over it and down again and then simply stopped in the grass, where somebody sixty years ago had stopped building them, or where the grass had taken them back.

Under the north pier it was dry and dim and smelled of old stone. There was nothing there. Callie looked, because she always looked, and Otto looked, because Otto always looked. There was a heap of blown grass against the pier, and a little dry sand, and nothing else at all.

They unloaded the crates.

Callie could not lift. She sat on the tailboard beside her mother and watched them do it. Katori and Otto took each crate down off the bed between them, Otto one-handed, Katori with both. Ines and Pell carried them in under the pier and stacked them against the concrete, two high, the way Katori had stacked them on the bed. It took a long time. Every one of them went down onto the sand with a soft heavy sound, and every time, Callie watched Katori's face, and Katori's face did not do anything.

When the eleventh was down, Katori stood in the dim under the pier and looked at them. Then she took the net off the wagon and laid it over the whole stack, and weighted the edges with stones.

"Pell," she said.

Pell wrote. He wrote for a long time. When he was done he turned the book round, not to Callie but to Katori, and Katori read it, and nodded.

Then Pell turned it round to all of them.

*Crates, eleven, all, under the north pier of the flyover on the high road. K.'s call.*

"Concur," said Pell, and wrote it.

"Concur," said Ines.

"Concur," said Otto.

Pell looked at Callie.

She sat on the tailboard. She looked at the dim space under the pier, and the net weighted with stones, and Katori standing beside it with her hands at her sides.

"I can't," Callie said. "I don't know what's in them. I don't know what I'd be agreeing to."

"Then don't," said Katori. "It's mine."

Pell wrote. When he turned the book round again the line said: *Concur — P., I., O. C. declines — doesn't know what's in them. Correct of her.*

"Correct of her," said Katori, reading it, and something moved at the corner of her mouth. "Pell, you old fraud."

"I write down what happened," said Pell, and shut the book.

---

Without the crates the wagon rode high and loose and rattled on every tar seam, and Duchess pulled it.

She did not pull it well. She went at a walk, short on the near hind, with her head low. Every hour Pell stopped her and let her stand for as long as she wanted, and nobody argued. But she pulled. The empty wagon came along behind her with the barrow lashed on the bed and the casks and the tins of ash, and Callie's mother lying against the bedding where the crates had been, and it was a thing a lame horse could pull.

They made nine miles that day.

Nobody said anything about it. Pell wrote the number in the book at the halt and did not show it to anybody. Katori walked at the head of the wagon beside Otto, looking north, and did not look back once toward the flyover. By the evening it was a small grey shape on the edge of the plain behind them, and then it was nothing.

"What was in them?" said Callie, at the evening halt, to Ines, very low. She had not meant to ask. It came out.

Ines looked at her for a moment across the fire.

"I don't know," she said. "I've carried them nine hundred miles and I don't know. Neither does Otto. Pell might." She put an arrow back in the quiver. "It's something she promised somebody she'd carry. That's all she ever told me. And she just put it under a bridge for your mother."

Callie sat with that.

"I didn't ask her to," she said.

"No," said Ines. "Nobody asked her to. That's how you know it was hers."

---

Her mother's words came back by the second day without the crates. Not all of them. Enough.

She lay on the bedding with her hat tipped over her eyes against the sun and talked, when she could. She talked about clouds again, and about the wind. She talked about Thistle Ford, which she never talked about. She told Otto about the grain elevator and the tackle that ran up its face and the men hauling on the fall, and Otto listened as if it were the best story he had ever heard, and asked what was in the buildings. When a word would not come she stopped and waited for it, and if it did not come she used another one, and once she used the wrong one entirely and called the sky *the ceiling*. She heard herself do it, and laughed, and said the sky had been a ceiling all her life and she had only just noticed.

In the afternoon, when the others were walking ahead, she said, "Callie. Come up here a minute."

Callie climbed up onto the bed, slowly, and sat down beside her.

"The jar," said her mother.

"It's there."

"How many?"

"Four."

"I want one." Her mother did not lift the hat off her eyes. "Not now. At the gate. The morning we get there. I've been thinking about it since the hill." She was quiet. "I'm not going in through that gate on my back. I've been carried a long way by a great many people who had other things to do, and I've let them, and I'll let them again. But not through the gate. I want to walk in. On my own two feet. I want them to see me walk in."

"It'll cost you," said Callie. "After. The day after the hill, you couldn't — "

"I know what it'll cost me. I was there." Her mother lifted the hat at last and looked at her. "It's mine to spend. We settled that at the bottom of the scarp. The other three are yours. I'm not going to argue about the other three."

"They're not mine. They're ours."

"They're yours." Her mother put the hat back. "And now I'll tell you the other thing, and then I'm going to sleep, because I'm tired, and I'd like to be tired about something else for a while.

"When we get there, you're going to want to sign things." She said it very evenly. "You're going to stand in front of whoever's got the book, and they're going to tell you what it costs, whatever it is. And you're going to say yes to all of it before they've finished the sentence, because it's me. I know you. I've known you since you had your head stuck in the elevator rails." She was quiet for a moment. "Don't. Not without me. Whatever it is. Whatever they say. You don't sign one thing for me without me there to hear it."

Callie sat on the wagon bed and could not say anything at all.

"Promise me," said her mother.

"I promise."

"Good." Her mother's left hand came out from under the blanket and found Callie's knee and patted it, once. "Now go and read some grass. I'm going to have opinions about clouds."

---

On the third day without the crates Ines took out Callie's stitches.

They stopped at noon in the lee of the wagon. Ines sat Callie down on the tailboard with her back to her, and parted her hair with two fingers, and looked for a long time without saying anything.

"It's closed," she said at last. "It's ugly. It'll be a scar. You'll have a bald line an inch long over your left ear for the rest of your life."

"I don't care."

"You'll care when you're twenty. Everybody does." Ines got the small scissors out of the kit, the ones for thread. "Hold still. This is the part people don't like."

It did not hurt much. It was three small tugs, one after another, and a sting each time, and then Ines put the three bits of black thread on the tailboard beside her in a row, the way she laid out arrows.

"There," she said. "Three."

Callie looked at them. They were very small.

"You're good at it," she said. "Sewing people."

"I'm not good at it. I was good at dresses. I had to get good at people on this road because nobody else was going to." Ines put the scissors away. "Your mother taught me more about it in one night in a pump house than I'd learned in eleven years." She was quiet for a moment, with the kit in her lap. "She's a good teacher. She doesn't tell you you're doing it right. She just tells you *smaller* until you are."

"She did that to me with snares."

"I believe it," said Ines.

---

Callie told her mother the lines that evening.

She had not meant to tell anybody but Pell. But her mother asked, lying in the last of the light with her hat off and her hair in Ines's braid, and Callie found she wanted to say them to somebody who was not writing them down.

"*Callie. Hunter. I read ground,*" she said. "That's the first. And then *two doors*. I've decided I'm putting that. Pell says his book's only worth anything because there's nothing in it that's a lie, and I don't want to put a lie in theirs either. Even if it's theirs."

"Go on."

"I don't know what comes after. I don't know how much to tell them. If I tell them everything, they'll make a column for it. Katori said. And then they'll decide what the column's for."

Her mother lay quiet for a while.

"Your grandfather sold a horse once," she said. "At the Reach, before you were born. A good horse. And he stood in front of the man and told him every single thing that was wrong with it. The splint on the off fore. The way it wouldn't cross running water. The day it put him in a ditch. Everything. And I said to him afterward, Dad, you'll never sell a horse again, talking like that." She smiled a little at the sky. "And he said, *the man knows what he's buying now. He'll never come back to me angry. And he'll never try to make that horse into something it isn't.*"

Callie thought about it.

"Put the edges in," said her mother. "What you can't do. People who make columns, they like a thing with edges. It's the things with no edges that frighten them. They'll make a column for anything they can't see the end of." She shut her eyes. "Put the edges in, love. Put all of them in. Then they'll know what they've got."

---

That night Callie lay on the wagon bed beside her mother and put the rest of it together, the way she put a husk line together, one sign at a time.

*I can make an edge come off my hook. One shape. It's ugly and I can't change it. About a minute and I'm half empty.*

*I can stand in a ward. I can't walk in it. If I lift my foot it comes off.*

*I can put the ward into my hook instead of me. Then nothing can move the hook. But then I've got nothing on me at all. Ninety-one seconds was everything I had.*

*I can't do any of it at present. I'm dry.*

She lay there with it for a long time. It was all true. There was nothing in it that was not in Pell's book already, one way or another.

Then she thought of the thing that was not in there. The word.

*Light.* Katori's grandmother's word, the one Katori had said beside a hook lying in the gravel at the bottom of a scarp. *It's the only thing my family ever gave me that I haven't used.* It was not a thing Callie could do. It was not an edge. It was a word that belonged to somebody else's family, that had been given to her beside a hook in the gravel.

She would leave it out. That was the one thing she would leave out. Not because it was a lie, but because it was not hers to give them.

She lay in the dark and felt the decision settle in her, the way the weight of a sack settles when you have got under it properly. Then she slept.

---

On the fourth day the country began to change.

It happened slowly, and then all at once. In the morning the plain was the plain, pale and flat and going away forever. By noon there were fences. They were not the old sagging strands of rust Callie had been looking at for a week. They were new, or newer: posts that had not been sawn off, and wire strung tight between them, and a gate in one of them with a chain on it and a lock on the chain. By the middle of the afternoon there was a field with a crop in it, something low and green in rows. There was nobody working it. But somebody had planted it, in rows, and somebody would come back.

"We're close," said Pell.

At dusk they saw it properly for the first time.

It was not a light any more. It was a place. It lay across the north edge of the plain in a long low line, a bank of earth higher than any wall Callie had ever seen, green on top, running east and west further than she could see. There were lights on it. They were small and white and steady, spaced out along the top of the bank at even distances, and there were more of them behind it, low down, where the bank hid whatever was inside. And at one point in the middle, lower down than the others, there was a single light brighter than the rest.

"The gate," said Katori.

The rain came an hour after dark.

It came in off the west on a cold wind, the first rain Callie had felt since before the causeway. It came slowly at first, big separate drops that hit the canvas like thrown gravel, and then all at once, grey and steady, drumming on the wagon bed and running off the hoops and the cut rear hoop that still sagged into the canvas where the Edge had gone through it on the first night. Duchess put her head down and stood. They could not go on in it, and they did not try. They got under the wagon and under the canvas, all of them, and sat in the wet and the dark, and waited.

Callie lay on the bed under the canvas beside her mother and looked north through the rain.

She could see the lights on the bank, blurred and small. She watched them for a long time. And then, at the time it always went out, the whole of it went out, every light on the whole length of the bank at once, and there was nothing out there in the rain but the dark.

"It's a decision," said Pell, somewhere under the wagon. He said it very quietly, to himself. "It's not broken. It's a decision."

---

Her mother drank her Handful at first light, sitting up on the tailboard with the rain gone and the whole plain steaming.

Callie tipped it into the tin cup from the jar herself. Her hand did not shake at all this time. She put the lid back on the jar with three Handfuls in it and handed her mother the cup, and her mother drank it the way she took all her medicine, in one go, with a face.

"It tastes of nothing," said her mother.

"I know."

They waited.

It took longer than on the hill. Callie counted, and did not say that she was counting. At a hundred her mother had not moved. At a hundred and fifty her mother put her left hand flat on the tailboard. At a hundred and eighty she took a breath that went all the way down.

Then she put her right foot on the ground.

She did not stand on it. Not yet. She sat on the tailboard with her right foot flat on the wet road and looked down at it. She moved it, a little. It moved.

"Oh," said her mother. "There you are."

She stood up. She did it slowly, with her left hand on the tailboard and Ines at her elbow, and when she was up she swayed. And then she did not sway. She stood on the road on both feet in the steaming morning, grey under the eyes, her right hand hanging against her side like a glove on a table, and looked north.

"Right," she said. "Somebody find my hat."

---

The road that went to the gate was not the road they had come in on.

It was bigger. It came in from the south-west, a great wide road of old concrete six lanes across, with a steel rail running down the middle of it and another rail at each edge. Their own road ran into it a few miles south of the bank and stopped, the way roads did, and they went up the ramp onto the big road with Duchess leaning into the collar, and Pell said, "Well, that's more like it," and meant the width.

On either side of the big road the country was all grass, rough and wet and pale, dropping away very slightly on each side to a ditch. Callie could see the bank ahead now in daylight, green and huge, with the gate in the middle of it, three miles off.

She was sitting on the board beside Pell. She saw it first because she was the one looking.

"Stop," she said.

Pell stopped.

She got down. It took her a long time and nobody helped her, because they could see from her face that she did not want help. She walked out to the edge of the big road, to the rail on the east side, and stood there with her hand on it, looking down.

The rail was bent. Something had hit it very hard from the road side and bent a length of it outward like a bow, twice the length of the wagon, and then gone on.

And in the wet grass beyond the rail there were marks.

---

She read them the way she read everything, one sign at a time.

There had been a husk line across this pasture once, maybe. It was gone now. Something else was written over the top of it, the way the fresh trough had been written over the old ones on the scarp. It had come across the grass from the east. It had come heavy. The ground was pressed down in holes the size of a bucket, deep, one after another, and there were three of them to each stride and not four. Three legs. Heavy, and set wide, the way a thing stands that is made for pushing.

And beside the three there was a fourth mark. It was not a hole. It was a line, a long straight cut through the grass and into the turf underneath, as clean as a knife goes through a loaf. It was the length of a man. It went on, and stopped, and began again further on, and the grass on either side of it had been sliced off level, as if somebody had been mowing with a blade as long as the wagon.

The marks went across the pasture and up onto the big road and hit the rail and went on. Callie followed them with her eyes. They went up the wet grass toward a long straight ditch that ran beside the road, deeper than the others, a drainage cut. And there, at the edge of the ditch, the grass was flattened in a great wide circle, and the ground was scraped.

It had been scraped properly. She could see that from here. Somebody had come out with bins and lifted it, a lot of it, more than she had ever seen lifted in one place. They had done it carefully. They had done it along the seams, not across, and there were round marks in the grass where the bins had stood, in a row. But lifters always missed something. And in the bottom of the ditch, where the water ran, there was still a thin line of silver lying in the low place, going nowhere, the way it lay in the seams of the causeway.

At the edge of the ditch, where the grass was flattest, there was a hollow in the ground. It was the size of a person lying down.

"Ines," said Callie. Her voice came out very quiet.

Ines came and stood beside her at the rail. She looked for a long time.

"That's not a Flask," Ines said at last. "That's not anything I've got a word for."

"There's a word," said Pell, from the board. He had not got down. He was looking at the bent rail. "I've heard it said twice in my life. A Barrel." He was quiet. "I never met anybody who'd seen one and was still standing up."

"Somebody saw this one," said Callie. She was looking at the hollow at the edge of the ditch. "Somebody got down in that ditch under it. And it's dead. And it's been lifted." She looked at the three-legged marks going up the grass, and the long clean cut beside them. "Somebody killed it."

Nobody said anything.

Behind them, on the wagon bed, her mother was standing up with one hand on the hoop and her hat pulled down, looking at the marks in the grass the way she looked at a ditch.

"Well," she said. "Someone's had a day."

---

It was Otto who found the other thing. Of course it was.

He had walked on up the road beside Duchess's head while the others stood at the rail, because the mare was restless and wanted moving. Fifty paces on he stopped. He stood looking at something on the west side of the road. Then he turned round and looked back at Pell, and did not say anything, and his face was red.

Pell got down off the board.

Callie went too, slowly. By the time she got there, Pell was standing at the west rail with his hands at his sides.

On the far side of the rail, just off the road, the turf had been cut and lifted and put back. It was a long low mound, longer than a man, the earth still dark and wet under the new rain. Somebody had dug it with care. The edges were square. And at the head of it somebody had driven a stake into the ground, and nailed a board to the stake, and painted something on the board in thick white paint, a little crooked, by a hand that had not done much painting.

It was a single word.

*PITCH.*

Pell stood and looked at it for a long time.

He did not say anything. He did not take the atlas out. The wind came across the wet pasture from the west, and the grass moved round the mound, and Duchess put her head down and blew. Callie stood beside him and waited, because she did not know what else to do.

"Somebody named it," Pell said at last.

His voice was perfectly level. It was the voice he used for distances. It did not go up at the end.

"It was a mule," said Callie. "It must have been. That size. It must have been theirs, whoever it was." She looked at the board. "Somebody wanted it written down."

"Yes," said Pell. "Somebody did."

He stood there a moment longer. Then he put out his hand and laid it flat on the top of the board, the way he had laid it on Duchess's leg at first light, and held it there, and took it away.

"Right," he said, and turned round. "That's a gate up there, and it's got a lamp on it in daylight, and I'd like to be in front of it before this lady's good day runs out. Otto, walk on."
