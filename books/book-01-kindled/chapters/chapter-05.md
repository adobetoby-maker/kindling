# Chapter 5 — Jab

The twists of bark were folded in birch paper the way the Elder folded them, and there were nineteen.

Jab counted them by touch, because striking a light would have woken Tonk, and Tonk had been asleep for less than an hour. Nineteen. Two a day was the schedule. The road had six days left in it if the weather held and eight if it did not, and nineteen halved came to nine and a half, which was more than eight. He had margin. The only reason a person counted margin at the cold end of a night was that he was about to spend some of it.

Tonk's breathing had gone short and quick again. He was not crying. Tonk cried when he was frightened and went quiet when it hurt, which Jab wished were the other way around, because frightened he could do something about.

"I'm here," Jab said. "I've got it."

He took one twist out and folded the paper back over the others and put them in the inside pocket, and then he sat in the dark with the twist in his fist and did the arithmetic one more time, because it was not only a twist. The night dose belonged four hours from now. Spending it now meant the morning dose came late, and the morning was the long climb out of the draw, and a late dose meant carrying Tonk up eight hundred feet of switchback through the worst hour the bark could give him.

Or he could let his brother lie here until first light with his breath coming like that.

It was not a decision. It had never once been a decision. He only made himself walk all the way through it every time so that he would not become a person who stopped walking through it.

He tipped a little water into the cup, shook the shaved bark in, and worked it with his thumb until the water went the color of weak tea and smelled like a wet stump. Then he got his arm under Tonk's shoulders and lifted him enough.

"Bark," Tonk said, from a long way down.

"Bark."

"It's the wrong one."

"It's the only one there is."

Tonk drank it in three swallows with his face screwed up, and shuddered at the end the way he always did, and said, "It tastes like a cold spoon," which was the truest thing anybody had ever said about it and which he said every single time.

"It does."

"You have some."

"I had mine."

"You didn't." Tonk's eyes were shut. His hand came up and patted around until it found Jab's jaw, and stayed there. "Liar."

"Go to sleep."

He held his fingers against the side of Tonk's throat until the fast little pulse under them began, slowly, to slow. The fever was up but not at the top of its arc — he had learned the difference in the first week, the way the skin went from hot-and-damp to hot-and-dry, and this was the damp kind, which was the better kind. He laid his brother back down on the folded hide and tucked the blanket under him and left the bad leg out from under it the way it had to be.

Then he sat with his back against the lattice of the frame and did not sleep, because it was two hours to light and sleeping for two hours was worse than not sleeping at all.

---

They were nine days out. The Elder had counted the road at eighteen days for a man walking loaded, and twenty if the weather turned, and she had said both numbers in the same voice she used for saying how much salt a fish took, which was her way of telling him she had not made them up to comfort him.

Jab had done it in nine and a half days so far, and he had done it carrying a four-year-old.

He broke camp in the gray. It had a fixed order now, and the order was the only thing standing between him and the kind of mistake that ended people out here: fire drowned and scattered; water skins filled and both stoppered, because he had lost half a skin on the third day to one that was not; food bag closed with the double turn; blanket rolled inside the hide so the hide took the wet. Then the frame.

The frame was his own work, built in two evenings in a house with nobody left in it, and it was the best thing he had ever made.

It was lattice, split ash over a willow spine, lashed at every crossing with hide strips soaked and shrunk on — the same weave anybody in the valley could put together for a grandmother who could no longer walk the fish path, or for a baby on steep ground. He had built it at a height to carry a child of Tonk's weight against his back, with the load riding on a folded hide belt at his hips rather than hanging off his shoulders, because shoulders gave out in a day and hips would go for a week. On the right side he had lashed a rail of green ash, curved, running out and down, so that Tonk's bad leg could lie along it and be strapped at the ankle and the thigh and never, ever hang.

The rail was why he could not walk through a gap narrower than his own shoulders and a hand more. The belt was why he could not get out of the thing with one hand. Both of those were the price of Tonk not screaming, and Jab had paid it nine days running and would go on paying it.

He got the frame onto a rock at the right height and came back for his brother.

"Up we go."

"Not yet."

"Now."

"I'm asleep."

"You're talking."

"Asleep people talk," Tonk said, with his eyes shut and his mouth doing something that was very nearly a grin, and Jab, who had slept perhaps four hours in three days, found that he had laughed.

Loading took a long time and could not be hurried. He put his hands under and lifted with the bad leg supported all the way along its length, elbow under the knee, forearm under the calf, the way the Elder had shown him with her hands on his hands, and Tonk still made a sound at the turn no matter how slowly it was done. Then the seat, then the ankle strap, then the thigh strap, then the chest band, then the blanket, then the hide over the top, then the wooden fish put into Tonk's hand because if the fish was not in his hand before the hood went up there would be a search, and a search meant unloading.

The fish was as long as Jab's thumb, smoked black by a lifetime of hanging near a fire, with one fin broken off before Tonk was born. Their mother had made it. Tonk called it Little and had never once been persuaded that a fish could have another name.

"Little's cold," Tonk said.

"Put him under the blanket."

"He wants to see."

"Then he'll be cold."

Tonk considered this with the seriousness of a man weighing a season's fish. "He'll see," he decided.

Jab backed into the frame, got the belt around his hips and cinched it on the fourth hole, which had been the third hole nine days ago, and stood up out of his knees with a hundred and some pounds of brother and food and hide and water coming up with him.

The old sore on the point of his left hip opened as it took the weight. It did that every morning. It had got down to a rhythm — a bright hot line for twenty steps, then a dull one for the rest of the day, then the discovery every night of what it had become. He had looked at it once, four days ago, and had decided not to look at it again until Meridian, because there was nothing in the bag that would make it better and only one thing that would make it worse, and that was knowing.

"Ready," he said.

"Ready," Tonk said.

---

The climb out of the draw took two hours and ten rests.

Jab counted in rests, not in miles. Miles were a flat country's idea. In the high country a person got a certain number of standings-up out of a day, and that was the true measure of what he had left, and by the tenth rest of a morning he could tell you what kind of afternoon was coming.

The trail went up the north wall in switchbacks somebody had cut a long time ago and nobody had touched since, so that half the corners had slumped into the drop and had to be taken on the inside with a hand on the rock. That was the rail's ground: the rail rode out over nothing on the corners, and Jab took every one of them slowly, with his weight into the hill, feeling for where his own width had gone. Twice he had to stop and back up and take a corner at a different angle, because the rail had found a root or a rock and he could not see it and could only feel the frame try to turn him.

At the fourth rest he set the frame's foot on a boulder and leaned forward into the belt with his hands on his knees and breathed until the gray went out of the edges of things.

"Four," Tonk said, behind his ear.

"Four."

"How many are there?"

"Ten, probably."

"That's a lot."

"It's not so many."

"It's a *lot*," said Tonk, with feeling, and then, because he had been four for six weeks and the world was still mostly a thing to be named: "That rock looks like a man sitting down."

Jab looked. Forty feet up the slope a slab had come off the cliff and landed on end, with a second stone on top of it and a heap of scree fanned out below like a spread coat.

"It does," he said.

"That's the Sitting Man."

"All right."

"When we come back we'll say hello to him."

Jab kept his eyes on the trail and the gray went on standing at the edges of things for a little while longer than it had any business doing.

"All right," he said.

They did not come back. There was nothing to come back to, and Tonk knew that in the way a four-year-old knew it, which was that he knew it on some days and on other days he did not, and there was no telling in advance which kind of day it would be. Jab had learned to let it stand. Correcting him took nothing away from him and cost them both an hour.

He got the seventh corner wrong.

It was a simple corner and he had taken forty like it. The outer half had gone down the hill in some spring or other and left a lip of turf a foot and a half wide with a root growing through it, and he stepped onto the root instead of over it because he was tired and because at the moment he stepped his mind was in the food bag, doing the thing it did unasked: fourteen fish, a double handful of dried root, four days of eating if he ate like a man walking loaded, six if he ate like a man who had decided not to.

The root rolled. His boot went off the front of it and the frame came around with all its weight and took him toward the drop.

He got his left hand into the rock. There was nothing in the rock to get a hand into — no crack, no ledge — so what he actually did was press his whole forearm flat against the cliff and pull himself into it the way you pull a door shut, and the frame swung back, and Tonk shrieked once, short, more startled than hurt, and then they were standing still on a foot and a half of turf with Jab's cheek against cold stone.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. It's all right. I'm sorry."

"You *stepped* wrong."

"I did."

"You said don't step on roots."

"I did say that."

Tonk was silent for a moment. Then, generously: "You can have one more."

Jab put his forehead against the rock and laughed, and it came out shaky. His arms had begun the fine shudder they did afterward. He stayed where he was until it stopped, because this was the tenth time in nine days that something like that had happened, and he had made himself a rule about it after the second: never take the next step angry. The next step was the one that mattered. The one behind him was finished.

They made the top at the tenth rest, and he had been right about ten, and he took no pleasure in it.

---

The high ground ran south along the shoulder of the range in open larch, half of it bare already, the rest gone the yellow they went before they let go. Under it the ground was springy and good, all needle and moss, and the walking was the best it had been since the second day. He got two hours of it and made the most of them.

He talked the whole way. That had started as a thing to keep Tonk awake when Tonk had first begun sliding into the heavy fever sleep that was not sleep, and it had stayed because it turned out to be good for both of them.

"See the bird?"

"Where?"

"Dead tree, four up from the one that's fallen."

"I see it. What is it?"

"It's a jay."

"What does he do?"

"He steals."

"What does he steal?"

"Everything. He steals from squirrels. He watches where a squirrel puts his nuts, and then he waits until the squirrel goes away, and then he takes them, and then he tells all the other jays about it very loudly, because he can't help it."

"That's bad," Tonk said, delighted.

"It's very bad."

"Say what he says."

Jab said what the jay said, twice, badly enough that the actual jay took offense and left, and Tonk laughed until he coughed, and the cough turned into a small sound at the end of it as the coughing moved the leg, and the laughing stopped.

They took the noon rest on a windfall with a view down the length of the valley they had come up.

Jab got him out of the frame for it, which he did twice a day, because a body that stayed in one position all day came apart in ways he had been warned about and had already begun to see at the shoulder blades and the base of the spine. He laid Tonk on the hide with the leg supported, and got the wrappings off, and made himself look at it in daylight.

The lower leg was not the right shape. He had known that from the first night, when he had got Tonk up out of the water onto the gravel and had seen the line of it and had understood, in a cold clean way that had nothing to do with grief, that his brother's life had just changed. Six weeks on, the swelling had gone hard and shiny and hot to the back of his hand, and the whole lower leg was a color that a leg should not be, going toward the ankle in a way that was worse this week than last. Low on the outside there was a small place where the skin had opened over the bone, and the Elder had drawn it on the dirt floor with a stick, the bone and the dark line running up the inside of it, and had said, *That is what will kill him, not the break. The break is only what let it in.*

He cleaned it with boiled water gone cold in the second skin, and put on the last of the salve, and made a note in the part of his head that kept the ledger — *salve finished, day nine* — and wrapped it again, not tight.

"Look at me," he said. "How is it?"

"It hurts."

"A little or a lot?"

Tonk thought about it. This had become a real conversation between them over the weeks, with a real vocabulary that they had built together, because *does it hurt* was a useless question to ask a person who was always in pain.

"It's the sharp one," Tonk said. "Not the big one."

"The sharp one's when I move it?"

"The sharp one's all the time. It's just *small*." He was watching Jab's face, and Jab made sure there was nothing on it worth reading. "The big one's later."

"When later?"

"When it's dark."

Which was true, and which was also the medicine wearing off, and which was also, Jab had come to suspect, not the whole story — because the big one had begun to come at other times lately, in the middle of good afternoons, out of nothing. He filed that too.

He fed them both. Half a smoked fish each and a piece of root that had to be chewed for a long time before it agreed to be food. Thirteen fish. Tonk ate all of his, which was the best news of the day, and asked for more, which was the second-best, and Jab gave him a second half out of tomorrow and told himself he would set snares tonight.

"Sing the one," Tonk said afterward, lying back with his eyes half shut and the fish in his fist.

"Which one?"

"The one about the boat."

There was no one about a boat. There had never been one about a boat. There had been an evening in the smoke house in the summer when their father had been in a certain mood and had made up eleven verses about a boat that could not decide where to go, and everybody had contributed a verse, and their mother's verse had been the funniest and had involved the boat's opinion of a particular cousin, and Tonk had been three and had laughed so hard he had fallen off the bench. Nobody had written it down. Nobody wrote anything down.

Jab could remember four of the eleven verses. He had been able to remember six in the first week.

"I'll sing you the four I've got," he said.

"Sing the *whole* one."

"The four's what there is."

"You're not *trying*."

"I'm not," Jab agreed, and sang the four, and Tonk corrected him twice on words he was fairly sure he had right, and Jab let him have both.

---

The rain came at the middle of the afternoon.

He had watched it come for an hour, a gray wall dragging itself up the valley from the southwest with the tops of the ridges going out of the world one after another ahead of it, and he had spent that hour deciding.

The plan had been to stop at the head of the long descent, where the map in his head — the Elder's map, given to him in an evening, said twice, made him say back — put a bench of flat ground and a stand of fir. Stopping there meant a wet camp in open larch with the rain coming across the shoulder and nothing to break it. The alternative was to go over the shoulder and down into the creek country in the rain, another two hours at least, with the light going, on ground he did not know, looking for a fir stand he had been told was there and had never seen.

Wet camp meant Tonk wet. Tonk wet meant Tonk cold. Tonk cold, with the fever running its arc, was the thing the Elder had spent the longest on and had come back to twice, and that was how Jab knew which part of her instructions she had actually been frightened about.

He went over the shoulder.

The rain caught them a quarter of the way down and it was not the soft kind. It came sideways with the last of the autumn's temper in it, cold enough to make his hands stupid inside ten minutes, and the trail turned into a shallow creek of its own, running brown over the rock, and every step had to be put down with the toe first and tested.

He got the hide over Tonk and tied it at the front and at the rail, and left one hand's width open for air, and after that he could not see his brother at all and could only talk.

"Still with me?"

"Yes."

"Little all right?"

"He's *under*," Tonk said, from inside, offended on the fish's behalf, and then, a hundred steps later, smaller: "Jab. It's loud."

"That's just the rain on the hide. It's right by your ear, that's all. It's not as loud out here."

"It's loud *in* here."

"I know."

"I don't like the hide."

"I know," Jab said. "Twenty more minutes and then a fire."

He did not know whether it would be twenty minutes. He had said a number because a number was something Tonk could hold, and because he had learned in the first week that the truth, delivered whole to a four-year-old on a bad afternoon, was not honesty; it was just weight put down on somebody who could not carry it.

It was fifty minutes.

The creek country came up out of the rain at him in pieces — a sound first, a big unbroken roar down and to the left that he did not like the size of, then alder, then the dark of the fir stand exactly where the Elder had put it, and he went into the trees and out of the wind and stood still for a moment in the sudden quiet with water running off his chin.

He built the camp in the dark. Hide up on a frame of cut poles with the low side to the wind; a bed of fir tips a foot deep, because the ground would take more heat out of a person in a night than the air ever would; then fire, which took him three tries and most of what was left of his hands' feeling, and then the whole thing again in reverse order to get Tonk out of the frame and dry and into the blanket.

Tonk had gone quiet somewhere in the last mile. Not asleep. Quiet.

Jab got his wet shirt off him and got him dry and found him hot — the dry kind of hot, now, the kind that came at the top of the arc — and gave him the night dose two hours before the night dose was due, which made eighteen, then seventeen when he thought about the hour before dawn and took the next one out early so his hands would not have to find it in the dark.

Then he sat with his brother's head on his thigh and his hand flat on his brother's chest, feeling it go up and down, too fast, and watched the rain come off the edge of the hide in a line.

And something under his own breastbone turned over.

He had his hand on Tonk. That was all it was. It was the same hand that had been on him a hundred times a day for six weeks, and the warmth in his palm was the fever, and the warmth in his chest was — he waited for it to be his own heart, because that was what it had been the last three times he had noticed it, three nights in a row now, always with his hand on Tonk and always at the bad end of the night.

It was not his heart. It was under the place his heart was. It was where the metal had gone in, four years ago, when he was thirteen and standing in the river with his whole family on the bar behind him.

Jab sat very still.

The rain came down. The fire took a piece of wet fir and spat at him. Under his palm his brother's chest went up, and down, and up, too fast, and something in his own chest went with it — not in time with it, that was the thing, not in time with his own pulse either, but leaning toward it, the way a compass needle leans.

He had spent four years learning what his own power could do. It could cut green willow. It could split a fish. It could take a hand off a man, if a man ever gave him cause, and had once opened a log jam that would have taken six people a day.

None of that was any use at all against what was in his brother's leg.

He took his hand off Tonk's chest, and the feeling went, and he put it back, and after a moment it came again — faint, unmistakable, and entirely useless, since he had no idea what it was or what to do with it.

"All right," he said out loud, to nobody, in a voice that sounded almost steady. "All right."

Then he put more wood on the fire, and reset his hand where it had been, and made himself stay awake for the hour that the fever would take to turn, because whatever else was true, that hour was coming and somebody had to be in it with him.
