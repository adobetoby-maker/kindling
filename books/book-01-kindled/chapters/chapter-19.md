# Chapter 19 — Callie

Callie walked the scarp at first light, alone, with the hook over her shoulder and nothing lit in her at all. At the bottom of the cut, before she had gone a hundred paces up it, she found out what the hill was.

It was a drain.

She should have known it from the flats. She had stood on the causeway and said it out loud to Katori: *The whole flat drains north-east. Everything that comes across this country comes across that.* But she had been thinking about the pan, and the pan was only the bottom of it. The country above the scarp drained too, all of it, the whole high plain for miles. And when the people who built the road had cut their groove straight up the face of the hill, they had made the lowest, smoothest, straightest way down off the high country in forty miles.

Water had found it first. There were gullies washed down both edges of the road where the ditches had been, and fans of red silt spread out at the bottom where each rain had dumped what it carried. The husks had found it after the water.

The sign was all down the middle of the cut. It went down, not up, and it was in more layers than she had ever seen in one place.

She crouched in the road and read them one at a time, the way you read a page. There was a long shallow trough through the silt fan at the bottom, three or four abreast, crusted over and cracked. That was old, a week or more. Over the top of it was a second trough, narrower, two abreast, with the crust just starting. That was two days. And over the top of that, fresh, with the silt still dark at the bottom where the crust had broken, was a single trough. It was one husk, from last night. It had come down the whole length of the cut and gone out onto the flats and away east, following the grade, the way they all went.

She went on up and found more of the same. It was all going down.

By the time she reached the steep part she understood it. This was not a line crossing a road. It was a line that *was* the road, and had been for years. It did not run like a river. It ran like a leaking tap. Nothing up on the high plain had any reason to come down in a crowd. They drifted into the top of the cut one or two at a time, whenever the drift brought them. They came down it because down was the only way a husk knew, and it went on happening, day after day, a few at a time.

She stood in the road at the bottom of the steep part and did the arithmetic.

One from last night. Two, two days ago. Four or five a week ago. The tap dripped every day or so. Now and then it ran for a while.

The steep part itself was about three hundred paces. The walls of the cut went up on both sides here, twenty feet of grey rock with red earth on top. The road between them was hardly wider than the wagon. It went up at a slant that made her calves ache after fifty paces. At the side of the road, every forty paces, there was a stub of rusted steel flush with the ground, sawn off clean. Those had been the guard posts. Somebody had taken the rest for the steel long ago, as she had told Pell they had.

Near the top, where the cut went through solid rock, there was a crack across the road. It was a seam in the grey stone that the road-builders had cut through, running from one wall of the cut to the other, about a hand deep and two fingers wide. Callie knelt and put her fingers in it. It was full of old silt. The stone on either side was sound.

She knelt there for a while, looking at it. Then she stood up and went back down to tell them.

---

"It comes down," she said. "All of it, all the time. Not a lot at once. A few every day or so, like a tap. There was one last night."

"And today?" said Katori.

"Nobody can say about today. The tap drips when it drips." Callie had squatted down by the fire, and she drew it in the dirt with a stick because she needed her hands doing something. "But they come down the middle. Always the middle. They don't go up the sides and they don't come over the top of the walls. They come straight down the road, because it's the lowest way."

"Into us," said Ines.

"Into whatever's on the road. And on the steep part there's no getting off the road. There's nowhere to put a wagon. The cut's just wide enough for one." She looked up. "It's the causeway again, turned on its end."

Pell had the atlas open on his knee. He did not write anything.

"The good news," Callie said, "is it's one at a time, mostly. That's all a tap is. And it's daylight. And I'm dark."

"Until you're not," said Katori.

Callie did not answer that.

---

They spent the morning at the bottom of the cut practising. Pell said the mare needed a day to rest the leg before he'd put her at that hill, whatever anybody thought of it. Nobody thought anything of it except that he was right.

Callie could stand in the Ward now, a little.

That was what she learned in the first hour. If she dropped her weight and put her back foot behind her and dug it in, and set her body for the catch the way her grandfather had taught her, it came. Not every time. At first it came one time in three, and then one time in two, and then nearly every time. The faint grey shine came over her, the old-glass shine, and Ines's stones stopped a finger's width off her coat and dropped at her feet.

But it would not walk.

She found that out the first time she tried to take a step in it. She lifted her back foot out of the plant, and the whole thing went off her like water off a tipped plate. She tried again, and it went again. She tried sliding her foot instead of lifting it, and it went again.

"On the causeway it walked," she said. "I walked all the way down the wagon in it. I walked two miles in it. I sat in a doorway all night in it, and I got up and went out to them and came back. It came with me everywhere, like a coat. Now it won't come one step."

"On the causeway you weren't holding it," said Katori. "It was holding you. You couldn't get it off. You'd have given anything to have it stay put, on the causeway." She was sitting on a rock at the side of the road with her Ember in her hand, turning it over and over, the flat grey slab. "That's the difference between a thing that happens to you and a thing you're doing. The thing that happens to you doesn't need you to know how. The thing you're doing does. You know how to stand in it now. You don't know how to walk in it yet."

"How do I learn?"

"I don't know," said Katori. "I've told you that. You're going to have to keep telling me things, and I'll keep telling you whether they sound like anything."

So Callie could stand in the Ward, and she could not walk in it. She could lean and get the Edge, the long ugly shape, and come up off it again. She could do either. She could not do both.

She tried. She tried a dozen times. She planted, and the Ward came, and she leaned into the hook for the Edge, and the moment she leaned, her weight went forward off the plant and the Ward went off her. She leaned first and got the Edge, and then tried to plant, and the moment she dropped her weight and set herself, she was coming up off the lean. The grey went out of the hook.

"It's like trying to pull and push at the same time," she said. "With the same arms."

"You've got one pair of feet," said Katori. "And both of them want your feet." She turned the Ember over in her fingers. "On the causeway you had both, for thirteen hours. So it can be done. But you weren't doing it. Something was doing it to you."

---

At noon they ate, and Callie drank more of her tenth. She put two fingers of ash in the cup and felt it go down into the well like rain. Pell watched her do it and did not say anything this time, and did not write anything down.

Katori came and sat beside her.

"My grandmother," said Katori, and stopped.

Callie waited. In fifteen days she had heard Katori say one thing about her family, on the tailgate in the dark: a room with eleven of them in it, and a grandmother saying words. That had been about the Kindling, not about them. There had been the name at the tailgate before dawn too, the one Callie had not caught. She did not count that, because she had not been meant to hear it.

"My grandmother's people kept some old words," Katori said. "Older than the Kindling. Older than the words they say over you. They were Ember people, stone people, and they had words for the Ember that nobody else used." She turned the slab over. "She used to say that there was one thing behind all the doors. Not three. One. That the doors were only how it got into the room, and people argued about the doors because they couldn't see what was on the other side. She called it light. Not the kind you see. Just *light*. That was the word."

"Is it true?"

"I don't know. I didn't believe her. I was thirteen and four months, and I had one door, and it did one thing, and I thought she was an old woman talking the way old women talk." Katori looked at the hook lying across Callie's knees. "But you told me, on the span, that the Edge came up out of a well. And this morning you told me the other one goes down into the same place. Not a different one. The same one."

"The same one," said Callie. "I'm sure. It's the same well."

"Then either you're wrong, or she was right, or it's something else that neither of you knows." Katori stood up. "I don't know which. I just thought you should have the word. It's the only thing my family ever gave me that I haven't used."

---

The wheel was Pell's worry, and it was the wheel that did it.

The spare was a finger wider through the hub than the old one, and after twelve miles of flats it had started to run warm. Pell put his palm flat on it at the noon halt and took it away again quickly and said something short under his breath. He said it would need to come off and be scraped and greased properly before he'd put it at the hill. That meant getting the corner of the wagon up again. The concrete blocks were fifteen miles back at the pump house.

"Pole," said Otto, "and something to prop it on."

They used the wagon pole for a lever again, over a round of stone Otto rolled up from the ditch. Otto and Ines threw their weight on the long end, and the off rear corner of the wagon came up, groaning. For a prop, while they worked, there was nothing on the whole gravel lay-by longer than a boot except the hook.

"Put it under," said Pell. "The curve under the axle, the conduit down to the ground. It's four feet of steel. It'll hold her for ten minutes while I get the nut off."

Callie put it under. She set the forged curve up under the axle beside the hub, and the butt of the conduit down on the gravel, standing nearly straight, and held it steady with both hands. Otto and Ines eased off the lever, slowly, and the weight of that corner of the wagon came down onto the hook.

It held. Pell got down on his knees and went at the axle nut with the spanner.

Then the gravel under the butt of the conduit moved.

It was a small movement. It was only a stone rolling out from under a stone, down in the loose stuff at the edge of the lay-by. But it put the butt of the conduit an inch sideways, and the whole prop leaned, and the weight of the wagon came down its length the wrong way. The butt began to kick out along the ground. Once it went, the wagon would come down on its own axle, onto Pell, who was kneeling underneath with his arm up to the shoulder in the wheel.

Callie did not think. There was no time for it.

Her hands were on the conduit already. She hauled on it, back toward herself, to stop the kick. That was the lean, all her weight going into the steel. And in the same moment her feet set themselves in the gravel. Her back foot went behind her and dug in, and her whole body dropped for the catch, the way it had dropped under the crate the night before and under every sack she had ever caught. That was the brace.

Both at once. She was leaning into the steel and bracing against the ground at the same time.

And because her hands were on the conduit, the brace did not go into her body. It went into the steel.

She felt it go. It went down her arms and into the conduit and along it, the whole length of the pipe and up into the forged curve of the hook. It went into the steel the way the cold of the Edge went into the steel, but it was not the Edge. There was no grey sweep. It was the other thing, the thing that had been closed over her on the causeway, and it went *into the metal*.

The butt of the conduit stopped. It stopped in the middle of kicking out, at a slant no prop could stand at, with the wagon's whole corner on the top of it and nothing under the bottom of it but loose gravel. It did not slide another hair.

The steel had gone pale.

Callie saw it with her own eyes, a foot from her face. It was not light, not any kind you could read by. The dark grey of the conduit and the old black of the forged curve had gone pale, all the way along, the way metal goes pale in a hard frost before the frost shows. It looked as if something were standing inside the steel.

"Don't," said Pell, from under the wagon, very quietly, "move."

"I'm not."

"Otto. The nut. It's off. Get the wheel."

Otto got the wheel. He came in one-armed and dragged it off the axle, and Ines scraped the hub and slapped grease into it with her bare hand, and Otto dragged it back on. Pell spun the nut down and pinned it. It took about as long as it took Callie to count to twenty.

At eleven, she noticed the cold. It was in her hands, and then in her wrists. It was not the cold of the Edge. It was deeper and faster, like a well being drawn down by two buckets at once.

At fourteen, she noticed that Ines, crouched at the hub, had turned her head and was looking at her. Not at the hook. At *her*.

At sixteen, she understood why. There was no shine on her. There was none of the old-glass sheen on her coat or her hands or anywhere on her at all. She was standing in the gravel as bare as a girl in a field. Everything that had closed over her on the causeway was in the steel, and none of it was left for her.

At twenty, Pell said, "Done. Out. Everybody out," and came out from under on his back, and Otto and Ines threw their weight on the lever again, and the corner came up off the hook.

Callie let go.

She meant to let go of the whole thing at once. She did not quite know how. She took her hands off the conduit, and for about three breaths the steel stayed pale, standing there on its own. It was a four-foot pipe and a hook, leaning at an impossible slant in the gravel, holding up nothing now, not falling.

Then she felt it going. It went out of the steel and back toward her, the way a rope goes slack when the person on the far end lets go. The pale drained out of the conduit first, from the bottom up. Then it went out of the hook. Then the whole thing fell over in the gravel with a clang like any piece of pipe.

She sat down next to it.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time. Then everybody did.

"What was that," said Otto. "What *was* that — "

"It didn't move," said Ines. "It didn't move. The butt was going. I saw it going. It was going, and then it wasn't."

"There was nothing on you," said Pell. He was sitting up on the gravel with grease to the elbow. He was looking at Callie in a way he had not looked at her before, the way he looked at a hill. "Not on you. I looked. It was in the hook."

Katori had not said anything. She had come across the lay-by when Pell called and had seen the last of it. Now she crouched down next to the hook where it lay in the gravel. She did not touch it. She looked at it the way she had looked at the snare on the first morning.

"Light," she said.

Callie looked at her.

"That's her word," said Katori. "My grandmother's word." She reached out, slowly, and put one finger on the forged curve, and took it away. "It's cold."

"It's always cold. When it's mine it's cold."

"I know." Katori sat back on her heels. "That wasn't an Edge. There was no shape on it. And it wasn't a ward, not on you. It was the ward, and you put it into the metal. The metal the Edge comes through." She looked up. "What did you do?"

Callie told her. She had to tell it twice, because the first time she said it wrong. She had been leaning to stop the kick and she had been bracing at the same time, both of them, the way she had been trying to do all morning and couldn't. And the difference was her hands. Her hands had been on the steel. So when she braced, the brace had nowhere to go but into the steel. And the steel was already hers. The Edge had made it hers on the causeway, the moment it went cold. So the Ward had simply gone the way the Edge went. In through the door that was already open.

"Two doors," said Katori slowly. "And the same way in."

"I don't know," said Callie. "I don't know anything. I just know it went there."

"Can you do it again?"

---

She could not do it again. Not at first.

She stood in the lay-by with the hook upright in front of her, the butt of the conduit on the gravel. She took hold of it with both hands and planted her feet and braced. The shine came over her coat, the old-glass sheen, and Ines's stone stopped a finger's width off her shoulder. The Ward was on her body. The hook in her hands was only a hook.

"It went onto you," said Ines.

"I know."

She tried again. She gripped the conduit harder, as hard as she could. She thought she had to hold it tighter to get the Ward into it, to squeeze it down her arms into the steel. The Ward came onto her body again, and her hands ached, and the hook was only a hook.

She tried leaning first. She leaned into the hook until the Edge came, the long grey sweep lying out over the road. Then she planted and braced, and the Edge went out as the brace came, and the Ward came onto her body instead. She was back where she had been all morning.

She stood there breathing.

"You're holding it," said her mother.

Callie turned round. Her mother was sitting on the tailboard in the shade with a blanket round her shoulders. Nobody had seen her come. She had been watching the whole thing, the way she watched everything, and her face had nothing in it but attention.

"You're gripping it," her mother said. "Like you're afraid it's going to get away. You weren't gripping it the first time. You were catching it. It was going and you caught it. Your grandfather never once gripped a sack in his life. You don't grip a sack. You get under it and you let it come onto you."

Callie looked at her hands on the conduit.

Then she did the other thing. She stopped trying to push the Ward into the steel. She set the butt of the conduit in the gravel a little behind her, at a slant, and leaned her weight into it the way you lean on a staff going downhill. She let the conduit be her back foot. She let it be the place she planted. She didn't grip it. She stood on it. She set herself for the catch *through* the steel, and the ground, and down.

The pale went into the metal like frost going into a window.

She let out her breath. Ines, without saying a word, walked round behind her and pushed on the top of the hook with the flat of her hand, and then leaned on it with her whole weight, and then hung off it. It did not move a hair. It stood at its slant in the gravel as if it had been sunk in concrete.

Then Ines stepped back and threw a stone at Callie, and it hit her on the arm.

"Ow."

"That's what I thought," said Ines, and did not apologize. "It's one or the other. It's on you or it's on the hook. It can't be both."

"Count," said Katori.

Ines counted. She had been counting already. Of course she had.

Callie held it. The cold came up her arms, faster than the Edge had ever made it come. It came like the drawing-down of a well by two buckets. At ten it was at her elbows. At fourteen it was in her shoulders, and she was shaking, and the pale in the steel began to flicker at the bottom of the conduit. At seventeen it went out of the pipe altogether and stood only in the forged curve for a moment. Then it went out of that as well.

She let go of the hook before it fell. She did not want to hear the clang again.

"Seventeen," said Ines.

Callie sat down on the gravel. She was so hungry she could have eaten the gravel. When she went down into herself to look, the well was nearly dry again, after the whole morning of two fingers of ash and a noon's rest.

"Seventeen seconds," she said. "Half a well. For seventeen seconds."

"Seventeen seconds," Katori agreed, "that nothing in this country could move."

---

They all sat in the lay-by for a while in the heat, and nobody said the obvious thing, because it was too obvious to say.

Pell said it in the end, because Pell always said the thing.

"The top block," he said.

Callie looked up at the scarp, the long pale groove of the cut going up into the sky. At the top of the steep part, where the road went through the grey rock, there was a seam across the road a hand deep and two fingers wide. She had knelt in the road that morning and put her fingers in it.

"There's a crack in the rock," she said. "Near the top. Across the road. It's sound on both sides. You could seat a hook in it."

"A hook in a crack won't hold a wagon," said Pell. "Not with the whole wagon hanging off it. The hook'll bend, or the rock'll pop, or the crack will spread."

"Not if it can't move," said Callie.

Pell looked at her.

"It held a corner of the wagon on loose gravel, at a slant," Callie said. "For seventeen seconds. It held it at an angle a prop can't stand at. It didn't bend and it didn't move and it didn't sink. Nothing moved it. Ines hung off it."

"I hung off it," Ines agreed.

"Seventeen seconds," said Pell. "The steep part's three hundred paces. You don't haul three hundred paces of wagon in seventeen seconds, with a sore horse on the fall. You don't haul thirty."

"Then I'll get more than seventeen."

"Before tomorrow?"

"I don't know." She looked up at the hill. "Nobody knows. That's what today's for."

Pell looked at her for a long time. Then he opened the atlas, and wrote something small in the margin, and turned it round so she could read it.

*Top block — C.'s hook. Seventeen seconds. Get more.*

"That's not an agreement," said Pell. "It's a note."

"I know," said Callie. And she got up off the gravel, and picked up the hook, and went to find the bottom of the well again.
