# Chapter 22 — Callie

Katori got to her first, and Katori's hand did not stop a finger's width off anything.

It came down flat on Callie's shoulder, hard and thin and real, and stayed there. Callie lay in the road and felt it. There was ash on it. There was grey ash all the way up Katori's arm to the elbow and across the back of her hand, and it was the best thing Callie had felt since the dew outside the pump house.

"Where," said Katori.

"Left side. Ribs." Callie breathed halfway in. "And my head. It's all right. It's not — I can breathe halfway."

"Halfway's not all right. Don't move."

Katori put her hands on her. She did it the way Callie's mother had looked at Otto's arm, slowly and thoroughly, from the top, and she did not say anything the whole time. She went over the side of Callie's head where it had hit the wall of the cut, and came away with blood on her fingers, and looked at it without any expression at all. She went down the ribs on the left side through the coat, pressing one at a time, and at the third one Callie made a noise she had not meant to make.

"There," said Katori. "And the next one, probably. Cracked. Not broken through, or you'd not be breathing even halfway, and you'd know it." She sat back on her heels. "You'll live. You'll hate every breath for three weeks, and you'll live."

"The wagon?"

"Is at the top of the pitch, chocked, and Pell's sitting on the tailboard with his head in his hands. The horse is two hundred paces up the road with Otto hanging off her neck. Three husks are ash on the high plain." Katori looked at her. "You could have called me."

"You were busy."

"I was fifty paces up the road. I'd have come."

"You'd have come fifty paces," said Callie, "and it'd have been on me before you got ten." She shut her eyes. "And you were lit. You lit yourself. On purpose. I saw your Ember come out."

"You told me to stay at the lip unless you called me," said Katori. "You didn't say one word about lighting."

Callie laughed. It was a very bad idea. She lay in the road and held her left side and tried not to laugh, and laughed anyway, in small stabbing pieces. Katori sat beside her and did not help at all.

---

Her mother came down off the rim on Ines's arm.

It took them a long time. It was the cattle track down the red earth, and then along the top of the cut to the lip, and then down the fifteen paces of road. The whole way Callie could hear her mother saying things like "I'm *fine*, Ines," and "I can see perfectly well where my feet are going," and "If you ask me once more I'll push you in." Ines said nothing at all, and did not let go.

Then her mother was there. She lowered herself down onto the road beside Callie in stages, one hand on Ines and one on the rock. She did not ask anything. She put her left hand on Callie's forehead, above the cut, and left it there.

"I told you to look at me," she said.

"I did."

"You looked away at the end."

"Something came over the lip."

"I know. I saw it. I was the one who told you." Her mother's thumb moved once across her forehead. "I'm not complaining. I'm just saying, for the record, that for about a minute and a half there you did exactly as you were told. It's the first time since you were four, and I want it written down somewhere."

"Pell will write it down."

"Pell had better." She looked up. "Ines. She's cut her head and cracked some ribs. I'll want to bind the ribs before she moves, and I'll want the head cleaned before it's dressed. Do you still have that needle?"

"It needs stitching?" said Callie.

"I haven't looked yet," said her mother. "I'm asking about the needle so that you'll lie still while I look, because you'll be too busy being frightened of it to move." She bent over Callie's head. "Hold still, love. It's only a scalp. Scalps bleed. It's what they're for."

It needed three stitches. Ines did them, and Callie's mother held her head, and told Ines smaller, and Callie held Katori's wrist the whole time. It was a real wrist, and she held it hard.

---

Pell came down the road from the wagon with the atlas under his arm. He stood over them for a moment without saying anything. His hat was gone, and his hair stood up in grey tufts, and his face looked like a man who has been on the brake of a wagon hanging off a hook in a crack.

Then he sat down in the road, all at once, the way Callie had sat down after the span.

"Well," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"I'll have that down in the book," Pell said, "and then I'm going to sit here for a while, and I don't want anybody to talk to me about hills for the rest of my life."

He opened the atlas on his knee and licked the pencil, which was a disgusting habit, and wrote. It took him longer than usual. When he had finished, he turned the book round and held it down where Callie could see it, lying in the road.

*Pitch. Two pulls, tackle, C.'s hook in the seam. Ninety-one seconds.*
*C.'s call. Concur — K., P., I., O.*

And then, under that, in the same small hand, a little smaller:

*— and one more, who called it from the rim.*

Callie's mother leaned over and read it upside down and said nothing. After a moment she reached out with her left hand and took the pencil from behind Pell's ear. Slowly, left-handed, in letters that went a little crooked at the end, she wrote a single *T.* at the end of the line, as if signing for a parcel. Then she gave him the pencil back.

"There," she said. "Now it's in the book."

"It's in the book," Pell agreed, and shut it.

---

Otto brought Duchess back at dusk.

He brought her down the high road slowly, a long way off at first, a small dark shape and a big one against the pale country. He was walking at her head with his good hand on the cheekpiece and his sling hanging off him in rags, and she was walking beside him. She was dark with sweat from her ears to her hocks. She was short on the near hind, worse than before, and her head was low.

Callie was sitting up against the wall of the cut by then with her ribs bound in a strip of blanket, and she watched them come.

At forty paces the mare's head came up.

She had seen Callie. She stopped. Her ears went round, and she stood there on the high road pointing at her with her whole body, the way she had pointed at her every day since the causeway.

Callie did not move.

She was dark. She had nothing in her at all. The well was dry to the stone, with not even a finger mark of damp at the bottom. There was nothing on her anywhere that a horse or a husk or anything else in the world could feel. She was a girl with her ribs bound in a blanket, sitting against a rock.

Duchess stood for a long time.

Then Otto said something to her that Callie could not hear, and the mare put her head down and came on. Thirty paces. Twenty. She came to fifteen, and stopped, and stood there with her head low, blowing, one ear on Otto and one ear on Callie. It was the nearest she had come since the causeway.

"She hauled," said Otto. His voice was wrecked from shouting. "She hauled it all. She went, and I pointed her, and she hauled it, and she — " He stopped, and wiped his face on his shoulder, and left a long streak of dirt across it. "I thought she was going to go over the side. I thought I'd lost her. She didn't. She went where I pointed."

"You pointed her right," said Pell, from the tailboard.

"I didn't know if I would."

"Nobody ever knows," said Pell. "That's the whole trade." He looked at the mare for a long moment in the last of the light. Then he said, in the voice he used for distances, "Duchess."

Otto looked round.

"Her name," said Pell, "is Duchess. I've decided. Don't look at me like that, Otto, I'm sixty-one and I'm allowed to change my mind once a decade, and this is the once." He turned back to the wagon. "Now get her rubbed down before she stiffens, and see to that leg, and somebody find me my hat."

---

Ines went back down the pitch in the last of the light with Otto to find her arrows. Callie watched them go.

They came back up with both. They were lying in the road on the high plain where the second husk had come apart, side by side in the ash, as if somebody had put them there. Ines held them up so Callie could see them, and turned them over in the lamplight, and ran her thumb down each shaft.

"Six," she said, and put them in the quiver one at a time. She did not look at Callie. Then she stopped with the last one in her fingers, halfway to the quiver, and did look at her.

"When it hit the hook," said Ines. "The husk. It went a hand's width left at the last. Away from you. Toward the hook."

"I know."

"I was counting. I was looking right at it. It went for the hook, not you. You were standing beside it with nothing on you at all, and it went for the steel." She put the arrow away. "I don't know what that means. I just thought somebody should tell you I saw it."

"It's lit," said Callie. "The hook. When it's in there, it's lit. It's the brightest thing."

"Brighter than you."

"Brighter than me."

Ines was quiet for a moment.

"When I sat in my mother's kitchen and nothing happened," she said, "I used to think that if I'd had a door, any door, even one that didn't do much, everything would have been different. I used to think the whole of my life was the difference between one door and none." She buckled the quiver shut. "I watched you today. You've got two, and you nearly got your head stove in against a rock, and the thing that saved you was a bit of pipe out of a wall. And knowing where to put it." She stood up. "I don't know what that means either. I'm going to think about it for a while."

She went to see to the fire.

---

Katori sat with her when it was dark.

Everyone else was asleep, or pretending to be, round the fire in the lee of the wagon at the top of the pitch. Callie's mother was under two blankets on the wagon bed. She had been asleep since an hour after sunset and she had gone to sleep all at once, in the middle of a sentence, the way the lamp goes out when the oil is gone. Her borrowed day was spent. Callie had watched it go out of her face as the sun went down, the colour first, then the straightness, then the words. She had been wanting all evening to ask Katori if that was what it looked like when you paid a borrowed day back. She did not ask, because she did not want to know yet.

"Tell me what you know," said Katori. "Now. While it's fresh. I want to hear you say it, all of it, and I'll tell you if it sounds like anything."

Callie told her. She did it in order, the way she told Pell a road.

"It's one or the other. It's on me or it's in the hook. It can't be both. When it's in the hook I'm bare. Ines has thrown enough stones at me to prove that."

"Go on."

"It goes into the hook through the same door the Edge comes through. Not the pipe. Just the hook. The hook's the thing that went cold on the causeway, so that's the thing that's mine. The pipe's just a pipe."

"Go on."

"It holds as long as I don't hold it. I don't understand that one. But it's true. If I grip it, it drains out of me like two buckets. If I let it stand, and don't look at it, and don't argue with it, it stands." She breathed halfway in. "Ninety-one seconds today, and that was all of me. There's nothing left. I'm drier than the pump house."

"Go on."

"It's lit. Everything comes to it, the way they came to me on the causeway. Worse. It went for the hook over me." She stopped. "And I still can't walk in the ward. I can stand in it and I can't take a step. And I can't make the Edge any shape but the ugly one. And I can't have both on me at once, like I did the first night, when I didn't know anything. It's like I could do more when I didn't know how."

"That's how it goes," said Katori. "That's how it always goes, in the first year. You'll hate it for a long time." She was quiet for a while. "Is there anything else?"

Callie thought about it.

"When the husk hit the hook," she said slowly, "I felt it. Here." She touched her own arm. "Like a bucket dropped down a well. It hit the steel and I felt it land."

Katori nodded.

"Then it's still you," she said. "Whatever you put in that hook, it's you. You didn't make a tool. You put yourself in the steel." She looked into the fire. "I don't know if that's what my grandmother meant by light. I don't know if it's what anybody means by anything. But I know I've had this" — she touched her coat, where the slab was — "for eleven years. And in eleven years nobody has ever taught me one single thing about my own door that I didn't already know. And today you did. Twice."

"What did I teach you?"

"That you can stop holding a thing, and it goes on being held." Katori did not look at her. "I didn't know that. I've been holding things very hard for a long time."

Callie did not say anything. She had a strong sense that she was not meant to, and that she had been told something she would not understand for years. So she said nothing, and let it be.

---

It was Pell who saw the light. He saw it because he was the one who could not sleep. He was sitting up on the tailboard with the atlas on his knee, looking north, the way Katori always looked north.

"Callie," he said, quietly. "Come here and look. If you can stand."

She could stand, just. Katori got her up. She went and stood at the tailboard beside Pell and looked north over the high plain.

It was full dark, the dark of the high country, huge and cold and clear, with no moon. The plain went away north in front of them, flat and black, for further than she could have walked in a week. And at the very edge of it, where the land met the sky, low down, there was a light.

It was not a fire. A fire moves. It was not a star either. It was too low, and too wide, and the wrong colour, a pale steady white like nothing she had ever seen. It sat on the edge of the world and lit the bottom of the clouds above it from underneath, very faintly. It did not flicker at all. It had not flickered, she understood, for seventy years.

"That's it," said Pell. "That's the only thing between here and the sea that's lit like that."

Callie looked at it for a long time.

"How far?"

Pell did the arithmetic out loud, the way he always did, so that somebody could catch him getting it wrong.

"Seven days, on a good road with two good animals. We've got one, and she's lame." He licked the pencil. "Nine. Call it nine, and three ifs on top. Ten or eleven, and I'll be glad to be wrong."

Callie thought of her mother asleep on the boards behind her, who had gone out like a lamp at sunset. She thought of the plucking hand at two in the morning, and a minute and a bit more. *Longer than the last one.*

"Nine," she said.

"Nine," Pell agreed, and wrote it down.

---

Katori came and stood at the other side of her. For a while all three of them looked north at the light without speaking.

"I said I'd stand for you at that gate," said Katori at last. "I will. I want you to know I haven't changed my mind about it."

"I know."

"But I have to tell you what that means now. It meant one thing on the first morning, and it means something else tonight." Katori kept her eyes on the light. "When I stand for somebody at Meridian, I stand for what they are. I say, this is a girl I hired, a hunter, she reads ground better than anyone I've met, and she's good for it. And they write it down. They write everything down at that gate. Pell's not the only one with a book."

"Meridian has columns," said Pell quietly, to the atlas.

"Meridian has columns," Katori agreed. "It's got a column for Ember people and a column for hand-tradition. It's got a column for people with no door at all, like Ines. It's got a column for hunters and one for lifters, and one for sick women who need a doctor." She was quiet for a moment. "It hasn't got a column for you. Nobody has. And the kind of people who make columns don't like a thing that doesn't fit in one. They make a new column for it. And then they decide what the column's for."

Callie stood between them at the tailboard, holding her left side, and looked at the light that had not flickered in seventy years.

She thought about the jar at the bottom of the barrow. There had been five handfuls in it, and now there were four, because her mother had spent one on a hill. She thought about her tenth, in Pell's book, which had been more than the jar many times over. She had drunk most of it in three days, two fingers at a time, at the bottom of a scarp and on the top of one. She had drunk it to buy seventeen seconds, then forty-four, then ninety-one. It had been her gate money. It had been the thing she meant to put down on the table in front of the columns so that they would see she could pay.

It was nearly gone. She had spent it, and she would have spent it again, and there was a hill behind her to show for it.

What she had left to put on the table was what she was.

"Then I'll tell them," she said.

Katori turned her head.

"When we get there," said Callie. "At the gate. When you stand for me. I'll tell them myself what goes in the column. Not you. Not Pell. Me." She was surprised by how steady her voice was. "I'll tell them what I want them to know, and not one thing more. I'll decide it before we get there, all of it. I've got nine days."

Katori looked at her for a long moment across the dark. Then something moved at the corner of her mouth that was almost a smile, and was not one yet.

"That's your call," she said.

"It's my call."

"Pell," said Katori, without looking round.

"I heard," said Pell, and wrote it down.

Callie stood at the tailboard until the cold got into her ribs. She looked north across the dark at the only lit thing between here and the sea. It sat on the edge of the world, nine days away, with a doctor inside it and a book at its gate.

Then she went down into herself one more time before she slept, the way she had every night since the pump house, to see.

The well was dry. It was dry right down to the stone.

But at the very bottom, so faint she nearly missed it, there was a mark of damp, like a finger drawn once through dust. It had not been there at sunset. Nobody had put it there. No ash had gone into her since the hill.

It was filling. Slowly, on its own, the way Katori had said it would, it had begun to fill.
