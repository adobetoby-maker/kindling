# Chapter 41 — Toren

"I was born at a place called Lowry Bend," said Rook, "which is about four hundred miles south and east of here, in country you've never heard of and wouldn't like, and there were a hundred and forty of us in it, and my mother did nothing with her hands.

"I'll explain that. In your part of the world, you've got eleven stones for four hundred people, and every one of them's got a history attached, and a person who hasn't got one and can't get one is a person nothing happened to. Where I'm from, there wasn't a stone within sixty miles. Nobody had one. Nobody had ever had one, and if you'd put one on the table at Lowry Bend, the general opinion would have been that it was a clever bit of old-world rubbish and a poor substitute for doing the thing properly.

"We did it with our hands. That's all it is. You've seen it — the woman in the cut with the wall, that's the same trade, that's my whole village. My mother could put her hands on a rip in a cow and have it hold for a week. My grandmother could do a wall about eight feet across and hold it for four minutes, which in my family made her the greatest woman who ever lived, and I was raised on stories about that wall the way this one" — he tipped his head at Toren without looking at him — "was raised on stories about a disk.

"And I could do nothing at all."

He said it without any particular weight.

"Thirteen, door open, a whole village of people who do it with their hands, and I sat in my mother's kitchen and put my hands out and got nothing for eleven months. Not a wall. Not a mend. A smell of hot metal once, that I think I imagined." He turned his head very slightly toward the stone where Dessa was sitting. "So you and I have that in common, and I've known it since Hobb's Wall, and I've never said a word about it to you, which is one of about nine things I'm going to be answering for tonight."

Dessa did not say anything at all. Toren, sitting four feet from her, heard her breathe out very slowly through her nose.

---

"My mother's lungs went in the winter I turned fourteen.

"It wasn't the radiation; it was ordinary — it was wet and cold and a bad chimney and forty years of it. She could do a mend on a cow, and she couldn't do one on herself. That's not irony; it's just how it works: you can't get the angle. And there was nothing at Lowry Bend and nothing at the market town and nothing anywhere we could walk to.

"Then men came through in the spring with a wagon and a proper doctor sitting on it.

"They were from a place called Homura."

He let the word out into the firelight, and it sat there, and Toren thought: *there it is. That's the first one. That's a name that's never been said in front of us.*

"They had a valley two hundred miles north, up against a fracture — that's a place where the tearing's constant, where you get weather out of it, where the ground hums. Nobody lives at a fracture. They'd built at one on purpose. And what they were doing there was studying what the Kindling actually does to a person's head, and they were doing it with instruments, and they had the only working instruments of that kind that I have ever heard of before or since.

"They wanted subjects. That's the word they used, and they used it to my face on the first day, which I've thought about a great deal since, because they could have said *students*, and they didn't.

"They wanted young ones, because it's clearest in the young. They wanted them from everywhere — and that was the thing about Homura, that's the whole of what Homura was, and I want you to hear it properly: they did not care which door you came through. They took stone-tradition children off the coast and hand-tradition children out of villages like mine, and they took two boys from a monastery in the east who'd never touched either and did the whole thing sitting still in a room, and they put every one of us through the same machines and wrote it all down in the same book.

"Nobody does that. You've been in exactly one settlement that isn't yours, and you'll be in forty more before you're grown, and every one of them will have picked one door and decided the other two are a trick, a toy, or a heresy. Hobb's Wall weighs you. Lowry Bend would have laughed at that stone in your pocket. It is the oldest and stupidest argument in the world, and the whole world is in it.

"Homura was one valley where it wasn't happening."

"And they paid?" said Wyck.

It was the first thing anybody had said, and Rook did not look annoyed about it.

"They paid," he said. "They paid in what a family like mine actually wanted, which wasn't coin. A doctor twice a year at Lowry Bend, for everybody, as long as I was there. Medicine off the wagon. A name on a list somewhere that meant if the fracture threw something big at us, somebody who owed us would come.

"I went in the autumn I turned fourteen, and my mother stood at the top of the lane and did not cry until I was out of sight, and I know that because my brother told me nine years later, and it is one of about four things I have left of her."

Milo made a small sound.

"So the answer to your question," said Rook, "is that I sold myself for five years of medicine for a sick woman who couldn't walk to a wall, and then I walked north with a stranger, and I have been on the road with a cart and a sick old woman in it for twenty-seven days, and I would like all five of you to understand that I have known exactly what I was doing every single day of it."

Nobody said anything for a while. Senna, in the cart, had put her mug down.

---

"Homura was a valley with wire round it and eleven buildings in it and about sixty people, of whom forty were us.

"It was the best place I have ever been in my life. I want that said before the rest, because everything else I've got to tell you will make it sound like a hard place, and it wasn't. There was hot water. There was a stove in every room and a cook who thought forty children was a personal challenge. Nobody hit anybody. They taught us to read properly, all of us, including the ones who came in at sixteen and were ashamed about it, and they did it in the evenings and never once made a thing of it.

"And every day they put us through the machines and asked us to do our tricks and wrote down what happened in our heads while we did them.

"And the second week I was there, a girl about a foot shorter than me stood in a corridor and told me that what my mother did with her hands was a party trick for people too backward to have been given a proper stone."

Dessa said, "Oh."

"Yes," said Rook.

"Her name was Vera."

---

He stopped there for a while, and none of them filled it.

The fire had got to the good stage, the one it never reached on a road night, where the bed of it was red the whole way across, and you could look into it.

"Vera Kess," he said. "Fifteen. Stone tradition, eight generations of it, out of a clan up in the north-east that had kept hold of more of what it came from than anybody else I've ever met — they'd kept their own words for things, which nobody has, and I'll come back to that because it matters later.

"She was better than me at everything. That is not a soft thing to say about somebody, it's a fact, and it was the central fact of the first two years. She'd had a stone in her hand since she was thirteen and could do things with it I hadn't known were in the world, and she could hold a shape for an hour, and she was clever, and she was the sort of person who's been told all her life she's the best of her generation and has decided that the only sane response is to be unbearable about it.

"We despised each other for about eleven months."

"Really?" said Milo.

"Properly. It wasn't a game, and it wasn't the other thing that people say it is afterward." Rook turned his hand over on his knee. "She thought hand-tradition was a leftover. I thought stone-tradition was a person renting power off an object and calling it his own, and I could argue it, because at fourteen I could argue anything, and I was very poor, and she was not, and we both knew what that had to do with which of us had been handed a stone at thirteen.

"We had that argument in front of forty other children for a year, and the reason we had it is that it was the *only* argument. Every single one of us had brought it in from home. There were forty children in that valley from nine different traditions, and every one of us had been told, by people who loved us, that ours was the real one.

"And I want you to understand what the people running that place were doing about it, because it was the cleverest thing I have ever seen anybody do, and they never once said it out loud.

"They put us in pairs. Across traditions. Always. You did your tests with the same partner every week for years, and the partner was never from your door, and they never explained why, and if you complained, they moved you to a worse room."

---

"What changed it," said Senna.

It was not really a question.

"She got hurt," said Rook. "Which is not the answer you're expecting, and it's the true one.

"Second winter. There was a run of bad weather off the fracture, and something came through into the top of the valley, and it got into the sheds before anybody had it called, and Vera Kess went into a shed with a stone in her hand because there were two of the little ones in it, and she came out of it with her leg open from the hip to the knee."

Dessa shifted on her stone and did not say anything.

"And there was nobody to put a hand on her," said Rook.

"They had instruments that could look at the inside of a living skull, and they had no healer, because the hand-tradition people in that valley were fourteen years old and had never done a mend in their lives, and I was the worst of them, because I couldn't do one at all.

"I sat in a corridor outside that room for nine hours.

"And somewhere in about the sixth hour of it — and I have had eleven years to be honest with myself about this, so I'll be honest with you — I stopped caring about the entire argument. Not decided against it. Stopped being able to locate it. I sat on a floor, and I wanted one thing, and the thing I wanted was for somebody to put their hands on that girl, and there was no part of me anywhere that cared what tradition the hands came out of or what the person holding them believed about where it came from.

"And that's it. That's the whole mechanism, and it is extremely stupid, and nobody has ever wanted to hear it.

"I'd had the argument put to me in words a hundred times by people cleverer than me. There was a woman at that station — her name was Orrin Vell and she ran the whole place and she was sixty and she had it all, she had the numbers, she could show you two children's readings side by side and the same shape in both — and I had nodded at her for two years and not moved one inch.

"It came off me in a corridor because I loved somebody who was on the wrong side of it."

---

"Now I'm going to give you three words," said Rook, "because you'll need them, and because they're hers, and because I have not said any of them out loud in front of another living person in eleven years, and I'd rather do it in one go.

"The first one is the Path.

"That's what the station called what it was looking for. It's a plain word for a plain thing, and there's nothing mystical in it, and I've been walking the four of you along it since the morning after the fire at the field wall without once telling you what it was called.

"The Path is this: it's one force. There's one thing, and it comes from inside a person, and it always did. The stone doesn't have it. The stone's a window, and there's nothing in a window. He worked that out on a hillside eight days ago on his own, and I nearly had to sit down." Toren felt his own face go hot. "The rift doesn't grant it. My mother's hands didn't contain it. The monastery boys who sat still in a room for six years and could stop a knife weren't drawing on anything the rest of us weren't.

"Three doors into one house, and every settlement between here and the coast standing in the hall arguing about which wall the house is on.

"That's the Path. That's the thing. And you can be told it — I've just told you, it took nine seconds — and being told it does nothing whatsoever, and I can prove that, because I was told it for two years by the finest mind I've ever been in a room with, and it didn't take, and then a girl I'd been arguing with for a year got her leg opened, and it went all the way down in about four hours on a corridor floor.

"That's why I don't explain things. That's the whole reason, and it's the only apology any of you are getting for the first eight days. Knowing a thing and having it be true about you are two different skills, and only one of them can be handed over."

---

"The second word is Satori.

"That one's Vera's. Her clan brought it with them out of the old world the way they brought everything else, and it's what her people called the moment it happens — the moment the door goes. Not the arguing. Not the agreeing with it. The moment you stop being able to find the wall you'd been standing on one side of.

"She'd had it a year before I did, at thirteen, on her own, in a barn, and nobody had believed her.

"And she gave me the word in a corridor at about the ninth hour, when they finally let me in, and she was grey and furious and stitched from hip to knee, and I sat down and said a thing I don't remember, and she looked at me and said: *oh — you've had it.*

"And then she told me what it was called, because she'd been waiting two years for somebody to say it to."

Nobody moved.

"What's it feel like?" said Milo.

"Nothing," said Rook.

"That's not—"

"I'm not being clever. It feels like nothing at all, and that's the mark of it, and it's why every person who's had it sounds like an idiot afterward. It isn't a light and it isn't a voice and nothing arrives. It's the going-away of a thing you'd been carrying so long you'd stopped knowing you were carrying it." He looked at the fire. "There's an emptiness to it, and there's an openness to it, and I can't put it better than that, and Orrin Vell wrote four pages on it and couldn't either.

"What it does is this. Before it, you fill through your one door, and the door is narrow, and when you're empty, you're empty for a week." He glanced at Toren. "Two days before you could hold the spike; a week before it was worth anything. That's a person filling at a person's speed, and it's the only speed there is on that side of it.

"After it — you fill from the whole of it. Straight off. That's all the fast recovery anybody's ever seen in me, and it is not a trick I learned, and I could not teach it to you in a hundred years. It isn't a skill. It's a consequence."

He turned his head.

"And the other thing it does is that you stop being stuck with the one door. Which is the part everybody actually wants and the part that means nothing on its own. There's a word for a person who's got there — they call it unbound, and it's a poor word, and it makes people think of a chain coming off, and it's more like finding out a wall you'd been leaning on was a curtain."

Wyck said, "That's you."

"That's me."

"You do the wall and the stone and the running thing."

"I do the wall and the stone and the running thing," said Rook, "and there's a fourth, and I haven't got it, and there's people who'd tell you there's further to go after that, and I don't know, and I've stopped needing to."

---

"The third word is a Reading, and it's a small one, and it's practical, and I'll come back to it tomorrow because one of you is going to need it.

"At the station, they had machines. Out here nobody's got machines. So they'd trained a thing to go with the machines — a way of sitting down and taking honest stock of yourself from the inside, so that when you told somebody how much you had left, it was a number and not a boast. You say it as a fraction. *A fifth of the room's lit.* *Half.* *I'm out.*

"It sounds like nothing. It is the single most useful thing that place ever gave me, and it's the only thing I do every day that came out of that valley and has nothing to do with anybody being dead."

---

He stopped and put a stick on the fire and pushed it in with his boot, and Toren realised, with a small cold shock, that they had come to it.

"Now the rest," said Rook.

"Homura had four years of readings on forty children from nine traditions, and by the time I was eighteen they had it. They had it in the numbers. Orrin Vell showed me two sheets once — me and Vera, one from a valley of hand-people and one from eight generations of stone, and the shape on the paper was the same shape. Not similar. The same. And under it forty more, all of them the same, and the two monastery boys who'd never had a stone or a rift in their lives, same again.

"You understand what that is.

"That's the end of the argument. Not an opinion about the argument, not a clever man's theory — that's a stack of paper you could put in front of a weighmaster at Hobb's Wall and a magister on the coast and an elder at Lowry Bend, and every one of them would have had to look at it. Every settlement in this country has built its whole idea of who's worth something on which door a child fell through at thirteen, and there was a room in a valley with the answer in it, written down, in a hand anybody could read.

"They were two years off publishing. They had it copied four times, and they were arguing about how to say it so it wouldn't sound like a boast." He rubbed his thumb across his knee once. "I was nineteen. I used to sit in the back of those arguments and think it was the dullest thing I'd ever heard."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Cinder Company came over the wire on the eleventh of the ninth month, at about two in the morning, and burned the whole of it, and everyone in it."

---

Toren heard the fire tick. He heard Pitch shift on the hobble. He found that he had put his hand flat on the ground beside him.

"I'll tell you the part you need, and I won't tell you the rest, and that's not me protecting you; it's me." Rook's voice had not changed at all, which was the worst of it. "Here's the part you need.

"They didn't come like Riftspawn. That's what I've been teaching you for four days, and that's why. Riftspawn come *at* you. A husk sees a living thing and goes at it and cannot have a second idea, and everything I taught you in the first eight days works because of that, and none of it worked that night, because men don't do that.

"They came in at the top of the valley through the sheds. They came in quiet, and they came in *organised*, and the first thing they did was take the gate — not to get in, they were already in, they took the gate so that the sixty of us could not get out, and it took four of them about a minute, and that minute was the whole night.

"I was in the long room with eleven others, and we did exactly what you'd expect a lot of frightened young people to do, which is go for the door in a bunch, and there was one man standing outside it, and he counted us as we came, and he had all the time in the world.

"Vera got a shape up. She got a shape up across that doorway in about two seconds with a stone the size of my palm, and it was the fastest piece of work I have ever seen anybody do, and she held that door for eleven of us and got nine of them out, and the second one they sent round came through the window behind her."

He stopped.

Milo was crying without any noise at all.

"She lived about four minutes," said Rook. "And I want to say one thing about those four minutes, and then I'm going to leave them alone, because they're mine.

"She knew what was happening to the station. She could hear it. And what she spent about half of that on was making me say, out loud, that I'd get the papers out.

"I didn't get the papers out. I got about sixty feet from the records hut, and the roof came in. I went back at first light with two others, and there was nothing there at all — not burnt paper, nothing, and I've had eleven years to think about what that means, and the only thing it can mean is that somebody carried them out before it went up.

"So they didn't come to kill children. That's the thing I want said at this fire, because it's the thing that makes them what they are and not just a bad lot with a wagon. They came for a stack of paper that proved the world was arguing about nothing. They took it, and then they burned the house down around it so that nobody would go looking for a second copy.

"Somebody paid them to do it. Somebody with something to lose if four hundred settlements stopped sorting people by which door they fell through." He put another stick on. "I don't know who, and I've asked in about sixty places, and I've got nothing, and that's the honest state of it."

---

"Eleven of us got out over the west wire. Nine of them were children.

"I walked south for two months with a fourteen-year-old girl called Dee Wren who had a broken wrist and no shoes, and I put her in at a settlement on the river with people who'd take her, and I got to Lowry Bend in the spring and I told my mother I was finished with all of it.

"And I was. For two years, I was a man who dug. I want that on the record too, because all of you have a picture of me that involves a rock face, and for two years after Homura, I did nothing with what I had and told nobody I had it, and I was going to die at Lowry Bend at eighty having laid a good hedge.

"Then the fracture threw something big at us in the autumn, the way I'd been told it might when I was fourteen, and somebody sent to the nearest company for a defence.

"And a company came."

Toren felt the whole thing arrive about a second before it landed.

"They fought for us for two days, and they were good at it," said Rook. "They were, by the way. I'd have hired them. On the second night, there were three separate lines out past the mill in the dark, and a man on the north line decided the thing coming up the lane at him was not people, and it was people, and he had the numbers on his side, and he was wrong.

"My mother was in that lane. So were both my brothers. So were nine others.

"And the company paid for it. They paid a settlement to the village in the spring in proper coin, and they wrote a letter, and the letter said the man responsible had been put off the books, and the village took the coin because eleven people were dead, and the roof of the mill was gone, and there was nothing else on offer.

"I did not know who they were. I want that understood clearly. A company's a company. There are forty of them in that country, and they all wear the same, and they all pay their men the same clever way, and I was twenty-one, and I did not go and look at the letter, because I could not be in the room with it."

He looked up.

"I found out five years ago," he said. "In a weigh-yard, off a piece of brass exactly like the one in my coat, from a man who was selling boots.

"Eleven years since the valley. Nine since the lane. And for the first six of them, I was a man carrying two entirely separate holes, and then a bootmaker put a chit on a table and turned it over, and they were one hole, and they had been one hole the whole time, and I had been living next to it for six years like a man sleeping in a house with a body in it."

---

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Senna said, from the cart, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "And what have you been doing since, exactly?"

"Hunting them."

"How?"

"Poorly," said Rook. "I'll tell you how poorly, because it's the least flattering thing about me, and you may as well have all of it in one night. Six years. I've been in about ninety settlements. I've bought eleven chits, and I've got four of them left. I have been in the same country as that company twice, and both times I found the camp cold, and the second time I was four days behind them, and I walked into a burnt yard at a fuel relay north of the Drum on a road I had no business being on, because a woman at a wall had told me they'd been through in the spring.

"And then a road cut had four children and an old woman in it, and I stopped."

"You didn't stop," said Wyck.

"No," Rook agreed. "I didn't. That's fair."

---

"Right," said Dessa.

Her voice was not steady, and she used it anyway.

"I'm going to ask my thing now, because you said you'd answer it, and because if I don't ask it in the next thirty seconds, I'm going to lose my nerve."

"Ask it."

"My four seconds."

"Yes."

"You said I wasn't the first," said Dessa. "You said you'd seen it happen to three people including you, and the other two are dead. So one of them's her. Who's the third?"

"A boy called Ander Croft," said Rook, "who was eleven, and who got it on a Tuesday in front of the whole station because two of the little ones dared him to hold his hand in a lamp, and who died on the same night as everybody else. He's not important to your question. I only ever counted him because Vera made me count him."

"Then say the thing."

Rook looked across the fire at her.

"Fifteen days ago," he said, "on a night when nobody was watching you, you sat down on the ground with a stone in your hands, and you did not want anything, and the door went, and you filled straight off the whole of it for four seconds, and it felt like half a night to you because you were not filling through a door, and there is no clock on the other side of one.

"That was Satori. That's what it's called, and that's what happened, and it happened to you.

"It was not my stone. It was not lightning going through a bit of ground. There is no version of that night in which the stone did it, because I have been carrying that stone for eleven years, and I know precisely what it does in a hand that isn't doing anything, and what it does is lie there being a rock.

"You are the third person I have ever seen it happen to, and the other two were a girl from eight generations of stone and a boy with his hand in a lamp, and you are a girl from a kitchen where the door opened onto nothing at all — and you are therefore, all by yourself, sitting on that stone with a bad leg, the entire proof of the thing that a valley full of people died holding on to."

Dessa put both hands over her face.

"Which is why I wouldn't talk to you about it," said Rook. "Not because it was small. Because I could not open my mouth about your four seconds without the whole of tonight coming out of it, and I wasn't ready, and I made you carry that for fifteen days on your own, and it's the worst thing I've done since I've known any of you."

---

Milo got it first, which was strange, because Milo got almost nothing first.

"The stone," he said. "The stone's hers."

"Yes."

"You said — you said you carried it for eleven years, you said you've been carrying *that stone* for eleven years, but you're hand-tradition, you're from a village where nobody had — "

"Yes," said Rook.

He took it out of his coat pocket.

He did it slowly, and he held it flat on his palm where the five of them could see it: the small grey slab, water-grey, smaller than Toren's, with the two worn places on the long edges where a thumb and forefinger had gone for years.

"It came off the floor of the long room at about ten past two in the morning on the eleventh of the ninth month," he said, "and it is the only thing I own, and it has been in my hand every day for eleven years, and nobody has asked me one question about it in all that time because a man with an Ember is the least remarkable thing in the world."

Toren heard Dessa say, "*Oh,*" through her hands, and understood, all at once, what she was saying it about.

"It sat in my hands like it was cut for them," she said.

"It was cut for a fifteen-year-old girl with small hands," said Rook. "I've had to hold it wrong for eleven years."

---

The fire went down. Wyck fed it without being asked and sat back down.

"I'll do the last part quickly," said Rook, "because it's the part that's about the five of you, and you've earned it in front of the rest.

"What was taken at that station wasn't forty people. Forty people were taken, and that's the thing I think about at three in the morning, and it's not what was *destroyed*. What was destroyed was a piece of paper that would have told four hundred settlements that they've been sorting their children wrong since the Fall.

"I can kill the men who did it. I've spent six years arranging to, and I'm going to.

"And it won't put one word of that paper back.

"I worked that out in a field about a fortnight ago, on a morning after a night when a girl who was Kindled to nothing sat down with Vera Kess's stone in her hands and did the thing Vera did in a barn at thirteen. I stood at the top of a field wall with my hand over my mouth for an hour, and what I was doing up there was finding out that there's a second way to answer for it, and that it's slower, and that it works, and that I'd walked past it for six years because it doesn't look like anything.

"You can't print a paper. But you can put the thing itself into four living people and turn them loose on a road, and they'll be in forty settlements in ten years, and they'll be standing in somebody's kitchen doing a wall and a stone in the same minute in front of a lot of people who'll tell them it can't be done.

"That's what you've been standing in a line for," said Rook. "That's the whole of it. I'm teaching you the Path, which is the only thing I've got that's worth anything, and I started doing it on purpose the morning after that fire, and I have not told you because I could not say one word of it without saying all of tonight."

---

Wyck said, "What's your name?"

It went across the fire like a dropped plate.

"Wyck—" Milo said.

"No, it's the obvious one," said Wyck. "He's given us everything. He's given us her name and the place and the company and the lot. Rook's a bird. It's a callsign. It's what a man gets called on a crew." He did not take his eyes off him. "So what's your name?"

Rook did not look away either.

"No," he said.

"Why?"

"Because I haven't said it out loud in eleven years, and I've said quite a lot tonight, and that one's the last thing in the box, and I'm keeping it a while longer." He said it evenly, without apology. "I'll tell you a true thing about it instead, and you can decide if it's enough. The man with that name went to a valley at fourteen and came out of it at nineteen and did nothing with what he had for two years and then dug his family out of a lane, and I've been him for eleven years, and I have not yet got to the end of it.

"When it's over, you'll have it. You specifically. I'll say it to you first."

Wyck sat back slowly.

"Right," he said.

---

It was Toren who asked the last thing, and he asked it because he had been holding it against his breastbone for the entire night, and it had got heavier every hour.

"On the gravel," he said. "At the creek. You knew what it was in about a second."

"I did."

"You've never said."

"No."

"Will you say it now?"

Rook looked at him for a long moment across the good fire.

"It's called a Satori," he said. "The same word. Her people gave the thing and the state the same name on purpose, and I've never met anybody else who could tell me why.

"And that's all I'm giving you tonight, and it's not a deflection, and I'll say the reason plainly so it isn't one. I don't know what it is. Nobody does. I've got a belief about it that I've carried for eleven years, and I'm not going to put that belief into a thirteen-year-old's head at midnight at the end of a night like this one, because you'd take it as true — you'd take it as true because I've just spent four hours being honest with you, and it is the one thing I've said all night that I cannot show you the proof of.

"Ask me again when you can hear me say it and still weigh it. That'll be in about a year, and I'll tell you when I think you've got there, and until then keep it under your shirt."

"That's fair," said Toren, and was surprised to find that he meant it.

---

They let the fire go down after that.

Nobody went to bed for a long time, and nobody said much, and Toren could not afterward reconstruct any of the last hour of it in order: Milo asking what the cook at Homura had been like, and Rook answering, at length, and almost smiling; Senna asking whether Orrin Vell had been a tall woman and being told no, she was five foot nothing and everybody was terrified of her; Dessa sitting for twenty minutes with her hands open on her knees and her eyes shut in front of all of them for the first time ever, and getting nothing, and not minding.

Wyck was the one who said the thing that ended it.

He said it standing up, with the bar already in his hand.

"The eleventh of the ninth month," he said. "You gave the day for the valley."

"Yes."

"You didn't give one for the lane."

Rook looked at him.

"No," he said.

"Because you know that one as well," said Wyck.

"Yes."

Wyck nodded once, as though something had been confirmed that he had suspected for about four days. Then he put the bar up on his shoulder.

"You've kept three watches a night since the Drum," he said.

"I have."

"Not tonight."

Rook started to say something.

"That's not me asking," said Wyck Tanner, and went up the bank, and sat down facing the road, and did not come down until it got light.

Toren lay on his back in the grass and watched the sparks go up off the good fire and waited for the man to go up after him — which was what was going to happen, because it was what had happened every night for twenty-seven of them.

Rook put the last stick on. He sat for a while with his forearms across his knees.

Then he did a thing Toren had never once seen him do, and did it without any ceremony at all: he lay down on his side by the fire with his coat pulled over his shoulder and the small grey stone shut up in his fist, and he was asleep inside a minute, and he slept until it was light.
