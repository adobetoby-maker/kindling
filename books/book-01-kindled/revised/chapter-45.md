# Chapter 45 — Toren

There were four hours of nothing first, and Toren was grateful for every one of them.

They sat on the spur behind the engine house in the dark with the cart across the neck of it and the mule tied to the wheel, and there was no fire, and there was no moon, and the water went out around them in every direction with nothing on it. Wyck stood at the corner of the brick where he could see the neck. Dessa sat with her back against the wheel and her hands open on her knees and did not move for four hours and did not once stop being lit. Senna lay in the bed and was awake and said nothing.

Milo talked.

He talked the way he had talked at the halt on the first day out of the road cut, and in a tank at the Drum, and on eleven wet evenings on a verge, which was without any particular direction and without stopping and about nothing, and nobody told him to be quiet, and Toren understood, sitting four feet from him with his back against a brick wall, that not one person on that spur was going to tell Milo Faust to be quiet ever again as long as they all lived, and that Milo knew it, and that this was the only thing anybody had to give him.

At about the second hour he said, "Can I ask the thing?"

Rook was sitting on the ground with his back against the wheel hub and his left arm held across himself. He had been quiet a long time.

"Which thing?"

"The brothers thing." Milo had his knees up. "You said a month. It's eight days. I've counted them, I've counted them a lot, and I know it's eight, and I'm asking now, and if you say no I'll say all right and I won't do a face."

There was a long pause with the sluice in it.

"Two," said Rook. "Arn was four years older than me and he was the best hedger in that valley and he knew it, and he was the sort of man who'd take an hour to lay eight feet of hedge and then stand and look at it for ten minutes with his hands on his hips. He was dull. I want to be honest about him, because everybody gets made clever afterward and he wasn't clever, he was dull and kind and enormous and he could carry a sheep under each arm.

"And Tobin was two years younger than me and he was a horror. He was a thief, in a small way, of small things, and he never once got caught by anybody but me, and when I went to the station he cried and then punched me in the stomach at the top of the lane, in that order, and those were the last two things he ever did to me.

"They'd be thirty-six and thirty. They both look about nineteen in my head and they will now for the rest of my life, and that's the actual cost of it and nobody tells you that part."

Milo said, "Thank you."

"You were owed it. I said a month because I was being a coward about a fortnight." Rook shifted against the hub and stopped. "Ask the next one."

"There isn't a next one."

"There's always a next one with you."

"There is a next one," Milo admitted, "but it's a bad one, so I'll keep it."

"Keep it, then," said Rook, "and ask it tomorrow," and neither of them ever said what it was.

---

They fired the reeds at about the sixth hour.

It came up on the water side, south of the sluice, in a long bank of dry standing reed two hundred yards off — a small yellow crawl at the bottom of it first, and then a run of light going sideways along the edge faster than a man walks, and then the whole bed went up with a noise like cloth tearing, and the heat came across the water and hit Toren in the face from two hundred yards.

"Ah," said Rook, getting up. "Yes. That's good."

"That's *good*?"

"That's a man who's done this before doing the thing I'd have done and I'd like it noted that I didn't think of it, because I'm slow tonight." He was already moving along the wall. "Wyck. They're coming at the neck and they're coming now, while the light's behind them and in our eyes. Dessa, don't look at it. Don't look at it once, look at the ground and the dark and use your ears, you've got thirty feet and I need every foot of it."

The flat black water became a sheet of moving orange, and the reed beds nearest them stood up out of it in black lines, and the whole spur, the cart, the brick wall, the mule, all six of them — all of it came up lit. Their shadows went out enormous across the bank and lay down on the burning water.

Milo opened his hands, and closed them again, and looked at the light in them, which was a soft greenish nothing in the middle of all that, and Toren watched him understand.

"It's no good," Milo said.

"No," said Rook, going past. "Not tonight. Put it away."

---

They came at the neck of the spur three times in an hour and each time it was worse, because each time there were more of them and each time Wyck was slower.

Toren fought where he was put. That was the whole of his night and he could not afterward have drawn the shape of it: the neck of the spur was thirty feet of bank between the brick corner and the water, and Wyck stood in the middle of it, and the cart was behind him at an angle, and Toren worked the left, the water side, where a man could come up the slope, and Rook worked the right along the wall.

They got Wyck's bar away from him at the second push.

Three of them had it at once, and one of them simply walked backward with it. Wyck let it go rather than be pulled off his feet and stood in a thirty-foot gap in front of a cart with an old woman in it, with nothing in his hands at all. Somebody came at him with a spear. He took it on his forearms — the bad one and the good one — and turned it, got both hands on the shaft above the head, and then would not let go. For about five seconds there were two men at the other end of a spear trying to move Wyck Tanner and failing. Toren came across and put the spike through the shaft. It broke, and all three of them went down in the road.

"Bar," Wyck said, from underneath somebody.

"Here—"

"*Bar.*"

Milo got it. He went out into the neck of the spur on his hands and knees between men's legs and took the bar off the ground where it had been dropped. He came back with it, put it in Wyck's hand, and got kicked in the ribs on the way and did not make any noise about it.

They came again a quarter of an hour later and it went on for a long time, and at the end of it there were five men down in the neck of the spur and two of them were moving and the company went back thirty yards and stood in the light of the burning reeds and looked at them.

And a voice out there said, quite clearly, over the water:

"The cart. The cart, the cart — shoot the *cart*."

---

The first bolt went into the boards above Senna's head and came half through.

Toren got up into the bed and got his hands under his grandmother's shoulders and dragged her flat and lay across her, and the second one came through the side of the cart at the height his back had been, and the third one hit the iron of the tyre and screamed off it into the dark.

"Off the bed," Rook was shouting. "*Off* the bed. It's not a wall; it's a box—"

"She can't—"

"Take her out of it."

They took her out of it. Dessa had her legs and Toren had her under the arms. They got her down onto the bank behind the wheel with the blankets round her. Senna Voss, who had not once complained on any road in a month, made a sound that Toren had to put away somewhere and not look at for a long time afterward.

Rook came round the wheel with his hand pressed under his ribs.

"Right," he said. "Listen, all of you, and be quick, because this is going to be the worst thing I've said to you.

"They've stopped trying to get onto this spur. They've worked out they don't need to. They've got a lit bank and eleven crossbows and the whole night, and they will stand out there in the dark and put bolts into thirty feet of ground until there's nothing left on it that's standing up, and it will cost them nothing at all.

"So we can't stay. We have to go north over the arch, tonight, with the cart, and the arch is twelve feet wide with no parapet and it is the brightest piece of ground on this bank."

"How long?" said Wyck.

"To get a mule and a cart across it at a walk with all of us pushing? A minute. A minute and a half if the wheel catches."

Nobody said anything.

"That's the number," said Rook. "A minute and a half of open brick, lit up like a table, at forty yards, in front of men who have been shooting at a cart for a quarter of an hour. And there isn't another way off this spur, and if we're on it at first light we're finished, and I have spent the last ten minutes looking for the third thing and there isn't one."

Toren said, "We need them looking at something else."

"Yes."

"For a minute and a half."

"Yes," said Rook. "And there's nothing on this bank to give them."

---

Milo said, "There's me."

He said it from about four feet away, in a perfectly ordinary voice, the way he said most things, and every single one of them turned round.

"Don't," said Dessa.

"The sluice wall goes out forty feet from the far side of the arch," said Milo. "I've been looking at it since the afternoon. It's stone the whole way and it's wide enough to walk on and it comes off the bank on the *south* side, which is the way they're all facing, and if somebody goes out along it with a light over his head he'll be the only thing they can see and he will be forty feet further away from the arch every step he takes."

"Milo—"

"He taught me this." Milo turned round to Rook and his voice had gone up. "You taught me this, you taught me this in a field with a straw coat in it, you said a man comes for the light on purpose and knowing why — you said it's the whole answer and it took you three years to learn it and a man showed you in four words—"

"I said *put it down and stand next to it in the dark*," said Rook. "That's the four words. That's the whole of it."

"Yes," said Milo, "and I've done that. I did it this morning and they put two bolts in the road and it worked, and it worked because they came for the light and not the boy, and they'll do it again, and this time I'm not going to be standing next to it, I'm going to be *carrying* it, and that's the only difference between a thing that works and a thing that works better—"

"It is not the only difference."

"No," Milo agreed. "It isn't."

Toren discovered that he had got hold of Milo's sleeve. He did not remember doing it. Milo looked down at his hand and then up at him with the most patient face Toren had ever seen on him.

"Tor," he said. "There's your gran on the ground behind a wheel."

"I'll go."

"You can't. You're the only one who can kill anything and they're going to be on the arch behind us." He said it without any drama at all, as a piece of ordinary arithmetic that he had done some time ago and had been carrying around. "And Dessa can't run, and Wyck has to hold the neck, and he's got a hole in him and he can't do the fast thing and everybody's known it for four days.

"It's me. It's been me since about the sixth hour. I've been sitting there working out how to say it so nobody'd argue, and I couldn't think of a way. So I'm just going to go, because if we do this for two more minutes, somebody's going to say something kind and I'll stop being able to."

Rook said, "Milo."

"You said it this afternoon. You said the boy with the light is the most important person on this bank, and everybody's going to have to live with it, including him." Milo's chin had gone, but his voice held. "Well. I've lived with it for about six hours. That's what it turned out to be."

"Come back along the wall the moment the cart's across," said Rook. "The moment. Not one second after. Put the light down on the stone and come back in the dark and I will be at the end of that wall with my hand out."

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"Put it down and come back in the dark," said Milo, "and you'll be at the end of the wall."

He turned round and hugged Toren — hard, with both arms, the way he had at Hobb's Wall when they had decided not to be split up — and said something into his shoulder that Toren did not catch, and did not ask him to repeat, and would have given anything afterward to have heard.

Then he went across the arch in the dark, on his own, with his hands empty and shut.

---

The rest of it took a minute and a half.

Toren had the mule's head. That was his job and he did it and he has never been able to remember one single detail of the cart going over that arch, not the sound of the wheel on the brick, not where his hands were.

What he remembers is the light coming up out on the water on his left.

It came up small and green at the top of the sluice wall forty feet out from the bank, and it lifted, and went on lifting, until it was over the head of a boy walking out along a stone wall in the middle of a sheet of burning water with his arms straight up, and it was the least bright thing on that whole flat, and every man on that bank looked at it.

He heard the first bolt go. It went somewhere into the channel.

He heard the shouting change — the pitch of it, right along the south bank, the note men's voices make when they all turn at once — and somebody a long way off bellowing "*Left, on the wall, on the wall—*" and somebody else shouting a range.

And Milo Faust walked out along the sluice wall in the dark with a light over his head, at a steady pace, not hurrying, the way he had held it up over a wall in an orchard for eleven minutes with his arms shaking, and he got twenty feet, and thirty, and then he went down on the stone.

The light did not go out.

---

"*Keep going,*" Rook was shouting. "Keep the cart going. Get it off the brick. Get it *off*—"

Wyck was already past them.

He went back across the arch the other way, into it, with the burning water on his right hand, and out along the sluice wall in the open with bolts going into the stone around him, and he did not run, because Wyck Tanner did not run, and he picked Milo up off the wall with both arms and the bad one held, and he turned round and came back.

Somebody on the south bank was screaming at somebody else. Toren heard it over the water and over the reeds and over his own blood in his ears, a man's voice gone completely out of shape: "*He's a boy — he's a boy, you've shot a—*"

"Reload."

"He's a—"

"*Reload.*"

---

They put him in the cart bed on the north side of the arch. Senna, who had been on the ground behind a wheel four minutes before, got herself up into that bed on one arm with nobody helping her and nobody daring to stop her. She got Milo's head and shoulders into her lap.

There was a bolt in him under the collarbone on the left side, with about four inches of it standing out. He was breathing wrong. Toren had never heard anybody breathe like that and did not need to be told what it meant. He understood in the first half second, the way you understand a step that is not there.

Rook got up into the bed.

He put his hand flat on Milo's chest and held it there, and shut his eyes, and Toren watched the man's whole face go still in the way it went still when he was doing the thing nobody could see.

Then he took his hand away.

"Milo," he said.

"'S'all right," said Milo.

"Look at me a minute."

"Did it work?" His eyes came round and found Toren instead, which was a thing Toren was grateful for and ashamed of at the same time. "Tor. Did the cart go over?"

"It went over."

"All of it? Her too?"

"All of it," said Toren. "Everybody. The mule's fine, the wheel held, everybody's over."

"Good," said Milo. "Good. That's — good."

He lay there for a moment with his eyes going round the inside of the cart, at the boards, at the rolled blankets, at the four of them.

"I'm not going to be able to do the bread," he said.

Dessa made a sound and put both hands over her mouth.

"Dess. Don't." Milo's hand moved about two inches on the blanket, looking for hers, and she got hold of it. "It's the *end crust*. It's not — it's not hard. It's just Senna first and the end crust, because she said in the first week and nobody ever asked her again."

"I know the order," said Dessa.

"Say it."

"Senna, me, Wyck, him, Tor, you."

"Tor's next to last," said Milo. "I'm last. That's the actual order and you've had it wrong for a month."

Rook had not moved. He was kneeling in the cart bed with his ruined side and his hands open on his knees.

"Milo Faust," he said. "Listen to me, because I want to do this properly and I haven't got it in me to do it twice.

"You went out on a stone wall in front of eleven crossbows with a light over your head, on your own judgment, having thought about it for six hours and told nobody, and you took every man on that bank off a cart with an old woman in it for as long as it took to get her across. I watched you do it. There were five of us on the wrong side of that arch and there are five of us on this side and it is because of you, and it is not going to sound better in a year—"

He stopped.

Toren, on the tailgate, watched a man get to the end of a sentence he had said once before over a cairn in a road cut and find that it would not come out.

"—it's not going to sound better in a year," Rook said. "I've said that to you before about somebody else and I was right and it was the only thing I had, and I have not got anything better tonight, and I'm sorry."

"You did say it," Milo said. "At the stones. You said it like you were reading a weight off a scale." He was smiling, or his mouth was. "I've thought about that about four hundred times. It was the best thing anybody said to me the whole—"

He stopped, and his face changed, and Senna's hand came down across his forehead and stayed.

"It's all right," she said. "It's all right, my love. I've got you. I'm here, and I've got you, and I'm not going anywhere at all."

Milo said, "Don't stop saying it."

"Saying what, sweetheart?"

"Her name. Mine." He got it out with a great deal of difficulty and he got all of it out. "I couldn't say Marta for eleven days. It — went funny. It got to where I couldn't and then I could and it was better, and it's the only thing I know that I know properly, and nobody ever tells you, so I'm telling you. Say it out loud. Even when it's awful. *Especially* — "

He did not finish it.

He was there for a little while after that and not able to talk, and Senna held his head and talked to him steadily and quietly about nothing whatever — about a wedding in 2112, about a dog her mother had owned, about the price of salt — in a low, warm, entirely unhurried voice, until at some point in the middle of a sentence about salt she stopped, and put her hand over his eyes, and left it there.

---

Toren was still standing at the tailgate when Rook came down out of the cart.

He did it slowly, with his left hand under his ribs and his right on the rail. He stood on the road bed for a second with his back to all of them, and then he bent down and picked something up off the boards, and Toren saw what it was: the small grey stone, Vera Kess's stone, which he had put down on the cart bed to get both hands onto Milo's chest, and which he had left there for four minutes without once looking for it.

"Where are you going?" said Dessa.

Rook did not answer her.

He walked back toward the arch, the way they had all just come, into the light of the burning reeds, and his shadow went out in front of him along the road bed forty feet long, and Wyck said "Rook," and he did not stop, and Dessa said "*Rook*," and he did not stop.

Toren went after him.

He did it without deciding to, and he had no weapon in his hand, and he was thirteen years old, and he crossed that arch at a run with his heart going and the heat of two hundred yards of burning reed coming up off the water into his face, and he came off the south end of it onto the spur where the neck was, where the five men had gone down an hour ago, and stopped.

There were men on the far side of the neck, back down the bank in the dark, thirty or forty of them, and not one of them was coming forward, because of what was happening in the open ground in front of them.

Rook had a man on the road.

He had him on his back on the brick with a knee across him and a fistful of collar, and he had the stone in his other hand with nothing on the end of it yet, and he was not shouting, and Toren, ten feet away, heard every word.

"Look at me," said Rook.

The man on the ground looked at him. He was fifty-something, grey, thick through the neck, with an old scar under the eye that had taken the lashes away, and he looked up into the face above him for a long moment with the reeds burning behind it.

"I don't know you," he said.

"No," said Rook. "You never did. You stood outside the long room door at Homura on the eleventh of the ninth month and you counted us as we came out, and you had all the time in the world, and you got to nine."
