# Chapter 44 — Toren

An hour later there were still nine.

Toren counted them four times because he could not make himself believe it. Nine points of orange in a line across the bank, in the same places, at the same brightness, as though nothing down there had burned an hour of wood.

"They haven't moved," he said.

"No," said Rook. "And none of them have been fed, and none of them have gone down, and a fire a man's sitting at does both. Those are nine fires built to be counted. He told you a day and a half and then he lit you nine fires and went to bed, and the four of you have been standing up in the dark ever since watching them very carefully.

"Down. All of you. Boots off. Two hours each and I'll take the last."

"You're at a tenth," said Dessa.

"I'm at a tenth and I'm not going to be at a fifth in the morning whatever anybody does, and you four are going to be worth exactly what you've slept." He sat down on the cart shaft. "That's the first thing a company does and it doesn't cost them a thing. They can't get at you for a day so they spend the day making you tired, and the reason it works is that being frightened feels like doing something. Boots off."

Nobody slept much. Toren lay under the cart with his coat over him and his fist round the stone and listened to the sluice going, and the sluice went all night, and it sounded like a man breathing out slowly and not stopping, and every time he came up out of a half hour of nothing it was still doing it.

---

Dessa woke them before light with one word.

"Up."

She had not raised her voice and she was not looking at anything. She was sitting exactly where she had sat, cross-legged with the bad leg out, hands open on her knees, and her face had the look it had worn in the grass five nights ago, which was the look of somebody listening in a room with no sound in it.

"North," she said. "Across the arch. Two on the road bed at twenty-eight, and something on the water side of the bank below them that isn't standing up. They came past me while it was still dark and I had them and I let them, because there was nothing to be done about two men at four in the morning and I wanted to know if there'd be more."

"How many more."

"Six," said Dessa. "They've been coming up the slope out of the water in ones since about the fourth hour. That's eight north of us and they're all on the far side of the sluice and I can't get any further than thirty feet, so there might be twelve, and I'd rather say eight and be honest than say twelve and be useful."

Rook was already up and had his boots on.

"Eight," he said. "Say eight. They'd not risk more than that in the water in the dark, and the water's why they did it — they've come up the reed beds in flat boats from a mile south and got onto the bank behind us, and that's exactly what I'd have done, and I'd have done it last night while a lot of tired people watched nine fires."

"So we're between them," said Wyck.

"We're between them."

"Then the road north—"

"Was never on offer," said Rook. "It was on offer if I walked out, and it wasn't on offer then either, and the reason I know that is that he put eight men across it four hours after he said it." He looked at Toren, briefly. "That's the register. That's the whole of what I've been trying to teach you since the field with the sleeping places in it. Everything he said to us last night was true, including the bit about the wheel, and none of it was going to happen."

---

They did it in the grey, and Rook laid it out on the cart shaft with a finger, and it took him about a minute.

"North end first. Now, while it's still half dark and while they think we're deciding.

"Eight of them on that road, on ground they've had four hours on, with the main body two miles south of us and forty minutes behind whatever happens next. Forty minutes is everything we've got and it is not very much.

"I'm going up the road with Toren and Wyck and we are going to break that block northward, and by break I mean make it not worth eight men's morning, because I am not asking any of you to kill eight people and it isn't necessary. They're hired. They've had a fee and eleven days and their own opinion, and about half of them have never stood in front of a man who came forward. Men who are being paid have a number in their heads and you don't have to reach it in every one of them, you only have to reach it in the two who are actually holding the middle, and the rest will find a reason.

"Dessa stays with the cart on this side of the arch, because Dessa is the only reason we are not already dead this morning and I am not spending her in a road fight. Milo stays with Dessa. If anything comes at that cart from the water it comes at Dessa's call and she has thirty feet and Milo has a voice and there'll be no help coming for four minutes, and I'd like both of you to have heard me say the number now instead of finding it out."

"Four minutes," said Milo.

"Four minutes. Say it back."

"Four minutes."

"Senna stays in the bed and doesn't sit up," said Rook, "and if she sits up I'll say something I can't take back."

"I shall sit up," said Senna, from the blankets, "and you shall say it, and we shall both survive the experience."

---

They went up over the arch in the last of the dark with Toren in the middle and Wyck on the water side, and about eighty yards north of it the road ran between two long humps of spoil where the old diggers had thrown the bank up, and that was where the eight of them were.

Toren saw the first one at forty yards as a shape that was slightly the wrong shape for spoil, and knew he had seen it because Wyck's breathing changed.

"Milo would say it," Wyck said.

"Left hump, near end, low," said Toren, and his voice came out about a third of the size he had sent it.

"Say it like he says it."

"*Left hump, near end, low!*"

Something down there swore, and stood up, and a voice back in the dark said "Hold—" and another voice, further off, said "*Now,*" at the same instant, and Toren learned in that half second the single thing about human beings that a hundred husks had never taught him: they disagreed. Two men on the same side, twenty feet apart, with two different ideas about the next second, and both ideas happened.

Three of them came out from the left hump at a run, and one man on the right stayed exactly where he was and shouted at them to come back.

"Wyck," said Rook.

Wyck Tanner stepped one pace forward into the middle of the road and planted.

Toren had seen him do it on a press-house step in the dark with a bad arm, and it had frightened him then, and it frightened him again now for a different reason, because there was nothing to see. He simply became a thing in the road that the road had always had in it. The first man came at him with a boar spear at a full run and put the whole of himself behind it, and Wyck took the shaft on the bar and turned it eight inches and the man went past him and into the spoil, and Wyck's boots were where Wyck's boots had been.

The second one saw it happen and did not commit.

That was the moment Rook had been waiting for, and Toren only understood it afterward. The man checked — a half step, a shortening, the ordinary human instant of wanting to see how a thing went before being in it. Rook went past Toren's shoulder at a walk, took him off his feet with a shoulder and an elbow, and put him down on his back in the ruts. He stood on the man's wrist and said, without any heat at all, "Stay down, son, you've had your morning," and left him there, alive, shouting.

Which left the third.

The third had a hatchet and was twenty-two or so and had got past Wyck on the water side while the bar was still turning, and he was between Toren and nothing at all, and he came.

Toren did the thing his hands had been taught to do on four hundred yards of towpath with a sack of corn on his chest.

He did not look at his own fist. He got the spike out on the way forward and it came out hot and crooked and ugly the way it always came out, the way his father had told him to let it come out in a road cut a month ago, and he went low under the hatchet arm because that was where the man was not, and he put it through the outside of the man's thigh.

It went in like nothing.

That was the part nobody had prepared him for and there was no preparing anybody for it. A husk resisted. A husk was a sack of wrong meat and you had to shove. This went in the way a knife goes into a cheese, and the man's whole weight came onto it and then off it, and he sat down in the road with his leg out in front of him making a high sound with no words in it at all, and his hatchet was on the ground three feet away and neither of them looked at it.

Toren stood over him with a lit spike in his hand and the man looked up at him.

"Don't," the man said.

"Toren," said Rook. "Left."

He turned left because the voice made him turn left, and got his forearm up, and a man's fist with a knuckle-bar in it went off the bone of it and numbed his whole arm to the shoulder, and then Wyck came across and put the bar into the man's ribs and he went off the edge of the bank and down the grass slope into the water, and came up in the reeds swearing, and did not come back up the slope.

"*Hold!*" That was the voice from the right hump, the one that had been telling them to hold all along. "Hold, you idiots — Prewitt! Prewitt, get them *off* —"

And then, from further south, thin and clear across the water, a whistle.

The eight of them came off the road in about six seconds. Not a rout — Toren watched it and it was not a rout, they went backward down the slope facing the way they had come from and two of them stopped to pick up the one Rook had put in the ruts, and the man with the leg was dragged by the collar of his coat down the grass with his heels bouncing, and they went into the reeds and the boats and away, and the whole thing was over.

"We won," said Toren. His voice had gone strange.

"No," said Rook, already turning. "That's the whistle. Move."

---

The whistle was for the water.

They came off the arch at a dead run — Rook first and then Wyck and then Toren with his numbed arm swinging — and the light was coming up grey and flat over eleven miles of water, and they could see it from the crown of the arch: four flat boats out in the reeds two hundred yards south, coming in at the bank below the engine house, and the reeds going over in lines where men were already wading in front of them.

Dessa was standing on the cart step with one hand on the rail.

"Nine," she called. "Nine in the water and they're at the bank now. *Now,* Rook—"

"How's she got nine?" Wyck was saying. "She's thirty feet—"

"She hasn't got nine, she's counted the reeds going over," said Rook, "and she's right, and get on the arch."

Milo had had four minutes alone, without help. He had spent the first three doing exactly what he had been drilled to do eleven times on a flat by a gate. He had set his light down.

He had set it down on the road bed on the south side of the arch, out in the open, ten feet from the cart and twelve feet from where he was standing, and he was standing in the last of the dark in the lee of the wheel with his hands empty.

Two bolts came up out of the reeds while Toren was still running.

The first one went past the light and into the road bed and stood up out of the earth with its flights shivering. The second one hit the light itself and went through it — because there was nothing there to hit — and skittered away over the ruts into the grass on the far side.

"They *shot* at it," said Milo. He was white to the lips, and he was grinning — which was a thing his face did when it had nowhere else to go. "They shot at it twice. It's in the road. It's *in the road*; it's not in my hands; I did the thing—"

"Yes," said Rook, going past him. "Pick it up and put it out. It's daylight and you've spent it."

Milo got two steps and stopped.

"They didn't shoot at me."

"No."

"The first one shot where a person would have had to stand to hold it. The second one saw there wasn't a person and shot at the light anyway."

Rook came back one pace.

"Yes."

"So he knew it wasn't in anybody's hand."

"By the second shot."

"And he still put a bolt through it."

"Yes."

Milo looked at the green thing sitting by itself in the ruts, and then past it to the reeds where the two bolts had come from. He had stopped grinning. "Because he'd already chosen what he was shooting at."

"Because men shoot at what they can see," said Rook. "Once six men turn their heads, the seventh turns before he knows why. Useful isn't safe. Confuse the two and I'll explain it while we're both dead. Pick it up."

Milo picked it up. The light folded between his hands and was gone, and he went straight to the cart instead of looking to see whether anybody had noticed him do it.

---

What followed was an hour and it was not like anything.

Toren had been in four fights and all of them had been a short violent confusion that arrived, and emptied him, and was over, and left ash on the grass to be lifted. This was an hour long. It had stretches in it where nothing happened at all, where he stood on the crown of a brick arch with his heart going and his arm aching and watched men two hundred feet away doing sensible unhurried things: dragging a boat up, sitting down, changing a wet boot, arguing.

And then it would come again, in a rush of eleven seconds, and stop.

They came at the arch four times.

The first time was eight of them up the road bed from the south, shoulder to shoulder with three spears in front. Rook put the cart across the road behind the arch, with the mule out of the shafts and the tail toward them. Wyck stood in the twelve-foot gap on the crown with the sluice going underneath him. The eight came up the slope and broke on him the way water breaks on a stone.

It was the most frightening thing Toren had ever watched, because three of them got their hands on Wyck Tanner at once and he did not go backward and he did not go forward. Toren fought over his left shoulder, where the bad arm was and where they all came, and put the spike through a man's hand on a spear shaft and heard the man scream and did not look at it.

Then somebody down the slope shouted a name and they all went back twenty feet, in order, and stood there breathing, and one of them said something and two of them laughed.

"They're laughing," said Toren.

"They're frightened," said Rook. "So are you. You've gone the other way about it, that's all."

The second time they came at the water side, four of them up the bank slope onto the flat spur below the engine house. That was Dessa's, entirely. She called them out of the reeds one at a time while they were still crawling: "One at the sluice head, he's low, he's at the third gate — two more behind him on the stone — the far one's stopped, he's stopped, he's waiting for the other two."

Rook went down the inside of the bank and met the first man at the sluice head before the man had got his feet under him. That was the end of that. It was fast and it was ugly, and Toren, above on the arch, made himself watch it and wished, watching it, that he hadn't.

The third time they did not come at all. They came halfway and made a great deal of noise and went back, and while everybody's eyes were south a man got up the north slope on his own with a hooked pole and cut the mule's hobble and tried to drive Pitch off the bank.

Pitch, who had been through a road cut and a bridge in fog and an orchard in the dark, and who had a low and entirely accurate opinion of people, put his head down and hit the man with his shoulder and stood on his foot, and the man went off the bank backward, and Milo laughed with his whole body for about four seconds and then stopped and sat down on the road and put his hands over his face.

When he got up again, he pulled both bolts out of the road and laid them beside the wheel. "The first two came close together. After that, they shot five and then six."

"You counted."

"There wasn't anything else I could do."

"Counting was the right thing."

The fourth time they got Rook.

He was on the north side of the cart and there were two of them at the tailgate and he took the first one's spear away and killed him with it, and the second one — who was not a fighter, who was somebody's cousin with a fee in his pocket, who had a farrier's rasp filed to a point and both hands round it — got it into Rook's side under the ribs, and about four inches of it, and Toren heard the noise the man made afterward, which was not a noise of triumph and was closer to horror.

Rook took the rasp off him and put him on the ground and did not kill him.

"Go on," he said. "Get up. Go south."

And the man got up and went south, and fell over twice, and got up both times.

---

They went at about the eighth hour, all of them, and the whistle went twice, and the flat boats poled back out along the reed lanes toward the south end and the road bed went quiet, and Toren sat down on the arch with his legs shaking and could not make them stop.

He looked at the road.

It was the thing he could not get past that whole grey morning, and afterward he thought it was probably the moment he stopped being able to think about the two kinds of fighting as one subject with two chapters in it. There was a man in the road twenty feet from him with a spear in him. There was blood on the brickwork of the arch in a long smear where Wyck had come off it. There was a boot. There was a cap.

Nothing came apart. Nothing dissolved and ran down the slope of the road into the low places and lay there silver waiting to be lifted, and nobody got a flask out, and there was no yield, and there was not going to be any. The whole hour had produced nothing whatever except what was lying on it.

"Don't," said Rook, beside him, without looking.

"I wasn't—"

"You were about to work out what that's worth," said Rook. "It isn't worth anything. It's a man in a road and the ground keeps him. That's the whole difference and you'll spend the rest of your life on it and I can't shorten it for you."

He was sitting on the parapet stub with his coat open and his left hand pressed flat under his own ribs, and there was a great deal of blood on his shirt, and his face had gone a colour that Toren had never seen on him and had seen on his grandmother.

"Dessa," said Rook.

"I'm here."

"Get Milo's mother's flask out of the bottom of the bag."

Milo was already moving.

"No," said Rook. "Stop. Sit down, Milo. I said get it out, I didn't say open it, and I'm not going to touch it and neither is anybody else. I want it out of the bag and by the wheel where I can see it, and I want everybody to have looked at it, because at some point in the next day one of us is going to have to make a decision about that flask and I'd rather it wasn't made at four in the morning by somebody who's just remembered it exists."

---

They got him into the lee of the engine house wall and Wyck held the lamp and Toren held the cloth and Dessa did the packing, because Dessa's hands were the only ones in the party that were not shaking, and the whole time she worked she talked, quietly, without stopping, about where everybody was: Wyck at my shoulder, Toren at my knee, Milo up at the corner, Senna in the cart, mule at eleven feet, nothing in the water out to thirty — and Toren understood about halfway through that she was not doing it for them.

"It's not deep," she said, at the end. "It's four inches of a bad shape in a bad place and it's not deep."

"It's not deep," Rook agreed.

"Can you—"

"Not on myself. You can't get the angle." He got his coat closed one-handed. "It doesn't matter. What matters is the arithmetic and I'll give it you now and then I'd like an hour.

"They lost four dead and they've got six that won't stand up tomorrow and they got a whole morning of us being tired, and they'll count that a fair day's work, because they didn't come here to take a cart. They came to take me and they can do that on the ninth day as well as the first.

"We are not going north. The block will be back on that road before dark and there'll be more of it. We are not going south. So we go onto the spur, behind the brick, and we put the cart where the ground is narrow, and we hold a piece of bank thirty feet wide, and that is not a plan, it is what is left."

"That's a siege," said Wyck. "You said a held place is a grave."

"It is."

"So—"

"So we hold it for one night," said Rook, "because tonight is the new moon, and there'll be no light on this water at all, and eleven miles of embankment full of men who can't see is a different country from eleven miles of embankment in daylight. Tonight is the only piece of ground I've been given since the crossroads and I'm going to take it."

He looked round at the four of them — and last, and longest, at Milo Faust, sitting on the cart step with his arms round his knees and his hands hanging.

"Which makes the boy with the light the most important person on this bank," he said, "and everybody here is going to have to live with that, including him."

---

They moved the cart onto the spur in the last of the good light, and it took all five of them and the mule and an hour, and the wheel held.

The sun went down into the water in the west without a cloud anywhere near it, red and enormous, and the whole flat lit up for about four minutes like a sheet of hammered tin, and then began to go out.

Toren stood at the corner of the engine house and watched it go, and listened, and found that he could still hear the man from the morning.

He was down in the reeds somewhere off the south end, a long way out. They had dragged him into a boat and the boat had not gone anywhere, or it had gone somewhere and he had been put out of it, and he had been calling since about noon — not words any more, not for the last few hours. Just a voice, rising and falling, with the sluice going underneath it.

Twenty feet away, Milo was sitting on the tailgate with his hands together in his lap, opening them a little and closing them, opening them and closing them, checking.

Nobody explained anything to anybody. The light went out of the water, and the first stars came, and there was no moon at all.
