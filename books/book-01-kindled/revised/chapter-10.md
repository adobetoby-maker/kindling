# Chapter 10 — Toren

They got four hundred paces.

Toren counted them, because counting was something to do with the part of his head that wanted to run. Pitch's hooves on the cracked road. Senna's breathing behind him. The tin bath on the tipped cart going by on their left, still lashed down, still full of somebody's things.

At four hundred, the first one came over the guardrail.

It didn't jump the rail. It came *through* the gap where a length of it had rotted out, and it did that poorly, catching a foot and going down on the shoulder gravel and getting up again without any of the pause a person would have taken. That was the first thing Toren understood about them, before he understood anything else: it fell down, and it did not care that it had fallen down.

It was about the size of a man. It had been, maybe. Grey-brown, the color of a wasp nest, and dried-looking, with the skin drawn tight over everything so you could see how it was put together underneath, and it was put together nearly right and not quite. The arms were long. The head sat forward. There was no face on it that Toren could find, and he looked, and afterward he wished he hadn't looked.

Bram Sark shot it.

The sound went up the cut and came back twice. The thing folded at the hips and dropped and did not get up, and Toren had one clean second of relief — *oh, they die* — and then Bram broke the gun open and thumbed a shell in and said, in the same flat voice he used about weather, "That's a husk. Get behind the cart."

"How many —" Wyck started.

"Behind the cart."

The rock face on the eastern side began to move.

Not all at once. That was almost worse. They came down in ones and threes, sliding, tumbling, some of them landing wrong and getting up wrong, spilling gravel ahead of them in a long dirty rattle, and there were more coming through the rail gaps at road level, and more further up, and Toren stopped being able to count at somewhere past twenty and started just seeing *many*, which is what the eye does when the number stops being useful.

They did not organize. He noticed that even then. They didn't flank, they didn't wait, they didn't do the thing wolves did in the stories or the thing men did on the practice ground at Sallow Creek. Every single one of them came straight at the nearest living thing at the fastest speed it could manage, and if it fell, it got up, and if it hit the cart, it clawed the cart.

Garrick had the brush axe off the cart rail. He put himself at Pitch's shoulder, which was the only place to put himself, because a panicked mule in a stone corridor kills more people than anything with claws.

"Dessa! His head — hold his head, don't let him rear —"

"I've got him!"

"Marta!"

Marta Faust was already down off the cart step with her boots planted and her hands out low and open, and she said a word Toren didn't know, and the air about eight feet in front of her went wrong.

There was no light. He always remembered that, later, when people asked. There was no light and no sound and nothing you could point at. There was simply a place, roughly a wagon-width across, where the road stopped being available. The first husk to reach it hit it and stopped as though it had run into a wall in the dark, and went down, and the second one went over the top of the first and hit the same nothing and slid off it sideways.

Toren had lived eight years in the same settlement as that woman and had never seen her do anything but set bones.

"*Move,*" Marta said, through her teeth. "It's a door, not a house. Move!"

They moved.

---

Toren's Ember was in his hand.

He had no memory of taking it out of his pocket. It was just there, cool and flat, and he was running with the cart with his fist closed around it, and behind him something screamed — not a person, a husk, a thin dry sound like a board being pulled off a nail — and he thought, with total clarity: *now.*

He asked it for a blade.

He asked it the way you ask for something when you are thirteen and the ground has come apart: with everything, all at once, his whole chest behind it, *now, now, give me it, a blade, please.*

Nothing happened.

He looked down at his own hand as though his hand had betrayed him. Grey slab. Cool. Rounded at the corners. A river stone somebody had quit on.

"Toren!"

He looked up, and a husk came off the top of the guardrail at him.

He didn't decide anything. His left arm went up because arms do that, and he got it across his own face, and the thing hit him high, and they went down together into the gravel with the whole weight of it on his chest. It smelled like a dry cellar. It had no face, and it was trying very hard to get its arms past his arm, and it was not strong, that was the strange part, it was not much stronger than Wyck, it was only *relentless*, it had no idea at all that it might stop.

And the Ember in his right fist went hot.

Not warm. Hot, and long. It came out of the bottom of his hand in a shape he had not asked for — not a blade, nothing like a blade, a crooked spike about the length of his forearm with a bad thick heel to it. Because his right arm was already swinging in blind panic, the spike went into the husk under the arm and out the other side, and the thing came apart on it like wet paper.

It fell off him. He lay there. The spike was still out.

"*Up*," his father said, and hauled him up by the collar without slowing down.

---

Here is what Toren decided, in the ninety seconds after that, while running beside a cart in a stone corridor:

The Ember had done it.

It made sense. It made perfect sense, and it fit everything. He had begged for a blade and gotten nothing. He had not asked for anything at all, and it had given him a spike, and the spike had saved his life. He had thought it was a tool. It wasn't a tool. It was something closer to an animal, and it had decided, and the decision had been the right one, and his own opinion in the matter had been worth exactly nothing.

That thought settled into him in the middle of the worst thing that had ever happened to him, and it settled deep, the way things do when they arrive with your heart going like that. It would be a long time before anybody got it out of him.

So he stopped asking.

He ran with his fist out and the spike out ahead of it, and he did not think about the shape at all, and it held, and when a husk came off the eastern bank he did not aim, he put his arm out the way you put an arm out to stop a door, and it went through, and the thing dropped.

Then he got excited, and thought *yes, that, again, bigger*, and the spike guttered and shrank and went out and was a grey slab in his fist, and a husk hit him in the hip and knocked him into the wheel.

"Toren!" Milo, from the cart bed, both hands full of Senna's blankets. "*Toren!*"

He got up. His hip didn't work right. He got the Ember out in front of him, and he thought, very deliberately, about nothing, and after two seconds that lasted a year, the spike came back — smaller. Uglier. But out.

*It decides,* he thought. *Fine. Fine. Then I'll shut up.*

---

Bram Sark fired four more times, and Toren heard him counting them out loud each time — "three," "two," "one" — not for anyone else. For himself. For the arithmetic.

At *one*, the cut narrowed, and the cart's near wheel went into a break in the road bed, and everything stopped.

"Push!" Garrick was already at the wheel. "Wyck! On the spokes, *lift* —"

Wyck was there before the sentence finished, which Toren remembered afterward and never told him. Big hands on the spokes, back into it, boots skidding. Milo threw himself off the cart to make it lighter, and Senna said something furious about being carried, and nobody had time for her.

Marta's wall was eleven or twelve feet behind them, and the husks were stacking against it, three and four deep now, climbing over their own fallen, and Toren watched the woman's face and saw what it was costing her. There was sweat standing out on her upper lip in the cold. Her hands had started to shake.

"Bram," said Garrick. "*Bram.*"

Bram Sark broke the shotgun, looked into it, and closed it on nothing.

He set it down on the road, upright against the guardrail, the way you set a tool down when you mean to come back for it. Then he took the hand axe off his belt and walked back past Marta and stood in the gap on the far side of her wall, in the open, in the middle of the road.

"Get the wheel out," he said, "and don't stop for me."

"Bram —"

"You've got the old woman and four kids, Garrick. Get the wheel out."

He was a hired man. He had been with them twelve days. Toren had exchanged perhaps forty words with him in that time, and about thirty of them had been about the weather.

The husks came around Marta's wall on both sides, the way water comes around a stone, and Bram Sark went into them with a hand axe. He was good, he was *good* — he put down four of them in the first ten seconds with short economical strokes like a man splitting kindling. Then the fifth one got his arm, and the sixth got his leg, and he went down in the middle of the road, and the rest went over him the way they went over the guardrail, without any pause at all.

Dessa screamed. Not a word. Just the sound.

The wheel came out of the break with a crack and the cart lurched forward and Toren was running again with his hip on fire and his hand out, and he did not look back, because his father had said don't stop for me and it had not been his father who said it and he was already old enough to know the difference and not old enough to bear it.

---

Two hundred paces on, Garrick grabbed his wrist.

"Give it to me."

Toren didn't understand.

"The Ember." His father's face was grey with dust and there was a cut over his eyebrow and his voice had gone very calm, which was the worst thing about it. "Give it here. Now."

Toren put it in his hand.

Garrick closed his fist around it and set his jaw and asked it for the shape it had made for him for thirty-one years, the broad short blade Toren had seen a hundred times on the practice ground at Sallow Creek, and Toren watched his father's whole body brace for a weight that never came.

Nothing happened.

Garrick opened his hand. The Ember lay on his palm the color of dishwater, and it did not go hot, and it did not go cool, and it did not do anything at all, because it was not his any more.

Toren watched something go across his father's face and be put away.

"Right," Garrick said.

He pressed it back into Toren's hand and closed both his own around Toren's fist, hard enough to hurt, and held it there for one second in the middle of a road with the sound coming up behind them.

"It's yours now. That's not a thing I can take back, and it's not a thing you can give me. Don't ever offer it again in a fight. You understand me? You'll get somebody killed trying to be helpful."

"I'm sorry —"

"You've nothing to be sorry for. *Look at me.*" Toren looked. "You're doing fine. You're doing better than fine. Keep the ugly shape. Stop trying to be good at it."

Then he let go and turned around and went back three steps and took the brush axe up in both hands, because the cut ahead of them had a bend in it, and around the bend the road was already full.

They had come up the other way while everyone was watching the wall.

Not tactics. They hadn't *planned* it. They'd simply spilled down into the cut from both ends because that was where the noise was, and the noise was in the middle, and the middle was seven people and a mule.

Marta's wall broke behind them with a sound like a held breath letting go, and she staggered, and Milo caught her arm.

"Mum —"

"I'm all right. I'm all right." She was not all right. She got her feet under her and turned to face the way they'd come and put her hands out again, low and open, and Toren saw that she could hardly hold them still. "Garrick. I can give you one more. Small. Once."

"Then hold it," Garrick said, "until I say."

Ahead: the bend, and the road full.

Behind: the wall gone, and the road full.

Senna sat up in the cart bed, white to the lips, and said clearly, "Put me down and run," and nobody answered her, and Dessa took Pitch's cheek strap in both fists and put her forehead against the mule's neck and started swearing at him steadily and lovingly to keep him from bolting.

Toren stood at his father's shoulder with a crooked grey spike sticking out of the bottom of his fist and both ends of the world closing.
