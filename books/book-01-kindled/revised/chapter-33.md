# Chapter 33 — Toren

He did it at the drill, which Toren thought afterward was the only place he could have done it — and which he was fairly sure Wyck had worked out in advance.

The morning had gone on in the ordinary way for an hour and a half. They had come up out of the fold onto a green lane with good footing, and Rook had stopped them on it at about nine o'clock and put them across it, and they had done the thing they now did instead of the wall drill, which was the man drill, and it was the second time, and it was worse than the first.

He made Dessa be the man, because Dessa could not run, and a man who cannot run has to be clever, and Dessa was clever in a way that turned out to be genuinely upsetting: she walked straight up to Milo with both hands empty and said "Milo, your bootlace," and Milo looked down.

He made Milo be the man, and Milo could not do it at all, and stood in the middle of the lane apologising, and Rook let him off after two attempts, which he had never once done for anything.

Then he said, "Wyck. Line," and Wyck did not go to the line.

He stood where he was, in the middle of the lane, six feet in front of all of them, with the bar hanging down out of his bad hand, and said:

"No. I want to ask you something first."

---

Rook did not sigh and did not fold his arms and did not do any of the things a man does to buy himself a second.

"Go on," he said.

"In front of everybody."

"That's where you're standing."

"Right." Wyck's chin was up. There was a wash of colour across his cheekbones that had nothing to do with the cold. "Then here it is.

"What am I standing in a line for?"

The lane was very quiet. Down at the bottom of it, Pitch pulled at something in the hedge and the cart shifted six inches and stopped.

"I've thought about how to ask it properly for about three weeks," Wyck said, "and that's the best I've got, and I know it sounds like a boy being difficult, so I'm going to say the rest of it, and then you can answer whichever bit you want.

"You taught us survival. Eight days, shoving and shouting, and it was good, and I'm not stupid, I know I'd be dead four times over. But you finished that. You finished it before we left Hobb's Wall, and you've been doing something else since, and it's got — it's got *stages* to it. You do a thing, and then two days later you do a thing that only works if we did the first one. That's not a man keeping some children alive on a road. That's a course. My father put me through one of those to lay a hedge, and it took a year, and he told me at the start what I'd be able to do at the end of it.

"Yesterday you taught us where a person keeps the parts of him you'd cut, and you did it standing over a coat full of straw with the throat out of it, and then you put me on top of Toren in a field and made me take his Ember off him.

"And that camp is *in front of us*." His voice did not rise. It went flatter. "You've sat facing south every night since the Drum. Six nights. I've watched you do it, and so has everybody here, and none of us has said a word about it, and last night you turned round and faced north, and you didn't say anything about that either.

"So I'm not asking what you are. I've stopped caring what you are. I'm asking what I'm for." He shifted his grip on the bar. "You're building something out of four people, and you haven't asked one of us whether we wanted it built. I'd probably have said yes. That's the thing that's been keeping me up: I'd have said yes on the first morning if you'd asked. You didn't ask, so now I have to."

---

"Sit down," said Rook.

"Don't do that."

"I'm not doing anything. Sit down, all of you, this'll take a while, and I'm not going to stand in a lane and do it."

Nobody moved for a second. Then Dessa sat down on the bank at the side of the lane, which settled it, and Milo sat next to her, and Toren sat where he was, and Wyck went and sat on the other bank on his own, facing them, which was the exact opposite of what anybody had expected and was somehow the strongest thing he did all morning.

Rook stayed standing. He put his hands in his coat pockets.

"The practical answer first," he said, "because it's true, and you're owed it, and then we can stop pretending it's the answer.

"You're standing in a line because there are five of you on a road with a cart, one of you can't walk far, one of you can't be replaced" — he did not look at Milo when he said it — "and this country north of here is worse than the country behind. The drilling keeps you alive. All of that is true, and none of it is what you asked."

"No," Wyck agreed.

"So. The rest."

He took one hand back out of his pocket, looked at it, and put it back.

"Yes," said Rook. "I'm building something in the four of you on purpose. I started doing it about ten days ago, in a field, on a morning after a fire, and I did not decide to do it in advance, and I have not stopped since. You're right that it has stages. You're right that it's a course. You're right that I never asked, and you're right that it wasn't mine to not ask, and I'm not going to argue with a single word of it, because I've been standing in front of you for a fortnight knowing exactly what I was doing and telling myself that four children who can hold a line don't need the reason.

"And yes. There's a reason. There's one reason and it's the whole of it and it isn't a general one about the road or the winter or the state of the world. It's mine. It's the most personal thing there is about me."

Toren became aware that he had stopped breathing.

"And I'm not going to give it to you today," said Rook.

---

Milo made a small noise.

"That's it?" he said. "That's—"

"Wait."

"You can't just say there's a—"

"*Wait*," said Rook, not loudly, and Milo waited.

"Here's what I'll give you instead, and I'd like you to weigh it, because it's more than I've given anybody in a long while, and I'm aware that isn't the same as it being enough.

"One. It is not about him." He nodded at Toren. "Wyck, you've thought for three weeks that this is about the boy with the Ember and the dead father and the thing round his neck, and that the rest of you are in the line because you were standing near him. That's wrong. It's wrong in a way I could have fixed in one sentence any day since Hobb's Wall, and I didn't fix it, and that's mine, not yours."

Wyck's jaw moved. He said nothing.

"Two. Nothing I've taught any of you is aimed at getting you to do a thing for me. If I ever want something done, I'll ask for it out loud, in words, on a day when you can say no, and if I ever don't do that, you're entitled to stop trusting anything I've ever said. Write that down somewhere.

"Three." He stopped.

Something went across his face then — it was there and gone — and Toren, who had made a study of that face for twenty-four days, could not name it, which by now was not surprising, because he had never once been able to name the ones that mattered.

"Three. I'm not ready," said Rook. "That's the actual answer, and it's a poor one. It isn't that you can't be trusted with it, and it isn't that you're too young for it — because I was younger than the oldest of you when it started, and nobody asked whether I was too young. I'm not ready to say it out loud. I haven't said it out loud to anybody in — "

He stopped again.

"In a long while," he said.

---

Nobody spoke for a while.

Then Rook said, in a completely different voice, brisker, the voice he used to close a thing down:

"So here's what I'll do about it, and this is the part I want you to hear properly.

"I'll walk you to Meridian. All of you, the cart, the old woman, the whole of it, the way I said I'd walk you to Hobb's Wall and then didn't stop. I'll teach you every day between here and there, and I'll teach it as well as I know how. And when you're through the gate, and she's in front of somebody with proper instruments, I'll go, and you'll never have to wonder what any of it was for, because there won't be anybody standing in front of you not telling you."

"No," said Dessa.

She said it from the bank, without moving, in the voice she used for arithmetic.

"That's a good offer," she said. "It's a very good offer, and it's the second time you've made it, and I'd like everybody here to notice that both times you've made it, it was about four minutes after somebody asked you something you didn't want to answer."

Rook looked at her.

"At the weigh-yard, you offered to hand us to a woman with a book," said Dessa. "Today you're offering to hand us to a gate. And both times it's dressed up as you being decent, and both times what it actually is, is you arranging things so that the people who've got questions about you will be somebody else's problem before the questions come due."

The silence after that had a completely different quality.

"Dessa," said Milo, in a whisper.

"No, she's right," said Wyck.

He had not moved off his bank.

"She's right, and I'll tell you the other thing that's wrong with it," he said. "You just offered to leave. You offered it as if it's the worst thing you could do to us, as if it's the price. And it isn't, is it. It's the cheap way out for you, and it's the expensive one for us, and you know that, and you offered it anyway, which means either you don't think we'd mind—"

"I don't think that," said Rook.

"No," said Wyck. "I know you don't."

---

Toren, sitting in the green lane with his hand round a warm stone in his coat pocket, found that his own voice had arrived without being sent for.

"I want to say the thing about the middle," he said.

Everybody looked at him.

"You said it isn't about me." He had to stop and start again. "I've thought it was. For three weeks, I've thought it was, and I've been — I've been *carrying* that, and it turns out I was carrying something that wasn't there, and I'd like it on the record that I'm relieved and I'm also—"

He could not find the word.

"Insulted," said Senna, from the cart.

Toren shut his eyes.

"Thank you, Gran."

"You're extremely welcome."

She was sitting up. She had got herself up against the boards at some point during all of it, with the blanket round her shoulders, and she had a mug in both hands and the expression of a woman who has come in halfway through a play and is doing very well at it.

"I'd like to say one thing, and then I'll go back to being scenery," she said. "Nobody has to take it.

"Twenty-two nights ago, this man took the first watch. Then he took the second. Then he took most of the third, and he has done that every night since, and I have watched him do it from about four feet away, because I am an old woman who does not sleep, and there is nothing else to look at."

She drank some of the tea.

"He is not resting. I don't mean he's tired; we're all tired. I mean that whatever he is doing up there on the bank, it costs him something to do, and he is spending it on four children and a cart every night for nothing, and he has not once mentioned it or asked for a thing in return or made any of you feel it." She looked at Wyck. "You asked what you're standing in a line for. I don't know. But you've had twenty-two nights of a man paying for something, and I've been on this road long enough to tell you that when a person pays like that and won't say why, it is almost never because the reason does him credit."

"Gran—"

"I've finished," said Senna, and went back to her tea.

---

It was Wyck who ended it, and he did it standing up.

"Right," he said.

He came off the bank and down into the lane and stood in front of Rook with the bar in his hand, and Toren, watching, had one clear cold moment of thinking that this was going to go somewhere terrible.

"You've admitted there's a thing," said Wyck. "That's more than I expected to get, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't. So I'll take it, and I'll take it on those terms, and I'll say what the terms are so nobody can be confused about it later.

"I'm not asking you again. I've asked once, in the open, in front of everybody, and that's my go, and I've had it. I'm not going to needle you, and I'm not going to go round the back and ask Toren to ask you, and if anybody here does that, I'll say something about it." He said this to the lane in general. "And I'm going to stand in the line tomorrow and do the drill and do it properly, and not because I've forgiven you anything, because there's nothing to forgive yet, there's just a thing you haven't said.

"But when you say it — and you're going to say it, because you've told us there's something to say, and you can't unsay that — you say it to all five of us at once. Not to him. Not to her because she's clever. All of us, in one go, and nobody finds out second."

He held the bar out sideways and let go of it and let it fall in the grass, which was a thing Toren had never seen him do voluntarily in his life.

"That's it," said Wyck Tanner. "That's the whole of my go."

Rook stood in the lane and looked at him for a long moment.

"All five at once," he said.

"All five."

"Right," said Rook.

And he bent down and picked up the bar out of the grass and handed it back to him, handle first, and that was the end of it.

---

They made seven miles that afternoon, which was poor, and nobody minded.

The strange thing — and Toren turned it over for the rest of the day without getting anywhere near the bottom of it — was that the camp that evening was the best camp they had had since the three empty days above the Drum. Milo got two verses further into the counting song. Dessa won an argument about the corn. Wyck, unprompted, went and sat on the tailgate with Senna for half an hour and let her tell him about somebody's wedding in 2112.

Nothing had been answered. Toren kept checking it, the way you check a tooth. They knew precisely as much as they had known at breakfast, which was nothing at all, and they had been told to their faces that they were going to go on knowing nothing for an unspecified length of time.

And yet the thing that had been sitting on the camp for three weeks had gone off it.

He said so to Dessa, at the fire, after everybody else was down.

"It's not that he told us anything," Dessa said. "It's that he stopped pretending there was nothing to tell." She was working a knot out of a trace with her thumbnail. "There's a difference between a man with a secret and a man who's making you feel mad for thinking there's a secret. We've had the second one for three weeks. We've got the first one now."

"That's better?"

"It's *enormously* better, Tor. The second one's how you go peculiar."

She got the knot out.

"Also," she said, "you didn't tell him about the disk."

Toren did not say anything.

"I'm not having a go at you. I'm saying I watched you not do it, and I watched you think about doing it for about four seconds in the middle of all that, and I'd have thought about it too." She coiled the trace. "Wyck's just stood in a lane and said *nobody finds out second*, and I don't think he meant it about us, but it's going to be true about us, and I'd like you to have thought about that before it comes up rather than after."

"He already knows about the disk."

"He knows you've got one," said Dessa. "That's not the same as knowing what your father said about it, or what you did with it at the cut, or why you go white when anybody puts a hand near your collar." She looked up. "I'm not asking. I've got my own go, and I'm saving it."

---

He lay down after that and did not sleep for a while, and at some point he rolled over and looked up at the bank.

Rook was there, sitting the way he sat, with his forearms on his knees.

He was not facing south. He was not facing north either. He had turned himself about a quarter round from both and was looking out east across country at nothing in particular, at the long dun folds where a field gate was rutted going out and not coming in, and he had the piece of brass in his right hand, and he was not turning it over.

He was just holding it, quite still, the way you hold something when you have stopped noticing you are holding it.

Toren watched him for a long time, and then rolled onto his back and looked at the sky, and thought: *he said it was personal, and he said it was the whole of him, and he said he wasn't ready, and every single one of those was true, and he has never in twenty-four days said three true things in a row about himself before.*

And underneath that, quieter, in the place where he kept the things he did not say to Dessa:

*And he's going to say it, and I'm going to have to say mine after.*
