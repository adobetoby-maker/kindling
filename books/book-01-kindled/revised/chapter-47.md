# Chapter 47 — Toren

You cannot dig a grave in a road, and you cannot dig one in an embankment either, because eleven feet down an embankment is water and everybody on that bank knew it without anybody having to say so.

"Rock," said Wyck.

He said it the way he had said it in the road cut on the second of the month, standing in more or less the same posture, with his left wrist strapped to a piece of barrel stave and three fingers he could not feel, and Toren heard the whole of that afternoon come back up the road at him and had to walk twenty paces and stand with his hands on his knees.

There was rock. There was a great deal of it, and it was thirty feet away, and it had been in the water since before any of them were born: the parapet of the arch, which had gone into the sluice channel sometime in the last sixty years and lay there in the green, humped and slick and cut square by somebody's grandfather's grandfather.

They got it out of the channel a block at a time, standing in water to the thigh, passing it up hand to hand — Toren and Wyck in the channel; Dessa on the stone kerb where she could stand on the good leg and take the weight on her arms; Rook sitting on the bank with his back against the brick, because he could not lift anything and had been told so by all three of them at once.

They built it up on the spur below the engine house, on the flat piece where the ground was dry, facing west over eleven miles of water.

It took them most of the day. Nobody talked much. The reed fire had burned itself out along the south beds in the night and left a long black scar on the water that would be there for a year, and the smell of it was in everything, and twice Toren caught himself looking round for Milo to say something about the smell, because Milo would have said something about the smell, and that was the shape the whole day had: a series of small ordinary moments arriving one after another with nobody in them.

---

In the middle of the afternoon Rook made them go along the neck of the spur and do the other thing.

There were seven of them on that piece of ground and one more in the reeds below the bank, and the company had taken their own wounded and had not been able to take these, and the morning had got warm.

"You don't have to look at faces," Rook said. "You do have to do it. I'm not going to make anybody, and I'm going to tell you why you'd want to, once, and then it's yours.

"Somebody is going to come up this road in a fortnight. It'll be a salvager, or a family, or two boys off a farm, and they'll come round the engine house and find seven men lying about on a bank in the open in the middle of the summer, and there is not one thing in the world you can do for them after that except the thing you'd want done for you, and it takes four people about two hours."

They laid them along the foot of the bank on the dry side and put what was left of the reed thatch out of the engine house over them, and it was not a great deal and it was what there was.

The young one was the fourth.

Toren knew him before he got to him, from the boots, and stopped, and Rook — who was sitting on a stone twelve feet away because that was as far as he could go in one movement — said, "Yes. I saw him this morning."

"He ate the bread."

"He did."

"He came *back*." Toren heard his own voice doing something and could not stop it. "He went south with the cart and he came back in the night with them and he was on this bank and now he's—"

"He was told to," said Rook. "He'd been in that company eleven days and I'd guess he'd been handed a spear about four days ago by somebody who told him it was easy, and then he stood in a line in the dark with people shouting at him. There's nothing in this for anybody. Go through his coat."

"I'm not—"

"Go through his coat," said Rook, "because it's the last useful thing anybody can do for him. If there's a name in it or a settlement in it, somebody can be told one day, and the only way that ever happens is if somebody standing here does it now."

There was no name and there was no settlement. There was a clasp knife, a heel of bread wrapped in a rag, a bit of string with two knots in it that had meant something to somebody, and a joining chit: a thumbnail of brass with four letters and a six on it, bright, unrubbed, not eleven days off a table.

Toren carried it over and put it in Rook's hand.

Rook looked at it for a long moment. Then he opened his coat, one-handed, slowly, because of his back, and put it in with the other four.

He did not say anything about it and nobody asked him to, and it stayed in that coat.

---

They did the bread at the halt, because the day had to have a halt in it.

Dessa cut it. She did it poorly, in slabs, on the tailgate, with a lot of concentration, and then she stood there with the knife in her hand and the cloth in front of her and did not move for a while.

"Go on," said Senna.

"I know the order."

"I know you do."

"He told me the order and I had it wrong for a month," said Dessa, "and I'm going to do it right and I'm going to say it out loud, and everybody's going to have to put up with that, because he said say it out loud even when it's awful and he said it was the only thing he knew properly, and I'm not going to be a coward about it on the first day."

She picked up the end crust.

"Senna," she said, and put it in her hand.

"Thank you, my love."

"Me." She took a slab and put it down beside her own hand on the tailgate and did not eat it.

"Wyck."

Wyck took it.

"Rook."

Rook took it.

"Tor."

Toren took it and found that his hands did not want it.

"Milo," said Dessa, and cut the last slab, and put it on the cloth at the end of the row, and folded the cloth over it, and sat down on the cart step with her back to all of them.

Nobody said anything for a long time. Then Wyck said, "Right," which was not agreement with anything, and was the only thing anybody said about it, and it was enough.

---

The flask came up at dusk and it came up because Rook made it come up, which Toren understood afterward had been a kindness and had cost him something.

It was still by the wheel where he had asked for it to be put, thirty hours before. It was a small flat tin one, older than Rook's, with a dent in the side and a leather collar worn through at the seam, and it had come off Marta Faust's body in a road cut a month ago and had ridden in the bottom of a bag under the blankets ever since, unopened.

"Right," said Rook. "The flask.

"I said yesterday somebody was going to have to decide about it, and I said I'd rather it wasn't decided at four in the morning by whoever remembered it existed. So we'll do it now, in the light, with everybody here, and I'm going to say my piece first and then I'm going to shut up, because it isn't mine and I've no business having the last word on it."

"It's Milo's," said Toren.

"It's Milo's mother's," said Rook, "which is not the same thing, and the difference is the whole of it."

Dessa said, "It's a healer's kit."

"Go on."

"It's a flask of road-ash in a healer's kit," said Dessa. She had got up off the step and was standing with her arms folded. "It's not a keepsake. It was never a keepsake — it was the thing she carried so that when somebody on that road went down she'd have something to put into them, and she carried it for nine years and it's been in the bottom of a bag for a month because the only person who could have decided about it couldn't look at it.

"And there are two people on this cart who can't sit up."

"I'm not having it," said Senna.

"You're having it."

"Dessa Cole—"

"You're having it," said Dessa, "and I'll tell you the reason and then I'd like not to discuss it. He went out on a wall in the dark to get you across an arch. That's what it was for. That was the entire transaction and he's paid for it and if you sit here for four days being noble about a flask you will make it into nothing and I will not be able to stand it."

Senna looked at her for a long moment.

"Give me the wretched thing," she said.

Wyck opened it. His hands were the worst in the party and he did it anyway, propping the tin against his knee with the strapped wrist and working the cap with the other, and nobody offered to help him, because there were about four things left that Wyck Tanner could do and this was going to be one of them.

He poured a measure into Senna's cup.

Then he screwed the cap down, and looked at Rook, and said, "And a mouthful for him."

"No," said Rook.

"A mouthful."

"There's five days in that flask at the outside and she's got twelve days of road in front of her. Don't be—"

"You've got a hole through your back and you can't lift your arm above your shoulder and you're supposed to be holding a line in about two days," said Wyck, "and I'm not doing this with you. A mouthful, or I'll pour the lot on the grass and then nobody'll have it and we can all feel extremely principled about it in the morning."

There was a silence.

"He'd do it, as well," said Senna, with interest.

"He would," said Dessa.

Rook took the mouthful.

---

Toren was sick at about the ninth hour, behind the engine house, without any warning at all.

He had been perfectly all right. He had carried stone for six hours and laid out seven men and eaten bread he did not want and had a conversation with his grandmother about the wheel, and then he walked round the corner of a brick wall to get out of the wind and put his hand flat on the brickwork and was sick until there was nothing left, and then knelt in the grass shaking with his forehead against the wall.

There were two of them. That was what was in his head, and it would not go anywhere else. Not the fighting — he could not remember the fighting. The first one on the road in the morning, sitting down with his leg out in front of him, saying *don't*. And the one in the dark on the north side, who had not been looking at him.

He heard Dessa come round the corner on the stick and stop.

"Do you want me to go away?" she said.

"No."

"Do you want me to say anything?"

"No."

"Right," she said, and sat down against the wall about four feet from him with her leg out, and that is where they both were for the next hour, and she did not say one word, and neither did he.

---

Rook got himself across to the bank at the north end at about the eleventh hour, and it took him eleven minutes and Toren under his right arm, and by the end of it he was grey and had to stand still twice.

Wyck was sitting up there with the bar across his knees, facing south down the road, where he had been sitting since dark, because he had kept that watch every night since the night the man talked until the fire went down, and he had not asked anybody's permission for it then either.

Rook got himself down onto the grass beside him with his hand on Toren's shoulder the whole way.

"Go on," he said.

"I can—"

"Go and sit by the wheel."

Toren went fifteen paces down the bank and sat down with his back against the cart wheel and did what he had been taught to do by Dessa Cole, which was to be present and to not be part of it. He could see the two of them against the last grey in the west: a shape sitting upright with a bar across its knees, and a shape beside it with its head down.

He did not hear any of it. He heard the sluice going, the way he had heard it for two days, and once he heard Wyck's voice go up on one word and stop.

They were up there a long time.

---

Wyck came down on his own at about the first hour and sat down next to Toren at the wheel without being invited — which he had never once done in a month — and put the bar across his knees.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"He told you," said Toren.

"Yes."

"You're not going to—"

"No," said Wyck. "He'll say it in the morning to everybody. He told me that before he said it. He said he'd promised me first and not only, and he wasn't going to turn a thing he owed me into a thing he'd kept from you."

Toren looked up the bank.

"Was it—" He did not know how to finish it. "Is it a good one?"

Wyck made a noise that was almost a laugh and was mostly not.

"It's a name," he said. "That's all it is. It's a completely ordinary name and you could put it on a sack of flour." He turned the bar over once on his knees. "He had to say it twice. First time nothing came out. He got the first bit of it out and then he had to stop and sit there, and I sat there, and we did that for about a minute, and then he did it properly."

"What did you say?"

"Nothing," said Wyck. "What would I say? He didn't want anything said, he wanted it *heard*. He'd been carrying it about for eleven years like a stone in a boot." He looked at his own strapped wrist for a moment. "I asked him why now. Because it's not over. He said when it was finished I'd have it, and it isn't finished, it's been put down, and that's not the same and I wasn't going to pretend it was.

"And he said: no. It isn't finished, it's abandoned, and a man who abandons a thing doesn't get to pay out of that account. He said he was paying out of a different one." Wyck's voice had gone a little rough and he carried on regardless, in the manner of somebody walking over a bad patch of road without slowing down. "He said, *I gave a boy four words in a field about a straw coat and he did them exactly the way I taught him and it cost him everything, and if I'm handing out what I've got left I'd rather do it while there's still somebody here to hand it to.*"

---

He said it in the morning at the stones, with all of them there.

They went along to the spur at first light because the mule was loaded and the wheel was greased and there was nothing else to wait for, and they stood in a row in front of a heap of parapet stone with the water going out flat and pale on the other side of it and the burnt reeds black along the south.

Nobody had planned anything to say. Dessa said his name out loud, once, deliberately, and could not do the next part. Senna, from the cart, said something in a voice too low for anybody to hear except the stones, which was the only formal thing anybody did. Wyck put his hand on the top block for a second on his way past and took it off again.

Then Rook stepped forward and stood in front of it with his coat open and his left arm useless at his side.

"Milo Faust," he said. "I've two things and they're both short.

"The first is that I owe you a question and you never got to ask it, and I've thought about it all night and I don't know what it was, and I am going to be guessing at that for the rest of my life, which is exactly what I deserve for saying *a month* to a boy who'd waited eight days.

"The second is a thing I've been carrying for eleven years and wouldn't put down, and you asked me for it once, in a road, at a halt, in the sun, and I bit your head off for it. So I'm going to say it here first, before I say it anywhere else, to the one who isn't going to hear it, because that's what I've made of it and I'd rather everybody watched me pay for that than have it said kindly afterward."

He was not looking at any of them.

"My name is Elias Marsh," he said.

It went out over the water and there was nothing to catch it.

"Elias Marsh, out of Lowry Bend, the second of three. I was fourteen when I stopped using it and twenty-five when I found out why I'd stopped, and I have been called Rook by every living person for eleven years, and I'd like to be. That's not a test and there's no trick in it. Go on calling me what you call me.

"But four people on this bank know what it is now, and one of them was told last night because I promised it to him at a fire and a man keeps that sort of thing, and I'd like the other three to have it from my own mouth and not secondhand, and I'd like it said in front of the stones because that's the only place any of it means anything."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Senna Voss said, from the cart, quite clearly, "Elias."

Rook stood very still.

"Yes," he said.

"Nothing," said Senna. "I wanted to hear it said by somebody who didn't have to."

---

They went north at about the third hour.

Dessa had the mule's head. Wyck walked at the off-side wheel with the bar hooked through his belt because he could not carry it in his hand any more. Rook was in the cart bed with Senna, on his good side, because he could not walk two miles and had said so out loud without being asked, which was new. Toren walked at the near-side wheel with the spike cold in his coat pocket and the disk cold against his breastbone, and there was nobody behind the cart, and there was nobody ahead of it, and for the first mile he kept turning round.

At the top of the rise where the embankment began to come down into the grazing again, the road bent, and the engine house went behind a stand of willow, and the stones on the spur went out of sight.

He did not look back at them, and then he did.

"Four days to Ferrow," said Dessa, from the mule's head. "Four days to the wheelwright, if there is one, and if he's alive."

"He's ninety," said Toren.

"He's ninety," Dessa agreed.

Behind them, in the cart bed, in a voice that had a long way to come, Rook said, "He was ninety, eleven years ago. He'll have a son."

Nobody laughed, and it had not quite been a joke, and it had been about a quarter of one, and all five of them heard it for what it was: a man saying something that was not necessary, into a silence, because somebody had to start doing that again and there was nobody left in the party whose job it had been.

They went on north with the water going away behind them — three on their feet and two in the cart — with a hundred and twenty miles of bad road in front of them and not one wall on any of it until the end, and the whole of it to be walked, and none of it to be walked by the boy who had carried the light.
