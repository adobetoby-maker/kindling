# Chapter 8 — Jab

He walked the bank for an hour before he let himself look at the tree, because he wanted to be able to say afterward that he had looked at everything else first.

The Elder's crossing was gone. He found it — a wide flat place where the creek spread out over a gravel fan, the bank scoured low and open on both sides, exactly where she had put her stick on the table. In an ordinary autumn it would have been a boy's crossing. Today it was four feet of brown water moving at the speed of a trotting horse, and while he stood on the lip of it he heard a stone go down the bed underneath, one long knock, like something hitting the bottom of a boat.

He put his hand out flat to test the cold and did not put it in.

Downstream was the other answer, and it was a real one. Two days down, the creek went into the river, and where a creek went into a river in this country there was always a bar and always a way across the bar. Two days down and then a day and a half back up the far side to get back on the road, and three days of food at the rate he was eating and four at the rate he had started eating, and six twists of bark, and whatever the weather was doing by then.

He stood with that for a while. He was honest enough to notice that he wanted it to be impossible, and made himself do the arithmetic twice because of it. It came out the same both times: two and a half days he did not have, spent to avoid a thing that would take him twenty minutes.

Upstream was where he found the spruce.

It had come off an undercut bank about three hundred paces above the fan, root and all, sometime in the last few days — the break in the bank was raw, and the grass on the root ball was still green. It had gone over and its top had gone into the alders on the far side and stuck there, and now the whole tree lay across the creek at a shallow angle, butt on this side, crown in the alder on that side.

Jab stood on the bank below the root ball and read it the way his father had taught him to read a jam, which was slowly, and from the bottom up.

Length: forty-five feet of it over water, near enough. Height: at the butt, six feet above the surface; sagging in the middle to about four; then rising again toward the alders. Diameter at the butt, wider than his shoulders. At the middle, about the width of his two hands. That was the part that mattered, because that was the part he would be standing on.

Bark: wet. Not merely damp — the spray came up off the water under the sag and kept the middle third of the trunk running with it, and spruce bark wet was one of the four or five slickest things in the world.

Stubs: the branches on the upstream side had been broken off short by the water and the sticks it was carrying, and stood out of the trunk in a ragged row, some of them a hand long, some of them two. They were an obstacle and they were also the only things up there to hold.

He climbed up onto the root ball, and the whole tree moved.

It was not much — an inch, maybe, a slow settling shift — but he felt it all the way through the soles of his boots and out along forty-five feet of trunk, and he got down off it at once and stood on the bank with his heart going.

So: not anchored. The root ball was sitting in the mud of its own hole and the crown was tangled in alder, and between those two facts the tree was staying where it was. It would hold a man's weight. It had just told him that it would rather not.

He went and got his brother.

---

Tonk had not said anything for the whole hour. That was not like him and Jab had been listening to the silence the entire time he walked the bank.

He set the frame up on a rock at the head of the gravel bench, under the lee of the cut, and got down in front of it so that he was looking up rather than down.

"Right," he said. "I'm going to tell you the whole of it, and then you can say what you think, and then I'm going to do it anyway. That's how this one goes."

Tonk's eyes moved to the creek and came back.

"There's a tree fallen across the water up there. It's a big one. It's as wide as this rock and it's not going anywhere, and I can walk across it, and so can anybody. Here's the part you won't like. I'm going to take the bag over first, on my own, without you. Then I'm coming back. Then I'm carrying you over. That's two times over and one time back, and you'll be sitting right here in the middle of it on your own for about — " he did the count honestly " — for about as long as it takes to sing the four verses twice."

"No," Tonk said.

"That's the part you don't like."

"No. *No.*" His voice went up. "Don't go over the water. Don't go *on* it."

"Tonk —"

"You went on it *before*," Tonk said, and started to cry.

Jab had known it was coming and it went into him anyway, low down, under the ribs, where he did not have a guard for it.

His brother had been three and a half and he had been on Jab's back and he had been asleep. That was the one mercy in the whole thing and Jab had held onto it for six weeks like a coal in his fist: Tonk had been asleep when the span went, and could not possibly remember the part where the deck lurched and folded and the whole crossing tipped sideways into the dark, and could not possibly remember his mother's hand, or the noise, or the cold, or his brother holding him above water with one arm around a rock for however long that had been.

He remembered something, though. He remembered enough to look at a fallen tree over a creek and start crying.

"Come here," Jab said, and put his forehead against his brother's forehead, which was all the holding you could do to a boy strapped into a frame. "Listen. I know. I know what it looks like. I'm going to tell you two things and they're both true."

Tonk sobbed once.

"One. That bridge went down because it was old and forty people were standing on it and nobody had looked at the ropes since the spring. This is a tree. Nobody built it. It's been growing there for a hundred years and it came down four days ago and it weighs more than the house."

"Two?"

"Two is that I was carrying you when the bridge went," Jab said, "and I am still carrying you. Which means I'm the one who knows how to do this."

Tonk considered that for a long, wet, shuddering moment, and Jab watched a four-year-old take a piece of reasoning and decide whether to let it hold his weight.

"I want Little," Tonk said.

"He's in your hand."

Tonk looked down at his hand. "Oh," he said.

---

He put the bench in order before he went, and did it properly, because he had seen what happened to men who did the last part of a job in a hurry.

He moved the frame back from the edge and wedged the foot of it against a root and put two flat stones under it so it stood without leaning. He tucked the hide around Tonk to the chin and put the water skin in his lap under his good hand. He pointed out the exact rock he would be standing on when he came back into sight on the far bank, and made Tonk find it twice.

"And here's your job," he said. "See the little alder with the bent top?"

"Yes."

"The water's coming up. If it gets to that alder before I'm back, you shout, and you shout the whole time, and don't stop, and I'll come."

It was not much of a job. The water would not reach the alder in twenty minutes, and if it did, shouting would not help either of them. But there was one thing worse than being left alone on a bank at four years old, and that was being left alone on a bank at four years old with nothing to do, and Jab had learned that in the first week.

"What if you don't come," Tonk said.

"I'll come."

"But what if — "

"Tonk." Jab put his hand on the side of his brother's face. His own hand was not steady and he did not bother hiding it, because the one thing he had refused to do for six weeks was lie to him about anything that mattered. "I'll come. That's not a promise about the water. That's a promise about me."

---

He crossed the first time with the pack and it took a long time and he had expected that.

He went up the root ball and out along the butt, where the trunk was wide enough to be a floor, and stopped at the place where wide began turning into narrow, and crouched, and put his hand flat on the bark.

Slick as a fish. Worse than slick — the bark had a give to it, a skin over wet wood that would peel under a boot at exactly the moment a boot needed it not to.

So he took it off.

He brought the blade out and set the metal's spine against the top of the trunk and started to work backward along it, scraping a strip the width of both his hands, taking the bark off down to the pale sapwood underneath and then roughing that with short chopping cuts, a hand's width apart, all the way across. He did it with the edge dark for the scraping and lit for the cutting, and every time he kindled it the light ran up from the grip and stuck at the belly and guttered and caught, and he was so used to that after four years that he only noticed it now because this was the first crossing of his life where a quarter-second mattered.

It took the better part of an hour. He worked on his knees, backing along the log a foot at a time, with forty feet of brown water going by underneath and the spray coming up cold through the gap in his collar, and twice he had to stop and put his forehead down on the wood and wait, because he had not slept and the tree had begun doing the thing that rivers do when you look at them too long, which is stand still and take the world past with it.

At the sag he tied on the cord.

He had sixty feet of hide cord in the world and it was all doing something — shelter lines, frame lashings, the spare coil. He took the spare coil, which was eighteen feet, and rigged it as a handline over the worst of it: one end round a branch stub at the near end of the sag, then out through two more stubs as a guide, then the far end hauled taut round the last stub before the rise. It was not a rope to fall onto. It was a thing to put four fingers over while he walked, at about the height of his thigh, and it would keep a lean from turning into a fall, and that was all he was asking of it.

Then he went back for the pack and brought it over, and came back across a second time, and stood on the near bank breathing.

Two crossings. Nothing had happened on either. His legs were shaking from the work and the cold and not from anything that had gone wrong, and he made himself notice that, and stand still with it, and take it as evidence. *It holds. You've done it twice.*

He could hear his brother singing, badly, up on the bench. He was on the second verse for at least the third time.

---

Loading Tonk took nine minutes and he made himself spend all nine.

Ankle, thigh, seat, chest band, and then he checked every one of them again from the bottom, and then he checked the shoulder lashing where the boot-lace splint was, and it looked the way it had looked that morning — ugly, holding, three turns of lace over a cord that had gone to fur.

"Four days," he said to it. "You said four days."

He tightened the hip belt to the fifth hole, which he had never used, so that the whole load sat as high and close to his spine as it would go. Then he put the hide over Tonk, and stopped, and took it off again. On any other ground he wanted his brother covered. On this ground he wanted to be able to see him.

"Ready," he said.

"Not ready," said Tonk.

"All right. Not ready. Here's what's going to happen anyway. I'm going to be slow, and it's going to be loud, and you're going to feel me leaning about. That's me doing it right, not me doing it wrong. And you keep your arms in. Do you know why?"

"Because of the sticks."

"Because of the sticks." He had told him twice. He would have told him six times. "Arms in, hands on the straps, eyes wherever you like. If you want to look at the water, look at it. It's only water and it's going one way and it's not going to do anything clever."

"Can I shut my eyes?"

"You can shut your eyes."

"I'm going to shut my eyes," Tonk said, and shut them, hard, with his whole face.

---

He went up the root ball and out along the butt, and for the first fifteen feet it was the easiest thing in the world.

The stripped strip was good. It took his boot the way bare rough sapwood does, honest and dry-feeling even wet, and he found he could walk it nearly upright with his knees soft, hands out, letting the frame's weight ride in the belt. Under him the water went by. He did not look at it and he did not avoid looking at it; his father's rule was that the water was information and you took it in the same as anything else.

At twenty feet the trunk narrowed and began to flex.

He felt it come into the tree from somewhere ahead of him — a slow springing, a foot of travel up and down over several seconds, with his own steps feeding it. He stopped and let it die away and then went on shorter, putting his feet down closer together, and the flex stayed small.

At the sag the spray started coming up over the wood, and he got four fingers over the handline and stopped talking. He had been talking the whole way, some nonsense about the alders on the far side, and he stopped because he needed everything.

He was eight feet into the sag when the shoulder lashing let go.

There was no warning in it at all. There was a small dull sound, a tick, no louder than a knuckle, and then the entire left side of the frame dropped two inches and everything above his hips swung.

The load went left and back. His right foot was the one in the air. He put it down where he had been going to put it, which was the right thing to do and half a beat too late to be the right thing at all, because he was already leaning and the weight had already taken the lean and made it a turn — and his leg, which had eleven days and four hours of sleep in it, came up under him a little bit soft, the way a leg does when you have asked it for too much for too long, and did not lock.

His knee went down onto the log.

That saved them. It was not skill and he knew it while it was happening: he was falling, and he fell onto the tree instead of past it, and his left hand came over the handline and clamped, and his right hand went flat on the wet bark and skidded and found a stub, and his chest came down across the trunk. Behind him and above him Tonk screamed, one raw note, and the frame's weight kept going and tried to roll him off the far side.

He stopped it with his back.

For three or four seconds Jab lay along the top of a spruce log, four feet over a creek in flood, with one knee and one shin either side of the trunk and both hands full and a hundred and some pounds on his back trying, patiently, to turn him over.

Then it stopped trying, because he had not let it, and the tree bounced twice under them and went still.

"Tonk."

Nothing.

"*Tonk.*"

"Here," said a tiny voice, right by his ear.

"Are you in the straps?"

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"*Yes.*"

"Good. Don't move. Don't help. I've got you and this is fine."

It was not fine. He said it because it was what needed saying and because saying it in that voice made it a bit more true, and then he lay still and found out what he actually had.

The frame was hanging off him on one side. The left shoulder band had parted at the lashing and the whole yoke had dropped, which meant the load was now riding on the hip belt and the chest band and his own back, and every single thing he did from now on would be heavier on the left than the right.

And the rail was caught.

He could feel exactly where. The curved ash rail that carried Tonk's bad leg had swung with the drop and gone down past the side of the trunk, and one of the broken branch stubs was through it — up between the rail and the lattice, a hand's length of hard dead spruce, holding the whole right side of the frame against the tree like a hook in a mouth.

He could not stand up with it there. If he tried, the frame would turn him over.

He could not reach it with his hand; it was under him and behind him on the far side of his own hip.

And he could not get out of the frame, because the belt buckle was behind his back and took two hands and both of his were holding him onto a log.

"All right," Jab said out loud. "All right."

He got his breathing down. That was the first job; it was always the first job. Then he shifted his right knee until he had a real grip on the trunk with the inside of his thigh, and took his right hand off the stub, and held on with his left hand and his legs and his chest, and reached down and back along his own side until his fingers found the place where the rail met the spruce.

There it was. Hard and rough and wedged.

He put his hand flat in the air below it, and reached for the metal.

It did not come.

That had happened to him perhaps six times in four years, and never once when it mattered. The warmth was there, low, stubborn, but it would not open — there was nothing left behind it, or the part of him that did the opening was too busy holding onto a tree, or both. He felt the metal stir at his throat and settle again.

Under him the water went on grinding stones along the bed.

"Jab," said Tonk. "The stick's in my leg place."

"I know. I'm getting it."

"It *hurts.*"

"I know."

He shut his eyes, which was not a thing a person did four feet above a flood, and let go of everything else for one whole breath — the water, the weight, the cold, his brother's voice — and found the small warmth under his breastbone and opened it the way his father's aunt had taught him to breathe out, all the way, and then not.

The metal came off his chest and ran down his arm and made the blade.

He kindled it. The light ran up from the grip, hit the belly, guttered — one breath, two — and caught.

He could not see what he was cutting. He put his wrist against the rail to find the angle, laid the blade along the stub as flat to the trunk as he could get it, and cut with the belly, which was the part of the shape his father had explained to him on a gravel bar four years ago and which he had used a thousand times since on green wood he could not get behind.

Two strokes. The stub gave, and the frame's right side dropped free, and the whole load swung again — and he had known it would, and had his weight set for it, and it swung and stopped.

He put the light out and let the metal go home before the shaking got into his hand.

Then he crossed the last twelve feet on his knees and forearms, astride the log like a boy riding a fence, hauling himself along stub by stub with a hundred pounds of brother and supplies riding crooked on his back and the spray coming up over both of them, and he did not stand up again until the alders closed over his head and there was mud under his hands instead of bark.

---

He got four paces up the far bank and went down on his knees, deliberately, before his legs did it for him.

Getting Tonk out took a long time because the frame would not sit straight any more and everything had to be done left-handed and wrong. When the leg came off the rail his brother screamed in a way Jab had not heard since the first three nights, and then kept crying afterward in the exhausted, hopeless way that was far worse, and Jab knelt in the wet leaves holding him and saying the same six words over and over until the crying went down to hiccups.

"Bark," Tonk said.

"I know. You'll have it. Let me look first."

The leg had been jarred hard and twice. The wrapping had slipped and there was fresh clear wet on it, and the whole lower leg had gone tighter and hotter than it had been that morning, and Jab put his hands either side of it without touching and held them there and, for one bad moment, asked the thing in his chest the question he had promised himself he would only ask when he did not care about the answer.

It did not answer. He had nothing left to ask it with.

He got the wrapping right. He got Tonk propped against the pack under the biggest alder with the hide over him. Then he opened his coat for the birch paper and found the front of it soaked through where he had lain in the spray on the log, and the paper had gone soft, and when he unfolded it the outer two twists came apart on his fingers into a gray wet paste that smelled like a wet stump and was not medicine any more.

He looked at that for what felt like a while.

Then he picked the two ruined ones out and flicked them into the leaves, because there was nothing else to do with them, and counted what was left, twice, with his back turned so that his brother could not see his face while he did it.

Eleven.

"Right," he said, and turned round with a twist in his hand and his ordinary face on. "Here's yours. And then I'm going to build you the best fire you've ever seen, and I'm going to fix this frame, and you're going to tell me what we're calling that tree."

Tonk drank it and shuddered and said it tasted like a cold spoon.

Then he lay back against the pack, and looked across the creek at the spruce lying over the brown water with the bark stripped off its back in a long pale stripe, and thought about it for a while with his forehead furrowed.

"That one hasn't got a name," he said.
