# Chapter 39 — Toren

The thing began to come apart on the twenty-sixth day, and it began with Rook getting something wrong.

Not a large thing. He put them across a lane that had a five-bar gate at the end of it and did not say anything about the gate. They drilled there for twenty minutes. Then he stopped in the middle of a sentence, looked at it, and said, "Off. Now. All of you, off the lane and behind the hedge."

"What's wrong with—"

"Nothing's wrong with it," said Rook. "There's a gate at the end of it, and I've had you standing on a line for twenty minutes, and I put you there."

He did not explain further. He did not have to. Toren had it about four seconds later and wished he hadn't. A gate is a gap, and a gap is a line, and you did not stand in a lane with one open end when your whole afternoon had been about a thing that came in straight lines at no notice at all. Rook had spent three days making sure that none of them would ever do that again, and had then done it himself in front of all four of them.

"It's been three weeks since we've seen a Stilt," Milo offered.

"That's exactly the reasoning," said Rook. "Get the mule."

---

He had not slept.

Toren had known that in a general way for some days and now began keeping it the way he had once kept a tally, which was privately, on the back of his father's paper, in a column of his own.

*Twenty-third night — up when I went down, up when I woke.*

*Twenty-fourth — same. Sat facing east.*

*Twenty-fifth — walked out past the mule twice. Once for about an hour.*

He had always done some of this. That was the trouble with the column; there was no clean line in it. The man had taken three watches a night since the Drum, and he had done it without any visible cost, and Toren had folded it into the general category of things Rook could do that other people could not, along with crossing forty feet of ground without appearing to travel through any of it.

What was different was small and was everywhere. He asked Milo to say a call again, twice in a morning, when Milo had not got a call wrong in twenty days. He put his hand on the cart shaft going uphill, which he never did. Once, on the road, walking, he stopped for no reason whatsoever and stood for about four seconds and then went on, and when Dessa said, "What was it?" he said, "Nothing," and it was not the flat *nothing* he used to close a conversation. It was a man answering a question honestly and being unhappy with the answer.

---

The thing with Milo happened at the midday halt on the twenty-sixth.

It was nobody's fault, which was the worst of it. Milo had been carrying a question round for a day and a half and had been extremely pleased with himself for not asking it, and at the halt, in a good mood, with the sun out, he let it go the way he let everything go, sideways and grinning:

"Did you have brothers?"

Rook was cutting bread. He did not stop cutting bread.

"Why."

"No reason. It's just you do a thing." Milo was already committed and did it the way he did everything, at a run. "When Wyck's being unbearable you do a thing with your face, it's this — " he did an approximation " — and my cousin's got four brothers and his father does exactly that, and I thought, that's a man who's had to—"

"Don't do that," said Rook.

It came out of him like a door shutting.

"I wasn't—"

"You were reading me. You've been doing it for a week, all four of you have, and I've let it run because it's a good skill, and I taught you most of it, and I am telling you now to take it off me and put it back on the country where it belongs." He had not raised his voice at all. It was worse than if he had. "You've got no idea what you're looking at. You'll build a whole man out of four days of watching a face, and you'll be wrong in every particular, and then you'll act on it, and you'll do it at exactly the wrong moment because you'll have had a fortnight of being right about husks."

Milo's face did what Milo's face did, which was everything at once and immediately.

"All right," he said.

"It isn't a—"

"No, I said all right."

He went and sat on the far side of the cart with the bread he had not eaten and sat there very straight, and Dessa looked at Rook once, briefly, with an expression of such complete and undisguised judgment that Toren felt it go past him like weather.

Rook put the knife down.

He stood there for about five seconds with his hands on the tailgate.

Then he walked round the cart and crouched down in front of Milo Faust and said, "That was mine and not yours. There's nothing wrong with the question. Ask it again in a month and I'll answer it."

And he got up and walked out to the hedge and stood in the gap looking east.

Nobody said anything for a long time. It was the first time in twenty-six days that any of them had heard the man apologise for anything, and it did not feel like a thing being mended. It felt like hearing a beam creak in a roof.

---

Senna had a bad night.

It came on at about the second hour, the way the bad ones did, and it was not dramatic; it was the absence of drama that frightened Toren every time. She simply stopped being able to get enough of a breath to finish a sentence, and then to start one. She lay in the cart with her hand on Dessa's wrist and worked at it, patiently, the way she worked at everything. Toren sat on the tailgate with his back to the boards because she had told him years ago that she could not be doing with being looked at.

Rook was there before anyone called him. He did not ask what was happening. He got up into the cart bed, which he almost never did, and got his knee under her shoulders and got her sitting up against him, and put two fingers on the inside of her wrist and held them there a long time.

"Right," he said.

He took the Ember out of his coat and put it against her collarbone, flat, and put his other hand over the top of it and shut his eyes, and Toren, six feet away in the dark with his back to the boards, felt the wrongness come up in his back teeth the way it had at the road cut: something near, going the other way, like a gate being opened upstream.

It went on for a while. It went on longer than Toren had ever known it go on.

Afterward Rook sat back on the boards with his forearm across his knee and got the flat tin flask out and unscrewed it and drank, and did not stop at two swallows, and Toren heard him count under his breath — not swallows. Something else. He counted to four and stopped.

"She'll sleep," he said. "Get her flat when she's gone and put the second blanket under her, not over."

"Is she—" Milo, from somewhere in the dark.

"She's the same as she was yesterday," said Rook. "That's the honest answer, and I want you to hear the whole of it. She's the same as she was yesterday, and yesterday she was worse than she was at Hobb's Wall, and the reason she's the same as yesterday is that I've just spent something on it. That's what that was. It isn't medicine. I'm not a healer, and I've never claimed to be one; I can hold a gate shut for a night, and I can't fix a single thing that's actually wrong with her."

He screwed the cap down.

"There's a wall at the other end of this road with people who can," he said. "That's all there is. That's the whole of the plan, and there was never a better one."

---

Toren did his arithmetic the next morning, on the milestone that was not a milestone, and did it three times again, and hated it three times.

A hundred and ten miles, Nell Quill had said, when the road had still been the Meridian road. It was a hundred and eighty and counting on the road they were actually on, and they had done twenty-six days, and they had been making ten and eleven miles a day on good ground, and there was nothing in the sums that said they would not get there.

There was nothing in the sums about how many bad nights an old woman had in her.

He folded the paper up.

---

Rook went over on the twenty-seventh, at the midday halt, sitting against the cart wheel with his back to the spokes.

Toren looked up from the strap, and the man was asleep.

It was the most shocking thing he had seen in a month, and it took him a moment to work out why. It was not that Rook slept. He must sleep; everybody slept. They had all seen him lie down at some point in the small hours and had all assumed he did something in those hours that resembled sleeping. It was the *posture*. He had gone over sitting up with his chin down on his chest and one boot out at an angle that no one would ever choose. His hands had come open in his lap, and Rook's hands were never open.

Dessa came round the cart with the nosebag and stopped dead.

The three of them stood in a row and looked at him like people looking into a well.

"Do we—" Milo whispered.

"No," said Dessa.

"He said an hour—"

"*No*," said Dessa, and that was the end of the discussion, and they gave him three.

They did it beautifully, too, which Toren was proud of afterward. Dessa took Pitch eighty yards up the verge to eat. Milo took Senna's mug and sat on the tailgate and had a conversation with her at a volume Toren would not have believed him capable of. Wyck moved the entire contents of the cart bed from one side to the other in order to not need anything from the wheel side for three hours. Nobody discussed it.

---

He talked at about the second hour.

Toren was closest. He was sitting on the road bed twelve feet off with his back against the bank, working oil into the strap he had now repaired four separate times, and he heard it come out of the man like a cough.

"—up. Get *up*."

Toren went absolutely still.

It was not loud. It had the peculiar flatness that sleep-talk has, no shape to it, as though the words had been taken out of the mouth that would ordinarily say them. Rook's head had not moved. His hands had not closed.

"It's the gate," he said, quite clearly, to nobody, at the bottom of a road in the afternoon sun. "It's the gate, it's the *gate*—"

And then, on a completely different breath, in a completely different voice, quiet, and reasonable, and close, the way a man says a thing to somebody standing next to him:

"Vera."

Toren did not move for a count of ten.

When he turned his head, Milo Faust was standing at the corner of the cart with the empty mug in his hand and his whole face open, and he had been there for some time.

Neither of them said anything.

---

Rook woke at the third hour.

He came up out of it in one movement, all at once, the way he did everything: hands shut, boot in, on his feet with his back off the wheel before his eyes were properly working, and he stood there in the road for a second and a half with the look of a man reading a room, and Toren watched him find, one after another, the mule eighty yards up the verge, the redistributed cart, the boy sitting on the tailgate talking loudly about a wedding, and the angle of the sun.

He looked at the sun for a long moment.

"Three hours," he said.

"About that," said Dessa, from the mule's head.

"Whose idea."

"Everybody's."

He did not thank anybody. He got the cart moving with his shoulder against the shaft, and they made four miles before dark, which was nothing, and he did not run a drill that evening, which had happened twice in twenty-seven days, and both of the other times somebody had been bleeding.

---

They did it at the fire, after he had gone up the bank.

It was Dessa who started it, and she started it in the least dramatic way available, by saying, "Right," and putting a stick across her knees.

"I'm going to say a thing, and I want everybody to have a go at it," she said. "Wyck, you especially, because you've got a rule about this, and I'm not going round the back of it."

"Go on," said Wyck.

"He said a name."

Toren looked at Milo. Milo was already looking at Toren.

"Two of you heard it," said Dessa. "Tor's known since midday because I can read him like a signboard, and Milo's been about to burst since the halt, and he hasn't burst, which I want noted, because that's the hardest thing Milo Faust has ever done." Milo made a small grateful noise. "I don't want to hear the name. Don't say it. Not because I'm being noble, but because I think if that name gets said at this fire by one of us instead of by him, it'll go wrong in a way we can't take back."

"Then what are we—"

"I want to say what I think is happening," said Dessa, "and then I want to be told I'm wrong."

She turned the stick over.

"He's coming apart," she said. "Slowly, and quietly, and in a way you'd only see if you'd been four feet from him every day for a month, which we all have been. He got the lane wrong. He bit Milo's head off for nothing and then said sorry, and he doesn't say sorry, he says *that was mine and not yours* about nine days after the fact, if ever. He's been facing east for three nights. He hasn't slept properly in a week, and today he went over sitting up in the middle of the day like a man who's fallen off a wall.

"And I don't think it started at the camp in the field. I think it started at the Drum ten days ago when Ott said the name, and I think everything since then has been him holding a lid down with both hands, and I think the lid's going."

Nobody argued.

"So," said Dessa. "Two roads.

"One: we leave it alone. That's what Senna said at the Drum, and Senna's usually right. We say nothing and we do the drill and we wait for him to be ready, and that's a real choice and it might be the kind one.

"Two: we push."

"He said he'd tell us," said Milo. "He said it in the lane."

"He said he wasn't ready. He didn't say when. That was three days ago, and he's further off it now than he was then."

Wyck said, "That's not true."

Everybody looked at him.

"He's not further off it," said Wyck. "He's closer, and that's what's wrong with him. A man who's got a thing locked up and means to keep it locked up doesn't lose sleep over it; he's had it locked up for years, and it's a room he doesn't go in. This is what it looks like when a man's started going in the room." He flexed the fingers of his left hand. "My mother's the same about my father. Eleven years, and she's fine, and then somebody asks her one civil question at a wake, and she's finished for a month, and it isn't because she's forgotten him, it's because she'd just about got to the point of being able to say his name in the kitchen."

He looked up.

"He's been getting ready since the Drum," said Wyck. "Ten days. That's what all this is. And I'll tell you what I think happens if we leave him alone with it: I think he gets ready, and gets ready, and one night he decides he can't, and then he does the thing he offered to do in the lane."

"He wouldn't," said Milo.

"He offered it out loud, Milo. He offered it three days ago as a solution to exactly this problem, and none of you seem to have noticed that he meant it as a *kindness*." Wyck put another stick on. "He'll walk us to the gate, and he'll go. And it'll be the worst thing that ever happens to the five of us, and he'll think he's done us a favour, and the maddening thing is he'll have taught us everything he knows on the way, in full, without missing a day, because that's the sort of man he is."

---

The fire went down to the red of it.

"Right," said Dessa. "Then we push. Does anybody want to say no? Say it now. Tor."

"No," said Toren. "I mean — no, I don't want to say no. We push."

"Milo."

"Push," said Milo, in a very small voice, "but not — not at him. Not the way he did the coat. I don't want to do a thing *to* him."

"Noted, and I agree, and I'd like everybody to hold on to it," said Dessa. "Wyck. It's your rule. You said you'd had your go."

Wyck was quiet for a moment.

"I said I wasn't going to ask him again," he said. "And I'm not. I've had my go, and I meant it, and I'm not going back on it in a fortnight like a child.

"But I never said anything about the rest of you, and I'm not going to stand there and pretend I'm not in it. So here's what I'll do: I'll be there. I'll stand in it. And I won't open my mouth unless he asks me something directly, and if he tries to walk out of it, I'll be standing where he'd walk."

"That's not asking," said Dessa.

"No," said Wyck. "It's worse. But it's not asking."

She nodded slowly.

"Then it's tonight," she said. "Not tomorrow. Tomorrow he'll have had a night on the bank to get the lid back on, and we've all seen how good he is at that.

"He'll come down off the bank when the fire needs feeding — he always does, he does it at about the third hour, and he thinks nobody's noticed he does it. That's when. And he'll be standing up, and we'll be sitting down, and the fire'll be between him and the road, and that's not an accident, and I want everybody to be honest with themselves about the fact that we are, right now, setting a line."

Toren looked at the four of them around the low red fire and thought, with a lurch, that she was entirely right, and that they had learned it from the man they were about to use it on, and that he was going to know that the instant he saw it.

"And who says it?" he said. "When he comes down. Who actually—"

"Not you," said Dessa. "You've got the disk, and he'll think it's about the disk, and then it's a different conversation. Not me, because I'm the one he watches, and not Wyck, because Wyck's given his word, and I'd rather he kept it."

The three of them turned their heads at the same time.

Milo Faust sat on the other side of the fire with his arms round his knees and looked back at them with an expression of absolute horror.

"Oh, *no*," he said.
