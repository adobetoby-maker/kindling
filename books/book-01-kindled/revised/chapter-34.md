# Chapter 34 — Callie

Callie woke at first light and breathed in all the way before she remembered not to.

Something in her left side said *no*. It said it the way a door says it when you walk into one in the dark. She lay on her back under the wagon with her eyes watering and let the breath out a little at a time. She took the next one halfway, and the one after that halfway, and after a while the world came back.

Then she did the count, because she always did the count.

"Two ribs," she said to the underside of the wagon bed, not loud. "Three stitches. A finger of damp."

She had gone down to look before she opened her eyes. The mark at the bottom of the well was still there. It was no bigger than it had been at midnight. It was not smaller either.

"Four Handfuls in the jar. Scrapings in Pell's tin. One horse. Six arrows. Nine days."

Then there was the one she did not say out loud.

Her mother was asleep on the boards above her. Callie could hear her breathing through the planks. She listened to it the way she had listened to it every morning since Gaunt's Reach, in and out, in and out, and she let herself stop listening only when she had counted twenty.

---

Getting up took four tries and a great deal of language she kept behind her teeth.

She found that she could not roll onto her left side at all, and that rolling onto her right side was possible if she did it slowly and did not use her arms. She found that sitting up was a thing her stomach did and not her chest, and that her stomach had opinions about it. She found that the three stitches in her scalp pulled when she frowned, so she stopped frowning, which was harder than it sounded.

When she came out from under the wagon on her hands and knees, Pell was already at Duchess's hind leg.

He was crouched beside the mare in the grey light with one hand flat on the big muscle high up on the near side, where the leg met the body. He was not doing anything. He was only holding his hand there, the way her mother held a hand on a forehead. Duchess stood with the hoof tipped up on its toe and her head low. She did not look round at Callie. She had not looked round at her since the pitch.

"Hot," said Pell, without turning. "Hotter than last night. Not as hot as it'll be tonight."

"Will she pull?"

"On the flat." He took his hand away and wiped it on his coat. "At her pace, which is not my pace and it's not yours. An hour, and then she stands, and nobody argues with her about how long. No hills." He straightened, slowly, the way a man of sixty-one straightens. "If there's a hill between here and that light, I'll carry her up it myself, and I'd like it noted I said so before I'd seen it."

"It's noted."

"Good." He looked at her properly. "You came out from under there like a crab."

"I know."

"Don't do it again in front of your mother. She'll have something to say."

---

Otto's sling had not survived the pitch.

He was sitting by the ashes of the fire with the rags of it in his lap, trying to tie two ends together with one hand and his teeth. His stitched forearm lay across his knee. The stitches had held. The skin round them was red and angry, and he kept not looking at it, which was how Callie knew he had been looking at it all night.

Callie went to the barrow on the wagon bed and got her other shirt out of it. It was the grey one, the oldest of the three. It had the two dark lines worn across the chest where the strapping had gone for four hundred miles.

She gave it to him.

Otto looked at it. "That's your shirt."

"It's a sling now."

"It's got the — " He touched the dark lines with one finger, and went red, and did not finish.

"I'm not pulling anything for a while," said Callie. "It might as well carry something."

He tied it the way she showed him, across his shoulder and under the elbow, with the knot at the back of his neck where it would not rub. It took her a long time, because lifting her arms above her waist made her side say *no* again. Otto held still for the whole of it and looked at the horse.

"She hasn't looked at you," he said, when it was done. "Since. Duchess."

"I know."

"She looked at me all night. Every time I got up." He moved the arm in the new sling and seemed pleased with it. "I think that's good. I think she's decided I'm the one who's hers now." He thought about it. "I think she might have decided you're the one who isn't anything."

Callie had thought the same thing. She had not been sure whether to mind.

---

Her mother woke in the middle of the morning, which she never did.

Callie was sitting on the tailboard when it happened. Her mother opened her eyes and looked up at the canvas for a long time without moving, as if she were reading it.

"Well," she said. Her voice was thick, like the voice of somebody who had been asleep for a day and a night. "Somebody's come for the day back."

"How do you feel?"

"Like I've been — " She stopped.

Callie watched her look for the word. It was like watching somebody feel along a shelf in the dark for a thing they had put there themselves. Her mother's left hand moved on the blanket, very slightly, the fingers opening and closing.

"Like I've been run over," her mother said at last. "By the — the thing. With wheels."

"The wagon."

"The barrow." Her mother shut her eyes. "No. You're right. The wagon." She lay still. "It was a barrow when I got hold of it and it turned into a wagon on the way out. That's the trouble with this morning. Everything's turning into something else on the way out."

Callie said nothing.

"Don't do that."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing sums on my face." Her mother did not open her eyes. "Don't do sums on my face, love. It's rude. Do them on Pell's, he likes it."

She was sick twice before noon, over the side of the wagon, with Katori holding her shoulders. Her head hurt behind the left eye, she said, the way it had hurt in the spring before Gaunt's Reach. The brown bottle with the cork had been empty since the flats. Callie had carried it anyway, all the way up the scarp, because putting it down had felt like saying something.

Her mother's right hand lay against her side on the blanket. It lay there the way a glove lies on a table.

"The hill," her mother said, some time after the second time. "Yesterday. I walked up it."

"You did."

"I saw you at the top of it."

"I know. I saw you see me."

"Good." Her mother's face did not change, but something went out of it that had been holding on. "Then it was worth what it cost. Remember I said so. I'll want you to have it written down, for when I'm cross about today."

---

"You're riding," said Katori.

"I can walk."

"You can walk," Katori agreed. "I watched you come out from under that wagon. You can walk about as well as Duchess can, and you'll walk yourself into a fever by the third day, and then I'll have two of you on the boards instead of one." She did not raise her voice. She never did. "So you're riding. Not on the bed. On the board, with Pell."

"On the board?"

"You can see further from up there than you can from the road. You'll be five feet higher than you've ever been at the head of this wagon." Katori tipped her head north. "The job's the same. You tell us what's wrong before we get to it. You just tell us sitting down."

From the bed, without opening her eyes, Callie's mother said, "Undignified, isn't it."

"Yes."

"I want that understood."

"It's understood."

"Good," said her mother. "Now you know. Now we both know."

---

The high plain was the biggest thing Callie had ever seen.

She had walked across the Fenner flats, which were two miles of grey pan with a causeway laid across them like a seam. She had walked the long road north from Thistle Ford for a whole spring, through ditch country and hedge country and country that was all old fence line and sawn-off posts. None of it had been like this.

Up here the ground went away flat in every direction, further than a person could walk in a week. There was grass on it, short and pale and dry, bleached the colour of straw by a summer she had not been here for. There were no trees. There was nothing taller than a boot as far as she could see, except in one or two places, very far off, a thin grey line standing up against the sky that might have been an old tower or might have been nothing at all.

And the sky came all the way down. That was what she could not get used to. On the flats there had been the scarp at the north edge, and on the road there had been banks and ditches and hedges to put a limit on things. Up here there was no limit. The sky came down to the grass on every side like a bowl turned over a plate, and the wagon crawled across the middle of the plate. It was the only moving thing in all that country. It made her feel as if something very large was looking down at it.

The road ran straight north. It was an old road, older than the Fall, made of long grey slabs of concrete laid end to end with seams of black tar between them. Frost had heaved some of the slabs and cracked others across. Grass grew in all the seams. The tar had gone soft and then hard again so many times that it had the look of old candle wax.

Duchess walked it very slowly. Her near hind came down short every time, with a little dip in her whole body, the way Callie's own step dipped now on her left side.

Pell sat beside Callie on the board with the reins slack in his hands and did not hurry her.

"Well," he said, after the first mile. "Tell me what's wrong with it."

---

It took Callie until the middle of the afternoon to work out what was wrong with it.

She could see further from the board, as Katori had said. She could see the pale country going away for miles and the long straight line of the road across it. She could see the old fence lines, running off at right angles, most of them down, a few of them still standing in sagging strands of rust. She could see every post in them sawn off at the ground, the same as on the scarp. Somebody had taken all the steel out of this country a long time ago, one post at a time, and carried it away.

What she could not see was a line.

She looked for one all morning. She looked for the leaned grass and the pushed fence and the trough in the silt, the whole grammar of it, and she found sign. She found plenty of sign. A husk had crossed the road in front of them some days ago, going east. Another had crossed going west, a week before that. A third had come down the road itself for half a mile and then wandered off it at a slant, into the grass, going nowhere in particular.

That was what was wrong. They were going nowhere in particular.

"There's no grade," she said at last.

Pell turned his head.

"Up here. There's no downhill." Callie put her hand out over the side of the board, flat, the way you'd show the level of a table. "Husks go the way water goes. That's all they know how to do. On the flats they went down to the pan. On the scarp they came down the cut. But up here there's no down. It's all the same height. So they don't make a line. They just — drift. Any way. All ways."

"So we can't read them."

"We can read where they've been. We can't read where they're going, because they don't know either." She watched a patch of grass go by that had been leaned flat by something heavy, and then, three paces on, leaned the other way. "It's like trying to track a leaf on a pond."

Pell was quiet for a while. The wheels bumped over the tar seams, one after another, like somebody slowly counting.

"There's always a down," he said at last. "Even on a plate. There's a low place somewhere. Water finds it. I've never yet seen a country so flat that water couldn't find the low place in it."

Callie looked at him.

Then she looked out at the plain again, and this time she did not look for a line. She looked for the places where the grass was a different colour.

---

She found the first one an hour later.

It was off the road to the east, about four hundred paces out. It was a shallow round dip in the plain, so shallow she would never have seen it from the road on foot. From the board she could see that the grass in it was greener than the grass round it, and shorter, and that in the middle of it the ground went bare and grey and cracked, the way the bottom of a pond cracks when the pond has gone.

And the grass round the edge of it was leaned. All the way round. Leaned inward, toward the middle, from every direction.

"Stop," said Callie.

Pell stopped.

Katori came up from the back of the wagon without being called. Ines was already standing on the crates with her bow in her hand.

"There," said Callie, and pointed. "That dip. It's a pan. A little one. It holds water when it rains." She swallowed, and her side told her not to do that either. "They go in. Look at the grass. Everything that drifts across this country, sooner or later it drifts into a low place, and then it stops, because there's nowhere lower to go. And it lies up."

"Is there one in it now?" said Katori.

Callie looked for a long time. The grey cracked ground at the bottom of the pan was empty. The grass round the edge was leaned, but it was not fresh. It had begun to stand up again.

"No," she said. "Not now. Not for a while." She looked north, up the long straight road, where the plain went on and on to the edge of the sky. "But they pool. That's what they do up here, instead of lines. They pool. Like — "

"Like ash," said Pell.

"Like ash," Callie agreed.

Katori stood beside the wheel and looked at the pan for a while without saying anything. Then she said, "Mm," and went back down the wagon, and told Otto something Callie could not hear, and Otto moved round to the east side of the wagon with the pole.

Pell clicked his tongue, and Duchess leaned into the collar, and they went on.

---

At the noon halt Callie looked at the jar.

She did not mean to. She had gone to the barrow on the wagon bed for the bread, and the jar was there in its place under the flour, a screw-top jar with four Handfuls of ash in it. Next to the jar was Pell's tin with her name on it in his small neat hand. She did not need to open the tin. She knew what was in it. There was a grey film across the bottom and a little heap in one corner, less than a finger's worth. It was all that was left of the biggest thing she had ever owned.

She stood there with the bread in her hand.

"No," said Katori.

Callie turned round. Katori was sitting on the ground in the shade of the wagon with her cap pulled down, not looking at her.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at it the way Otto looks at a building." Katori took a bite of her own bread. "Let it fill. That's all you have to do this week. Let it fill on its own, the slow way."

"I'm no use dry."

"You're no use lit, either, with two ribs gone. You'd fall down the first time you leaned." Katori chewed. "And there's nothing on this road today that needs you lit. There's a lot on it that needs you looking. You can do that dry. You did it dry for two years before any of us had heard of you."

"And if something comes?"

"Then it'll need you thinking, and that doesn't cost you anything." Katori finally looked at her. "I said I'd teach you the Edge properly. In daylight. On purpose. I haven't forgotten. I'll do it when you've got something in you to learn with, and not one day before, and you can be as cross with me about it as you like."

Callie was cross with her about it for most of the afternoon. It did not seem to trouble Katori at all.

---

They made eight miles the first day, and stopped with the light still in the sky, because Duchess stopped.

She did not stop the way she stopped on the scarp, with her forefeet planted, refusing. She simply came to a halt in the middle of the road and stood, with the near hind tipped up on its toe, and let her head go down, and did not take another step. Pell looked at her for a moment and then put the brake on and got down and did not say anything to anybody.

"That's where we're sleeping, then," said Ines.

"That's where we're sleeping," said Pell.

They made camp on the road itself, because there was nowhere better. There was no ditch and no bank and no building. There was the plain, going away in every direction, and the long grey road across it, and the wagon.

Callie's mother had slept most of the afternoon. She woke at dusk and sat up against the crates, and said she was hungry, which was the best thing she had said all day, and ate half a bowl of the flour-and-water mess Otto cooked, slowly, left-handed. She did not spill much.

Afterward she put her left hand up to her hair.

It had come half undone in the night. Callie had braided it two mornings ago, at the bottom of the scarp, before the hill. Now it hung in a loose rope with the ends coming out of it, and her mother touched it and made a small noise.

"I'll do it," said Callie.

She got up onto the tailboard. It took her a while. She knelt behind her mother on the bedding and took the hair in her hands and began to comb it out with her fingers, and got about as far as lifting both arms to the height of her mother's head before her left side said *no*. It said it so hard that she stopped with her hands in the air and could not breathe at all for a moment.

She waited. She tried again. She got one pass in, and then had to put her hands down.

Her mother had gone very still.

"Callie," she said quietly. "Don't."

"I can do it."

"I know you can. I'm asking you not to." Her mother did not turn round. "Ines."

Ines looked up from the far side of the fire, where she was running her thumb down an arrow shaft.

"Would you do my hair," said Callie's mother. "My daughter's got a rib out of place and she's too proud to say so, and I've got a hand that's stopped taking instruction. And you've got two good ones, and you stitched her head yesterday without making a mess of it."

Ines looked at her for a moment. Then she put the arrow away in the quiver, one at a time, the way she always did, and got up and came round to the tailboard.

Callie got down. She did not decide to. Her body got down and gave Ines the place.

Ines knelt behind her mother and braided her hair. She did it badly and carefully, the way she had stitched Otto's arm at the pump house. Her fingers were not quick at it. They were very exact. She pulled each strand tight and checked it before she crossed it, and she did not say one word the whole time, and neither did Callie's mother.

Callie sat on the ground by the wheel and watched somebody else do the thing she had said she would do in the mornings.

"There," said Ines, when it was done.

"Thank you." Her mother put her left hand up and felt it. "It's very even."

"I braid the way I shoot."

"How's that?"

"Without any part left over," said Ines, and went back to her arrows.

Her mother laughed. It was a real laugh, small and tired. Then she looked down over the side of the tailboard at Callie, sitting against the wheel. She did not say anything. She put her left hand down over the edge of the boards, and Callie reached up and took it, and they held on for about three seconds, which was as long as either of them could stand.

---

When it was full dark, the light was there again.

It sat on the edge of the world to the north, low and pale and steady, lighting the bottom of the clouds. It did not look one mile nearer than it had from the top of the scarp. Callie had known it wouldn't. She looked at it anyway, for a long time, from where she lay on her right side under the wagon.

Pell was sitting on the tailboard above her with the atlas. She could hear the pencil going.

"Water," he said, to nobody in particular.

Callie knew what he meant. They had filled both casks at the seep at the foot of the scarp, before the climb. That had been two days ago. Duchess had drunk a bucket last night and a bucket this morning, and she would drink another before they slept, and a horse did not care whose water it was.

"How much?"

"A cask and a bit. Call it two days if she drinks what a horse wants, and three if she drinks what I let her." The pencil scratched. "Four if we drink nothing ourselves, which I don't propose."

"Is there anything on the atlas?"

The pencil stopped. After a moment Pell leaned down over the edge of the tailboard and held the book out where she could see it by the last of the firelight.

It was the page with the plain on it. The printed road ran straight up the middle of it, a thin red line. Nothing else was printed there at all, only a great deal of pale empty paper. But somebody had written on it in pencil, very small, in a hand that was not Pell's. There was a tiny drawing of a windmill, a stick with a cross on top of it, about a day and a half north of where Pell's thumb was. Beside it was a single word.

*Tank.*

"I bought the page off a man at the Warren," said Pell. "He'd come down this road nine years ago. He said there was a windmill and a stock tank, and the tank held rain, and the water in it was good." He took the book back. "Nine years. The windmill will have fallen down. The tank will have rusted through. Or the man will have been lying."

"Was he the kind of man who lies?"

"He was the kind of man who sold me a page of a book for a Handful." Pell closed the atlas. "That's all I know about him."

---

The second day was hotter than the first, and drier. The wind came across the plain from the west all morning, steady and warm, and dried the sweat on Duchess before it could run.

Callie sat on the board beside Pell and watched the grass. She found three more pans before noon. Two of them were empty and old. The third had fresh sign in it, a trough through the cracked grey mud at the bottom, and the grass round the edge was still leaned flat and had not begun to stand. There was nothing in it now. But something had lain there the night before and got up in the morning and drifted on.

They went round it wide.

"Have you started it?" said Pell, some time after that.

"Started what?"

"What you're going to tell them. At the gate." He did not look at her. He was watching Duchess's ears. "You said you had nine days. That's one gone. I'm asking how much of it you've spent."

Callie had spent most of the morning on it. She had thought about it every time she was not looking at the grass.

"One line," she said.

"Say it."

She had never said it out loud. It felt strange in her mouth, sitting up on a wagon board in the middle of a plain.

"*Callie. Hunter. I read ground.*"

Pell drove for a while without answering.

"That's a good first line," he said at last. "Do you know why?"

"Because it's true?"

"Because it's the one they'll want least." He moved the reins in his hands. "They'll want the other thing. The thing on the causeway, and the thing on the hill. Anybody would. And you've put the thing they don't want first, where they'll have to read it before they get to anything else." He glanced at her. "I've seen a great many people write down what they were at a great many gates, and most of them start with what they think the window's hoping for. You started with what you are."

"I haven't written the rest."

"No," Pell agreed. "That's the hard part. The first line's always the easy one." He clicked his tongue at the mare. "Take your time. You've got eight days."

---

They saw the windmill in the late afternoon.

Callie saw it first, from the board, because she was the one looking. It stood up out of the plain a long way ahead and a little east of the road, a thin grey line against the sky. It was the only thing taller than a boot for miles in any direction. As they came nearer she could see that it was a tower made of angled iron bars, four legs going up to a point, like the frame of a tent. There was a wheel at the top of it with most of the vanes gone. What was left of them stood out against the sky like the teeth of a broken comb.

At the foot of it there was something round and low and dark, a circle of old steel about as wide as the wagon was long.

"The tank," said Pell. His voice did not change, but his hands did something on the reins. "He wasn't lying."

Duchess had smelled it. Her head had come up for the first time all day, and her ears had gone forward, and she was walking faster on the bad leg than Pell wanted her to, pulling toward the east.

"Hold her," said Callie.

Pell looked at her.

"Hold her. Stop." Callie had her hand up to shade her eyes, and she could feel her heart going in her bruised side. "Look at the ground round it."

The windmill stood in the middle of a wide bare patch of plain. The grass had been trodden off it long ago and never grown back. On the downhill side of the tank, where there was a downhill, the smallest one in the world, the tank had overflowed in the last rain. The water had run out across the bare ground and made a long shallow wallow of mud. It shone in the low sun.

It was the lowest place in miles. Everything on the plain that drifted, sooner or later, drifted here.

And lying in the mud, grey-brown and still, the colour of an old wasp nest, there were shapes. They lay half in and half out of the wallow with their long arms stretched out in front of them and their heads down. They were not moving. They lay the way Katori had said they lay at the south end of the causeway, in the seams where the ash ran thickest. They lay like cattle in a pond.

Callie counted them. She counted them twice, to be sure.

"Five," she said.

Nobody said anything.

Behind her on the wagon bed, a cask sloshed as the wheel went over a seam. It was a small sound, a cask with very little left in it. Duchess stood in the road with her head up and her nostrils wide, staring at the water, and began, very quietly, to shake.
