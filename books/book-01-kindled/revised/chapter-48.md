# Chapter 48 — Toren

On the eleventh day out of the Level the road stopped being a road and started being an opinion about where a road had once been.

It had been a good one. That was the galling part. For six days past Ferrow they had walked on old six-lane with the crown still shedding water and the drainage still, against every reasonable expectation, draining, and Dessa had said on the second morning that if she had known roads like this existed she would have been considerably ruder about the one out of Sallow Creek. Then at about the fourth hour on the eleventh day the surface went soft under Pitch's off hind foot, and the mule stopped, and would not go on, and Dessa took his head and did not argue with him.

Toren went forward and put his heel into it.

It gave. Not like mud and not like rot. It went down about two inches under his weight and came back up when he took the weight off, slowly, the way a thing that is nearly full comes back up, and the road surface over it was unbroken, and there was nothing at all to see.

"Off it," said Rook, from the cart bed.

"It's not—"

"Off it. All the way off. Take the grass and go wide and don't come back on till I tell you."

They took the grass. It cost them the rest of the morning, and the wheel did not like the camber, and Wyck walked at the high side of it with his good shoulder against the rail and his strapped wrist tucked in his coat, and by noon they had made perhaps a mile and a half and could still see the place where they had left the road.

Rook had them halt on the shoulder and got himself up onto his elbow and looked back at it for a long time.

"That's the Sag," he said.

"That's a name."

"That's a name I heard in a flax shed six days ago from a man who's ninety, and he heard it from a carter, and the carter said it was eleven miles across and moving." He lay back down. "So it's forty miles across and it's been moving for two years and there's nobody to ask. That's the whole of what I've got and I'd rather you had it than not."

"Moving which way?" said Dessa.

"South," said Rook. "Out from Meridian. Which is the direction we're going in and I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

---

The signs came on over the next two days in the order of a thing getting closer — which was the wrong order for anyone's peace of mind, because it meant there was a middle to it and they were walking into the middle.

The first was the ash.

Toren found it in a ditch on the afternoon of the twelfth day, a drift of it about as long as the cart and three inches deep at the centre, grey-white, fine, banked up against the ditch wall the way snow banks. He knew what it looked like from twenty paces because he had been looking at it in flasks and in Ott's weigh-pans and in the bottom of a tin since the spring, and he went down into the ditch at a run with his heart going, and got a double handful of it, and it ran through his fingers exactly the way road-ash runs.

"Rook."

"I see it."

"There's *forty pounds* of it, there's—"

"There's nothing dead in it," said Rook.

Toren stopped with his hands still full.

"Go on then," said Rook. "You've got it in your hands; you tell me. Ash off a husk — what's in it?"

"Grit," said Toren, after a moment. "Bits. There's always bits."

"What sort of bits?"

"Bone, sometimes. Teeth. Whatever it had eaten. Stones out of the road."

"And what's in that?"

There was nothing in it. It was clean the way flour is clean, the whole length of the ditch, and once Toren had seen that he could not stop seeing it, and he opened his hands and let it go and climbed back up out of the ditch and wiped his palms on his coat twice.

"Nothing killed that," said Rook. "That's the ground's."

"Ground doesn't have ash."

"No," Rook agreed. "It doesn't."

They found it four more times before dark, in ditches and in the lee of the embankment and once in a long grey tongue lying out across two lanes of the six-lane where nothing had disturbed it, and nobody went down into any of the rest of it, and Dessa — who had a head for money and had spent five months in a tally room learning exactly what a flask was worth — walked past sixty pounds of free ash in the last of the light and did not say a single word about it, and that was the first time Toren was properly frightened.

The second sign came the following noon and lasted about a minute.

They were on the grass with the sun high and the country flat and pale in all directions, and Toren looked down and saw that he had two shadows.

They were not the same. One was his, short and black under his boots. The other lay out to the left of it, longer, thinner, softer at the edge, and it did not quite match him — when he lifted his arm it lifted its arm, but late, the way a reflection in bad water is late. He stood absolutely still in the middle of a field with his arm in the air and his mouth open.

Everything had two. The cart had two. Pitch had two, and one of them had four legs and the other one had four legs, and that was somehow much worse than if it hadn't.

"Don't look at it," said Rook, and his voice came out strange and flat and very fast. "All of you. Eyes on the road. Wyck — *don't*."

Wyck put his head down.

It went on for about a minute. Then it thinned, and went, and there was one shadow again, and the light was the light, and nobody said anything for a quarter of a mile.

"What was that?" said Toren.

"I don't know."

"You always—"

"I have been up and down this country for eleven years," said Rook, "and I have been at a fracture, and I lived in one for four years, and I have never once seen that, and I'm telling you I don't know because the alternative is that I make something up and one of you believes it." He put his forearm over his eyes. "There's a third one coming. There's always a third one."

The third one was the iron.

It started in the evening and it did not stop. The cart's tyre, the wheel hoop, Wyck's bar, the buckle on the mule's belly-band, the blade of Dessa's knife: warm. Not hot. Warm the way a cup is warm through a glove, warm the way a thing is warm when somebody has just put it down, and it did not cool overnight, and in the morning it was still warm, and it went on being warm for the next three days and never once got warmer or cooler than it had been the first evening.

And in Toren's coat pocket the grey slab that had been cool in his father's hands and cool in the coals at Sallow Creek and cool the way the inside of a well is cool would not go all the way cold.

He took it out about nine times the first night to check. On the tenth time he made himself stop, and put his hand flat on his chest instead, over his shirt, where the disk lay, and the disk was exactly as cold as it had always been, and that was the only thing in four square miles that was behaving itself.

---

Rook started him on the struts on the far side of Ferrow and went on with it every day after, from the cart bed, lying down, because he could not stand up for long enough to show anybody anything.

It was the worst teaching Toren had ever had from him and the best he had ever seen anybody do. Rook could not demonstrate. He could not walk out ten paces and be in a different place before the sound arrived. All he had was his voice and his eyes and a boy on a verge, and he used all three of them until Toren's legs shook.

"Again."

"They're not—"

"Again."

Toren put the spike away and reached for the other thing, which was in the same place and felt nothing like it. The spike was a fist: you had it or you did not. The struts were a decision made by the whole of both legs at once, and they came up thin and uneven and bright along the outside of his shins, and the left one was always a little late, and the first four times he had tried to use them he had put himself flat on his back in a flax field.

He fired them. He went eleven feet, which was a long way, and landed on his heels instead of his toes, and sat down hard.

"Better," said Rook.

"It hurt."

"It'll go on hurting. Get up. Now the changeover. Spike."

Toren got the spike out. It came in ugly and crooked and warm, exactly the shape it had been since a towpath in the spring, the shape Rook would not let him improve.

"Struts."

He let the spike go and reached for the struts, and they came.

"Spike."

He let the struts go and reached for the spike, and it came.

"Count it," said Rook.

"Count what?"

"Out loud. From when one's gone to when the other's there. Count in halves and don't be generous with yourself."

Toren counted it. He did it eleven times, standing on a verge in the wrong-lit afternoon with the iron of the cart warm behind him, and the best he ever got was one and a half, and the honest number was closer to two.

"Right," said Rook.

"Is that bad?"

"That's exactly what it should be for a boy who's been Ember since the spring, and if it were much better I'd be worried about what you'd been doing in the night." He shifted, carefully, and did not quite hide what it cost him. "Do it again tomorrow and the day after. It'll come down to one. It won't come down past one."

"Then I'll make it come down past one."

"No," said Rook.

Toren waited.

"You'll spend a year on that," said Rook. "Everybody does. Everybody stands on a verge and decides the answer is to be quicker at the changeover, and the good ones get quick enough that it takes eleven years to find out it was the wrong question, and I have been letting you think it because it's the only thing I've got that I can teach off the flat of my back." He looked up at the sky. "There's another way and it isn't a road lesson, and I'm not going to say it to you poorly the day before something, because if I say it poorly you'll do it poorly, and the doing poorly would be my fault and the cost would be yours."

"That's not—"

"I know what it isn't. Put the spike away and get the struts up and do it eleven more times, and then come and hold my arm while I get down off this cart, because I'd rather that was you than Wyck."

---

They needed a light on the fourteenth night and they did not have one.

It was nobody's fault and it was a small thing. The six-lane went into a cut with the walls close, and there was a culvert under it, an arched brick mouth eight feet high with black water going through it a foot deep, and Rook wanted to know if it was walkable because the alternative was a half-mile of the soft ground on the crown. Dessa had the best eyes. Dessa went down the bank on the stick and stood at the mouth of it and looked into a hole that went about nine feet and then stopped being anything.

"I can't see," she said.

"Take a brand out the fire," said Wyck.

"There's no fire yet."

"Then I'll make one."

"With what?" said Dessa. "There's no wood in four miles. We've been burning dung for two days."

There was a short silence on that bank, and it went on about two seconds too long, and then everybody understood at the same time why it had gone on two seconds too long, and Toren heard himself say, into it, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Milo would—"

He stopped.

Nobody helped him. That was the thing he thought about afterward, lying awake: that not one of the four of them said a single word to get him off it, and it was not cruelty, it was that all four of them had been about to say the same thing and had got to it a half-second later than he had, and there was nothing on that bank to say.

Dessa came back up. "It's walkable," she said. "It's walkable because I'm going to say it's walkable, and I'll go first, and if I'm wrong I'll be wet."

They walked it. It was walkable. It took nine minutes and it was the longest nine minutes of the day. Toren went through with his right hand flat against wet brick and the spike out in his left, its edge left dark. Lit, it would have made one thin line — enough to betray them and not enough to show him where to put his feet — because an Edge is a thing you put into something, not a lamp. The boy who had carried the light through Coldwater and the orchard and the reed beds and out along a sluice wall in the dark on his own judgement had been dead for sixteen days.

---

He went out past the cart after they made camp and got about forty paces and sat down on the rail.

He had meant to walk further. He had a clear picture of himself walking off up the road in the dark like a man with somewhere to be, and instead he got forty paces and his legs would not do the forty-first, and he sat down on a rusted guardrail in the middle of a dead six-lane and put his face in his hands and cried until he could not breathe properly.

It went on a long time. It was not dignified and there was nothing in it he recognised. He had been sick behind a wall on the bank at the Level and that had been a thing his body did to him; this was a thing he was doing, and he could not stop it, and about four minutes in he stopped wanting to.

He heard Wyck come up the road and stop about six feet away.

Wyck did not say do you want me to go away. Wyck stood there for a while, and then came the rest of the way and sat down on the rail, which took him two attempts because of the wrist, and put the bar across his knees, and looked out at the dark in the other direction.

Toren got himself down to shuddering and then to breathing and then to sitting there.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't."

"I'm not—"

"I said don't." Wyck turned the bar over once. "I've been doing it at about the third hour every night for a fortnight and I'd thank you not to make me have a conversation about it."

Toren looked at him.

"Every night?"

"Every night since the bank." Wyck said it the way he said everything, flat, like a man closing a ledger. "I take the watch and I sit there and about the third hour it comes and gets me and I sit there through it and then it goes and then it's the fourth hour and I'm still on watch. That's the whole of it. I'm not telling you for sympathy, I'm telling you because you've just done it in the road at the ninth hour like you've invented it, and you haven't."

"Does it get—"

"I don't know. Ask me in a year."

They sat on the rail. Out in the field on the left something small went through the grass and stopped.

"He was annoying," said Wyck.

"Wyck."

"He *was*. I'm going to say it once and then I'm never saying it again and neither is anybody else, and that's the problem." He was not looking at Toren. "He talked in my ear for eleven weeks. He asked me four hundred questions about a wall I didn't want to talk about. He put a light in my face at Coldwater and told me I had a good face for it, whatever that means, and I told him to get away from me, and I told him to get away from me about nine more times after that, and he never once believed me, not one single time, and he was right not to, and now everybody's going to spend the rest of their lives saying he was *kind*."

He stopped.

"He was kind," said Toren.

"He was kind and he was annoying and he was nine stone and he couldn't swim," said Wyck, "and if we only keep the good half of him we'll have got rid of him ourselves in about two years and saved everybody the bother, and I'm not doing it. That's all. That's the entire thing I came up the road to say and I've made a mess of it."

Toren wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"He used to do an impression of you," he said.

"I know he did."

"It was very good."

"It was *not* very good. It was extremely unfair. He did the voice like I'm a hundred and four—" Wyck stopped again and made a sound that was not a laugh and was not the other thing either, and sat there with the bar across his knees. "Right," he said.

They went back down to the cart together at about the tenth hour, and Dessa was awake and said nothing, and Senna was awake and said nothing, and Rook, who was never asleep, said nothing either, and Toren lay down under the tailgate and slept for six hours without dreaming, which he had not done since the Level.

---

In the morning they came to the flyover at Hallam Cross.

It stood up out of the flat country about three miles ahead and they had been looking at it since first light: an old road crossing an older one on four square piers, one span down at the north end and the rest of it standing, with the grass grown up the embankment and a stand of concrete revetments along the foot of it like a row of teeth. Rook said there had been a fuel halt on the east side and a weighbridge, and that he had slept in the weighbridge office twice and would not recommend it.

They came up on it at about the second hour. The iron was still warm. The ground under the six-lane had been firm for a mile and a half, which nobody trusted.

Wyck was at the off-side wheel, and Wyck saw it.

"Tor."

He had stopped with his hand on the cart rail. He was looking up and to the left, at the nearest pier of the flyover, where the concrete came down into the grass and there was an old crash rail bolted across the foot of it on four brackets to stop somebody in a lorry from hitting the pier at speed.

There were four lines gouged down through the rail.

Toren went cold from the back of his neck down. He knew them before he was near enough to count them, because he had been thirteen years old in a road cut, four months and a lifetime ago, standing behind two men who had stopped walking — looking at four parallel lines cut down through sixty years of rust into bright metal underneath: as long as his forearm, set apart about as wide as his spread hand, at the height of his chest.

These were at the height of his head. They were longer than his arm. They had gone through the rail and out the other side of it and four inches into the concrete of the pier behind, and the concrete was old and the cuts in it were bright.

"That's the same thing," said Toren. "That's the—"

"That's not the same thing," said Rook.

He had got himself up in the cart bed. He was looking north, past the pier, up the line of the crash rail where it ran away along the foot of the embankment toward the broken span.

"Dessa," he said. "Walk up the rail and tell me where the next set is. Don't go past it. Count your paces out loud."

Dessa went up the rail on the stick, counting.

At the cut, four months ago, the next set had been eight feet on. And the next past that. And the next — one after another, the whole visible length of the rail, as regular as a fence-mender's marks — because the thing that made them had walked.

Dessa got to eight feet and there was nothing.

She got to twenty and there was nothing, and her counting had gone quiet and flat, and Toren realised she was still counting only because he could see her mouth moving.

She stopped at ninety-one.

She stood there for a moment with her hand on the rail. Then she turned round and came back down the whole ninety-one paces without hurrying — which Toren understood afterward had cost her something — and stood at the tailgate and put her hand on it.

"Ninety-one," she said. "And then eleven feet past that there's another one, and then nothing again for a long way, and I didn't go and look."

Nobody said anything.

"Rook," said Senna, from inside the cart, in a voice like a dry stick breaking. "Say the word you're not saying."

Rook sat in the cart bed with the flyover's shadow coming across him and his hand flat on the boards.

"Get everything off the crown of the road," he said, "and get it in behind the revetments, and do it now, and do it quietly, and Toren — put that spike away and leave it away. It comes at worked power. Everything does. That one'll come at it from four miles."

They got the cart in behind the concrete in about forty minutes, and it rained a little and then stopped, and the wrong light came and went twice along the top of the embankment, and nobody ate anything.

Toren went back out once, at the end of it, on his own, and stood at the foot of the pier with his hands at his sides and looked at four bright cuts going four inches into sixty-year-old concrete, at the height of his own head; and then north, up the crash rail, where a girl's paces had stopped at ninety-one — four months and three hundred miles from a road cut where the same four marks had been eight feet apart, the whole visible length of the rail, as regular as a fence-mender's work, made by a thing that had walked.
