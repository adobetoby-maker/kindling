# Chapter 35 — Callie

"Tell me what we can do," said Katori, "and then tell me which of it's worst."

They had pulled back three hundred paces down the road and stopped, so the wagon was a shape on the plain and not a shape at the tank. Pell had Duchess by the head with both hands. The mare had stopped shaking and started pulling instead, a steady lean toward the water that she did not seem to know she was doing. Otto stood at her other side with his good hand on her neck.

Callie sat on the board. She could see the whole of it from there: the tower, the round dark tank at its foot, the long shining wallow on the low side, and the five grey shapes lying in the mud.

She made herself do it out loud, the way her grandfather had made her do it in ditches.

"We can wait for morning," she said. "They get up in the morning, mostly, and drift. By noon there might be none there. Or there might be eight. And we've got a cask and a bit, and she drinks tonight or she doesn't pull tomorrow."

"Go on."

"We can go in now. You go in. Lit. At dusk, on the only high thing in forty miles." She did not look at Katori when she said it. "Five of them. In mud. Mud's bad for them, it's soft, they can't brace in it. But it's bad for you too. And every one you put down leaves ash in the lowest place in the country, and it'll call everything that's left on the plain to the one water we've got."

"Go on."

Callie looked at the tin.

It was in her coat. She had taken it out of the barrow at noon, when she was looking at the jar and Katori had said no, and she had not put it back. She took it out now and held it in her hand. It was a small flat tin with a screw lid, and Pell had written *C. — tenth* across the top in his small neat hand, and it weighed almost nothing.

"Or we can call them off it," she said.

---

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Ash calls them," said Callie. "Everybody says so. It calls them to where it is and it doesn't care what's standing on it. We've watched it do it. On the causeway, all night." She turned the tin over. "So we put some somewhere else. Off the road, the other side from the tank, a good way out. In a hollow, where it'll pool. And we wait. And they get up out of the mud, one at a time, the way they do, and drift to it. And while they're drifting to it, we're at the tank."

"And then?"

"And then we fill both casks and she drinks, and we go. North. In the dark, a mile, two if she'll do it, and we sleep dark. And they're lying in a hollow a long way behind us, round some ash that isn't ours any more." She swallowed. "That's the cost. The ash. We don't get it back."

Katori looked at the tin in her hand.

"Whose ash?" she said.

Callie had known she would ask. She had worked out the answer before she opened her mouth, the way she had worked out *no* on the scarp before supper.

"Mine," she said. "The last of my tenth. It's not a Handful. It's not even a finger. But it's lifted and it's clean, and I don't think they count." She held the tin out. "It's the only ash on this wagon that's nobody's but mine. The jar's my mother's. The sacks are yours. This is what's left of mine."

Pell had turned round on the board. He did not say anything. He looked at the tin, with his own writing on the lid, and then at Callie, the way he looked at a hill.

"Say the other cost," said Katori.

"If it doesn't work, it doesn't work, and we've spent it on nothing. And then it's the second thing. You go in."

"Say the one you haven't said."

Callie was quiet for a moment.

"If they come to it and they don't stay," she said, "if they lie down round it for a bit and then get up again and drift, then they'll drift anywhere. Maybe after us." She put the tin back in her lap. "I don't think they will. Nothing I've seen lying up in ash ever got up again before morning. But I haven't seen enough to know."

Katori nodded slowly.

"That's three costs," she said. "I'll take all three over the second thing." She turned. "Pell."

Pell already had the pencil out. He wrote for a moment, and turned the book round, and held it out so she could see it in the last of the light.

*Windmill tank. Five lying up. C.'s tenth for bait. C.'s call. Concur — K., P.*

He looked at the line under it for a moment, where there was room. Then he wrote one more word, small.

*Spent.*

---

Ines took the tin.

Callie could not have walked three hundred paces out into the grass and back and done it fast, and everybody knew it, and nobody made her say so. Ines listened to where Callie wanted it, and what kind of ground, and then she nodded once and took the tin and went off across the plain at a walk that ate distance without seeming to hurry.

"West," Callie had said. "Away from the road and away from the tank, both. There's a dip out there, past that old fence line, see where the grass is greener. Tip it in the bottom. Don't spread it. Pour it all in one place, where the ground's lowest, and let it run."

Ines went out past the fallen fence. She went down into the dip until only her head and shoulders showed above the grass, and stayed there a moment, and came up again, and walked back. She did not hurry on the way back either.

"Done," she said, and gave Callie the empty tin. "It ran."

Callie put the tin in her coat. It weighed exactly what it had weighed before. That was the strange thing about it.

Then they waited.

---

It took nearly half an hour for the first one to move.

Callie watched it from the board. The light was going long and gold and sideways, the way it had gone on the causeway at dusk, and the wallow shone like a strip of metal. One of the grey shapes at the far end of it lifted its head. It did not look round. It had nothing to look with. It simply raised the front of itself out of the mud, the way a plant turns toward a window. Then it got its long arms under it and pulled itself up out of the wallow, all four points, and began to walk.

It walked west. It went past the end of the tank and out onto the bare ground and into the grass, not hurrying, not going straight either, drifting a little this way and a little that. But it was drifting west, toward the dip past the old fence.

"There," said Otto, very softly.

The second one got up a few minutes after. Then the third. The fourth lay a long time, and Callie thought it would not, and then it got up too, all at once, like somebody remembering an errand.

"Four," said Ines.

The fifth did not move.

Callie looked at it for a long time. It lay at the near end of the wallow, right up against the round steel side of the tank, where the mud was deepest. It lay with its head down and its arms stretched out, half sunk. As the light went she could see less and less of it. It was just a grey hump against the dark of the tank.

"It's not getting up," she said.

"No," said Katori.

"It might be stuck. The mud's deep there. Or it might just not be — " She did not know how to say it. "It might just be the one that doesn't."

"There's always one that doesn't." Katori was looking west, where the four grey shapes were almost at the fence line now, small in the grass. "How long before they're lying down round your ash?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe less."

"Then we go in nine," said Katori, "and we go in quiet, and we leave that one in its mud unless it asks for something."

---

They went in at a walk, with the brake half on so the wheels would not rattle.

Duchess did not need to be led. She went for the water as straight as a husk goes for a drain, and Pell had to lean back on the reins with all his weight to stop her from trotting on the bad leg. Otto walked at her head with his hand on the cheekpiece, talking to her, the way Pell talked to her. Katori walked beside the near wheel with her hands empty. Ines was up on the crates with an arrow on the string and her eyes on the fifth husk.

Callie stayed on the board. There was nothing else she could do.

They pulled up on the far side of the tank from the wallow. Up close it was bigger than it had looked. It was a ring of riveted steel as high as Callie's chest, rusted brown on the outside, and it was full nearly to the brim of dark clear water with the last of the sky lying on it. The windmill tower went up beside it, four iron legs, and a long rod came down out of the middle of the tower into a pipe at the tank's edge. There was a little shed beside the tower with its roof fallen in.

"Buckets," said Pell, very quietly. "We've got one."

They had one. It was the canvas bucket Duchess drank from, and it held about as much as a hat.

Otto was looking at the shed.

"Otto," said Katori.

"There's always something in a building," said Otto, and went.

He was back in less than a minute. He had a steel pail in his good hand with the handle half rusted through, and a length of old chain, and a look on his face that Callie would remember for a long time.

"See," he whispered.

Nobody told him he was right. There wasn't time. But Callie saw Ines's mouth move at the corner, up on the crates, and knew she had seen it too.

They filled the casks. Otto leaned over the rim of the tank and dipped the pail, and passed it to Katori, and Katori poured it through the bung hole of the first cask with the funnel Pell held. Pell had got Duchess's head down to the canvas bucket at last, and the mare was drinking in long pulls with her eyes half shut. Callie could hear her swallowing from the board. Every time Otto dipped, the water in the tank lapped at the steel with a small hollow sound.

"One," whispered Pell, and they started on the second cask.

The pail's rusted handle caught on the rim of the tank on the way out, and the whole thing went against the steel with a clang like a bell.

---

The fifth husk got up.

It got up out of the deep mud at the foot of the tank with a long wet sucking sound, all at once, the way a thing gets up that has been lying on a spring. It did not go round the tank. It could not see round anything. It came straight at the place where the sound had been, which was the steel wall of the tank itself, and it hit it with its shoulder, and the whole ring of water shivered.

Then it found the edge, and came round.

"*Forty,*" said Ines. "*Thirty-five.* Taking it?"

"No," said Katori. "You'll never find the arrow in that mud."

She walked round the tank to meet it.

Callie watched her go. She saw Katori's hand come out of her coat. She saw the grey blade, the length of a forearm, simply be there in her fist, and it seemed to Callie that it was the brightest thing she had ever seen on the plain, brighter than the sunset. It was not light. It was just *lit*. She could feel it from the board the way you feel a stove across a room.

The husk came round the curve of the tank on all four points, dripping mud, and Katori stepped across its line and put the blade in under its arm and out, once, short, with her elbow in, the way Callie had watched her do it at the front of the wagon on the causeway.

It came apart all at once and from everywhere. It came apart into the mud at the foot of the tank, and its ash went down into the water of the wallow and began to spread there, silver on the dark, going nowhere.

Katori's hand was empty again. She had been lit for two seconds. Perhaps three.

"That's done," she said. "Fill."

---

They filled.

Callie sat on the board and counted, because somebody had to and she was the only one not doing anything else. She counted the dips of the pail. She counted Duchess's swallows. And the whole time she watched the west.

At forty she saw the first one.

It was a long way out, past the fence, at the edge of the dip where Ines had poured the ash. It had been lying down. Now it was standing up, a small grey shape against the last of the light, and it was not drifting any more. It was coming back.

"Katori," she said.

"I know."

"It felt you. And the ash in the wallow. It's fresh. It's calling louder than mine." She watched a second shape stand up beside the first. "They're coming back."

"How long?"

Callie measured it with her eyes, the way she had measured the gap between a culvert wall and a husk, a lifetime ago, on the night Katori first saw her.

"Three hundred paces. Drifting, not running. Four minutes. Five."

"Pell."

"Nearly," said Pell. The second cask gurgled. Otto dipped. "Nearly."

"*Sixty,*" said Callie, out loud now, where they could all hear it. "*Sixty-one.*"

"Horse is done," said Pell. "Horse is done drinking, I'm taking her off it, and she won't like it."

Duchess did not like it. She fought the bit, and Pell leaned back, and Otto got in front of her with the pail still in his hand, and she stopped.

"Cask's full," said Katori, and hammered the bung in with the heel of her hand.

"*A hundred and twenty.*"

The first husk had come past the fence line. It was two hundred paces out, drifting across the bare ground toward the wallow, toward the silver spreading in the water where Katori had put the fifth down. It was not coming at them. It was coming at the ash.

"Go," said Katori.

Pell took the brake off.

---

They went north in the last of the light, with the full casks sloshing and the pail rattling on its chain against the side of the wagon, where Otto had hung it because he would not leave it behind. Duchess went at her own pace, short on the near hind, and nobody asked her to go faster. The wheels bumped over the tar seams. Behind them the windmill stood up against the west, black on red, the only high thing for forty miles.

Callie turned round on the board and watched it until it was too dark to see.

The last thing she saw was the four grey shapes, one after another, walking into the wallow and lying down in it again, round the silver in the water. They did not look after the wagon. They had nothing to look with.

"Well," said Pell beside her, very quietly, after a mile. "There's a Handful of Katori's lying in that mud that I'll never see in my book."

"I know."

"And a finger of yours in a hollow a quarter mile off it."

"I know."

"I'm not complaining." Pell flicked the reins. "I'm writing it down. There's a difference, and I've been trying to teach it to Otto for three years."

---

They went a mile and a half before Duchess stopped, and they slept on the road with no fire, dark, all of them. Nothing came.

Her mother slept the whole of it and woke at dawn and ate, and said she felt better than yesterday, which was true, a little. She walked for half an hour in the morning with one hand on the tailgate. When she got back up on the bed she said the word for the canvas bucket wrong twice, and the third time she said it right, and looked at Callie with her eyebrows up as if to say, *there*.

The third day was a day of nothing, which Callie had not had in a long time. The plain went by. The pans went by, empty and old. The light on the edge of the world got no nearer. Callie sat on the board beside Pell and read the grass and thought about the second line.

On the fourth morning, when she went down into herself to look, there was a hand of water at the bottom of the well.

She told Katori.

Katori looked at her across the ashes of last night's cold supper for a moment. Then she said, "At the noon halt. Bring your hook."

---

They did it in the lee of the wagon at the noon halt, with the others pretending to eat and not watch. Otto did not pretend very well.

"Go down," said Katori. "Find it. The way you did on the flats."

Callie found it. It was easier every time. The hook lay across her knees with the bolt in and the conduit laid along the ground. She went down through the steel and the pipe and her hands and down, and there was the top of the well, and the hand of water standing in the bottom of it, cold and clear.

"Now lean."

Callie leaned.

She did it the way she had always done it, the way she had done it on the causeway and at the side of the road on the flats. She dropped her hips and set her back foot and let her weight go down and back into the steel, the way you swing a sack off a rail.

It came. The cold came up through her like water up a pump. The long grey sweep came off the end of the forged curve and lay out over the road.

And her left side screamed.

It was not a *no* this time. It was worse than that. It was the husk's arm coming round at the height of her chest, all over again, and she could not breathe at all, and she came up off the Edge without deciding to. The grey went out of the hook. She sat on the road with both arms round her ribs and her eyes shut and the tears running out of them, breathing in little pieces.

Nobody moved. Katori waited until she could hear again.

"Where is it?" said Katori.

"My side. Where the — "

"Not that. The Edge. Where is it, when you lean?"

"In my hips," said Callie. "My back. My feet. It's the same as a sack. You drop down and back, and you let the weight go, and it comes." She got her breath. "I can't drop. That's all. I can't drop with my ribs like this. I'll have to wait till they mend."

"Mm," said Katori. "Watch me."

---

She took out her Ember.

She did not stand up to do it. She sat on the road where she was, cross-legged, with her back straight and her hands in her lap, and the flat grey slab lying in her right palm. She looked at Callie. She did not look at the slab.

The blade was in her hand.

Callie had been watching as hard as she had ever watched anything, harder than she had watched the husk line on the first night. Katori had not moved. Her hips had not moved. Her back had not moved. Her hand had not closed. The blade had simply been there, grey, the length of a forearm, lying across her palm the way the slab had lain across it a moment before.

Then it was gone. Then it was there. Then it was gone.

"I stopped moving," said Katori, "about the third year. I didn't decide to. I just noticed one day that I'd stopped." She put the slab away. "You found it with your body. That's right. You found everything with your body. Your grandfather taught you with his hand on the back of your neck in a ditch, and a sack on a rail, and that's how you learn a thing when you're nine, and it's how you learned this, and I'd never tell you it was wrong." She looked at Callie's ribs. "But it isn't where it lives. It's only where you found it."

"Then where does it live?"

"I don't know where yours lives," said Katori. "I only know it isn't in your hips, because mine isn't in mine. Find out."

---

Callie tried the obvious thing first. It was stupid, and she knew it was stupid while she was doing it, and she did it anyway, because you had to try the obvious thing to know it was stupid.

She sat still. She did not lean at all. She went down to the well and looked at the water standing in the bottom and wanted the Edge to come up.

Nothing came. The hook stayed the temperature of the noon.

"No," said Ines, from the tailboard, where she was running her thumb down an arrow. "That was nothing."

"I know it was nothing."

"I'm only counting. That was a nothing." She put the arrow back. "It's a sum. You had one answer that worked and one that didn't. Now you've got two that don't. You're closer."

Callie did not think she was closer. She sat with the hook across her knees and thought about it instead.

It was not the leaning that brought it. It was not nothing either. It was something in between. On the flats, when she had leaned too far toward a better shape, it had gone out. When she had not leaned at all, just now, it had not come. So it was not the size of the lean. It was something else about it.

She thought about the sack.

When you swung a sack off a rail, you dropped your hips and your weight went down and back. But the drop was not the thing. The drop was only how far you went. The thing was the *direction*, the moment your weight stopped going up to meet the sack and started going back and down to take it. Her grandfather used to say you could feel it in your heels before you felt it anywhere else. The heels knew first. The rest of you only followed.

She did not drop her hips. She sat up straight, the way Katori sat, and she did not move her back. She let her weight go, very slightly, into her heels, where they were set on the road in front of her. It was not a drop. It was only the beginning of one. It was the direction of a lean, without any of the distance.

It came.

It came thin at first, a grey shiver along the curve like breath on a window. Then it steadied and went on, the whole long ugly sweep of it, four feet of edge the colour of nothing, lying out over the road. She had not moved. Her side had not said anything at all.

"Oh," said Otto.

Callie came up off it. She did it the same way, the smallest version of finishing the pull, her weight coming off her heels and back over the middle of her. The hook went heavy. The grey was gone.

She sat there breathing halfway in, and it did not hurt.

"Again," said Katori.

---

She did it four more times, and Ines counted, and Katori did not say one word.

The second time it came slower. The third time it came as soon as her weight touched her heels, as if it had been waiting for her there. The fourth time she did it looking at Otto instead of the hook, and it came anyway, and Otto went red. The fifth time she tried to make it come with even less, with nothing but the thought of her heels. It guttered and did not come, and she had to let her weight go a little more, and then it came.

"Not nothing," she said. "It still has to be something."

"Everybody's is something," said Katori. "Mine's something. You just can't see it any more. That's all eleven years buys you." She looked at the hook. "What about the shape?"

"It's the same. It's still the ugly one."

"Good."

"And it costs the same. I counted." Callie went down into herself to look. The hand of water at the bottom of the well was half a hand now. "Five short ones. It's gone down as much as if I'd held one for forty."

"It'll always cost what it costs," said Katori. "Moving less doesn't make it cheaper. It only makes it so you can do it with two ribs gone, or with a husk on your chest, or sitting on a wagon board, or lying on your back in a road." She stood up. "That's all today. Eat something. And don't try the other one."

Callie looked up.

"I know you want to," said Katori. "Don't. Not today. You've got half a hand and two ribs, and I'm not going to be the one who explains to your mother why you're lying on the road again."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were," said Katori. "You were going to wait till we were all asleep and go behind the wagon and try it on your own. I'd have done the same." She went round to the front of the wagon. "Don't."

---

Callie waited till they were all asleep and went behind the wagon and tried it on her own.

She did not go far. She could not go far. She stood in the dark at the tailboard with the plain going away black in every direction and the stars coming down all round the edge of it, and she did not take the hook. She stood with her hands empty at her sides and her feet on the concrete of the old road.

The Ward lived in her feet. She had found that at the bottom of the scarp, with a crate coming off the wagon at her. She dropped her weight and put her back foot behind her and set herself for the catch, and it came. But the drop was the thing her ribs could not do. She knew that without trying it. Setting herself for the catch was her whole body going down and taking the weight, and her left side would not take any weight at all.

So she did not drop.

She stood up straight. She put her back foot behind her, a little, the way she would. And then, instead of dropping down onto it, she only pressed. She pressed her back heel down into the concrete, straight down, the way you press a spade into the ground with your foot before you lean on the handle. It was the beginning of the catch. It was the direction, without the distance.

For about two breaths, the old-glass shine was on the back of her hand.

She saw it by starlight. It was very faint, fainter than it had ever been, a grey sheen over her knuckles like the surface of a window in the rain. It did not hurt. Her side did not say anything.

Then her heel slid a hair on the concrete and it went off her like water off a tipped plate.

She stood there in the dark for a long time.

Then she went back and lay down on her right side under the wagon, and did not tell anybody, and lay awake with her hand against her chest, feeling her heart go.

---

Ines was on watch. Callie could see the shape of her sitting on the tailboard against the stars, very still, the way she had sat in the gully for five hours on the sixth day.

"You did it," said Ines, without turning round. She said it very low.

"Did what?"

"The other one. Behind the wagon. I saw the shine." Ines's head did not move. "I'm not going to tell her. I'm only telling you I saw it, because I'd want to know, if it was me."

Callie did not say anything for a while.

"It was two breaths," she said at last. "And I didn't drop. It came without the drop."

"Then that's a sum done," said Ines.

They were quiet. The light was there on the edge of the world to the north, low and steady and pale. Callie lay and looked at it. She had looked at it every night for four nights, and it had never got any nearer, and she had begun to think of it the way she thought of the moon.

Then it went out.

It did not flicker. It did not go dim. One moment it was there on the edge of the world, lighting the bottom of the clouds. The next moment it was not, and the dark came all the way down to the grass in the north the way it did in every other direction, and there was nothing there at all.

Callie sat up. She forgot about her ribs and sat straight up, and hit her head on the underside of the wagon bed, and did not care.

"It's all right," said Ines.

"It went *out* — "

"It went out last night too. And the night before. I didn't say anything." Ines still had not turned round. "It goes out at the same time every night. Near enough the same as I can tell. And I can tell."

"Why?"

"I don't know." Ines was quiet for a moment. "Pell says it isn't broken. He says a thing that goes out at the same time every night isn't broken. It's a decision."

Callie lay back down. She lay a long time, looking north at the dark where the light had been.

She thought about the second line. She had thought about it for three days. She had thought about whether to put the doors in at all. If she left them out, then the window would write down a girl who read ground, and nothing else, and that would be a true thing and a small one. And somewhere in Pell's book, in his small neat hand, was the line *Pitch. Two pulls, tackle, C.'s hook in the seam. Ninety-one seconds.* Five of them had signed under it, six counting her mother's crooked *T.* Pell had said the book was worth something only because nothing in it had ever been a lie.

She did not want to put a lie in somebody's book. Even if it was a book at a gate, belonging to people who would make a column for her and decide what the column was for.

*Two doors*, she thought. That was the second line. She did not know yet what came after it.

---

In the morning, Ines found the marks.

Callie saw her find them. Ines had gone out from the camp at first light the way she did every morning, walking a slow circle round the wagon at about a hundred paces, looking at the ground. Callie had watched her do it for fifteen days and had never once asked what she was looking for.

Halfway round the circle, on the west side, Ines stopped.

She stood still for a long time. Then she crouched down and put her fingers on the ground. Then she stood up again and walked on, slower, all the way round the circle, and at every quarter of it she stopped and crouched, and stood, and went on. When she came back round to where she had started, she stood there looking at the ground and did not come in.

Katori went out to her. Callie got down off the board, slowly, and went too.

It was not a husk line. Callie knew that before she was near enough to see it. A husk left a trough, a leaning, a thing pushed through. This was nothing like that. This was a track in the dust at the edge of the concrete, a small one, the size of the palm of her hand, with four toes and the marks of claws in front of them. And beside it another. And beside that another, overlapping, and another, and another. They went round the camp in a ring at a hundred paces, all the way round, the same distance out the whole way. They had not come in. They had gone round and round, and in places the dust was trodden smooth with it, as if something had walked the same circle many times, at a steady pace, in the dark, while everybody slept.

On the west side, where Ines had first stopped, the tracks stopped too. There were four sets of them together there, side by side, facing in toward the wagon. Something had stood there for a long time, all four together, very still.

Then the tracks went away west, into the grass.

"Hounds," said Ines.

She said it the way you'd say the name of somebody who had once done you a great harm and whom you had hoped never to see again.
