# Chapter 36 — Callie

"They were counting us," said Ines.

They stood in a half circle round the four sets of tracks on the west side of the camp, in the first grey light. Nobody had sat down to eat. Pell had Duchess in the traces already, because there was nothing else to do with his hands, and the mare stood with her near hind tipped up and her ears going.

"That's what the ring is." Ines did not point. She did not need to. The trodden circle went all the way round the wagon at a hundred paces, smooth in places where the dust had been walked on over and over. "They come in the dark and they walk round you. Round and round, the same distance, the same pace, all night if they want. They're finding out what you are. How many. Who's slow. Who's lit." She looked at the four sets of tracks, side by side, facing in. "Then they stand still and do the sum. And then they go away and come back when they've done it."

"When?" said Callie.

"When they've chosen." Ines looked north up the road. "Dusk, mostly. In the Warren it was always dusk."

"Chosen what?"

"A side." Ines said it flatly, like a range. "They pick a side of you and they come at it, all of them, all together. And once they've started they can't stop. They can't turn. They can't change their minds about which side. They've made the sum, and the sum's done, and whatever's on that side is what they get." She was quiet for a moment. "If somebody says the side out loud before they get there, everybody turns and meets them, and it's a bad night for the hounds. If nobody says it, it isn't."

Callie looked at the four sets of tracks facing in. They were not facing the middle of the wagon. She walked the line of them with her eyes, the way she walked a husk line back to where it came from.

They were facing the tailboard.

They were facing the end of the wagon bed where her mother slept.

"Ines," she said.

"I know," said Ines. "I saw."

---

There was no outrunning them. Nobody even said it. Duchess walked at her own pace or she did not walk, and a hound could go round the whole plain in the time it took her to cross a mile of it.

So they went on north, slowly, and they chose their ground.

Callie read the country from the board all morning, and she did not read it for husks. She read it the way she thought a hound would. She looked for anything that would let a thing get close without being seen. She looked for dips, and ditches, and old fence lines standing high enough to throw a shadow. Katori walked beside the wheel and listened, and every now and then she said "Mm," and once she said "No, too far," and once she said nothing at all for a mile.

In the early afternoon Callie found it.

It was not much. It was a long stretch where the old road ran across the plain with nothing on either side of it at all. There was no ditch. There was no fence standing, not even a sawn post. The grass on both sides was short and even and pale, and it went away flat for four hundred paces in every direction before there was so much as a dip in it.

"Here," she said.

Pell stopped.

"There's nowhere to come from," said Callie. "Anything that wants to get close has to come across that." She put her hand out over the grass. "In the open. Where we can see it the whole way. Where Ines can see it the whole way."

Ines looked out at the grass on the west side for a long time, and then the east, and then north and south along the road.

"Two hundred on the west before I'd take anything," she said. "Two-fifty on the east. The light'll be behind them on the west at dusk and in my eyes. East's better for me." She looked at Katori. "It's good ground. It's the best ground I've seen since the Warren."

"Then we stop here," said Katori, "and we wait for them to come to it."

---

It was Callie's idea, the hook. She would think about that afterward for a long time.

"They come at worked power," she said. "Everything that hunts. Everybody says so. It's the brightest thing in the country, and they come at it." She had the hook across her knees on the board, with the bolt out and the conduit laid in the wagon bed behind her. "On the hill, the husk came at the hook. Not me. It went a hand's width left at the last and went for the steel, because the steel was brightest. And nothing could hurt the steel. It folded on it like a coat on a post."

"Husks aren't hounds," said Ines.

"No. But it's the same thing that brings them. Isn't it?" Callie looked at Katori. "You said. Anything that hunts."

Katori was quiet.

"So I put the hook in the road," said Callie. "In a seam. On the side they stood on last night, the west. And when they go still, when they're doing the sum, I light it. And they see the brightest thing on the plain, and it's on the west, and it's steel, and it can't be hurt, and they come at it. And you're there. You and Ines." She stopped. "And they can't turn once they've started. Ines said."

"They come at worked power," said Ines slowly. "That's true. I've watched them come. But coming at it isn't the same as going for it." She frowned. "I don't know the difference. I only know it's not the same word."

"Say what you'd do instead," said Katori.

Ines thought about it. "Stand. Watch. Call the side when they go. Take what I can at two hundred and let Katori have the rest." She shrugged. "That's all I've ever done with hounds. It worked in the Warren, mostly."

"Mostly," said Katori.

"Mostly," Ines agreed, and did not say what the rest of it was.

Katori looked at the hook for a long moment, and then at Callie.

"We'll do it your way," she said. "And we'll do it ready for your way to be wrong. Everybody faces west, because that's where the hook is and that's where they stood. Otto takes the east with the pole, alone, and Pell holds the horse, and if Callie's wrong, the east is where it'll be wrong." She turned to Ines. "And you'll say it. Whatever they do. The moment they do it."

"I always say it," said Ines.

---

At the middle of the afternoon Katori came and sat on the tailboard beside Callie with her tin.

It was not the tin from the pump house. It was a bigger one, one of the ones from the causeway sacks, with a screw lid and grey ash inside it. She unscrewed it and held it out.

"How much is in you?" she said.

"Half a hand. From yesterday."

"Half a hand's forty seconds of that hook, if you let it stand. Twenty, if you grip it. Less than that, if you've got to do something else at the same time." Katori held the tin a little closer. "One finger. It's not a gift."

"What is it?"

"A loan. Pell."

"I heard," said Pell, from the front of the wagon, and did not turn round, and wrote something.

Callie looked at the ash for a long time. It was Katori's ash, the ash from the causeway, and it was not hers, and every finger of it was a thing Katori would have put on the table at the gate.

"I'll pay it back," she said.

"I know you will," said Katori. "It's written down."

Callie tipped a finger of it into her cup and drank it. It went down to the bottom of the well like rain into a dry ditch, and when she went down to look there was more than a hand of water there. It was not much. It was a long way from nothing.

---

Her mother would not lie down.

"If one of them gets up here," she said, "I'd like to have something to say to it."

She was sitting up against the crates on the wagon bed, with a blanket round her shoulders and her hat on. She had made Otto bring her the skillet. It lay in her lap under her left hand. She could not have lifted it high. She could lift it a little. She had tried it twice while Callie watched and pretended not to.

"Lie down," said Callie. "They can't see you if you're lying down."

"They saw me last night lying down. You showed me the marks." Her mother moved the skillet a quarter of an inch. "I'd rather they saw me sitting up. It's more dignified. And you can't say anything to anybody lying on your back, I've been trying for a month."

Callie stood at the tailboard and looked at her.

"I'll be on the west," she said. "At the hook. I can't come."

"I know."

"If they come at the east — "

"Then Otto's on the east, and Pell's at the front, and I've got a skillet, and you're on the west at your hook doing what you've said you'll do." Her mother looked at her steadily. "I watched you plan it all afternoon. It's a good plan. If it's wrong, it'll be wrong, and you'll see it's wrong faster than anybody, and you'll do something about it. That's what you do." She paused. "Go on. Go and stand where you said you'd stand."

Callie went.

---

She seated the hook in the road on the west side, forty paces out from the wagon.

There was a tar seam there between two of the old slabs, running straight across the road the way the seam had run across the rock at the top of the scarp. It was old and hard and full of dust. Callie knelt beside it, slowly, the way she knelt for everything now, and scraped the dust out with her fingers until the edges of the concrete were clean. Then she set the point of the hook down into it and pushed, until the curve stood up out of the road on its own.

She did not light it. Not yet. She stood beside it with her hand resting on the back of the curve and her feet set, and she waited.

Katori stood ten paces in front of her, further out on the west, with her hands empty. Ines was up on the crates with her bow. On the far side of the wagon Otto stood alone on the east with the pole across his body, one-handed, the good hand near the iron collar. Pell was at Duchess's head.

The sun went down behind the hounds' side of the plain. It went down red and huge and flat, and the grass on the west went black against it, and the long shadows of all of them lay out across the road to the east.

Callie did not see them come. Nobody did. There was the grass going black, and then there was something in the grass that had not been there.

"Hounds," said Ines, very quietly. "West. Two hundred and forty."

---

There were four of them.

They were long and low and the colour of wet ash, longer than a dog and lower, with their heads carried out in front of them on the level of their backs. Callie could not have said what was wrong about their shape, only that something was, the way something was wrong about a husk. They did not come in. They went round.

They went round the camp in a ring at two hundred paces. All four of them went round at the same speed, spaced out evenly, the way the tracks had gone round in the night. They did not hurry. They did not make a sound. They went down the west side against the sunset and across the south, and up the east side, where Callie could not see them past the wagon, and across the north, and back down the west. Then they did it again.

"Pacing," said Ines. "They're counting."

The third time round, one of them stopped.

It stopped on the west side, directly out from the hook, out past Katori, with its long head pointed at the wagon. And the moment it stopped, the other three stopped too, wherever they were in the ring. They did not look at each other. They did not need to. All four of them stood absolutely still in the grass at two hundred paces, facing in, the way they had stood in the dust last night.

"*Still,*" said Ines.

Callie lit the hook.

---

She did it the way she had found in the dark behind the wagon, two nights before. She did not drop. She could not drop. She stood up straight beside the hook with her hand flat on the back of the curve, and she pressed her back heel down into the concrete of the road, straight down, the beginning of the catch without any of the distance. And she set herself *through* the steel, and into the seam, and down.

The pale went into the forged curve like frost into a window. It stood there in the road, lit, and her side did not say anything at all.

She looked out at the hounds.

For about the space of a breath, nothing happened. All four of them stood where they were, heads pointed in.

Then the one on the west turned its head.

It did not come. It turned its head a little, the way a dog turns its head at a sound, and looked at the hook. And then all four of them moved at once. They broke from the stillness and went back to pacing, the whole ring of them, all at the same speed. They went round to the south, and round to the east, where the wagon was between Callie and them, and she could not see them any more.

"They've moved," said Ines. Her voice had changed. "They're going round."

"Where — "

"East. They're going east." Ines had turned on the crates, all the way round, with her bow. "*Still.* East. They've stopped on the east, all four, two hundred and ten."

Callie stood at the hook with her hand on the steel and the cold coming up her arm, and she understood.

They had not come at it. They had seen it, the brightest thing on the plain, standing on the west in the road. And they had gone round it. They had gone to the other side of the camp, as far from it as they could get, and stopped there, and started the sum again. And the other side of the camp was the east. The east was Otto alone with one arm and a pole, and Pell at the head of a lame horse. The east was the side of the wagon bed where her mother was sitting up against the crates with a skillet in her lap.

"It's not a lure," Callie said. She heard her own voice come out flat and very small. "It's a wall. They're going round it."

---

"The man in the Warren," said Ines. She was talking fast, which Ines never did. "The one with the ward, at the gate. The hounds never went at his gate. Not once, in all the years he stood in it. They went round to the fence. I'd forgotten. I'd forgotten it till now." She had an arrow on the string and her eyes on the east. "They don't go at the lit thing. They go at the side away from it."

"Turn," said Katori. "Everybody. East — "

"*No,*" said Callie.

Katori stopped.

"If we turn, they'll see us turn, and they'll do the sum again." Callie did not know how she knew it. She only knew it the way she had known on the causeway that the ash was the trap. "They're doing it now. They're still. They haven't gone. They're working out what's on the east. And if everybody's on the east, they'll come round again. And again. They'll go round and round till it's dark, and then we can't see them."

"Then what?"

Callie looked at the hook, standing pale in the seam under her hand. She looked east, across the wagon, at the place where the hounds were standing that she could not see.

*They go at the side away from it.*

"Then I'll be the wall," she said. "On the east."

---

She finished the pull.

She did it the small way, her weight coming up off her heel, and the pale went out of the hook, and it was only a hook in the road, the temperature of the evening. She did not pull it out of the seam. There was no time. She left it standing there, dark, and she went.

She ran.

It was not a run. It was the thing her body did instead, with two ribs gone on the left side and nothing in her lungs past halfway. She went along the west side of the wagon and round the tailboard, where her mother sat against the crates with the skillet, and her mother saw her go past and did not say anything. Every step went into her side like a nail. She could not breathe. She did not try to.

"*Still,*" said Ines, from the crates. "Still — they're still — "

Callie came round the tailboard onto the east side of the wagon.

She could see them now. Four long low shapes, standing in the grass at two hundred paces against the dark of the east, with their heads pointed in at the wagon. At her. At Otto, standing ten paces off with the pole. At the wagon bed behind her.

She did not drop. She could not drop. She stood beside the rear wheel with her hands at her sides and her feet on the road, and she put her back foot behind her, a little, and she pressed her heel straight down into the concrete.

The old-glass shine came over her. She saw it on the backs of her hands, and on the sleeves of her coat, grey and faint in the last of the light, like the surface of a window in the rain.

It did not hurt. She stood in it and breathed halfway and waited.

---

The hounds broke.

It happened all at once, the way the stillness had happened. One moment they were standing in the east with their heads pointed at her. The next moment they were not standing. They were moving, all four, and they were not coming in. They were going round again, south and west, round the tail of the wagon, away from the lit girl on the east side.

Round to the west, where the hook stood dark in the road, and Katori stood with her hands empty, and Ines was already turning on the crates with her bow.

"*Still!*" said Ines. "West — they've stopped — *west* — "

And then, before the word had finished, three of them came.

They did not make a sound. That was the thing Callie never forgot. There was no bark and no howl and no scrabble of feet. There were three long low shapes that had been standing still in the black grass on the west, and then they were running, flat out, all together, straight at the side of the camp they had chosen, and they could not stop now and they could not turn.

"*West!*" Callie screamed.

Everybody already knew. Nobody had turned.

---

Ines took the first at a hundred and forty.

Callie heard the bow go and saw nothing, because the wagon was between her and all of it. She heard Ines say "*Down,*" flat, and loose again, and say "*Down,*" again. She heard a sound out on the west like a sack falling off a cart.

She heard Katori.

There was no sound to Katori's Ember when it came out. There never was. But Callie felt it, through the whole wagon, the way she had felt it at the tank: the brightest thing on the plain, lit, all at once, on the west side of the road. And then there was a sound like a board coming off a nail, and another one right after it, close together, and then nothing.

"Two," said Katori's voice, from the west. It was not even out of breath. "Two. Ines?"

"One. Down. It's not getting up."

"Where's the fourth?"

Nobody answered.

Callie stood lit on the east side of the wagon with her hands at her sides, and looked for it, and did not see it. She looked south along the road, and east into the grass, and there was nothing there. The fourth had not come with the others. It had not committed. It had been in the ring and it had stopped when they stopped, and when the three had gone, it had not gone.

There's always one that doesn't, she thought. Katori had said it at the tank.

Then she saw it.

It was not on the east and it was not on the west. It was at the front. It had come round the whole camp, all the way, while everybody was looking at the three, and now it was coming in at the only place nobody was standing. It was coming along the road from the north, low and flat and silent, straight down the side of the wagon toward the traces, where Pell stood at the head of a lame horse. Past the horse was the wagon bed. And on the wagon bed was somebody sitting up against the crates, who could not run.

"*Front!*" Callie screamed. "Pell — *front* — "

---

She could not go.

That was the thing. She was standing in the Ward beside the rear wheel with her heel pressed into the road, and it was holding, and nothing in the world could have touched her. And she could not take one step in it. If she lifted her foot it would go off her like water off a plate, and she would be a girl with two ribs gone, running up the side of a wagon at a hound.

She took the step anyway. The Ward went off her. She went up the side of the wagon toward the front, and it was twenty-two feet, and it was the causeway again, twenty-two feet of wagon between her and her mother with a thing coming, and she was not going to be in time.

Otto swung the pole. He swung it one-handed across the front of the wagon at the height of a knee, the way he had swept the west shoulder of the causeway. The hound went under it. It did not even have to try.

It went past Pell. It went past Duchess's head and along her flank, low, toward the front of the wagon bed, where the crates were and the blanket and the woman with the skillet. It went past the mare's hindquarters in the traces.

And Duchess kicked it.

Callie saw it happen. She was ten feet away and she saw the whole of it, the way she had seen the hind leg come up at her on the causeway at the height of her chest. There was no warning. There never was. The near hind came up and went back, all four hundred kilos of her behind it, the same kick, the same leg. The leg she had kicked a wall with. The leg that had gone up the pitch.

It did not hit a wall this time.

It hit the hound in the side as it went past, and the hound went sideways off the road and into the rear wheel of the wagon with a sound Callie did not want to remember. It lay in the road beside the wheel, twisting, trying to get up, and it could not get up.

Then Katori was there. She had come round the front of the wagon from the west faster than Callie had ever seen anybody move, and the grey blade was in her hand, and she put it into the hound under the shoulder, once, short. It came apart all at once and from everywhere, against the spokes of the wheel.

Duchess screamed.

---

It was the worst sound Callie had heard her make, worse than the causeway. The mare stood in the traces with the near hind held up off the road, not touching it, not tipped on the toe, just held, and her whole body shaking, and her head up and her eyes showing white all the way round. Pell had both hands in her cheekpieces. He was talking to her. His voice had gone up at the end, just once, the way it had not gone up on the causeway, and then he had got it back down where he kept it.

"All right," he was saying. "All right, my girl. All right. That's done. That's done now. You did that. You did it."

Callie got to the tailboard.

Her mother was sitting up against the crates with her hat knocked crooked and the skillet held in her left hand, lifted, all of three inches off her lap. She was looking over the side of the bed at the place by the wheel where the hound had been, where there was grey ash lying in the road now, running down between the concrete slabs.

She looked at Callie.

"Well," she said. Her voice was perfectly steady and completely white. "That was the horse's turn."

Callie put her forehead down on the tailboard and stayed there.

---

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Ines came round the back of the wagon with her bow in her hand and stopped in front of Callie.

"You moved them," Ines said.

Callie lifted her head.

"You moved them. The hook went dark and you came round and lit up on the east, and they went back to the west. Where we were. Where nobody had to turn." Ines was looking at her the way she had looked at her at the bottom of the scarp, with a stone in her hand. "They didn't go at you. They went away from you. You put them where you wanted them."

"The fourth didn't go."

"No," said Ines. "The fourth never goes. There's always one." She looked at the ash lying by the wheel. "In the Warren, the fourth one killed a man I knew. It came in the side nobody was standing on, the same as tonight. Nobody had a horse." She was quiet for a moment. "I've been doing sums about hounds for eleven years, and I never once thought of standing on the side you didn't want them. I only ever thought of calling the side they'd chosen."

"I didn't think of it either," said Callie. "I thought of the other thing. The lure. And it was wrong."

"It was wrong," Ines agreed. "And you saw it was wrong in about three breaths, and you did something about it." She put an arrow back in the quiver. "That's the whole trade. Pell's always saying so."

---

When it was dark they lifted the ash.

There were four of them lying in the road and the grass, and every one of them was a Flask. Callie knew what that meant in a weighmaster's back room better than anybody on the wagon. A Flask was what nine husks made. It was what she would have lifted in a whole good month, before the causeway. There were four of them, silver in the starlight, going nowhere.

And every bit of it lying in the open was calling.

She went down on her knees at the first one, by the wheel. She got as far as putting her hand out over the ash, two fingers and the flat of the hand, and bending forward to skim it. Her left side said *no* so hard that she stayed there on her knees in the road with her hand out and could not move at all.

Otto knelt down beside her.

"Show me," he said.

She looked at him.

"You can't," said Otto. "And I can. And it's got to be done now, and it's got to be done right, and you're the only one who knows how to do it right." He had the steel pail from the windmill in his good hand, and a tin in his lap. "So show me."

So she showed him.

She knelt on the road beside him with her arms wrapped round her ribs and told him. Two fingers and the flat of the hand. Not across the seam, along it. Unhurried, the way you'd skim cream off milk. *Hurrying puts grit in it. Grit's the first thing a weighmaster looks for and the first thing he'll knock you down for.* Otto's hand was twice the size of hers and it was shaking, and the first time he did it he got grit in it, and she made him tip it out and start again.

The second time he did not get grit in it.

By the third Flask he was nearly as good as she was. He was slower. He did it with his tongue between his teeth. But the ash went into the tin clean and grey with that faint shine like something wet that wasn't, and when she tipped the tin to the starlight to look, there was nothing in it that shouldn't be.

"There," she said. "That's lifting. That's all it is."

Otto looked at the tin in his hand. "You did this every day," he said. "Since you were eleven."

"Most days."

"By yourself."

"There wasn't anybody else." Callie watched him screw the lid down. "There's somebody else now, I suppose."

Otto did not say anything. He went red in the dark. She could not see it, but she knew.

---

Pell wrote it in the book by lamplight on the tailboard, with the lamp turned very low. He took a long time over it. When he was finished he held it down where Callie could see it.

*Hounds, four, at dusk. C.'s hook on the west — they went round it. C. on the east — they went round her. Three came west.*
*K. two. I. one.*
*Duchess one.*

And under that, smaller:

*C.'s tenth of four Flasks. C. owes K. one finger. Duchess's near hind — I don't know.*

"You wrote the horse in," said Callie.

"I wrote down what happened." Pell closed the book. "She killed a hound. I've never written a horse into this book in eleven years, and I've never had one do anything worth the ink before." He looked across the dark at the mare. She stood on three legs at the end of the picket line, with the near hind held up off the ground, not touching it. She had not put it down since the kick. "I'll tell you something, and I'll only tell you once. She kicked you on the causeway. And she's been afraid of you ever since, because you were a wall and she didn't know what a wall was. And tonight she kicked the thing that was coming at your mother." He paused. "I don't know if that means anything. Horses don't mean things. But I'd like it written that she did it."

"It's written."

"It's written," Pell agreed.

He did not say what they would do in the morning with a horse that could not put her foot down. Nobody did. There was nothing to do about it in the dark. They could not move a mile, as Callie had wanted, to get away from where the ash had been. They could only sit where they were, dark, with four Flasks in tins on the wagon bed and a lame horse and the plain going away black in every direction, and wait for the light.

---

Her mother went off a little after midnight.

Callie was awake. She had been lying awake on the bed beside her, because she could not lie under the wagon with her ribs like this and because she had wanted to be near. She heard the breathing change. She was up on her elbow before she knew she was awake, the way she had been at the bottom of the scarp.

Her mother's eyes were half open. Her right hand was moving on the blanket, the small plucking motion, over and over, at a thread that was not there.

"I'm here," said Callie. "I'm here. I'm here."

She counted, because she always counted. Somebody else woke. She heard Katori sit up, and then Ines, and then nobody moved.

At sixty she said her mother's name.

At ninety her mother's left hand began to move as well, and Callie took it and held it, and it did not know she was holding it.

At a hundred and twenty the plucking had not stopped.

"Katori," said Callie. Her voice did something. "Katori, it's two minutes. It's longer than — it's two minutes — "

"I know," said Katori, very quietly, from the dark at the side of the wagon. She did not come nearer. There was nothing she could have done if she had. "Keep counting. Keep holding her. That's all there is."

Callie kept counting.

A hundred and forty. A hundred and forty-one. A hundred and forty-two.

Out on the plain to the north, low on the edge of the world, the light was still on. It sat there, steady and pale and four days away, lighting the bottom of the clouds, with a doctor inside it and a book at its gate. Callie looked at it over her mother's shoulder, and counted, and the plucking went on.
