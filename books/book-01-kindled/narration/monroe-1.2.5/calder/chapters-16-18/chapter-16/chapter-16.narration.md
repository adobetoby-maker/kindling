# Chapter 16 — Callie

The span was forty feet of nothing with rail iron laid over it.

Callie had told them about it on the first morning, standing in the road with Pell's atlas turned round toward her. *The deck's gone off the middle span and somebody laid rail iron across it.* She had said it as if it were a small thing. It had been a small thing in daylight, walking it with a barrow, when she could look down between the rails and see the old outlet channel at the bottom, dry and cracked and full of blown thistle, and know exactly how far down it was.

At night, from the tailgate of a wagon, it was a different animal.

The causeway simply stopped. The concrete deck ended in a broken lip with rusted rod hanging out of it like hair. Past the lip there were eight lengths of rail iron laid side by side, bolted down at either end, running out across the dark to where Katori's lamp could just find the far lip forty feet away. There were gaps between the rails a hand wide. Through the gaps there was nothing at all. The lamp went down into them and did not come back.

Duchess stood at the edge of it with her forefeet planted and her head up and her nostrils going. She was not screaming now. She was past that. She was simply not going to move, the way a post is not going to move, and Otto stood at her head stroking her neck with his good hand and saying, "It's all right, it's all right, it's all right," like a man bailing a boat with a cup.

Pell had got down off the board. He walked out onto the rail iron himself, three steps, and stamped on it, and it rang dully under his boot.

"The iron's fine," he said. "The iron'll take the wagon. It's taken wagons for eleven years, she says, and I believe her." He came back. "The iron's not the trouble. The trouble is that a horse puts her foot down on that and it goes into a hole, and a horse with her foot in a hole will break her own leg getting it out, and then I've no horse at all. She knows that. She knew it before any of us were born." He looked at the gaps. "She's right. That's the worst of it."

"Boards," said Katori.

"Boards," Pell agreed. "Lay a track for her feet. Crate lids and the bed boards, whatever's loose. Otto — "

"I've got one arm."

"Then use the one." Pell was already at the net, with his fingers working the knots. "And I'll want your coat for her eyes, because mine stinks of her already and she's had enough of it."

Callie stood twenty paces back in the dark and listened to it happen, and did not go up.

She could hear the husk before anybody else could.

---

It came up the west rock behind her, and it was not alone.

There were two of them. She knew it from the sound before she saw them, because two husks on riprap do not sound like one husk louder. They sound like two barrows tipping out of time with each other. One was on the west side and one was on the east, both coming straight up the slope at her, one a little ahead of the other.

She stood in the middle of the road and waited for the first.

It came over the west lip and she tipped it with the old curve, carefully, the way she had learned in the last hour, keeping the grey sweep up and out over its back. It went down. She turned for the second, fast, because the second was already at the lip on the east side, and the whole four feet of grey came round with her.

It went through the air a hand's breadth in front of Katori's face.

Callie had not heard her come. Katori had come back down the causeway with the lamp, quietly, to see what the second sound was. She had stopped six paces off, where she always stopped. And the new length of the hook had reached six paces, and it had gone past her nose, and she had not moved at all.

The husk came over the east lip. Callie took it the ugly way, with the grey, because it was the only part of the hook still pointed at it. The husk came apart all at once and its ash went across the deck.

Then it was quiet.

Katori stood with the lamp.

"Callie," she said. "Put that down."

"I can't."

"You're going to have to, before you take somebody's leg off." Katori did not raise her voice. "In about five minutes, Otto and Pell are going to lead a blind horse across forty feet of iron. Then the wagon goes over, with your mother on it. Then you go over, last, on your own. And every foot of that you'll be carrying a thing that goes through ash-wood like water, in the dark, with people in front of you. So it's going down. Now. And I'm going to help you."

"You said you didn't know how."

"I said I didn't know how to take off *that*." Katori tipped her head at the thing closed over Callie. "That's hand-tradition. That's a ward. I've never had one and I've never been inside one, and I'm not going to pretend. But that" — she looked at the hook — "that's mine. That's my door. I've put an Edge down ten thousand times. I can teach you to put an Edge down."

She set the lamp on the deck between them, so they could both see.

"Where's it coming from?" she said.

---

"The hook," said Callie.

"No. Think. Not where it *is*. Where's it coming *from*?"

Callie looked at the hook. The grey sweep came off the end of the old steel curve and went on, four feet of it, the colour of nothing. She could feel it the way she could feel her own tongue. She could feel it the way you feel a tooth you have just broken.

"I don't know what you're asking me."

"Yes, you do. You do it every day. You did it on the first morning, in front of Pell's book. You look at a bent thistle and tell me where a thing's been." Katori crouched down on the other side of the lamp, so their faces were level. "It's cold, isn't it? The hook."

"Yes."

"Where's it coldest?"

Callie shut her eyes.

She did it the way her grandfather had made her do it in a ditch when she was nine, with his hand on the back of her neck. *Stop trying to see the thing. See the grade.* She stopped looking at the grey part. She let her attention go along the steel the other way, backward, the way you walk a husk line backward to find out where it came from.

The grey part was cold. The old curve was colder. The socket, where the bolt went through, was colder still. And the cold did not stop at the socket. It came down the conduit into her hand, and up her wrist, and into her arm. That was the cold that had got as far as her shoulders now. It went up into her shoulder, and then it went *down*.

That was the direction. It was the same direction she could never explain, the way the whole thing had gone on the causeway at dusk when the horse kicked. It went down through her, deeper than a girl goes, to a place that had been there all her life.

It was not a door, exactly. It was more like the top of a well with the lid off. She was standing over it with her whole weight.

"Found it," she said. Her voice came out very small.

"Good. Don't look away." Katori's voice was close. "Now tell me what you're doing."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You are. You've been doing it since the causeway. Tell me what."

Callie stood over the well in the dark with her eyes shut, and looked at what she was doing, and saw it.

She was leaning.

She had been leaning since the moment she first put the hook over a husk's shoulder at the tailgate, with her back foot set and her weight going into the pull. It was the thing she did with every husk and every sack, the thing her body did without asking. She had leaned into the first one at the tailgate and she had never come back up. All this time, for two hours and more, she had been leaning into the well with her whole weight, and the well had been giving her everything she leaned for. That was the cold. It was going out of her as fast as she leaned.

"I'm leaning on it," she said.

Katori let out a breath. It was nearly a laugh.

"That's it," she said. "That's all an Edge is. You lean and it comes. You've been leaning on it for two hours. Now come up off it."

"How?"

"The way you come up off a sack. You've done it ten thousand times, just like me. You don't *drop* a sack. If you drop a sack it lands on your foot. You finish the pull, and you come back up, and you let the weight go off you." A pause. "Finish the pull, Callie."

Callie finished the pull.

She did it with her body first, because that was where it lived. Her hips came up and her back foot came off the brace and her weight settled back over her own heels, the way it did at the end of every sack on every rail she had ever swung one from. And somewhere down in the dark, over the top of the well, she came up off it.

There was no sound and there was no light.

The hook got heavy. That was all it was. It was only four feet of steel conduit and a foot of forged curve, and it weighed exactly what it had weighed every day of her life, and it hung off the end of her arms and pulled her forward a step.

The grey part was gone. She had not seen it go.

She opened her eyes. The hook was her grandfather's hook. It had the tape on the cross and the wing nut she had to watch, and there was a dull black smear of husk down the inside of the curve.

It was not cold any more. It was just the temperature of the night.

Callie sat down on the road. She did not decide to. Her legs decided, and she let them. She sat in the middle of the causeway with the hook across her knees, and she put one cold hand on the steel and felt nothing coming up out of it at all, and she did not know what she felt about that. She had not asked for it. She had not wanted it. It had been hers for two hours, the way her own tongue was hers, and now it was gone, and it felt like putting her hand in a pocket and finding a hole where something used to be.

"It's all right," said Katori.

"I know."

"It hasn't gone anywhere. You came up off it. It's still there, if you lean again."

"I know," Callie said, and she didn't, and they both knew she didn't. She looked up. "The other one's still on."

"Yes."

"I tried it the same way. Just now, when I came up. I tried to come up off both."

"And?"

"I'm not leaning on that one." She could feel it very clearly now that the Edge was gone. Its weight was all around her, closed over her from boots to crown, and she could feel how different it was. "I'm not leaning on it at all. It's leaning on me."

Katori looked at her across the lamp for a long moment. Then she picked the lamp up and stood.

"Then that's tomorrow's problem," she said. "Tonight's problem has a blind horse in it."

---

They got Duchess across with Otto's coat buttoned round her head and Pell walking backward in front of her on the boards. He talked to her the whole way, as he always did, one slow word to each step. Otto walked beside her shoulder with his good hand on her neck. She put each foot down as if the whole world might be a hole. Twice she stopped altogether in the middle of the span, with forty feet of dark under her and nobody on either side of her, and twice Pell waited until she went on, and did not hurry her, and did not stop talking.

Callie watched from the south lip, twenty paces back, with the ordinary hook. She did not breathe much.

The wagon went over after the horse, with Ines at the brake. The rails groaned under the wheels, a long, low iron complaint that went down into the dark and came back up. They flexed, visibly, in the lamplight. Callie watched the near wheel roll along the third rail from the edge with a hand of daylight between the tyre and the fourth. Her mother lay on the bedding at the back with her eyes open, looking up at the sky, and she did not once look down.

Then it was only Callie, on the south lip, alone.

Katori stood at the far end with the lamp held out over the rails. "Walk the middle," she called. "Don't look at the gaps."

"I'm not going to fall," said Callie. "I can't fall. Nothing can touch me."

She did not know whether that was true. She had a feeling, halfway across, with her boots on the rail iron and the dark going down forever through the gaps on both sides, that a fall might be the one thing that could touch her. The ground at the bottom of a fall doesn't come at you. You come at it. She didn't know which way round the thing on her would count that.

She did not look at the gaps. She walked the middle. She came off the far end onto the broken concrete, and Katori put a hand out and took her by the arm to steady her. Katori's hand stopped a finger's width off her sleeve, and closed on nothing, and Katori looked down at it.

"Mm," said Katori, and took the hand away.

---

The north end of the causeway came up out of the dark about an hour before midnight. It was a long slope of broken concrete, and then gravel, and then road, and then the flats were behind them.

There was a building. Callie had known it would be there, because she had walked past it on the way south in the spring, and she said so. It was a square concrete box beside the road at the head of the causeway. It had been some kind of pump house or keeper's house in the years when the reservoir was a reservoir. Its roof was long gone and its windows were holes, but its four walls stood, and it had one doorway and only one.

"There," said Callie. "One way in. The walls are too high for them to come over, and they won't try, they'll come round to the door. So we sit inside. And I sit in the door."

Pell looked at her from the board.

"You'll sit in the door," he said.

"They're coming at me anyway." She was too tired to be careful how she said it. "Whatever I do. They'll come round to wherever I am. So I'm in the door, and they come to the door, one at a time, and everybody else is behind the wall asleep."

"Everybody else," said Pell, "is going to be behind the wall, awake, listening to you do that."

"Then everybody else can listen."

Pell looked at Katori, and Katori looked at Callie, and after a moment Katori nodded once.

"She's right," Katori said. "Unhitch."

---

They got Otto's arm open in the lamplight at about midnight.

Callie saw it from the doorway. She sat on the sill with her back against the concrete jamb and the hook across her knees, where she could see out into the dark and back into the room at the same time. What she saw of the arm, she put away quickly. It was open from a hand below the elbow nearly to the wrist, where the edge of a crate lid had gone into it and been dragged. The scarf had stopped the worst of it. The scarf had not stopped all of it.

Ines had the kit out, a flat tin box with a curved needle and a card of thread, which she clearly knew how to use for a dress and clearly did not know how to use for this. She sat looking at the needle for long enough that everybody in the room began to notice.

"I'll do it," said Callie's mother.

She was propped against the back wall on the bedding they had carried in, with a blanket round her. Nobody had heard her wake up. Her right hand was lying in her lap, very still, and her left hand was out, palm up, toward Ines.

"You can't," said Ines. It was not unkind. It was a range, said out loud.

"I can't *sew*," Callie's mother agreed. "My right hand has stopped taking instruction from me, which I've been meaning to discuss with it. But I've stitched more men off the elevator floor at Thistle Ford than you've had hot dinners, and I can see, and I can talk. So you'll be my hands, and I'll be the rest of it. Bring him here. Bring the lamp. Otto, sit down on the floor where I can reach you, and give me the arm, and don't look at it."

Otto sat down. He did not look at it. He looked at the ceiling that was not there, at the stars.

Callie's mother took the lamp in her left hand and held it up and looked at the arm the way Callie looked at a ditch. It took a long time. Nobody said anything.

"Clean it first," she said. "All of it. The whole length, from the top, with the water we boiled, and don't be gentle. Gentle leaves grit and grit kills you slower. Then we'll start at the elbow end, because that's the deep end, and we'll work down."

Ines cleaned it. Otto made a noise and then made himself stop making it.

"Now the needle. In a finger's width back from the edge. No. *Smaller.* You're sewing a man, not a sack of meal. Closer together. There. Tie it off. Do that again."

Ines did it again. Her hands, which could put an arrow into a hound at ninety paces in bad light, were not quite steady, and Callie's mother saw it, and did not say anything about it. She just held the lamp a little closer.

"Tell me something, Otto," she said. "Anything. Where were you born?"

"The Warren."

"What's it like?"

Otto swallowed. "Wet," he said. "Very wet. It rains four days in five. Everything's green, even the walls. There's a kind of snail that gets in the bread."

"That's a terrible place."

"It's all right," said Otto, and his voice wobbled. "You get used to the snails."

"You'd have to," said Callie's mother. "Ines. Smaller. Good. Otto, what's in the buildings in the Warren? Callie tells me you're a great one for buildings."

Callie, in the doorway, heard her own name, and looked away out into the dark, where a husk was coming. She could hear it on the gravel a long way off, grinding toward her in no hurry at all.

She stood up and went and met it in the road, twenty paces from the door. She tipped it into the ditch and it did not get up. It was the eighteenth. She came back and sat down on the sill again.

Behind her, her mother was saying, "Twenty-two. That's twenty-two. You've got a good arm, Otto, it's going to be an ugly arm, but it's going to be an arm," and Otto was laughing and crying at the same time and trying to do neither.

---

It was Pell who brought her the water.

He came to the doorway with a tin cup and a heel of bread and a strip of the dried meat they had been saving since the Warren. He stood them on the sill beside her and did not say anything, which was his way of saying something. Callie realized, looking at the bread, that she had not stopped being hungry since the ninth husk on the causeway. The hunger had got so big and so constant that she'd stopped noticing it was hunger. It had turned into the general shape of the world.

She picked up the bread. She could hold it. She had known she could, because she had been holding the hook all night. It felt like holding bread in a heavy pair of leather gloves, the way you held a hot pan. She could feel that it was bread-shaped and roughly bread-weight and nothing else at all.

She lifted it toward her mouth.

It stopped a finger's width off her lips.

She tried again, slower, as if it were a matter of care. The bread stopped where it had stopped before. She could not put it any closer. The thing closed over her hand met the thing closed over her face. They touched each other, and neither of them would give.

She put the bread down.

She picked up the cup and tipped it, very gently, toward her mouth. The water ran off the air in front of her lips and down her chin, never touching it, and pooled on the sill. It went dark on the concrete. She watched it.

Pell had not moved.

"Well," he said, very quietly. "There's a thing."

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You've been lit for five hours, and it's eating you, and you can't eat." He said it like a distance. "Katori."

Katori came.

She looked at the water on the sill and the bread on the sill and Callie's face, and she did not say anything for a while. Callie could see her doing arithmetic. She could see that she didn't like the answer.

"How long," said Callie, "can a person go without water?"

"Don't," said Katori.

"I want to know. I want the number. I'm not asking to be frightened. I'm asking to know."

Katori looked at her.

"Three days," she said. "Maybe. For somebody sitting still in the shade. You're not sitting still, and whatever that is, it's taking out of you what a day's walking would take, every hour." She crouched in the doorway. "But you're not going to get to three days, because you're going to get it off. You found the first one tonight. You'll find this."

"You don't know that."

"No," Katori agreed. "I don't. I'm saying it anyway, because it's true that you found the first one, and I'd rather you heard that from me than worked it out in the dark on your own." She stood. "Sit in your door. I'll sit up with you."

---

Near two in the morning her mother called her.

Callie had thought she was asleep. The room had gone quiet behind her, the way rooms go quiet when everyone in them has finally stopped trying to talk. Pell was a long shape under a blanket along the east wall. Otto was sitting up asleep with his arm across his chest. Ines lay flat on her back with her bow beside her and her eyes shut, which didn't mean anything. Katori was on the other side of the doorway with the lamp turned down almost to nothing.

"Callie," said her mother, very softly. "Come here a minute."

Callie looked at Katori. Katori moved her chin. *Go on. I've got it.*

She went. She knelt down on the bedding beside her mother, carefully, with the hook laid on the concrete behind her where she could feel it. In the last of the lamp her mother's face was grey and tired and entirely awake.

"I don't like the doorway," her mother said. "I want that said."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it for the record. Pell isn't the only one who keeps a book." She was quiet for a moment. "You were very good tonight."

"I wasn't. I went down the rock after the hook. I nearly — "

"I was there. I saw what you did." Her mother's voice was very even. "You were very good. I'm saying that for the record as well, because nobody else is going to say it tonight, because they're all too frightened of you to say it."

"They're not frightened of me."

"The horse is frightened of you. Otto isn't, because Otto isn't frightened of anything, which is going to be a problem for him one day. The rest of them are being very careful." She moved. "Come here."

Her mother lifted her left hand, slowly, and put it out toward Callie's face.

Callie knew before it got there. She knew and she didn't move, because she wanted to be wrong. The hand came toward her cheek the way it had come toward her cheek every night of her life that she could remember. It was the same hand that had braided her hair when she was small, the hand that had stood behind her in a ditch, the hand that had closed round her wrist on the tailgate three nights ago and held on, hard, for about three seconds.

It stopped a finger's width off her face.

Her mother's fingers pressed against nothing. Callie could see the tips of them go pale where they pressed. She could see her mother push, very gently, and then a little harder. She felt nothing at all. There was no warmth and no touch, and no dry rough skin at the tip of the thumb. There was only a pressure, very faint and very far away, like the wind on a house.

Her mother kept her hand there. She didn't take it away. She held it against the air in front of her daughter's face for a long time, and her eyes did not leave Callie's, and her face did not do anything at all.

"Well," she said at last, very quietly. "We'll just have to see about that."

Out in the dark, on the gravel, another one was coming.
