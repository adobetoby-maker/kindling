# Chapter 46 — Toren

The man on the ground did not say anything for a while, and the reeds burned, and forty men stood in the dark down the bank and did not come forward.

"Tull," he said, at last.

"I know what you're called."

"Then you know it's on a table at Barrow Gate and it's been on a table somewhere for nineteen years, and I've never once hidden it, and a man who's been looking for me for eleven years has been looking for me poorly." He lay still under the knee on his chest. He did not thrash, and he did not beg, and he spoke like a man conducting the last piece of business in a long day. "Homura. That's the valley up north with the wire round it."

"Yes."

"That was a contract and it was a bad one and I was paid for it, and I'm not going to tell you it wasn't me, because you've got me on a road and you'd not believe me and I'd only be dying with a lie in my mouth." He moved his head slightly. "I don't remember you. I want that said. You're in front of me with a face like the end of the world and I'd not know you from a gatepost, and I've had eleven years of my life since then, and that's the truth of it and you can do what you like with it."

Toren, ten feet away on the brick, with the heat coming off the water and his hands shaking, watched Rook take that in.

"The gate," said Rook.

"We took the gate."

"Say why."

"So nobody'd get out," said Tull. "That's the job. You take the gate first and then you've got all night." He said it without any relish and without any apology, in the voice of a man describing how a wheel is shod. "It's not a thing I'd argue about. It's the trade. They pay for it because nobody else will do it."

"And the papers."

"There were boxes came out and went on a cart and I never saw the inside of one," said Tull. "I was on the gate. The man who took the cart isn't in this company, he wasn't in this company then, and he was not one of ours, and that is the single most valuable thing I have got and you can have it for nothing because it won't do you any good. I don't know his name. I never did. I got told where to stand."

Rook was quiet.

"Nine," he said.

"What?"

"There were eleven of us in the long room and a girl held the door and got nine of us past you, and you stood outside it and counted, and you got to nine, and then you went round the back." Rook's voice had not risen once. "I was the fourth. You looked at me. You looked at every single one of us, because you were counting, and you have looked me in the face from four feet and you do not know me from a gatepost."

Tull lay there.

"No," he said. "I don't."

---

Down the bank somebody shifted, and Rook's head came round about an inch, and everything down the bank stopped shifting.

"They won't come," Tull said. "Not while you've got me. Don't flatter yourself about it — they're not fond of me. It's that Hoyle's got the paybook off a man in the yard and he'll not open it while I'm alive, and none of them get a coin out of this month's work if I go under a boy's cart on a sluice. That's the whole of what's keeping forty men still and you should know what it's worth, because it stops being worth it the instant I stop breathing."

"I know exactly what it's worth."

"Then get on with it."

Rook did not move.

"There's one more," he said. "And then I'll get on with it.

"Nine years ago, in the autumn, a company took a defence contract at a village four hundred miles south of here on the edge of a fracture. Three lines out past a mill on the second night. The north line."

Something went across the man's face, and Toren saw it, and it was not guilt.

It was irritation.

"Lowry Bend," said Tull.

"Yes."

"I was docked a year for Lowry Bend." He let his head go back against the brick. "Eleven of theirs came up a lane in the dark and my line was told there was nothing of theirs west of the mill and there was, and we put it into them, and I had a man of my own in that lane too and he went down with the rest and nobody's ever docked anybody for him. They paid the village and they wrote a letter and they put me off the books for four months and then they put me back on, because you don't get men like me out of a hedge. I've been paying for that lane ever since in one way or another and I'll tell you what it's cost me—"

"Eleven of theirs," said Rook.

"What?"

"You said eleven of theirs. Say the rest of it. Say what they were."

"How should I know what they were?" said Tull, and he said it with a first real edge of temper, a man pushed past the point of being reasonable about a long-settled grievance. "It was dark and it was a lane and there were people in it who'd been told to stay behind a wall. I've had eleven hundred nights since and four of them were worse."

"My mother," said Rook. "And Arn. And Tobin."

The reeds crackled two hundred yards out.

"Ah," said Tull.

And then, after a moment, in an entirely different voice, one that was tired and flat and not performing anything at all:

"Well. There it is, then. I'd wondered what it was. Six years of a man buying our brass in every wall on the coast." He looked up. "Go on. I'd rather it was for the lane than for the valley, if it's all the same to you. The valley was work. The lane was me being told a wrong thing and believing it, which is what a man's actually made of."

---

The knife came out of Rook's coat and went in under the man's jaw.

Toren saw it happen. He saw the blade go in against the skin and stop, and he saw the line of blood come out from under it and run down into the man's collar, and he saw Tull's eyes close, and the whole of it took about a second.

Then Dessa's voice came across the arch.

She was two hundred feet away, on the north side, and she pitched it the way she had been taught to pitch a call and it came over the water and the burning and the whole of that bank as clearly as if she had been standing at his elbow.

"*Rook! North side, six of them, they're on the bank, they're thirty feet from the cart—*"

And then, high and cracking, from Wyck: "*On me! Dessa, on me, get her behind me—*"

Rook did not move for one second.

Toren was ten feet away and he was watching the man's hands and not his face, which is the only reason he can say what happened, and he has said it the same way every time he has ever been asked, which has been many times, because he was the only one who saw it.

The knife did not come out. It went in a little further. There was a second, one whole second, in which Toren was entirely certain he was about to watch a man die on a road, and in which the man on the ground was entirely certain of it too, and had shut his eyes and stopped talking and gone still in the way that people do.

Then Rook took the knife away.

He stood up off him. He did it slowly, because of his side, and he stood over the man with the knife hanging in his hand and his chest going, and he said, "Get up."

"What?"

"*Get up,*" said Rook, "and walk in front of me, and if any man of yours comes forward before I'm across that arch I will put this through your kidney and neither of us will care by then whose fault it was."

---

They went back up the road with Tull in front, and Toren went ahead of both of them because he had been told to in three words, and the south bank came up the slope behind them at a walking pace, the whole of it, forty men, close enough to hear their boots and not one step closer than that.

At the south end of the arch Rook put his hand flat between the man's shoulder blades and stopped him there.

"Hoyle," he said, to the dark.

"Here."

"He's yours. I'm not taking him and I'm not trading him and there's nothing in this for anybody. He walks to you from here and I cross, and that's the end of my part of tonight."

"You had him," said Hoyle's voice, out of the dark, and it was not a question, and there was something in it that Toren did not understand until he was much older. "I watched you have him."

"Yes."

"For a good while."

"Yes," said Rook. "Take him."

He took his hand off Tull's back and the man walked away down the slope into the dark with his collar black and did not look round, and Rook turned and went out onto the arch.

---

He got to the middle of it and stopped, and turned round, and put both hands out at the height of his chest with the stone shut up in the left one.

Toren was six feet behind him, on the north side, and had not been told to go on, and did not.

Nothing happened for about two seconds.

Then the air across the neck of the arch went the way air goes over a fire, a sort of leaning, and something came up between his hands and stood there — not a wall, not a light, nothing you could point at and describe to somebody who had not seen it. A twelve-foot span of brick with something across it. It was the thing he had watched a man do in a road cut on the first day of everything, and again on a bridge in the fog, and it was the thing that same man had told them all four nights ago on a dark verge that he could not do this week, in advance, in daylight, so that nobody would be carrying a picture of it.

"*Now,*" Rook said, through his teeth, "north side, go — Toren, *go*, I've got this end—"

Toren ran.

He ran the two hundred feet of road bed north with the cart coming up out of the dark in front of him and Dessa's voice going steadily over the top of everything — *two at the wheel, one behind you Wyck, he's on your left and he's low* — and Wyck Tanner standing across the neck of the road in front of a cart with a dead boy in it, with the bar in both hands and his left arm bent the wrong way at the wrist, hitting men.

There were six and then there were four.

Toren came into them from behind at a dead run with the spike coming out of his fist on the way, and he did not hesitate, and there was no part of it he thought about, and he put it into the first man's back below the shoulder blade and got it out again and turned, and the second man dropped a hooked pole and put his hands up and shouted something and Toren nearly did it anyway and did not, and the man went down the bank into the reeds on his backside and was gone.

The other two went after him.

It was over in less time than it takes to walk across a room, and Toren stood in the middle of the road bed with his hands shaking and the whole of his arm to the shoulder lit up, and turned round, and saw the arch.

There was a man on the sluice wall.

He was coming along the stone from the south side — the same forty feet of wall that a boy with a light had walked out along an hour before — and he had got to the point where the wall met the brick, and he was five feet from Rook's back with a spear in both hands, and Rook was standing in the middle of the arch with both hands out in front of him holding a thing across it.

Toren screamed.

Rook did not turn round and did not lift his hands.

The spear went into him high in the back, through the meat above the shoulder blade on the left side, hard enough to move him six inches forward on his feet, and he made a sound Toren had never heard out of him and did not lift his hands, and the thing across the arch did not go out.

Toren got there.

He does not remember the two hundred feet. He remembers arriving, and the man on the wall turning with the spear half out, and doing what he did, and the man going off the sluice wall into the channel, and the water taking him under the arch and out.

And Rook standing in the middle of the brick with a hole through his back and his hands out, holding a twelve-foot span of nothing against forty men, for a minute, and another minute, and another.

He held it until the light came up grey behind the burning reeds and the water on the east side went from black to pewter.

Then the south bank went away.

They did it in order, the way they had come off the road the morning before: the wounded first, and two of them walking backward, and a voice down there counting people into the boats; and the last thing Toren saw of Cinder Company on the Level was a big man in a good coat standing on the bank at the bottom of the slope with his hands at his sides, looking up at the arch for about four seconds before he turned round and followed them into the reeds.

Rook let his hands down.

The thing across the arch did not fade or dim or anything of the kind. It simply was not there any more, and the arch was a piece of wet brick with the morning coming up on it, and the man standing in the middle of it went down on one knee and then onto both, and Toren got under his arm and could not hold him, and the pair of them sat down on the crown of the arch together in a way that had nothing dignified about it at all.

---

"Right," said Rook, after a while.

His voice had gone to almost nothing.

"Before anybody says anything to me, I'm going to say the account, because if I don't say it now I'll start arranging it, and in about a day I'll have arranged it into something that flatters me, and I've watched better men than me do exactly that.

"I had him on the ground under my knee. He was not armed, he was not getting up, and there was no power in it anywhere — I had a knife in my hand and a man under it and all I had to do was lean. That's all it was. It was leaning on a knife and it was two seconds and there was nothing in the world stopping me, and I want that said in front of the boy because he saw it and I'd rather he heard me say it than carried it round wondering whether I knew.

"And what I had left after that was about enough for one thing, and the one thing was either the last two seconds of him or twelve feet of arch, and I have spent eleven years telling myself I'd know which way I'd go and I did not know. I was not certain until my hands moved."

Dessa had come along the road bed on the stick. She stopped about six feet away and stood there and did not say anything.

"So here's the bill," said Rook, "and it's all due this morning.

"Tull's alive and he's in a boat and by tonight he'll be forty miles from here, and this company will break up and re-form under another name at another wall before the next moon, because that is precisely what they do, and I have been in the same country as them three times in six years and I am never going to get a fourth. That's finished. I want that understood as a fact and not as something anybody needs to be kind to me about: it is over, and I did it, and I did it on purpose.

"Second. I'm no use. I've got a rasp hole under my ribs and a spear hole through my back and I'm going to be carried on that cart for a week, on a road where there is no carrying to spare, and the four of you are going to have to do the whole of it.

"Third." He stopped, and looked up the road bed to where the cart stood with the tail down. "Third is hers, and it's the one that costs, and I'm going to say it to her face rather than behind the wheel.

"Senna. I gave you four days out of a flask five nights ago and I've spent the whole of tonight, and I have not got one thing left to put into you this morning or tomorrow morning either. Those four days are gone. They went into twelve feet of brick. That's what they bought and I'd like you to know the price of the thing you're sitting in."

There was a silence.

"I should think so," said Senna Voss, from the cart, in a voice like a dry stick breaking. "I'd have been very annoyed to be carried over an arch by a lot of children and then wasted."

---

Toren said, "Why?"

He had not meant to say it out loud and it came out of him more or less sideways, and everybody looked at him.

"Say the whole question," said Rook.

"You've been — " His voice went. He got it back. "Eleven years. And Vera, and your mum, and your brothers, and you *had* him, you had him on the ground and he *said* it, he said it about the lane like it was a thing that had happened to *him*, and you let him get up and walk away and you gave him to Hoyle like a — like a *parcel* — "

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

Rook sat on the brick with his back against nothing and the morning coming up on the water, and took a while about it.

"Because there were four of you on the other side of that arch," he said, "and one of you was already dead.

"That's the whole answer and it's a poor one and it's the only one there is. I'd like to give you a better one. I'd like to tell you that I stood on that road and thought about Homura and about what killing him would and wouldn't put back, and that I chose the long way of answering for it, the way I stood up at a fire six nights ago and told all of you I had. That would be a very good answer and I have been assembling it in my head for the last quarter of an hour and it is not true.

"What actually happened is that a girl shouted a number over a burning reed bed and my hands moved before I did, and the thing I found out about myself on that road is that I have been four people for six years and I do not get to choose which one of them answers when somebody calls."

He shut his eyes.

"Don't ask me today whether I'd do it again," he said. "Ask me in about a year. I'll answer it then, and you'll be old enough to hear whatever it turns out to be."
