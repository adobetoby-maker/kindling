# Chapter 7 — Jab

The bad hour came at the bottom of the night and it brought a friend.

Jab knew the shape of the bad hour by now. It started with Tonk going still and shallow, and then the shivering that was not cold, and then a stretch of forty or fifty minutes when there was nothing anybody could do except be there and not flinch, and then it let go all at once and Tonk slept and Jab sat with his hands shaking.

This one did not let go.

It ran its forty minutes and then it kept going, and the bark had been in him since dusk and was doing nothing that Jab could see, and at some point past the middle of the night Tonk stopped saying words and only made a sound on the out-breath, over and over, low, the same note.

Jab put his hand on his brother's chest and felt the heat coming up through the blanket into his palm like a stone left in a fire, and he understood, quite calmly, that he had come to the end of what he had.

He had a fire. He had cold cloths and a skin of water and a dwindling fold of birch paper with seventeen twists in it. He had six weeks of a healer's instructions memorized down to the order she had said them in. He had two hands that could lash, split, cut, build, snare, and carry, and not one of those was a thing this night wanted.

And he had a piece of river metal at his throat that could cut through anything in the fir stand and could not touch what was in his brother's leg.

"All right," he said.

He took his hand off Tonk's chest and stood up and went two steps out from the shelter into the dark, and reached for it, and brought the blade out.

It came the way it always came, easy as a breath, that fat-bellied ugly working shape sitting on his fist like a tool waiting to be told a job. He kindled the light. It ran up from the grip, stuck at the belly, guttered, caught, went on. Thin amber, steady, useless.

He stood in the wet fir needles holding it out in front of him, and there was nothing to point it at. There was no thing. There was heat in a leg and a dark line going up the inside of a bone, and he could have cut down every tree on this slope and it would not have moved that line by the width of a hair.

For about three seconds Jab considered doing it anyway — putting the blade into something, anything, just to have done a thing in the world.

Instead he put it out and let the metal come back and lie against his sternum where it belonged, and went and knelt by his brother again, and said, out loud, in the flat voice he used for counting fish:

"Then it's the other one."

---

He had a theory. He had been building it for three nights without letting himself look at it directly, the way you do with a thought you suspect of being wishful.

It went like this. The warmth under his breastbone and the warmth in his hand had never been two warmths — that was the first thing anybody learned, that was the whole of the Kindling, that was the one fact about all of this that everyone in the valley agreed on. One warmth in two places. It went out of his chest and down his arm and into the metal and the metal did what it was told.

So it moved. It moved from one place to another place, through him, and it came out at his hand.

And for three nights, with his hand on his brother's chest, something in that place under his breastbone had leaned.

Jab laid his right palm flat on Tonk's sternum, over the wet shirt, and shut his eyes, and pushed.

He did it the way he did it for the blade. That was all he had to go on. He found the small low warmth and he opened it toward his arm the way you open a hand, and he sent it down the inside of the elbow and through the wrist and out into the palm, and he held it there and pressed it at his brother like a man leaning on a door.

Nothing happened.

He did it again, harder. Nothing happened, harder.

He changed things. He tried both hands. He tried a hand on the chest and a hand on the leg, above the wrapping, where the heat was worst and where the skin was so tight it felt like something about to split. He tried breathing out while he pushed, the way his father's aunt had made him breathe out on the gravel bar. He tried saying it, which he was glad nobody heard. He tried holding the metal in his other fist so that it would be in the circuit, which felt clever for about four seconds and then felt like a boy shaking a bucket to make it rain.

He pushed for a long time.

And it did cost. That was the part he had not expected, because nothing had happened and he could not see how nothing could be expensive. But somewhere in the middle of it a coldness started up behind his eyes, and his hands went heavy, and the small warmth under his breastbone thinned out like a fire with too much on it — and he knew that feeling, he had known it since he was fourteen and had tried to hold the blade for a whole afternoon on the jam to prove he could. That was the bottom of it. That was what it felt like when a person spent what they had.

He kept going past it anyway, because his brother was making that sound.

The next part he did not experience as a decision. The fire, which he had banked at the start of the night to go till morning, burned down through its good wood and into its poor wood and began to smoke. The cold came in under the shelter's low side. He knew both of those things were happening, in a distant way, the way you know it is raining while you are asleep, and he stayed where he was with his hand on his brother's chest, leaning on a door that was not a door.

Then Tonk coughed and the cough went wrong.

He had been lying too flat, and his head had gone back, and the cough caught something and Tonk made a sound like a small animal and then made no sound at all.

Jab was two arm's-lengths from him and it took him three heartbeats to get there.

He had never in his life had that happen. He had always simply been where he needed to be, fast, without the between part; his body had done that for him since he was nine years old on a slick bar with fish coming in. This time he came up off his knees and the world went sideways and gray, and he had to put a hand down on the ground and wait for it, three whole beats, listening to his brother not breathe.

Then he got there and got him up on his side and got the flat of his hand between the shoulder blades, and Tonk whooped in a breath and coughed it back out and began to cry — a real four-year-old's crying, frightened and furious, the good kind, the kind that meant air.

"I've got you. I've got you. Cough it out. Good. Again."

Tonk coughed and cried and was sick, a little, down the front of Jab's shirt, and cried harder about that than about any of it.

"That's all right. That's nothing. That's a shirt."

"I don't *like* it —"

"I know."

"I want to go *home* —"

"I know," Jab said, and held his brother's head against his collarbone with one hand and reached out with the other and dragged the poor smoking wood off the fire's heart and put a dry split of spruce on, because he had let the fire go and it was cold in here, and he could feel his own hands shaking hard enough to be a separate event from the shaking he was holding.

He got the fire back. He got Tonk propped up on the rolled hide, half sitting, which was where he should have been for the last two hours, and wiped his face and gave him water in small sips, and put his own coat over his shirt so that his brother did not have to look at what was on it.

Then he sat down against the shelter post because his legs had made it clear that was where he was going.

Best part of an hour, he thought. He had spent the best part of an hour of the worst night they had had, pushing at a door, while the fire went down and his brother lay flat.

"Never again," he said.

He meant it about as much as a man means anything at that hour. But he made himself say the whole of it out loud, to fix it: "You don't get to try things on him. You try them on you, or you don't try them."

---

Here was the thing he could not put down, though, and it was the reason he did not sleep in the hour he had left.

Sometime in the middle of the pushing — before the cough, before the fire went — he had known that it was getting worse.

Not guessed. Not reasoned it out from the sound of the breathing. He had been kneeling with his eyes shut and his hand on his brother's chest and he had *known*, the way you know a person has walked into the room behind you, that the thing in Tonk was going up. He had opened his eyes and put the back of his hand to Tonk's neck and the skin had been drier and hotter than it had been ten minutes before, exactly as he had known it would be.

He had noticed it at the time the way you notice a bird while you are arguing with somebody.

Now, with his brother asleep sitting up and the fire honest again and his own hands finally still, Jab sat in the dark and looked at it.

"All right," he said. "Test it."

He shut his eyes. He took his hand off. He sat with both hands on his own knees, three feet away, and asked himself the question — *up or down* — and waited to see whether anything answered.

Something answered. It was not a voice and it was not a picture. It was closer to the feeling of a slope under your feet in the dark: a sense of which way the ground went.

Down, it said. Going down.

He opened his eyes and put the back of his hand against Tonk's neck. Cooler. Damp, now, not dry. Going down.

He waited twenty minutes and did it again. Down, it said, and it was down.

He did it a third time an hour later, out of the shallow sleep he had fallen into against the post, and it said *down*, and he checked, and it was up — not much, but up, and his brother's breathing had got that catch in it again at the top.

Wrong. He sat with that. He had been right twice and wrong once, and a person who had been right twice and wrong once knew nothing at all yet; he had watched his aunt's daughter guess the weather for a whole summer at better odds than that.

But it was not nothing either, because when he had been wrong, he had been wrong in a particular direction. He had wanted it to be going down. He had been tired and frightened and he had wanted it, and the thing had told him what he wanted.

"So don't ask it when you want an answer," he said. "Ask it when you don't care."

That was a rule he could actually use, and it was the first useful thing he had got out of the entire night.

He used it before dawn.

He was not thinking about the fever at all. He was breaking the shelter down in the dark, working by feel, coiling the hide cords, and he came past his brother to lift him, and as he got his arms in under him something in the middle of Jab's chest said, quite plainly and with no encouragement at all: *here it comes*.

He did not argue with it. He set Tonk down again, and turned him on his side, and had the cloth wet and ready and the water skin in his lap before anything happened at all.

Ninety seconds later Tonk went rigid and made the sound, and the bad wave came through him, and Jab was already there with his hand where it needed to be — not beginning to get there, not reaching, already there — and it went through and it went past, and it was over faster than any of them had been in a week.

Faster because he had been ready. Not because he had done anything to it. He knew the difference and he made himself keep knowing it.

He sat back on his heels in the dark, and put his hand flat on his own breastbone, over the metal, over the small stubborn warmth that had been there for four years and had never once done anything he had not asked it to.

"What are you," he said.

It did not tell him. It sat there being warm, the way it had while he split fish and cut willow and hauled logs and lit a blade over a log jam, the way it had been while his parents went down the river in the dark and he lay on a gravel slope with his brother's blood on his hands and could not do one single thing about any of it.

He got up. There were miles in the day and the first of them needed light.

---

They went on at dawn with fifteen twists of bark and thirteen fish, into a morning that had turned mean.

The sleet had stopped in the night and left the fir stand glazed and dripping, and the temperature had dropped through the bottom of the autumn into the front edge of the next thing. Every needle had a bead of ice on it. The moss cracked underfoot. Jab's hands went dead in the first half hour and stayed that way, which made every lashing, every strap, every knot into a job that had to be done twice.

He was worse than he had been in nine days and he knew exactly why and there was nothing to be done about it. He had spent a night's sleep, an hour of attention, and the bottom of his own reserve on a door that did not open. His legs came up the first rise like somebody else's legs. Twice in the first hour he heard himself talking to Tonk and could not remember starting.

So he shortened the rests and took more of them, which was the trade you made when you were this kind of tired, and he told his brother about the jay again, and about the marten they had seen on the fourth day, and about the time their father fell in the water in front of forty people on the bar and came up holding the fish.

"Was he cross?"

"He was furious. He kept the fish, though. He walked all the way up the bar with it and put it on the rack and didn't say a single word to anybody, and he had weed in his hair."

"Did you laugh?"

"Everybody laughed. Mum laughed so hard she sat down."

"Did *he* laugh?"

"Not that day," Jab said. "He laughed about it for eleven years after."

The larch gave out at the shoulder and the ground began to fall away south and west, and they came down through a long slope of alder and old burn into the creek country the Elder had described to him across a table with a stick in her hand.

He heard it before he saw it.

He had heard it the night before, in the rain, and had thought the sound was too big. He had been right.

The trail came down a last pitch through alder and delivered them onto a gravel bench, and below the bench the creek went by in flood.

It was forty feet across and the color of tea with milk in it, and it was not making the noise that water makes going over stones — it was making the low grinding noise water makes when it is rolling stones along the bottom. Whole trees were going down it. As Jab stood there with the frame on his hips and the cold coming up off the water into his face, a root ball the size of a house came around the upstream bend, turned over once, and went past them at the speed of a man running.

Somewhere under all of that there was a crossing. The Elder had drawn it on the table: a wide shallow braid where the creek spread out over gravel, knee-deep in the ordinary autumn, a thing a loaded man walked across without taking off his boots.

"Jab," said Tonk, behind his ear. He had gone very quiet in the last hundred steps. "Jab. That's water."

"I know."

"That's *water*."

"I know it is. We're not going in it." Jab took his eyes off the flood and made himself say the next part in the voice he used about jays. "We're going to stand here a minute and I'm going to look at it, and then we're going to go along the bank and find a better place, and you're going to help me look. All right?"

Tonk did not answer for a long moment.

"I don't want to help," he said.
