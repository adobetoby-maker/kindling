# Chapter 41 — Kiva: Not the Copper

There was a boy on the Coper Street step when the tram let them down, and he had a slate-blue card in his fist.

He was one of Graywater's runners. He had plainly been told to wait for a girl with a strapped knee, and he looked at Kiva's knee before he looked at her face.

"From the Torch," he said. "I'm to see you read it."

It was three words long. *Yard. Now. Van.*

The bells were still going. There were three of them, and they did not keep together. Mast five rang its two and two from over the Sag. The weigh-house rang three and three. The third came up off the river on a code that Kiva did not know, and each of them was a little out with the other two, so that the whole sky over the east bank sounded like a clockmaker's shop at noon.

"That's an attached team turned out," said Mara. She had the trunk plan rolled under her arm still. "Go on. I'll not be at home in any case. Rusk will want a reader at mast five within the hour, and I'm the only one he has who'll go up a mast at dusk without being paid double."

"You said it wasn't mine."

"It isn't. A bell is. You're attached to a company." Mara looked at her hand, and then did not say what she had been going to. "Tell me after what you saw, and what you think, and don't mix them. I shall be doing the same thing a mile off, and I'd like one of us to be tidy."

Kiva went with the boy.

---

The Graywater watch room had three lamps lit on its map wall, and nobody in it was pretending to be calm.

They were calm all the same. That was the difference between a company and a school. Rhea Sorn stood in front of the wall with her coat on and her hands in its pockets, and read the three small lights as a carter reads a street.

"The weir. The weigh-house. Mast five." She did not turn round. "Three bells inside a minute, on three masts that are a mile apart. I've been twenty-three years in this room and I've seen two together, once, in a flood. Imrie, you've the second van and the weigh-house; it's a street, so it's yours. The Home Line has mast five. I'll take the weir." The pale eyes came round at last and went along the wall bench. "Seven."

They were all there. Anwen had come from the laundry with flour on her cuff. Perrin had come at a run and was pretending that he had not. Kellan stood at the end in his own coat, and Jude Talley beside him, and Pru had her cord out already, wound round both hands.

"You're attached. You'll ride with me, and you'll do what you're entered for and nothing else. Rowan."

"Recovery," said Kiva. "Dark hands. And I'm barred any go until the first; Aske's said so." She held up the left. "Two don't report. Finch has the cord from the first turn. That's arranged."

"I know that it is. I was in the Pit." Rhea took her hands out of her pockets. "Say it anyway. Pike."

"Two sheets on the van," said the square catcher. "And I've a word to say to that first-year about her third turn, on the way."

It was dusk when they came down the bank into the weir meadow.

Kiva had sat on that bank in the spring with sixty other candidates and watched Graywater take a Flask to pieces. It looked smaller without one. There was the long grass, grey in the last of the light, and the weir beyond it making its one continual noise, and the mast on its three legs with the bell under it still swinging.

There was nothing else.

Four wards in blue brought a gate up at the head of the meadow all the same. It stood there, silver and patient, lighting the seed-heads. Rhea walked the ground behind it with her hands in her pockets. She walked the whole of it, and she looked behind her twice.

"What's there?" she said.

"Nothing," said Anwen. "The grass isn't lying. There's no cold. I've stood at the tape of a tear all day, and the back of your neck knows before the rest of you." She frowned. "Mine doesn't know anything."

"Cade."

"A weir. A mast. A bell that somebody ought to stop." Perrin considered. "And a cow, on the far bank, who's as surprised as I am."

"Renn."

"There's no light in it," said Kellan slowly. "A tear's blue from the side. I've walked round that mast and it's only an evening."

Rhea did not ask Kiva. She looked at her, which was a different thing.

Kiva had been standing on something. She had known it through her boot for a minute or more, without attending to it, as one knows that a tram is coming before one hears it. She knelt down in the grass.

It was a square of iron, a yard across, sunk level with the turf. She had seen a spawn stop on that plate in the spring, on its way to the wall, and stand there as if it had heard its name.

She laid her right palm on it, and beside that the heel of her left.

It came up slow and even and many. It was not strong. It was the hum of the tram sheds heard from the far end of a street. It was the thing that she had felt in a doorpost on the Brickfields road at four o'clock that afternoon, with her aunt beside her reading needles.

"It's keeping time," she said. "This is. There's water under it."

"That's the sluice feeder," said Pike. He had come up behind her with a roll of cord. "It runs under the meadow to the Ferren main."

"Then it's the main." She heard how that sounded, and took her hands off the iron. "I can't weigh it. It's what comes through a plate. I'd not have anybody stand a wall down on my saying so."

"Nobody's standing one down. You were asked what's there." Rhea looked at the plate, and then at the girl kneeling by it. "You've an aunt reading drums for the Municipal Works. Tell her tonight. Leave the *can't weigh it* in."

A man was coming down the bank with a lantern. He came at a walk, as he came everywhere, and he had his hat on.

"Nothing at five," said Hollis Vane. "I've had a closing crew stand round an empty field for an hour like mourners at the wrong grave. Imrie's sent from the weigh-house. Nothing there but a carter who thinks that we owe him a night's sleep." He stopped beside the gate and looked at four wards burning ash for the benefit of a meadow. "Stand it down, Sorn, if you're content. I've the reason, such as it is. It came from her aunt, up a ladder."

"Go on."

"A mast rings when its drum climbs past a mark. The mark's set on the grid. Mrs. Rowan says that the three drums didn't climb. She says the mark came down to meet them, for about as long as it takes to breathe in, and went back." He said it flatly, like a casualty list. "All three inside the same minute. She says it'll do so again at the top of some other breath, tonight or Thursday, and she can't tell me which. She says she can stop the bells by having the marks moved."

"And then they'll not ring when there is one," said Rhea.

"She said that as well. She said it first."

Nobody spoke. The four wards let their wall go out, one after another, and the meadow went dark as a room does when a lamp is carried from it.

"Then a bell's no use to me until further word," said Rhea. "I'll have eyes on every mast in my district by morning. People, sitting, with a drum in front of them and a boy to run." She looked up the bank. "That's forty hands I haven't got."

"You've a Circuit full of students who can read a tape," said Kellan. He said it to the grass. "There's one of them reads it better than anybody in Meridian. I'd begin with him."

"Bexley."

"He'd do it for nothing. He'd very likely pay you."

Rhea almost smiled. It did not reach her mouth.

"Bring me him, Renn, and a dozen more who won't fall asleep." She turned to go up the bank. "Finch. Pike has a word to say to you about a cord."

He said it in the van, by the light of the shuttered lamp, the whole way back along the Brickfields road. Pru crossed a waxed cord on her knee, over and over, with the square grey catcher watching her hands. Whenever a crossing went wrong he put one thick finger on her wrist, and she stopped, and looked at it, and began again.

"Those are the ones that'd be a lost bag," said Pike. "Do you know what they've in common?"

"They're the ones where I was thinking about it."

"That's the first sensible thing anybody's said tonight." He took the cord from her and laid it across her palm again. "Then you'll do it until you're not."

---

Mara went to drum twenty-two on Tuesday evening, since it was twenty-two's turn, and she took Kiva to carry the lamp.

"The Works' rate doesn't run to a second pair of hands," she said. "You'll stand on the pavement. You'll hold the lamp when I ask. You're not to come over a sill, and if you feel anything through a wall you're to say so in the same words that you used to Sorn."

"I told you those."

"You did. I've had them in my head all day, like a tune."

A drum house was not much to look at, and Kiva had walked past a hundred of them without seeing one. It was a brick hut no bigger than a coal store, set back from the road behind an iron rail, with a slate roof and a green door and a number on the lintel. Inside was one drum, taller than a man, that fed three or four streets. There was a needle under glass on its face.

Twenty-two stood on the Kiln loop. The copper left the Brickfields road at the gasworks corner, after the twenty-first drum, and went east in a long bend round the old brick kilns and the rows of small houses that had been built for the kiln hands. It came back to the road a mile farther on. Mara had explained that on the tram, with her finger on the plan.

"There," she said, from inside the door. "Come to the sill and look. That's a drum minding its own business."

The needle stood. It trembled a little, as a needle does, on a time of its own.

"It's due," said Kiva.

"It's due tonight, if the thing holds to its two days. Nineteen on the Wednesday, twenty on the Friday, twenty-one on Sunday." Mara had her left palm on the drum's iron side. "I can't feel that it's so much as thinking of it."

They waited. It got dark. A lamplighter went along the row on his lift-board, a hand's height off the paving, touching each lamp awake. Mara looked at the needle now and then, and Kiva held the lamp, and nothing whatever happened.

Some time after eight somebody came round the bend of the loop on a Works bicycle, very fast, ringing the bell.

"*Mrs. Rowan!*" It was a boy in an apprentice's apron. He had no breath. "Mr. Rusk says—you're to come—it's gone in. Not here. It's twenty-five."

"Twenty-five," said Mara.

"On Stopgate. The foot of the hill. The warden there had her hand on the door for her eight o'clock and saw it nodding." He leaned on the handlebars. "It's gone past three. It's skipped the whole loop. Mr. Rusk says it's got faster. He says, if it can do three in a night—"

"I can do that sum for myself, thank you." Mara had already shut the green door and turned the key. "Give me the plan, Kiva. No. Hold it. Walk."

It was twelve minutes to the foot of Stopgate, going quickly.

Kiva knew the street, as everybody at the Crooked Line knew it. It was the long hill that climbed from the Brickfields road to the bottom wall of the school, straight as a rule, with a tram line up the middle and tall narrow houses on both sides. She had gone up it every morning for six months. She had never once wondered why it was called that.

There was a small crowd at the rail of the twenty-fifth drum house. A woman with a warden's handbell stood at the open door with her arms folded.

Mara went in. Kiva stopped at the rail.

She could see the needle from there. It went up, and it came down. It did it slowly, and with a horrible ease, like something that had been doing it for years.

"When?" said Mara's voice.

"I can't say when. I looked at eight. It was nodding at eight."

"And at seven?"

"I don't look at seven."

Kiva was not listening any longer. She had turned round.

It was behind her. She had felt it as she came along the pavement, in the soles of her feet, and had taken it for the tram. There was no tram. The line up the middle of Stopgate lay empty under the lamps, wet and shining, and the hum was not in the rails.

It was in the crown of the road. She walked out to it.

There was a grating there, between the rails, a yard long, of the old thick iron. She had stepped over it every morning of the summer. She knelt beside it, on the wet setts, and put her right palm flat on the bars.

The same push came up into her hand, unhurried, from a long way under.

And there was a sound under it. It was water. It drew in, with a soft hollow noise, like breath taken through the teeth. Then it let go.

"Mara," she said. She did not shout it. "It's here. It isn't at the wall. It's in the road."

Her aunt came out to the rail.

"Say it properly."

"There's a grating. There's water under it, and the water's breathing. I can hear it." She took her hand away. "I can't tell you more than that. But you could look. It's only a hole with water in it."

Mara stood quite still for a moment. Then she took the lamp out of Kiva's hand, and came through the rail, and knelt down on the setts in her work skirt, and lowered the lamp on its chain between the bars as far as the chain would go.

It lit a round brick throat, green with slime, and at the bottom of it a black shine.

The shine rose. It came up the brick by the width of two fingers, and left a wet mark, and went down again.

Mara watched it rise and fall, and rise. She did not move.

"That's the Ferren main," she said. She said it very quietly, to the hole. "It's the storm drain for the whole of this side. It runs from the top of this hill to the sump under the pump house, and three pumps lift what's in it over the levee." She did not look up. "Three pumps, on a bank of four-and-twenty. I've stood in that house since the Pit and listened to them all take their stroke together. I thought that it was rather restful."

"It isn't in the copper," said Kiva.

"No. I said so yesterday, and I thought I was saying that I didn't know where it was." Mara drew the lamp up, hand over hand. "Give me the plan. Hold the corner down; it's wet."

She spread it on the setts, under the lamp, and Kiva held it with the heel of her left hand.

"There's the copper. Twenty-one at the gasworks. Then it goes off round the kilns, twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four, and comes back in here, at the foot of the hill. Twenty-five." Her finger went back to the gasworks corner, left the red line, and went straight up the road, along a thin blue one that Kiva had taken for the edge of the paper. "And there's the main. It doesn't go round the kilns. Why should it? Water won't climb a bend to oblige a brickmaker. It comes straight down Stopgate and straight along the road."

"So twenty-five's the next one. On the drain."

"On the drain, twenty-five is the next after twenty-one. It hasn't skipped anything. It's kept its two days to the hour." Mara sat back on her heels. "And the three on the loop aren't due at all. They never were."

The warden had come to the rail to see what the Works was doing on its knees in the road. The apprentice had come with her.

"Then it hasn't got faster," said the boy.

"It hasn't got faster. It's got nearer." Mara's finger moved up the blue line. There were four drums on it between the foot of the hill and the top, and then a square that was the School. "I said eighteen days yesterday, to my niece, in a doorway." She took her hand off the paper. "It's ten. If nothing changes."

Nobody said anything. In the hole between them the water drew in, and let go.

"Is that enough?" said Kiva.

"It's one drum." Mara began to fold the plan. Her voice was level, and Kiva knew that levelness; it was the voice in which she read a figure that she did not like. "The first three were on the copper and the drain alike, so they tell me nothing. I've a single drum out of its turn, and a drain underneath it, and a girl who says she can't tell me more than that she felt it. A man could drive a cart through what it isn't."

"What would make it enough?"

"A second one. Called beforehand, by somebody who stood to look a fool." She got up, and brushed the wet from her knees, and it did no good. "I shall have to go and be that person in front of Rusk. I'd hoped for a quiet autumn."

---

They had the Chair's hearing room again on Wednesday morning, and this time there were more than forty.

The Municipal Works had the left of the long table. Examiner Rusk sat in the middle of it, broad and tidy, with linen guards over his cuffs and the trunk plan pinned out before him. He had three of his own people. He had also Mr. Adlam, who sat a little apart from them in his black coat, as a guest sits who has been asked for one thing only.

The wardens of Stopgate had the right. There were a dozen of them, give or take. The School had sent its bursar, a thin worried man in a gown, and the keeper of its core house, who was a short woman named Ansty with a ring of keys at her belt and did not look worried at all.

Rhea Sorn and Hollis Vane sat together at the far end, which Kiva had not seen before.

Kiva had a place on the wall bench, behind Mara, with the plan tubes across her knees, and she had been told that she was there to carry.

"I'll say what I am not," said Ysra Crest. "I am not the Works, and I'm not the Line. I've a Circuit, and a school of that Circuit stands at the head of a hill with seven hundred students in it. Mr. Rusk has been good enough to ask for this room. I've lent it. It's his."

Rusk stood up.

He did it heavily, with his scarred thumbs on the edge of the table.

"Then I'll be short, since I've been wrong once this week already. On Monday I had a thing walking my trunk a drum every two days, and eighteen days to the School. Last night it was at the foot of Stopgate, and Mrs. Rowan says ten." He looked along the wardens. "There are two accounts of how it came there. I'll give you mine, and then I'll have my reader give you hers, since she'll not thank me for softening it."

"Yours first," said Ysra.

"It goes from ash to ash. That's the only way that anybody has ever seen a cell take a beat, in three hundred years of the trade. Mr. Adlam will bear me out; it's his house's patent." Adlam inclined his head. "A bank's put into step by salting it with ash that already keeps one. So I say that something is carrying from drum to drum, along my trunk, by a road I can't see, and that last night it found a short way. If I'm right, it wants ash to walk on. I can take the ash away."

He put a thumb on the plan.

"Six-and-twenty, and seven-and-twenty. I quench the pair of them tonight and I leave them dry for a week. Two dead drums, halfway up the hill. A firebreak. Whatever it is can come up to the edge of that and look across."

"And the streets on those two?" said a warden.

"On their boxes. Six hundred households, for a week, in the tenth month. A box is three days. So I'll want cans, and the casualty reserve has undertaken to find them." Rusk glanced up the table. "It'll be asking the Circuit's teams to lend at par, I'm told. I'd not have chosen to come to students with my cap out."

"We'll speak of that," said Ysra. "Mrs. Rowan."

Mara did not stand. She laid her left hand flat on the table, as she laid it on a drum.

"It didn't find a short way. It went to the next drum that the Ferren main runs under. I've the plan. I've the hour for each of the four. And the water in that main is rising and falling by two fingers at the foot of Stopgate, in time with the pumps' stroke. I watched it against a mark on the brick, with a lamp, last night, and again at six this morning with Mr. Rusk beside me."

"I saw it," said Rusk. "I don't dispute that it moves."

"Then here's what I think, and I'll say plainly that I only think it. Three pumps that used to take their stroke each in its own time now take it together. So the whole main draws and lets go like a lung. Every drum house on that line has had the same slow push come up through its floor since the seventeenth." Her voice did not alter. "I don't say that water can step a cell. Nobody's ever seen it. I say that the drums have fallen in exactly in the order that the water reaches them, and not in the order of the copper, and that I've one drum to prove it by."

"One," said Rusk. He said it without unkindness.

"One. If you quench two drums and I'm wrong, you've stopped it, and all it's cost you is a week of boxes and my good name. If you quench two and I'm right, it won't know that they're dry. It'll go underneath."

"Mr. Adlam," said Ysra. "You've a patent."

"I've a patent in which no drop of water is mentioned from the first leaf to the last, ma'am." He had his hat on his knee, and he turned it. "I should be astonished if a storm drain could do what the Works spent forty years learning to do with a pinch of salt. I've been astonished five times since the spring. I've stopped regarding it as evidence." He looked along the table at Mara, quite pleasantly. "If the lady will put a date to it, I shall be very glad to be astonished a sixth."

"Twenty-eight," said Mara. "On Monday the third, with two dry drums below it. If it's the copper or the ash, nothing happens on Monday, and I'm wrong."

"That's five days," said the Chair. "You'll both be very tired of one another."

"We were that by Thursday last," said Rusk. "I'll quench tonight. I'd be a fool not to. It's cheap, and it mends, and if she's wrong I've saved a school." He sat down. "And if she's right I've put six hundred families on boxes for nothing, and I shall have that to carry. I'd sooner carry it than not try."

It was a fair thing to say. Kiva could feel how fair it was, as she had once felt the fairness of an offer for a bag.

She had a question. She had had it since Monday, in a doorway, and it was not hers to ask in that room. She leaned forward and said it into the back of her aunt's collar.

Mara's head turned a quarter of an inch.

"My clerk wants to know what the shutdown is," she said. "It's a good question, and I ought to have asked it myself. You've a firebreak, Mr. Rusk. That's a fence. If the thing comes through the fence and reaches the head of that hill, what do you pull?"

"The core scrams," said the keeper, Ansty. It was the first thing that she had said. "On its own weight. I've a red lever with a pin in it, and I've pulled it twice in a drill and never in earnest. The rods fall. There's nothing in my house that needs ash to make it safe."

"So the School goes dark and nobody's hurt."

"The School goes onto boxes. I'd have the first-years sent home, all the same. I don't like them near anything that I haven't a lever for."

"Nor do I," said Rusk. "And it isn't the core that keeps me awake. It's the pumps. I've a bank of four-and-twenty that was built so that any one could fail alone. Now none of them can do anything alone. If one's fouled, I think they all cook. Then there's no stroke at all, in a wet autumn, and the Hythe has water in its cellars in half an hour." He rubbed his eyes with a thumb. "I can't quench that bank and fill it fresh. By the rule it's one crew and a cell at a time, and I'd want four hours with the pumps standing. I've been given the rainfall for the month."

"There's a third thing," said Vane.

He had not moved. He had his hat on the table in front of him.

"A bank fell into step on the seventeenth, and in the same minute four husks stood a hold over a tear in the Pit. I don't say that the one made the other. I've a catcher who's taught me to be particular." He did not look at the wall bench. "I say that I had three bells on Monday and no tear under any of them. I say that every tear this spring has opened by water. The culverts. The flats. The weir. The Sag. I've a main drain breathing under a hill with a school at the top, and I don't know what a tear does when it finds the ground already keeping time."

"Nor do I," said Rhea Sorn. "I'd as soon not learn it with seven hundred children watching."

"Then I'll say what's mine," said Ysra, "and it's small. The School sends its first-years home from Friday. The rest stay; it's ten days, and I won't empty a hill on one drum. Officer Vane will draw me a plan to clear that school and that street in the dark, by litter where it's wanted, and it will be walked on Saturday night by everybody who'd have to do it. Every four that holds a second step turns out under its officer. I mean to know before I need it whether the thing can be done."

"I've never drilled a street," said the bursar faintly.

"No. You'll find that it's very like a lane, with more people in the way."

---

Seven settled its accounts that evening at the Rowan kitchen table. There were five sitting, and a sixth who would not sit.

Jude had come for his terms. He stood by the dresser, as he had on Sunday. Pru had come since she had been told to, and had been given the stool.

"We've five and sixty-two held," said Kellan, "and eighteen credited from the Pit. The reserve's asking every four on the roster to lend at par, for the boxes on the two dry drums. It's a loan. It comes back when the drums are filled. They've put no figure on it."

"Then we put one," said Perrin. "Four drams."

They looked at him.

"I've been on a box. In the spring, when the Pellow's core went, my aunt on Weighbridge Street had the baby's milk on a biscuit tin till noon. It isn't a hardship. It's only that you lie awake and listen for it to stop." He had gone red. "I'm the one who said *no* to six-tenths in the seventh month. So when I say four, it's four."

"Four, then," said Anwen. "I won't argue with a man who's found his brake. Call reserve. Half a dram a fighter, for as long as the bells are doubtful. That's five."

"Five?" said Jude.

"You, if you'll stand. By name, on the line, with a head's share of anything that's tinned. Those were your three things. I've not forgotten the third; she sets the mark for the blade she's got."

"There are two blades," said Jude. He did not look at Kellan.

"There are," said Kellan. "I've thought about that. Talley's the Kill. He was asked, and he stood the Regional, and I went and wore somebody else's jacket. I'm on this team as a blade who's told where he's wanted." His ears had gone white. "I'd sooner say it than have it settled round me."

Jude scratched his jaw.

"I come down heavy," he said. "You come down like a cat. There's work for a cat that I'd make a mess of, and I can think of a paving stone or two that'd turn your edge. I'd say we ask the catcher, each time, and neither of us sulks."

"I don't sulk."

"You went a week without speaking in the fifth month."

"That was thinking."

"It looked a good deal like sulking from the bench."

Kiva had a thing to say. She had found it on the way back from the weir, in a van, watching a first-year cross a cord in the dark until her hands did it without her.

It was not a comfortable thing. It had no shine on it at all.

"We need a second sheet," she said. "Poles and a cord. Three drams, at the office price."

"We've a sheet," said Perrin.

"We've one. And one catcher, with eight fingers, who's entered for recovery and nothing else." She kept her eyes on the table. "If I'm ever told to do the other thing again, I'm not at a hem. I'm four paces off with a hand that's gone. There's a rung coming down all the same, and nobody kneeling to it. I don't want to find that out on a floor. Pru's to have a sheet. Not mine, lent. Hers."

Nobody spoke.

Pru had gone the color of a brick.

"I still cross it wrong," she said. "Pike stops my hand."

"He stops it when you're thinking. I watched him." Kiva made herself look round. "I can't do the third turn at all. I shan't ever. So you're better at it than I am now. That's only so."

There was a short silence. Then Anwen laughed, once, through her nose.

"That's the first time you've ever asked for a thing for somebody else that wasn't really for you," she said. "I looked. It isn't. Second sheet and poles, three drams. Finch to catch when Rowan is otherwise seated. Pru. You'll want to tell Sorn. It changes what she sends you to."

"She'll have to learn my name," said Pru. She said it in a very small voice, and then she sat on her hands.

"Then that's two and a half on the call reserve, three on the sheet, four lent," said Kellan. "That leaves fourteen and twelve hundredths held. And the sealed two, medical, not opened." He sat back. "It adds."

Then he sat for a moment, looking at nothing.

"I've answered Birch," he said. "This afternoon. I went to the yard and said I'd take it. Tuesdays and Thursdays, the contained string, nothing owed." He did not look up. "Nobody told me to. One of you tried, and took it back, and I've been rather grateful. I worked it out. I'm a better blade for that yard. I turned my back on it once at the Regional, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and I told him that too."

"What did he say?"

"Nothing. He doesn't say much. He'll look at my feet on Tuesday and I shall know."

"That's handsome of the Works," said Perrin.

"It isn't the Works. It's Birch. I've begun to be able to tell them apart." Kellan stood up. "You've a plate tomorrow, Cade. I'm told that you're passed over your head."

The Hall's plate lay on the Long Terrace at Stonehand on Thursday afternoon, and Perrin Cade walked round it twice before he would look at it.

It was not much to see. It was a slab of grey iron the size of a door, with two brass rings let into it, and a figure cut in the stone of the terrace beside it. There had been one figure for thirty-one years. Since the spring there had been three more, each newer and sharper than the last. *340. 350. 355. 362.*

"I was withdrawn," he said. "On the fourth of the fifth month. I'd my ribs in linen. I sat in the front row and watched three people I mess with beat a thing that had stood since my father was courting." He rolled his shoulders. "I've had five months to mind that."

"What do you want it to be?" said Kiva.

She had his coat. She had been given it, without a word, as a thing that wanted two arms and no fingers.

"The hold made. That's the Hall's second team. That's a place of my own, in my own colors, with nobody's brackets on it." He grinned, rather whitely. "I've been told not to want the other."

There were a dozen people on the terrace. The Hall's master was there, with his chisel in his apron. Lowen Brask stood at the rail with his arms folded. Noa had come, and stood where he would be in nobody's way.

A healer strapped Perrin's wrists. The plate was loaded, slab by slab, by two third-years with a hoist, and the master called the weight when it was on.

"Three hundred and forty."

Perrin stood over it. He set his feet. The oak of the terrace hummed.

He lifted.

He did it as he had carried a girl across the Pit, without hurry and with his whole attention. It came up to his thighs, and his chest, and then he turned his hands under it and it went over his head, and stood there on his two locked arms like a roof.

Kiva watched the big red face go still, and stiller, and she saw that he was listening to something, as a man listens to his own ribs.

"*Hold made,*" said the master.

He did not put it down.

"Cade," said the master.

"Put forty on," said Perrin. His voice came out squeezed, and perfectly polite. "If it's allowed. I'm up here now."

The master looked at the healer. The healer looked at Perrin's wrists, and his color, and nodded once. The third-years ran the hoist. It was done with the plate in the air, a slab on each side, as a man is handed two more parcels.

"Three hundred and eighty."

Something happened to the terrace. Kiva felt it in her boots. A still door that had been open went a great deal wider, quietly, like a gate swung back against a wall.

It was the longest hold that she had ever watched. His arms began to shake. Then the shaking stopped, which was worse. He had his eyes shut. Somebody at the rail had stopped breathing, and Kiva found that it was herself.

"*Hold made!*"

He gave it to the ground, letting it down through himself and into the oak, by its corners, and the Long Terrace boomed once from end to end.

Then he sat down on the plate and put his head between his knees.

Nobody cheered at first. The master had taken the chisel out of his apron and was looking at it.

"That's eighteen kilos over the best that this Hall ever saw," he said. "By a first-year. With two ribs that were in pieces at midsummer." He knelt, stiffly, at the edge of the stone. "I've cut that line four times since the thaw. I cut it once in thirty-one years before that. I said it was a strong year. I said it in the spring and I said it in the summer. I'm getting tired of hearing myself."

He began to cut.

Noa had come to stand by Kiva. He was looking at the new figure as he looked at a notch.

"That's the fourth time on one stone in one year," he said, under his breath. "And it's not only stone. The Hold figures off the mast go up. Draw goes down. And the other side goes up as well: tears, and skins, and a thing that stood a square. They both climb. They've climbed faster since the fifth month than they did before it."

"What does it mean?"

"Nothing, yet. It's two things going the same way." He looked at her. "Adlam says if I'd a third, from somewhere that wasn't Meridian, somebody would have to listen."

Perrin had lifted his head.

"I don't mind," he said hoarsely. "Not any of it. I've looked." He wiped his face on his forearm. "Is that my coat? Give it here. I'm going to go and tell my dad, and he's going to cry on a tram."

Drum twenty-six did not fall in on Thursday night. There was nothing in it to fall.

They had quenched it at midnight on Wednesday, and twenty-seven after it, and the Works had hung a tag on each green door. Six hundred households, in the streets that the two drums fed, had gone onto their boxes. The casualty reserve had sent a cart round with cans.

At ten that night, when the hour for it had gone by, a clerk chalked one word on the board outside the Placement Hall. *Held.*

There were people who cheered. Kiva heard them from the tram.

She went up the hill with Mara late that night, since Mara meant to see for herself. They stopped at the rail of twenty-five, where the needle nodded, and at twenty-six, where a needle lay against its pin behind a dark glass and did nothing.

"Well, it's dry," said Mara. "I'll say that for him. He's quenched it to the bone."

Kiva had gone out into the road.

The grating was there, between the rails. The water drew in under it, and let go. She did not need to kneel. She walked on up the hill, past twenty-seven, with her hand trailing along the wet iron rail of each area as she went, until she came to the next green door.

Twenty-eight. The needle stood on its own time. She could see it through the glass.

She laid her right palm on the paving in front of the sill.

There was a wall. There was cold stone, and the wet. And under that, so faint that she would not have sworn to it before anybody, there was something pushing.

She put her left beside it. The heel felt it, and the last two fingers. The first two told her that there was a pavement.

"It's here already," she said. "It's very small."

"Is that a result?"

"No. It's what comes through a flagstone." She stood up. "It's a reason to look."

"So it is." Mara stood at the rail with the plan under her arm and did not unroll it. "I've a bag of corks at the shop. Tomorrow I shall walk the main up from the pump house, a cork down every manhole and a watch for it at the next. If the water goes where the plan says it goes, the corks will come through and tell me so." She looked down the long wet street, to where a word was chalked on a board a mile off. She turned her collar up against the wet. "I'd be glad to be wrong. I've lain awake hoping for it."

"I know."

"I shan't be, though. Shall I?"

Kiva did not answer.

They walked down together. Behind them, between the rails, the hill went on breathing.
