# Chapter 30 — Kiva: What's Behind You

Rhea Sorn came round the legs of the Flask as a person comes round a parked wagon in a narrow lane, sideways, with her coat held in.

She looked at Nairn first. She looked for about a second, and then she crouched and put two fingers on the cobbles beside his hip, well clear of the grey.

"Below the knee?"

"Below. I felt it go." His voice was thin and careful. "It's standing on the weight. There's a lead weight in that hem and my shin's between the two."

"Then you'll keep still, and you won't be brave." She stood. "Seven. Faces to me. Is anybody else under anything?"

"No," said Kiva.

"Then you may stand, slowly, and come out by the west side. Not Finch. Finch, crawl. Keep off your hands; there's wax on that sheet."

They came out. Kiva had not known how much she was shaking until she tried to walk. The Flask did not stir. Its blunt top lay against the silver eight paces up the hill, and beside it, small as a dog at a gate, the third husk stood with its head pressed to the wall.

Down the street there was a sound of iron.

It was a railing, being worked at. Kiva turned. A dozen paces below, on the east pavement, a grey shape with a shine on its shoulders had got its head and one forelimb through the area bars of the house with the cracked lintel, and was reaching for the window beneath the front step. There was a board across that window on the inside. Somebody had put it there in a hurry.

"That one's going in," said Rhea.

She said it to nobody. Then she turned her head a little, so that the four of them and the man on the ground could hear, and she spoke as Hollis Vane had spoken on a cinder floor, with the same flat care.

"There's a ground-floor front behind that board with people in it. No cloth I can get at, and no time to lay one. I'm going to cut it where it stands, on a pavement, over a cellar grating. The ash will go down the grating. It'll foul that building's core, and likely more." She was already moving. "A window before a cellar. That's my trade, and the choice is mine."

She did not run. She went down the east curb with long, even strides, and stopped a pace behind the thing at the railings, where its shine could not come round to her.

Her left foot came down. Her right came down.

The line that went up from her fist was white, and no wider than a finger, and it was there for less time than it takes to blink.

The husk fell in through its own shoulders. Four-and-twenty drams of open ash poured down the front of the area steps, soft and quick, and Kiva saw it lean as it fell. It ran for the grating as water runs to a drain.

For a breath, nothing.

Then, from somewhere under the street, came a deep, flat *clunk* that she felt in the soles of her feet. She had felt that once before, at a quarter to three in the morning, in a hallway on Coper Street.

Inside the house with the cracked lintel a handbell began to ring, hard and even.

"*Pellow!*" It was a woman's voice, an old one, coming down a stairwell. "Core's down! You're on your boxes! Nobody touches a red handle but me!"

Rhea was walking back up the hill. Her face showed nothing whatever.

"That's the Pellow on reserve," she said. "Eight-and-forty flats. Now. I've got a blade under a Flask." She stopped beside Nairn. "I can't cut what's on him; the legs go last, and if I take the top off this now, sixty drams come down on a man who can't crawl out from under it. I can't walk the gate; the thing would follow it straight across him. I want that leg up a hand's breadth for a few breaths, and there isn't a still-door on my van."

There was one three paces from her. Kiva had set a whole yard under it on Wednesday. Her paper said *not Hold, not Carry,* and nothing of hers open within three paces of a charged cell, on a street with a core under every doorstep; and the woman who had asked to hear that paper at six o'clock did not so much as look round. Kiva kept her hands at her sides.

Perrin had come down the pavement while she spoke. He stood with his arms held away from his ribs, breathing through his mouth.

"There's one here," he said.

"I know what your healer told you."

"So do I. I can't, and I'm not going to say I can; I'd fold in the middle and then you'd have two." He said it plainly, though it cost him. "But there are three up at that gate who lift tram bogies for their living. My father's a still-door. He never went near a Hall. He'd tell you he only stands under things."

Rhea looked at him for the space of a breath.

"Can you teach a man to give a load back, in a minute, in the dark?"

"I can teach that one. He taught me to ease a brake."

"Fetch them. Dark, at a walk, on the west pavement." She turned. "Renn. You carried a woman off a floor at the Pit with nothing lit. Say it."

"Renn, Seven. Carry."

"Ruddock's at the curb with a board. You take him under the arms when I say *now* and not before, and you don't stop for any noise he makes. Rowan, Finch. You're the road. I want that pole and that cord and every weight you can reach out of his way. Dark hands. Go."

---

Kiva knelt beside Nairn in the cold that came down off the lowest knot.

The cord lay across his hip. She drew it clear, a hand's width at a time, and passed it back to Pru. She unhooked the weight at the south-west corner and slid it away over the cobbles. The one that mattered she could not reach. It was under the edge of the grey, and his leg was on it.

"You're being rescued," she said. "In about a minute. You'll be told when."

"Is that Cade's line?" His eyes were shut. "He told me in the van. He says he likes to be told."

"He lends it out."

"Tell him it's a comfort. It isn't, but tell him." A breath. "I had my back to you. I was looking for her hand. I should have had my eyes on the three of you and I had them up the hill, like a first-year waiting to be nodded at." His mouth moved. "So don't carry all of this home with you. Some of it's mine."

"I called the lure."

"You did. I'd have called it too, at seventeen. I did, once, on the river flats, and a man called Ostrey lost two teeth." His hand found the hem of the sheet and gripped it. "It lay very well, your canvas. I noticed that."

There were boots on the pavement.

Three people in leather aprons came down out of the dark behind Perrin, a short wide man with a grey mustache, a tall woman with her sleeves rolled, and a thick-necked young man who kept looking up at the knots and then at his feet. They stopped where Perrin stopped. None of them said a word. They had the stillness of people who work at night beside heavy things that can move.

"That's a leg," said Perrin. He pointed with his chin. "It's nothing but weight. You've had a bogie come off the jack in pit three, Dad; you told me. It's that. You're not lifting it. Nobody could. You're getting in under it and letting the floor have it."

"Under where?" said his father. His voice was deep and quiet.

"There. A pace above the lad's knee, where it thickens. Three of you, shoulder on, like the pit props. Dulcie on the outside; you're tallest." Perrin wet his lips. "Open your stance when I say and not before. Then there are two words. On *give* you ease off, like easing a shoe. You'll feel it settle onto you. On *take* you stand up into it, all at once, and you let it go straight down through your boots. Don't hold it in your back. Your back's got nothing to do with it. It's the street that's strong."

"Give, and take."

"It's what we say at a lid. It works on a brake as well." He glanced at Kiva. "It'll work on this."

"The moment the three of you light," said Rhea, "that thing may turn its head to see what's behind it. If it does, my line takes it back. You'll have a moment, and you'll use it to finish what you're doing. You don't look up."

"We don't generally," said the tall woman.

They went in. Kiva moved back to give them room. She saw Perrin's father set his shoulder to the grey as he might have set it to the frame of a car, and frown, and shift his feet a few inches wider on the wet canvas.

"When I say," said Perrin. He drew a breath that hurt him; Kiva saw it. "*Open.*"

There was no ring to it, as there would have been in the Hall. Three ordinary still-doors came open in the dark, as dull as stove lids, and she felt them through the cobbles, the plain heaviness of people who had never once been put on a mast.

Above her the lowest knot turned a quarter, slowly, and stopped.

"*Give.*"

The leg settled. It came down onto three shoulders with a creak that was not a sound a living thing makes.

"*Take!*"

They stood up into it.

It rose. It rose about the width of a hand, and held there, trembling, with Nairn's shin lying under it in the wrong shape.

"*Now,*" said Rhea.

Kellan had him under the arms. He went backward off the sheet in four long steps, bent double, and Nairn made one noise, a short one, with his teeth shut. Then there was a board, and Ruddock's bald head, and a satchel coming open.

"He's out," said Kiva.

"Then give it back," said Perrin. His voice shook. "Slowly, Dad. It isn't a wrench. Let the ground have it a bit at a time, and then walk out from under."

The leg came down onto the lead weight. The three of them stepped clear, one after another, and shut what they had opened, and stood in the roadway looking at their own hands.

The top knot had not left the wall.

Perrin's father rubbed his shoulder. He looked up at the grey column, the whole height of it, and then at his son, standing on the curb with his arms out from his sides like a man in a barrel.

"You never lifted it," he said.

"No."

"They've you strapped."

"To the armpits. I'm not let."

"And you told her you couldn't. With her wanting one." The grey mustache moved. "Good lad. That's the first sensible thing I've known a Hall to teach anybody. I'd thought it was all plates." He put his cap on. "Does the lady want us for anything else? We've a quiet night up there. All the cars are asleep."

---

Rhea Sorn wanted them for a good deal, and she had about a minute in which to decide what.

She stood on the west curb and made herself look at all of it at once. That was the trade. Anybody could look at a Flask.

*What's there?*

A string on her gate, a little over twenty paces above the place where it had come out. Four wards on that gate, and they had been lit four minutes; Imrie's forearms were already steaming. One Handful, plain, butting the wall at the west end. A tear fourteen paces below her that had stopped keeping time. A tripped core under forty-eight flats, and a child's physic in a cupboard that was now running on a box the size of a biscuit tin.

One blade, where there had been two. Ruddock said both bones. Nairn would walk in the autumn.

And four students on a curb, who had done exactly what a qualifier had taught them, a few minutes after it had been praised.

*Whose is that?*

She had asked them in the van what was behind them. She had liked the question. She had thought it rather a neat one, and she had let it lie there for them to pick up, and had turned to the front. *I won't ask it again tonight.* She had said that. She had sent three words to a frightened girl by a runner and promised to say why later, when it mattered.

It had mattered at about twenty-eight minutes to three. She had been twenty paces up a hill with her hand raised.

*Mine,* thought Rhea. *Then do it properly.*

"Everything in this street stays dark but that wall," she said. She did not raise her voice. "Now. Roles. Every one of us, and mine first, aloud, with the reason, as though nobody here had ever done it before. We've just learned what happens when I take it as read."

They had come round her. The four students. Pike. Three people in aprons who had not been told to go.

"Sorn. Kill. But not here. This street's a row of lamps with brick over them, and I won't open sixty drams again and again between two lines of gratings. The wharf has no cellars. I cut there." She turned her head. "Imrie's four. Hold. Pike. Catch, at the wharf, with seven sheets and the last one doubled. Rowan catches second, turn about with him, on her own call. That's her paper to the letter. The reason's the clock: two catchers halve it, and there's a building on its boxes."

"Second," said the girl. She had gone white, and her voice held.

"Renn. Corners, for whichever of them is kneeling. Nothing here for you to cut, and you won't be asked." He took it without a flicker; she noted that. "Cade. You go up the Pellow's stair to the warden, at a walk. You find me the child at the second-floor front and you tell me what that cupboard's running on and how long it's got. You're the only one here who can be spared to be slow."

"And us?" said the man with the mustache.

"Can you keep a door shut while there's ash open beside you, and hold the corner of a sheet, and do what a square man tells you?"

"It's most of what I do."

"Then you're corners, the three of you. Wharf. Go with Pike." She breathed. "That leaves the Handful on my wall, and the turn."

The Handful she dealt with first, because it was simple, and because it was going to cost her.

It stood a pace from the west end of the gate with its head against the silver. There was no skin on it. She could take it from any side. And wherever she took it, twenty-four drams would fall at the foot of a lit wall, inside the reach of four wards that could not go dark for one breath without a Flask walking off to find a cellar.

"I'm going to print that," she said. "On my own line's light. It'll be salted, every dram. That's a Handful of clean tin gone for the sake of a clear gate, and I chose it."

Nobody answered. She did it. It took four-tenths of a second, and afterward a low grey heap lay along the foot of the wall with a thread of silver running through it, as bright as a hair.

Now the turn.

The gate stood uphill of the string. The wharf lay down. To lead a Flask south she wanted a light to the south of it, inside twenty-five paces, and nearer than anything else it could read. She had four wards, and all four were under its chin. If she split them two and two, each half would be a wall that a child could lean through.

A fifth would have done it. Stroud would have done it without being asked, and Stroud was at home with a spoon. The Pryce girl would have done it, and she was holding a lamp over a drain on the Sag, since Rhea's own company had sent her there, and rightly.

*A corner and a lamp. Not a wall.*

She looked at the curb.

The first-year was sitting on it with her arms round her knees and wax all down her back. She was not talking. Rhea had been told that she talked without stopping, and she had not said a word since a leg came down.

"Finch."

The round face came up.

"I sent you three words. Tell me why."

"Because a wall has to hold something." It was a very small voice. "And I can't."

"And a lamp?"

"Only has to be seen."

"By whom?"

The girl's mouth opened and stayed open. She looked at the grey column, and then down the street at the blue stroke, and Rhea watched her work out the thing that she should have been made to work out in a van.

"That's what I didn't ask," said Finch. "I lit it for the husk. I never asked who else." She swallowed. "That reads me. From well back. Anything that comes out of the gap reads me close. And nothing else in this street's alight, so I'd be the nearest. I'd be the only."

"Then say it as you'd declare it."

"Finch. Seven. Lamp." Her chin was shaking. "For the Flask, from twenty paces, and I don't know for how long."

"For as long as it takes to walk it to the wharf. You'll go down the east pavement, dark, till you're level with that door with the crack. You'll stand in the road and light. When it turns to you, you'll walk backward down the hill and it will follow, and you will never once let it come nearer than four paces, because a lamp can't be leaned on. My line runs round by the west and makes a gate below you. When Imrie says *mine,* you drop. That's the whole of it." Rhea held her eye. "I'm told you put out a wall at a meet when you chose to, and not when it was taken off you. That's why I sent for you. It wasn't for what you can hold."

"I can't walk backward downhill past that. I'll look round. I know I will."

"You won't need to. I'll have you by the belt." Rhea heard herself say it, and it was simply true. "I'm what's behind you. That's my job tonight, and I'd let it slip. You look at the knot, and talk if you want to, and leave the street to me."

The girl lit at the Pellow's door. The tea tray hummed between her hands. Up the hill, on a word, four wards went dark together, and the Flask stood for a moment with its blunt head leaning on nothing.

Then the top knot swung round.

"It's seen me," said Finch. "It's turned. It's lifting a—it's coming. I'm going back. I'm going back now."

"Left a little," said Rhea. She had two fingers through the back of the girl's belt and her own eyes on the cobbles. "There's a grating. Now straight."

"It doesn't hurry, does it? I'd thought it would. I'd thought they'd be quick, all of them, like the husks. It's like a cart. It's like a cart coming down a hill with nobody in it."

They went down past the Pellow. A grey spill lay across the pavement there and over the area step, gone dull, and Rhea looked at it as she passed. Four-and-twenty drams. A board at a window, and behind it a bell still ringing.

The tear was on her right hand now. They were going by it at arm's length, on the east side, with two paces of road to spare. She could feel it on her cheek. It was colder than the night, and it made no sound at all.

"Is it nearer? It's nearer. Is that the blue? I can see blue. I'm not looking."

"You're not looking. It's where it was. Keep going."

"I'm narrating."

"I know. Go on. It's the best company I've had all night."

Twenty paces below, four people ran out into the roadway from the west gutter and turned, and the street filled with silver.

"*Mine,*" said Imrie's voice.

The tea tray went out. It went out between one syllable and the next, clean, with nothing left hanging.

Rhea drew her back against a railing, with two fingers still in the belt, and watched the top knot. It had been on a lamp. Now it was on a wall. It took its next step without so much as a check, and went by them at four paces' distance down the hill, and she had not heard the join.

"Was that it?" said Finch. "Was that staying?"

"That was staying." Rhea let go of the belt. The blue stroke hung in the road behind them, and as she looked, it widened by a finger. "And here's another. Seven!"

---

Kiva had the team's sheet half rolled when she heard her team called.

It had taken the two of them to drag it clear of where the Flask had stood. She let go of it and turned, and Kellan turned beside her.

A husk had come out of the tear. It stood a dozen paces below them on the crown of the road, a plain one, the grey of slate, with nothing lit anywhere near it. Its head went slowly from side to side.

"Seven!" Rhea was thirty paces down the hill and going farther, at the tail of the gate. "That's inside your step, and it's yours. I've nobody else. *Tell me how before you touch it.* Loud."

There were two of them. Pru was a lamp. Perrin was on a stair. A team that did three of the four had failed its response, and they had one and a half.

"Corners," Kiva said to Kellan. "We want two that'll stay dark."

"The shed hands. That pair." He was already beckoning. The tall woman and the thick-necked young man had stopped on the west pavement with one of Pike's sheets between them. "And a lure, and we haven't got a ward."

"A blade's a lamp. You've said so."

"I have." His eyes came round to her. "Who else reads it?"

It was strange to hear it asked. It was strange how much room there was in a street, once it had been.

"The Flask," said Kiva. She looked. The top knot was thirty paces below them and going away at its slow cart's pace. "It reads to twenty-five. It's past that, and it's on a wall, and the wall's nearer to it than you'd be." She turned her head. "The tear, if it gives another. Then that one's close to you, and it comes as well."

"So I'm lit for as short a time as will do it."

"For a breath, and no more. You light at twelve paces from it, low, and you walk it up the hill to us as Finch walked the other down. It crosses the cloth to get to you." She was reading the ground as she spoke, and for the first time that night she was not reading only the ground. "What's behind us? The Pellow. It's on its boxes; every one of them is alight now, and there's a cupboard. So the cloth goes on the crown and as far above that door as we can get it. Level with the broken window."

She cupped her hands.

"*Seven! One Handful, plain! Cloth on the crown, level with the broken window, above the Pellow; its boxes are lit! Shed hands on two corners, dark! Renn lights a blade at twelve paces, low, and walks it up onto the cloth, and cuts! A breath of light and no more! Flask's past twenty-five and on a gate!*"

There was a pause. It felt a good deal longer than it was.

"Who's watching the gap while your heads are down?"

Kiva's mouth opened.

Nobody was. Nobody had been the first time, either. Four people had knelt round a sheet with their eyes on a cord, and behind them a blue stroke had opened like a mouth.

"*South corner!*" she called. "*He faces it! He says 'gap' if it moves, and we stand!*"

"Then go!"

They went.

It was not tidy. The sheet was their own, and it had a mark on it the size of a dinner plate where a leg had stood, and a dark stain along the south hem that was not wax. The tall woman, whose name was Dulcie, held her corner as she would have held a tarpaulin in a gale, and had to be told twice to keep her knee on the lead. The young man was called Stott. Kiva put him at the south hem and turned him by the shoulders until he was looking down the hill.

"That blue thing. If it gets any wider, you say *gap.* That's all you do. You don't look at anything else, whatever you hear."

"Gap," said Stott. He swallowed. "Aye."

She set the mark. Then she stood up and looked all the way round, the whole circle, before she knelt.

"Now," she said.

A dozen paces below the hem, Kellan's blade came alight, low, like a lantern held for a dog.

The husk's head stopped going from side to side. It came.

Kellan went backward up the crown of the road with the blue-white held at his knee. He did not hurry. He stepped onto the canvas, and across it, and off at the north hem, and the thing trotted after him with nothing in it but direction. When its feet were on the cloth he did not bound. There was nothing to bound over. He took one step to the side, as a man steps out of a doorway, and cut.

"Dark!"

It was out before she had finished the word. She had the hem. She had the cord at her elbow from the first turn, and it was slow, and she let it be slow.

"Cord check."

"I don't know what you're asking me," said Dulcie.

"Is it over, and then under toward me?"

"It's over. And—aye. Under."

"Sealed."

It had been slower than the first by a long way. Stott had not once turned his head, and nothing in the street had turned to look at them.

"That's a slow one," said Kellan. He was breathing hard, and he was looking at the bag with an expression she had not seen on him before. "I'd have been ashamed of that at the Kennel." He picked up a pole. "I'm not. I can't make out why."

"Because nothing happened," said Kiva.

---

The wharf was forty paces wide and had no cellar under any of it.

It was only cobbles, and mooring posts, and the weigh-house with its mast, and beside that the old weighbridge itself, an iron platform set flush in the stone where carts had been driven on to be weighed for three hundred years. The canal lay beyond, black, with the Lantern's long white wash on the far side of it. There was light enough from that to see a cord by.

Pike had seven sheets down in a row from east to west, and he was standing at the first of them with his hands behind him.

"Odd rungs are mine," he said. "Evens are yours. You'll take your word from me the first time and from yourself after. Don't look at it. It's got nothing to tell you."

The gate came down onto the wharf walking backward, and the Flask came after it.

Kiva had seen this from a bank, and from a cinder floor. She had never been the one kneeling. She watched Pike's cord hand through the first, because Bettan had told her to, months ago, and it did not change. *Catcher? Not yet. Now.* The white line. The fall. *Dark.* The four wards drew off ten paces along the stone and stood, lit and level, and the headless thing swayed and went to them.

The shine on it did not break. It slid down a knot, as it had at the Pit, and lay there thicker.

"Sealed," said Pike. "Yours."

She knelt at the north hem of the second sheet with Kellan and Dulcie and Perrin's father.

"Catcher?"

It was Rhea's voice, from behind the grey. Kiva looked at the canvas. It lay flat. She looked at the fall of the stone, which was toward the water, and at a strand of weed on a mooring post, which hung straight.

"Not yet. It's a foot short. Another step."

"Step," said Imrie.

"Now."

Sixty drams came down in front of her like sand out of a split sack.

"Dark!"

It was heavier than a Handful by more than she had reckoned. The neck was as thick as her thigh. She brought it in at the elbow and took her time over it, and at the third turn she called the check and a tram mechanic of fifty answered her as gravely as if she had been the foreman of his shed.

"Sealed."

Pike's next went as his first had. Her own next was slower, because something had changed and she had been late in knowing what. The weed on the mooring post had begun to lean. There was air coming up off the water, the turn of the tide or the turn of the night, cold on her wet knees, and when the rung fell the south edge of the heap lifted like the hem of a skirt.

"Corners up! Now!"

They brought them up. The grey settled. She did not lose a pinch of it, and she did not know that until the neck was shut.

By the sixth she could no longer feel her left hand.

It had not gone white. It had only gone away, the whole of it, as it had gone once in the spring, at Stonehand, twelve seconds from time. Four necks had been hauled shut across the crook of that elbow since half past two, two of them as thick as her thigh, with the whole drag of each on the one cord, over a nerve that had been emptied on Wednesday and had not finished filling. The cord was in the crook of her elbow and round her right fist, and at the second turn her arm simply opened.

"Cord," she said. "Mine's gone. Pru. Yours. Now."

Pru had come down from the street some while ago, very quiet, and taken the south hem without being told.

"*Yours,*" said Kiva.

"*Mine,*" said Pru, and took it, and crossed it right.

"Sealed," said Kiva.

She sat back on her heels. Behind the grey, Rhea Sorn had not said *catcher* for some time.

Kiva looked, since there was no ash open. The woman with the white streak was standing a long pace behind what was left of the Flask, two knots and six legs under a lid of frost as thick as a paving stone, and she had her hands on her knees. Six cuts. Each had been longer than the last, since the shine thickened with every rung, and there was nobody to spell her.

Kellan was looking as well. He had been watching her light at every cut, the way he watched a board.

"It's longer each time," he said, under his breath. "Look at it. The first was a blink. That last was most of a second, and it shook at the top. That's as much light in a quarter of an hour as she showed at the weir in a morning, and the worst cut's still to come. And there's no second blade on this wharf who's allowed."

"No."

"I cut a rung at the Pit."

"Under a foreman, in a qualifier. This isn't one."

"I know what it isn't." He looked away from her. "I'm only saying what it costs her that I'm standing here with a corner in my hand."

"Double sheet," said Pike. "On the bridge. It's the only flat that's left. Rowan, I'll have you at my north hem; I need one hand there that knows what it's doing."

They laid it on the old iron platform, two thicknesses, and Kiva knelt on the lead with her right hand on the hem and her left in her lap.

"Catcher?" said Rhea. Her voice was hoarse.

"Not yet," said Pike. "Bring it on, Imrie. All of it. Both knots over iron." A long pause. "Now."

The line was level this time. It went through the grey broadside, at the height of a man's belt, and it stayed for longer than Kiva would have believed that a person could hold anything.

Then there were no legs. A hundred and twenty drams came down onto the weighbridge together, and the frost came down on top of them and broke like a plate.

"Dark," said Pike.

She had the hem in her right hand.

She felt it before the neck had come in. It was not the curved thing that she knew, the bend in the grain that every skinned Handful had carried since the spring. That was there, all through, as it always was. This was under it. It was the beat she had felt once in a bag on a cinder floor, slow and even, one pen on one drum.

It was not one pen now. It was a shed full. It came up the canvas into her palm as the hum of the sheds had come down the street, even and many and all together, and there was nothing faint about it.

The catcher owned the floor. She set her teeth and kept her palm on the hem until the neck was shut.

"Sealed," said Pike.

"Pike." She had it out before the word was cold. "That bag's keeping time. All of it. Loud."

He turned his square head.

"Say that again."

"It's beating. Like a bank that's been stepped. The others weren't; there was a trace in mine and I'd know it, it was in ours at the Pit. This isn't a trace." She made herself go on. "I can't weigh it. I haven't got an instrument. It's only what comes through the hem."

Pike looked at the bag between his knees. He laid his broad right hand flat on the neck of it and kept it there a long moment.

"Nothing," he said. "Never have had. I catch by the cord." He took the hand away. "But there's a comb in the van, and there's a girl here who told me inside one breath, with her own name going on five tags tonight. Two reasons to look." He got up. "It's panned last, and nobody touches it till I've held a reed over it. Renn. Pole."

Up the street, somebody shouted.

It was a woman's voice, cracked and carrying, and Kiva knew it from a bank on the Sag Line. She had not seen the closing crew come down. They had been at the blue for the better part of half an hour, six people and a handcart, while the wharf worked.

"*Closed!*" called Bettan. "Twenty to four, and it's shut!"

Rhea Sorn sat down on a mooring post.

She did it all at once, as a person does who has been told that she may. She put her elbows on her knees and her face in her hands, and for a little while she did not move at all.

Behind her, across the water, the Lantern burned as it had always burned. On this side the street went up the hill without one light in it, and halfway along, in a house with a crack over the door, eight-and-forty flats were running down their boxes in the dark.
