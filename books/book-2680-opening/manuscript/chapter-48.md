# Chapter 48 — Kiva: Why It Is Crooked

The watch was not sitting on Thursday. The Ferren said so before anybody had taken a chair.

"It sat twice for you this year, and that's twice more than it sits for most," she said. "This is the Line, and the people who answer for you, in a hall, and two who don't, and a girl. I'll thank the two who don't to be brief."

Kiva knew the oak table, and the window over the levee. She had not seen the room in daylight. Vane stood at the window with his hat in his hand. Rhea Sorn had the end of the bench, with her coat on, as though she might be called out of it. Orla sat beside her with her stick.

The two who did not were at the other end. One was the School's bursar, a thin worried man, with a flat deal box on his lap, tied with tape, and he held it as a man holds a thing that he would sooner not open. The other was a small upright woman with two pairs of spectacles, who had come up from the Compact Library on the tram and had plainly been looking forward to it.

"Rowan," said the Ferren.

She did not get up. She was seventy, and she sat with her hands folded on the oak, and she looked at Kiva across it as a river pilot looks at a channel.

"Rowan, K. Catcher. Hand as before." She let that lie a moment. "A Line foreman may call you at a line-hand's day rate, with your sponsor's leave, for the work that you've shown us. Setting a mark. Reading ground. Calling what's yours and naming what isn't."

Kiva found that she had nothing to say. She stood with her hands at her sides.

*As before,* Aske had said on Monday, with the towel over his knee, and she had been glad of the words without quite knowing why. The two fingers as before. The arm as before. She had not thought to hear the words again in a room over the levee, from a woman who had never seen her hand, meaning something else by them and the same thing. It meant that nothing had been mended and nothing more had been taken, and that the Line would send for her as she was.

"It isn't a rank," said the Ferren. "You'll not get a better seat at anything by it. It means that when a foreman's short a catcher at two in the morning, he may send a boy to Coper Street. I've been sent for since I was nineteen. I've never been thanked for it, and nor will you be."

"Yes, ma'am."

"With her sponsor's leave," said Orla, from the bench. "You have it. Somebody's to wake me as well, if it's two in the morning. I'm old enough to want to know where she's gone."

"You'll be woken," said Vane.

"And if two of us want her at two in the morning," said Rhea Sorn, "the Line and my van, on the same night?"

"Then you'll argue it in the street in your nightgowns," said the Ferren, "and she'll go wherever the bell's nearest, which is what a catcher's for. I'll not have it settled for you in advance. It does you both good to be inconvenienced."

The Ferren's eyes went down the bench.

"Now the other thing, which is not ours, and which a gentleman has been sitting on like a hen."

The bursar got up.

He had been a good deal thinner and more worried since a Saturday night in a yard.

"The School has a plate in its case," he said. "It's been in the press for longer than I've had the keys. The plate of the first teachers, we call it. I've never opened the box." He put it on the table. "I was shown a post last week. It had been retained for ceremonial continuity since 2603, and it turned out to be the handle of a gate. I thought that I had better go and look at what else we retain."

He untied the tape, and lifted the lid.

There was a bed of yellowed wool. In it lay a disk the width of a palm, of a dull white, with rings cut into its face.

It was plaster.

Kiva did not need to be told. One could see the grain of it, and a chip at the edge where it had been dropped.

"I know that cast," said the Reader.

She had risen. She was not much taller standing.

"I've packed it every spring for thirty years, when the School lends its case to the Library for the founders' week, and unpacked it again in the summer. It's plaster. I've told three bursars so, and each of them thanked me and put it back in the press." She looked at the bursar without unkindness. "Nobody ever asked me what it was a cast of."

"I'm asking," said the bursar.

"Then let me lay it beside the one it was taken from." She turned to Kiva. "I shan't touch yours. Nobody's hand but the bearer's, and I know it. If you'll put it down, I'll put mine beside it. It's yours to refuse. Nobody in here chose you."

Kiva drew the cord out from her collar.

She had worn it for four years. She had shown it to a steward once, and to a watch officer, and had never laid it on a table in a lit room for anybody to look at. It came over her head, and she held it in her right palm, and set the back of that hand down on the oak.

It lay there, dark.

The Reader lifted the plaster out of its wool with both hands and laid it beside Kiva's palm, so close that the two edges almost touched. Then she bent over them. She had both pairs of spectacles on.

"The fifth ring wider than the rest," she said. "On both. And there's a flaw across the third, at about four o'clock, like a hair. Look at the plaster, child. No. You look. I've seen what I came for."

Kiva looked. There was a raised thread across the third ring of the cast, where the plaster had gone into a scratch.

There was a scratch on the disk. She had known it since she was thirteen. She had thought that she had made it herself, on a stair.

"That one's a cast," said the Reader. She straightened, a hand in the small of her back. "It was taken by leave of a bearer, once, so that students might know the look of the thing. That one," and she nodded at Kiva's palm, "is the plate of nine rings that the first teachers carried. It was never the School's. It's released, by appointment, and it's gone where it was sent." She took off the upper spectacles. "That's my trade, to know one old thing from another."

The bursar sat down rather suddenly.

"What's it for?" said Kiva.

She had not meant to ask. It was out before she had thought that this was a room where somebody might know.

The Reader looked at her quite kindly.

"I can tell you what it is, and that it's the one the cast was taken from. I can't tell you what it's for. Nobody in this room can, and I'd distrust anybody outside it who said he could."

"It's cold," said Kiva.

"I'd expect so."

Kiva closed her hand.

It was cold. It had been cold on the table, as it had been cold in the Old Yard at midnight with an arm of a string under her heel. She put the cord over her head.

She had been told what it was. It did not feel any different. She had rather thought that it would not.

Rhea Sorn got up off the bench, and put her hands in her pockets.

"That's the Line's business done, then, and the Library's," she said. "I've some of my own. Not here. Tomorrow, at six, in my yard. Bring Finch."

The board in the Placement Hall basement had a crowd round it on Friday, and for once nobody was reading over anybody's shoulder to see whether a name was there.

*The Meridian Collegiate Circuit, 2680. The Regional Gates: 1. Halden License. 2. Old Four. 3. Team Twenty-Eight. 4. T7.*

"They've gone," said Perrin.

He was standing very upright, since he was strapped again, from the armpits to the hips.

"The brackets. Off Old Four, and off us. *Second step, in its own right.*" He read it with his lips. "I said in the ninth month I didn't mind them. I said it'd be like losing a tooth." He considered. "It is. I keep feeling for it."

"There's more under," said Kellan.

*The first company goes forward to the National Gates, at Saltmere, in the third month, 2681.*

And under that, in a level upright hand that Kiva knew from the foot of a notice in the spring:

*Rowan, K., Third Division, the Crooked Line: seated. Catcher. Not thereby classified. Y. Crest.*

Nobody moved their kit away. Nobody had, for some weeks. She noticed that she had stopped looking to see.

"*Seated,*" said Anwen. "On what?"

"There's somebody wants to tell you," said a voice at Kiva's elbow.

It was Wilf Marten, the long apologetic boy in Wren House green. He said it to the board, as he said most things.

"She's at the Pit. On the stair. The healer has her going up and down it, and she says she'll do it where she was pinned or not at all." He shifted his feet. "She asked would you come. She said she'd not be carried up a hill to ask twice."

---

The Pit stood empty in the afternoon, with its dead lamp rim and its race running dark underneath, and Selka Dray was halfway up its north stair on two sticks.

She had her leg in a lighter board now, from the knee down. She went up a step, and stood, and went up another. She did not look round when Kiva came in at the bottom. She came down, and went up again, before she stopped.

"That's where it had me," she said. She nodded at the cinders below, without looking at them. "From here you can see the whole of it. I never could, from the floor."

Kiva looked.

It was smaller than she had been carrying it about in her head, as the Old Yard had been smaller by day. The lamp rim stood dead round the floor in its iron ring, and the race went by underneath with a sound like somebody running a bath in another house. Somebody had raked the cinders. There was no mark on them of four grey shapes standing a square, or of a girl lying on her back with a cord across her leg, or of anything at all.

"They've raked it," she said.

"They rake it every week. It's a floor." Dray shifted her grip on the sticks. "Don't come up. I can see you thinking of it. If you put a hand under my elbow, I shall fall down the rest of this stair on purpose, and you'll have that to carry."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You had the look of somebody about to be useful." She went up one more step, and stood, breathing through her nose. "Marten said that you've had your arm back a week. All of it?"

"As before."

"Good." She did not say it warmly. She said it as one says that a bag is sealed. "Then I'll not be beating half a catcher at Saltmere. I'd not have cared for that."

"Marten said you'd something to say."

"I have." She turned on the step, carefully, and sat down on it, with the board out in front of her. "My rule goes to Saltmere. Any candidate with more than one door gets read off a sanctioned mast by the Chair's reader, at the National Gates as at the Regional. With my name on it. The Chair granted it this morning."

"I heard."

"And I objected to you twice, and I've not changed my mind. I'd not have anybody think that a chair and a cold night brought me round." The grey eyes were as sunk as ever, and quite steady. "But I asked the Chair what you were seated on. She told me. It's not the three doors. It's that you stood on a lid and told a watch officer every piece of the thing that wasn't yours, and who had it, before you'd put a hand up." Her jaw moved. "I'd have seated you on that. I'd not have seated you on the other."

"It's the same night."

"It isn't the same thing. I've been told that you're particular about that."

Kiva stood at the foot of the stair. She did not go up. It was not her stair.

"Twenty-Eight goes to Saltmere," said Dray. "So does Seven. I shall be on my feet by the third month. I mean to beat you in a lane there, on a declared call, with a needle on you."

"I'd expect nothing else."

"That's a Halden man's phrase."

"I know. I've borrowed it."

Something happened to Selka Dray's mouth. It was not a smile. It was the place where one would have been, on somebody else.

"Go away," she said. "I've a stair to do. I intend to argue with it."

---

There was no van out at six. There was only the Graywater hall yard, and the hooded lamp, and a tall woman sitting on an upturned tub with her elbows on her knees. Pike stood behind her with his hands at his back.

"I told you on the cinders that I wasn't offering you anything," said Rhea. "I wasn't. You were seventeen, with a hand in a cloth, and your team was attached to me." She did not get up. "You're seventeen still. The brackets came off this morning. So I've nothing on you any longer but what I choose to put, and I've thought for a week about how little that ought to be."

Pru was standing a pace behind Kiva's shoulder with her hands in her sleeves.

"Here's all of it," said Rhea. "Catcher's second, to Pike. The night van, two nights in the month, from the new year, when your healer lets you light. Dark hands. No door on my floor that isn't on your certificate. Finch with you for the cord, if Finch will. If Seven's called on a night of mine, you're Seven's and I go short. Until the end of the third month. Then we look again, and either of us may say no. No seat is promised."

It was a small thing. It was an edge of a wall and a lamp, said out long.

"I've put the ends on it on purpose," said Rhea. "It's got *either of us* in it. Notice that. I can learn."

"What's a catcher's second?"

"She kneels at my other hem," said Pike. "She sets nothing. She calls nothing. She watches a man who's been thought a great hand for six-and-twenty years, and she tells him afterward what he did that he didn't say." He nearly smiled. "I'm told there's a deal of it. I've a first-year to thank for the notion."

"Show her," said Rhea.

There was a sheet already folded by the stove, and the hoist, and a sandbag. Pike threw the sheet as a man shakes out a blanket on his own bed. He knelt at the north hem. Kiva went to the south one, opposite him, and knelt, and kept her hands on her knees, which was the hardest thing that she had done all week.

"Bag," said Pike.

The sand came down off the hoist and hit the canvas.

"*Dark. Corners.*"

He did not hurry. He brought his corner in low, and the hall's two night-van hands brought theirs, and the neck came up between them like a mouth closing. It was beautifully done. Kiva watched his hands, and his knees, and his face, and she did not open her mouth.

"Sealed," said Pike, and sat back on his heels. "Well?"

"You put your thumb on the neck before the last corner was in," said Kiva. "On the west side. You did it before you'd looked to see that it was coming. Then you looked."

Pike was quiet a moment.

"Did I?"

"You did it twice," said Pru, from the stove. "I saw the second."

"Then I've been doing it for twenty years and nobody's ever said." He looked at his own thumb, quite without resentment, as a man looks at a tool that has turned out to have a habit. "It's never cost me a bag."

"It's never had to," said Rhea. "That's what a second's for."

"And Pru?"

"Ask her. I did, an hour ago, at her mother's door."

"I said I'd have to think," said Pru. "Then I said yes so quickly that I bit my tongue. Then I said I'd only come if I was asked by you as well, since I'm not your cord. I'm a catcher with a sheet. I've got a red tag in a cold room." She went scarlet. "That came out bigger than I meant."

"It came out the right size," said Kiva.

She had wanted a thing like this since she sat on the Placement Hall steps in the spring and watched a boy with dark eyes shake hands under three striped awnings. She had wanted it on ordinary terms. She had thought that when it came she would know what to say.

*What's it for, and what's it cost?* That was Anwen's. She found that she had it ready, and that it was a better thing to have ready than an answer.

"Two things," she said. "Before I say. The first is that I hear the price before, on your floor. Every time. Not after."

"That's how I do it now," said Rhea. "I'd look a fool stopping."

"And I can't say yes for the four. If Seven wants me those nights for a thing that isn't a call, a floor, or a lane before Saltmere, they've the first word. I'd have to ask them. I don't know what they'll say."

Rhea looked at her for some time.

"I had a speech for this," she said. "In case you said yes at once. It was about not being grateful." She got up off the tub, stiffly. "I shan't want it. Go home. Ask your four. Tell me on the first of the month." She put her hands in her pockets. "I'll say one thing, since I'm told that I'm allowed a piece of advice a season. You'll hate being a second. You'll kneel at a hem and see every single thing that you'd do differently, and you're not to open your mouth until the bag's sealed."

"I've been a corner. For Pru."

"I heard. I heard that you were corrected."

"All evening."

"Good," said Rhea Sorn. "That's about right."

Seven closed its season on Saturday the 15th, at the Rowan kitchen table.

Most of it they had heard before. Seven-tenths of the call drams were spent, Anwen's forty-one hundredths on a wall the most of that and Kiva's share none. The three-door line had gone whole on the 5th. The four drams lent had come home on the 4th.

"Credited from Stopgate, by the foreman's pot," said Kellan, when he came to what was new. "To Talley, by name, a head's share: one and eight-tenths. To the team, seven and two."

"That's seven and eight-tenths with my name on it," said Jude. He was by the dresser, as ever. "In one autumn. A draft board will think I've been robbing a van."

"Held, twenty-six and fifty-three. Sealed, medical, eight-tenths. Opened once, on the 6th." Kellan sat back. "That adds."

"It can't be made up," said Anwen. "The reserve. I asked at the ash office. There isn't a pinch of plain ward-trace to be bought in Meridian; it's all had the hair since the thaw. So it's eight-tenths, and it stays eight-tenths until somebody kills something quiet." She did not sound sorry. "I'd sooner have it short and know what it was for."

"I've a line," said Jude.

They looked at him. He had never proposed one. He was on the line by name, for a share, and had been careful since the seventh month to ask for nothing else.

"Stopgate. The top of it. There are four streets on boxes off the top drum, for a month, since a plank split. It was nobody's plank. It was two knots down the hill from mine." He scratched his jaw. "A dram, at par, to the wardens' account. I asked last week what ash was for, and a man told me. It'd be a thing to have done once on purpose."

"That was my line," said Perrin. "In the seventh month. I'm not jealous. I'm flattered. It's a new feeling and I don't trust it."

"A dram to the wardens," said Anwen. "Twenty-five and fifty-three held."

"Saltmere," said Kellan. "The office hasn't posted a warrant for it. Nobody from this Circuit has been sent to the coast in a dozen years. It was two a head to a regional qualifier, and it'll not be less. Five heads. Ten, at the least. We've five-and-twenty." He looked faintly surprised. "I've never before had a figure come out with something over."

"Five?" said Jude.

"If you'll stand. By name. On your three things."

"I'll stand. I'd want to see the sea." The chipped tooth showed. "And Renn's feet on a strange floor. I've a notion they'll fall in love again."

"They're cured."

"Nobody's cured. Ask Aske."

Kiva told them then. She told them what Sorn had offered, and the two things that she had asked for in the yard, and what she would do if they said no.

"Six nights," said Kellan. "Two in a month, for three months. It costs the team nothing. If we want a floor on one of them, she's ours; Sorn said so." He frowned at the table. "I can't find the catch. I've looked for it as I'd look for one of Adlam's."

"I mind," said Perrin.

He had his hands flat on the table. He was strapped to the armpits again, and sat like a man in a barrel.

"I mind that it's Sorn, who's had us in brackets since the fifth month. I mind that it's you, again. I've looked under both of those, and there's nothing there that would bear weight." He drew what breath he could. "I've a place in my Hall's second team from the new year, in my own colors. I've a figure cut in a stone. So I'm for it. I minded properly, and at length, and it was only habit."

"What's it cost?" said Anwen.

"A hem. Pike. And my mouth shut until the bag's sealed."

"For three months."

"Yes."

Anwen considered that for some time.

"I'd pay to watch," she said.

"I'm not one of the four," said Jude, from the dresser. "So nobody's asked me. I'll say it all the same. Go. You'll come back knowing a thing about a hem that I'll have off you for nothing in a lane."

"That's the whole of your reasoning?" said Kellan.

"It's more than you had for the Halden yard."

Mara came up the shop stair while they were still laughing. She had her left hand held a little away from her side, as she had held it for a week, as though it were a thing that might yet be dropped.

"I'm told that nobody's to ask me whether she ought," she said. She looked at the table, and the five of them round it. "I notice that nobody has."

"I asked them," said Kiva.

"So I heard. Through a floor." Something happened at the corner of Mara's mouth. "It took a good deal longer than telling them would have."

---

Orla Dane had moved her crate.

It was not far. It was a pace to the north, out of the line of the south row. A watch officer had pointed out, quite civilly, that she had been sitting on a casualty road for one-and-twenty years, and she had not been able to find an answer to it.

It was Sunday evening. The yard was empty. She had the lamp on its hook.

The School had not run that lane dark with the call since a committee sat in 2603. The Line had gone on running its own, on the Sag, out of nothing more than stubbornness, and the School had let it, and had lit its yard and called that progress. Tonight the juniors were to run it dark from the sheds. She had asked for them herself, on Friday, at the bursar's door, and the bursar had said yes before she had finished, like a man glad to be asked for anything that did not come in a deal box.

The ash pole hung on the bottom wall now, on two iron hooks that a fitter had let into the brick, at the height of a man's shoulder. Under it stood the post, black and split and banded, with its old short bar back through the top where it had always been. At the post's foot, where a cross had been scratched by a girl's heel all summer, lay a square of iron with a ring in it.

*You're no help,* she had said to that post. She had said it for years.

She had had word on Friday, by a Saltmere officer on her way to the boat, who had stopped at the side gate in a green coat and asked for Dane.

*There's a pump-fitter at the harbour,* the officer had said. *Hart. One hand. He knew what our bank was before anybody told him. He said he knew it with the hand, the bad one. He said to tell you that, and to ask whether the one with three would show him what she does, if he came up the river.*

Orla had sent word south in the seventh month, and had not looked to be answered. She had said so, to a Placement Chair, across a desk, and had been rather proud of the sentence.

She found that it had been a lie of the tidying kind. She had wanted this for twenty years. She had only arranged matters so that she should not have to know it.

He had not said that he forgave her. She went over the officer's words for that, as a wall-holder goes over a wall for a thin place, and it was not there. He had said that he fitted pumps. He had said that the hand was bad. And he had asked for something, plainly, of a girl whom he had never seen, since she had a thing that he wanted.

*I did not know to teach you that.*

It seemed that somebody had taught him to ask.

There were feet in the lane. There were a good many, and they were small.

---

There were fourteen of them, in their grey smocks, in a huddle by the first shed. Kiva had come by the side gate with Anwen. She had not known that there would be children.

The sandy one with the plait was in front. She came down the lane with the rest behind her, and she stopped at Orla's crate and looked past it, at Kiva.

"Miss Dane says that we're to run it," she said. "Dark. From the sheds." She had her eyes on the bar. "You said that you didn't know why it was crooked. I asked you. You said that you'd wondered for four years."

"I had."

"Do you know now?"

Kiva looked at Orla.

"I'll ask it as well," said Orla. She did not get up. "Should they be told? Why it leans. It's your post, rather more than mine, this autumn."

She could have told them. She had it ready. It would have taken about a minute.

It was about a plan that had been tidied by a helpful clerk, and a post that had been aimed and called crooked, and a girl who had gone east in the Pit and had nearly gone east again. It was about there being room on a bar for two. It had a shine on it that one could have shaved by.

The child would have nodded. She would have gone round the post by her little sideways step, exactly as before, with a sentence in her head in place of a blank.

"Run it dark," said Kiva. "Make them say who has the bar."

The child frowned.

"I haven't got the bar."

"No. So you'll have to say who has. When you get there. That's the whole of it."

The child thought about that. She looked along the fourteen of them, and at the post, and at the wall.

"There's got to be two," she said slowly. "It's too long for one. Somebody'd have to be at the other end." Her face cleared. "Bettony. You're tall. You're the bar with me."

"I'm the bar with you," said a long thin girl, in a voice of doom.

They went up to the sheds in a body, arguing about it.

Kiva walked back along the lane to the first shed, and stood under the lamp, where Orla had sat for so long. Anwen came with her. Neither of them stood at the post.

"You could have told her," said Anwen.

"I had it ready."

"I know. I watched you put it in your pocket." She was not smiling. She had the look that she wore when a wall had been gone over and had no thin place in it.

Kiva looked down at her left wrist. The cuff had come undone again. A button wanted an edge found, and a hole, and one thread told from two.

"Anwen," she said. "My cuff."

She said it before she had tried. It was not hard to say. That was what surprised her. She had hated it for a fortnight, and Pike had promised her a year.

"Give it here," said Anwen.

Kiva held it out, and looked over the bottom wall while it was done.

It was a clear night, with a frost coming. Beyond thirty feet of brick the long hill went down, straight as a rule, with a lamp at every twentieth pace, to the Brickfields road and the river and the dark.

There was a night van in it now, two nights in the month, if her four said yes. There was a line on a board about the National Gates at Saltmere, in the third month. There was a pump-fitter on the south coast who knew a thing by his bad hand and wanted to see her work. Somewhere beyond all of them, in a pump house she had never heard of, a keeper had run a fever once, and never told anybody.

It was two hundred miles to the coast. She had seen the map on the wall of the watch room. The road went round the Sag, and doubled at a ferry, and followed a river that had never in its life gone where a clerk would have drawn it.

"Your cuff's done," said Anwen. "You may have your arm back."

Kiva took it back.

The lamp was put out. Orla did it herself, reaching up from the crate with her stick, and the Old Yard went as black as the inside of a boot.

They came.

They came out of the dark at the sheds' end in a pounding, uneven line, fourteen pairs of boots on packed dirt, with nothing lit. They went by Kiva and Anwen like a flock of starlings going over a roof. Kiva felt the air of them on her face.

She heard their palms find the posts, left and right. She heard a yelp, and somebody saying *sorry* to the wood, and somebody else saying *that's not a post, that's me,* and a scuffle, and a giggle that was put out at once like a match.

"*Hands out!*" That was a boy, near the back, very stern. "*You're meant to have your hands out!*"

"*I've got my hands out! I've got them on you!*"

Beside her Anwen made a sound that might have been a cough. On the crate, Orla had her palm flat on the dirt, as a woman feels for a tram coming, and she did not say a word.

They went on. The noise of them drew off down the lane toward the far end, where the wall was, and the gap, and a black post that stood a pace out from its row with a bar through its top. A small, breathless, sandy voice went up ahead of them before the eighth.

"*Turn! Left!*"

"*Left!*" said the ones behind, more or less together, and turned.

Not one of them struck the post.

Kiva heard two of them reach it. She heard hands find old iron in the dark, and somebody gasp, and a second pair of boots arrive, and then the child's voice again, very high and very certain, at the end of the lane.

"*Bettony has the bar! And me! We've got the bar!*"
