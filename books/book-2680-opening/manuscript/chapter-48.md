# Chapter 48 — Kiva: Why It Is Crooked

There were more than seven in the room on Thursday, and that had not happened in the Ferren's time.

Kiva knew the oak table, and the window over the levee, and the black box. She had not seen the bench along the wall with anybody on it. Orla sat there now, with her stick. Beside her was a small upright woman with two pairs of spectacles, and beside the Reader, very straight, with his cap on his knee and a roll of tape that he had not been asked to bring, was Noa.

The School's bursar had the end of the bench. He had a flat deal box on his lap, tied with tape, and he held it as a man holds a thing that he would sooner not open.

"We go by our watches in here," said the Ferren. "That's for the seven. The rest of you are whoever you are, and I'll thank you to be it briefly." She had her hands folded on the table. "Two things. The first is ours. River Flats."

The broad, careful man had a paper in his cuff, as he had once before. Kiva had been told about that paper. He laid it on the oak.

"I moved a recall in the seventh month," he said. "I'd have the bearer know that I was the one, and that I thought myself right. I said that the plate had never been carried by anybody with so many people looking at her. I said that it should lie in its box until the season was read." He did not look at her. He looked at the paper. "The season's read. I've a second motion. I'd sooner make it myself than have it made at me."

"Go on."

"The watch doesn't choose a bearer. It's on the first leaf, and I'd not alter it. But the Line keeps a roll, and the watch may say who's on it." He unfolded the sheet. "*That K. Rowan be entered on the Home Line's roll, as catcher. Not a seat. Not a wage, save a line-hand's day rate when she's called. To be called by a Line foreman, with her sponsor's leave while she has a sponsor, for the work that she's shown: to set a mark, to read ground, to call a count that is hers and name the ones that are not.*" He cleared his throat. "*For cause written. The cause is two entries in the Directions to the Unplaced, and what's in the third column of each.*"

"The Sag will read the cause," said the Ferren.

Vane read it. He read both directions, in his outdoor voice, with the cost before and the cost after, and he did not lean on any word.

Seven hands went down flat on the oak.

The Lantern's went first. She looked rather pleased to have a thing that she could be quick about.

"Entered," said the Ferren. "Sag. Write it where E.K. wrote the other. There's room; nobody's used the page in two hundred years."

He wrote. He turned the old ledger so that Kiva could see the line come.

*Rowan, K. Entered on the Line's roll as catcher, autumn 2680. Hand as before.*

She had written those last three words herself, on a Sunday night in the ninth month, in a canvas book, since nobody else had a place to put them. It was a different thing to watch them go down in a watch officer's hand, under a line of 2471.

She found that her eyes had stung. She kept them on the ink until they had stopped.

"It isn't a rank," said the Ferren. "You'll not get a better seat at anything by it. It's a list of people whom we send for. I've been on it since I was nineteen. I've never been thanked for it, and nor will you be." The river pilot's eyes went to the bench. "Now the second thing, which is not ours, and which a gentleman has been sitting on like a hen."

The bursar got up.

He was a thin worried man, and he had been a good deal thinner and more worried since a Saturday night in a yard.

"I asked this watch in the seventh month whether I might borrow its plate for the School's anniversary case," he said. "I was answered out of our own fourth edition. I didn't care for the answer. I'd the ninth, which says that the plate of the first teachers is preserved by the School." He put the deal box on the table. "It's been in the conservation press for longer than I've had the keys. The Reader has asked to see it every spring for thirty years. I've told her every spring that it was being conserved. So had the bursar before me."

"Thirty-one," said the Reader.

"Thirty-one. I beg your pardon." He untied the tape. "I was shown a post last week. It had been retained for ceremonial continuity since 2603, and it turned out to be the handle of a gate. I thought that I had better go and look at what else we retain."

He lifted the lid.

There was a bed of yellowed wool. In it lay a disk the width of a palm, of a dull white, with nine rings cut into its face.

It was plaster.

Kiva did not need to be told. One could see the grain of it, and a chip at the edge where it had been dropped. It was a cast.

"There's a label," said the bursar. His voice was not steady. "On the underside of the lid. I've read it eleven times."

He read it a twelfth.

"*Cast of the plate of nine rings that was carried by the first teachers. Taken by leave of the bearer, for the School's case, that students may know its look. The plate went out again with the Home Line the same day, on its cord. It is not the School's and never was. We do not show the plate. We show this.*" He put the lid down. "Somebody stopped reading that label, and somebody else wrote a ninth edition. I don't think that either of them meant a lie. I think that they tidied."

Nobody at the table spoke. It was a word that had been used a good deal lately.

"I've a request," said the Reader.

She had risen. She was not much taller standing.

"I can't lay a hand on the Line's plate. I've read the watch's ruling of the first of the seventh month; I had it copied. No hand but the bearer's. I ask whether the bearer may lay it beside that cast, in her own hand, for as long as it takes an old woman to count to nine."

"That wants a vote," said the Ferren. "To show it."

Seven hands were flat already. They had not been lifted.

"Then that's voted. Miss Rowan. If you're willing. It's yours to refuse; nobody in here chose you."

Kiva drew the cord out from her collar.

She had worn it for four years. She had shown it to a steward once, with a paper, and to a watch officer, and had never laid it on a table in a lit room for anybody to look at. It came over her head, and she held it in her right palm, and set the back of that hand down on the oak beside the box.

It lay there, dark, with its nine grooves.

The Reader bent over it. She had both pairs of spectacles on. She did not touch.

"Nine," she said. "And nine. The fifth ring's wider than the rest; so is the cast's. There's a flaw across the third, at about four o'clock, like a hair. Look at the plaster, child. No. You look. I've seen what I came for."

Kiva looked. There was a raised thread across the third ring of the cast, where the plaster had gone into a scratch.

There was a scratch on the disk. She had known it since she was thirteen. She had thought that she had made it herself, on a stair.

"It's the same," said Noa.

He had come off the bench without being asked, and stood a pace behind the Reader, with his hands shut behind his back so that nobody should think he meant to reach.

"I drew that. In the sixth month. I drew the nine rings off the plate in the fourth edition of the Founders' History, with the fifth one wider, and I wrote beside it, *Is hers the same?*" His narrow face had gone red. "I never asked to see. It wasn't mine to ask. Then the watch said that it wasn't anybody's. I've had it on a page ever since, with a question mark."

"You may take the question mark off," said the Reader. She straightened, a hand in the small of her back. "I'll say it, since I'm the only one in this room whose trade it is to say that one old thing is another. That is the plate of nine rings that the first teachers carried. It was never preserved by anybody. It was *released.* It has gone from hand to hand by appointment, with a watch to witness and not to choose, for as long as there has been a Line, and it has come down that road to a catcher of seventeen who was named by a woman who liked a question that she'd asked." She took off the upper spectacles. "I have asked the School for it for thirty-one years. I have been looking at a girl's collar since the sixth month."

"The tenth edition will say so," said the bursar. "Out of the fourth. Word for word. I've the printer on Monday."

"*It is released to one who carries it,*" said Orla, from the bench. Her voice was rough. "*We have not been told why and have not thought it ours to ask.* I learned that off a first-year who'd been to a library. I was twenty years on the Line and nobody'd told me."

"What's it for?" said Kiva.

She had not meant to ask. She had asked it of a levee catcher's line, and a watch officer, and an empty kitchen. It was out before she had thought that this was a room where somebody might know.

The Reader looked at her quite kindly.

"I can tell you whose it's been. I can tell you that it's the one in the picture. That's the whole of what I've given you, and you had most of it already." She folded the spectacles. "The first book's lost. I've looked for that as well."

"I asked a thing in the summer," said the Ferren. "I'd had four-and-thirty years of *what does it do.* I said that I'd begun to wonder what else was happening when it did." She was looking at the dark disk on the girl's palm. "It's had two bearers put *warm* in my book, and each put it once, after a first crossing, and each put *cold* for every crossing since. That's all that I know. I'm seventy-one in the spring. I've decided that I can bear to leave a question in a book with nothing under it."

Kiva closed her hand.

It was cold. It had been cold on a table in front of twelve people, as it had been cold in the Old Yard at midnight with an arm of a string under her heel. She put the cord over her head.

She had been told what it was. It did not feel any different. She had rather thought that it would not.

The board in the Measure Hall basement had a crowd round it on Friday, and for once nobody was reading over anybody's shoulder to see whether a name was there.

*The Meridian Collegiate Circuit, 2680. The season is read.*

*The Regional Measure: 1. Halden License. 2. Old Four. 3. Team Twenty-Eight. 4. T7.*

"They've gone," said Perrin.

He was standing very upright, since he was strapped again, from the armpits to the hips.

"The brackets. Off Old Four, and off us. *Second step, in its own right.*" He read it with his lips. "I said in the ninth month I didn't mind them. I said it'd be like losing a tooth." He considered. "It is. I keep feeling for it."

"There's a line under," said Kellan.

There were three.

The first was a clerk's. *The first company goes forward to the National Measure, at Saltmere, in the third month, 2681. Entries under each team's own hand.*

The second was level and upright, and Kiva knew it from the foot of a notice in the spring.

*Entered by the Chair. Rowan, K., Third Division, the Crooked Line: seated. No longer provisional. Catcher. Her sponsor's line and every condition on her certificate stand as written. She is not thereby classified. I still have no column. I have a page, and it has a second line on it. Y. Crest.*

Nobody moved their kit away. Nobody had, for some weeks. She noticed that she had stopped looking to see.

"*Seated,*" said Anwen. "On what?"

"On having said who had the corner," said a voice behind them.

It was a hard, exact voice. It came from about the height of Kiva's elbow.

Selka Dray was in the infirmary's chair. Wilf Marten had the handles. She had her leg out before her in its board, and a rug, and a face like a shut door.

"I've read her page. The Chair let me, since I asked, and since my name's on a rule. It doesn't say what you did with three doors. It says that you stood on a lid and told a watch officer every piece of the thing that wasn't yours, and who had it, before you'd put a hand up." Her jaw moved. "I'd have seated you on that. I'd not have seated you on the other."

"It's the same night."

"It isn't the same thing. I've been told that you're particular about that."

"The third line's hers," said Marten. He said it to the board, apologetically, as he said most things.

It was in pencil, fair-copied by a clerk. *On the request of S. Dray, blade, Team Twenty-Eight: that at the National Measure any candidate with more than one door be read from a sanctioned mast, by a reader of this office, as at the Regional. Granted, and sent to Saltmere under seal. She states that she has objected to the candidate twice and has not changed her mind. She states that she called a count for her on the 5th because she was the one who could. Both are entered.*

"I said that I'd write it," said Kiva. "You said that you'd take my word."

"I did. Then I thought that a thing like that ought to be in a hand that isn't yours." Dray looked up at her. The grey eyes were as sunk as ever, and quite steady. "Twenty-Eight goes to Saltmere. So does Seven. I shall be on my feet by the third month. I mean to beat you in a lane there, on a declared count, with a needle on you, in front of whoever they've got instead of eleven hundred people."

"I'd expect nothing else."

"That's a Halden man's phrase."

"I know. I've been keeping it."

Something happened to Selka Dray's mouth. It was not a smile. It was the place where one would have been, on somebody else.

"Push," she said, to Marten. "I've a healer at eleven. He says that I'm to bend it, and I intend to argue."

Rhea Sorn sent a boy with a card that evening. It was four words long. *Yard. Six. Bring Finch.*

There was no van out. There was only the hall yard, and the hooded lamp, and a tall woman sitting on an upturned tub with her elbows on her knees. Pike stood behind her with his hands at his back.

"I told you on the cinders that I wasn't offering you anything," said Rhea. "I wasn't. You were seventeen, with a hand in a cloth, and your team had my name on its entry." She did not get up. "You're seventeen still. The brackets came off this morning. So I've nothing on you any longer but what I choose to put, and I've thought for a week about how little that ought to be."

She held out a card, slate blue, with a good deal more than twelve words on it.

Kiva took it in her right hand, and read it under the lamp.

*Rowan, K. Catcher's second, to Pike. The night van, two nights in the month, from the first of the new year, when her healer's card is up. Dark hands. No door on my floor that is not on her certificate. Finch with her, for the cord, if Finch will. If T7 is called on a night of mine, she is T7's and I go short. Until the last of the third month. Then we look again, and either of us may say no. No seat is promised. R.S.*

It was a small thing. It was an edge of a wall and a lamp, written out long.

"That's all of it," said Rhea. "I've put the ends on it on purpose. I burned a card once with two words on it about you. One of the two was *mine.* I'd had no business writing it, and I knew it the same night." The pale eyes came up. "This one's got *either of us* on it. I'd like you to notice that I can learn."

"What's a catcher's second?"

"She kneels at my other hem," said Pike. "She sets nothing. She calls nothing. She watches a man who's been thought a great hand for six-and-twenty years, and she tells him afterward what he did that he didn't say aloud." He nearly smiled. "I'm told there's a deal of it. I've a first-year to thank for the notion."

"And Pru?"

"Ask her. I did, an hour ago, at her mother's door."

Pru was standing a pace behind Kiva's shoulder with her hands in her sleeves.

"I said I'd have to think," she said. "Then I said yes so quickly that I bit my tongue. Then I said I'd only come if I was asked by you as well, since I'm not your cord. I'm a catcher with a sheet. I've got a red tag in a cold room." She went scarlet. "That came out bigger than I meant."

"It came out the right size," said Kiva.

She looked at the card again.

She had wanted a thing like this since she sat on the Measure Hall steps in the spring and watched a boy with steady eyes shake hands under three striped awnings. She had wanted it on ordinary terms. She had thought that when it came she would know what to say.

*What's behind it?* That was Anwen's question. She found that she had it ready, and that it was a better thing to have ready than an answer.

"Two things," she said. "Before I say. I'd like the *before* column read out, each night, on the slate, as you read it on the fifth. Whatever's in it. To all of us, not to me."

"That's on the slate already. It's ruled. I'd look a fool rubbing it out."

"And I can't say yes for the four. If Seven wants me those nights for a thing that isn't a call, a floor, or a lane before Saltmere, they've the first word. I'd have to ask them. I don't know what they'll say."

Rhea looked at her for some time.

"I had a speech for this," she said. "In case you said yes at once. It was about not being grateful." She got up off the tub, stiffly. "I shan't want it. Take the card home. Ask your four. Bring it back signed or bring it back torn, and tell me which on the first of the month." She put her hands in her pockets. "I'll say one thing, since I'm told that I'm allowed a piece of advice a season. You'll hate being a second. You'll kneel at a hem and see every single thing that you'd do differently, and you're not to open your mouth until the bag's sealed."

"I've been a corner. For Pru."

"I heard. I heard that you were corrected eleven times."

"Fourteen, by the end."

"Good," said Rhea Sorn. "That's about the right number."

Four of them signed the book on Saturday the 15th, at the Rowan kitchen table, and Kellan read the whole of it, since it was the last of the season.

Most of it they had heard before: seven-tenths of the call drams spent, Anwen's forty-one hundredths on a wall the most of that and Kiva's share none; the three-door line, spent whole on the 5th; the four drams lent and come home on the 4th.

"*Credited from Stopgate, by the foreman's pot: 9.0,*" he said, when he came to what was new. "*To Talley, J., Kill, by name, a head's share: 1.8. To the book: 7.2.*"

"That's seven and eight-tenths with my name on it," said Jude. He was by the dresser, as ever. "In one autumn. A draft board will think I've been robbing a van."

"*Held: 26.53. Sealed, medical, W: 0.8. Opened once; see the 6th.*" Kellan blotted it. "That adds. I've done it three ways."

"It can't be made up," said Anwen. "The reserve. I asked at the ash office. There isn't a pinch of plain ward-trace to be bought in Meridian; it's all had the hair since the thaw. So it's eight-tenths, and it stays eight-tenths until somebody kills something quiet." She did not sound sorry. "I'd sooner have it short and know what it was for."

"I've a line," said Jude.

They looked at him. He had never proposed one. He was on the book by name, for a share, and had been careful since the seventh month to ask for nothing else.

"Stopgate. The top of it. There are four streets on boxes off drum twenty-nine, for a month, since a plank split. It was nobody's plank. It was two knots down the hill from mine." He scratched his jaw. "A dram, at par, to the wardens' account. I asked last week what ash was for, and a man told me. I'd like to have done it once on purpose."

"That was my line," said Perrin. "In the seventh month. I'm not jealous. I'm flattered. It's a new feeling and I don't trust it."

Anwen wrote it. *Returned at par to the wardens of Stopgate, for the boxes on drum twenty-nine: 1.0. Held: 25.53.*

"Saltmere," said Kellan. "The office hasn't posted a warrant for it. Nobody from this Circuit has been sent to the coast in eleven years. It was two a head to a regional qualifier, and it'll not be less." He did the sum aloud, since he could not help it. "Five heads. Ten, at the least. We've five-and-twenty. I've never before had a figure come out with something over."

"Five?" said Jude.

"If you'll stand. By name. On your three things."

"I'll stand. I'd want to see the sea." The chipped tooth showed. "And Renn's feet on a strange floor. I've a notion they'll fall in love again."

"They're cured."

"Nobody's cured. Ask Aske."

Kiva had the card in her coat, where she had carried it since six on Friday.

She laid it on the table, on the green cover, and read it to them. She read every word, down to *No seat is promised.* Then she told them the two things that she had asked for in the yard, and what she would do if they said no.

"Six nights," said Kellan. "Two in a month, for three months. It costs the book nothing. If we want a floor on one of them, she's ours; it's written." He turned the card over, to see whether there was anything on the back. "I can't find the catch. I've looked for it as I'd look for one of Adlam's."

"I mind," said Perrin.

He had his hands flat on the table. He was strapped to the armpits again, and sat like a man in a barrel.

"I mind that it's Sorn, who's had us in brackets since the fifth month. I mind that it's you, again, with a card. I've looked under both of those, and there's nothing there that would bear weight." He drew what breath he could. "I've a place in my Hall's second team from the new year, in my own colors. I've a figure cut in a stone. So I'm signing, and I'd like it noted that I minded properly, and at length, and that it was only habit."

"What's behind it?" said Anwen.

"A hem. Pike. And my mouth shut until the bag's sealed."

"For three months."

"Yes."

Anwen considered that for some time.

"I'd pay to watch," she said. She signed the back of a chartered company's card, in brown ink, which nobody at the ash office had a rule for. The others signed under her.

Mara came up the shop stair as they were blotting it. She had eleven sheets under her arm, tied with tape, and she carried them in her left hand.

"That's for the coast boat on Monday," she said. "*How the Ferren bank was taken out of step, with what was got wrong first.* I've put Rusk's firebreak in it, by his leave. He said that if I left it out, he'd come and write it in himself." She laid the bundle on the dresser and put the brass weight on it. Then she looked at the table, and the book, and four names on a slate-blue card. "I'm told that nobody's to ask me whether she ought. I notice that nobody has."

"I asked them," said Kiva.

"So I heard. Through a floor." Something happened at the corner of Mara's mouth. "It took a good deal longer than telling them would have."

Orla Dane had moved her crate.

It was not far. It was a pace to the north, out of the line of the south row. A watch officer had pointed out, quite civilly, that she had been sitting on a casualty road for one-and-twenty years, and she had not been able to find an answer to it.

It was Sunday evening. The yard was empty. She had the lamp on its hook.

There was a bar on the bottom wall now. It hung on two iron hooks that a fitter had let into the brick, at the height of a man's shoulder, an ash pole as thick as a wrist. Under it stood the post, black and split and banded. At the post's foot, where a cross had been scratched for her by a girl's heel all summer, lay a square of iron with a ring in it.

*You're no help,* she had said to that post. She had said it for years.

She took the card out of her coat again.

It had come on Friday, by the coast boat. It was a plain card, of the cheap sort that pump-fitters used for their accounts, and it had four lines on it in a round, rather childish hand that she would have known in the dark.

*Your letter was four lines. So is this.*

*I am a pump-fitter. Our harbour bank went into step on the 17th, at twenty past two. I knew what it was before anybody told me. I knew it with the hand, the bad one.*

*Tell the one who writes everything down that I should like to read it.*

*J.H.*

She had not written to be answered. She had said so, to a Placement Chair, across a desk, and had been rather proud of the sentence.

She found that it had been a lie of the tidying kind. She had wanted this for twenty years. She had only arranged matters so that she should not have to know it.

He had not said that he forgave her. She examined the card for that, as a wall-holder goes over a wall for a thin place, and it was not there. He had said that he fitted pumps. He had said that the hand was bad. And he had asked for something, plainly, of a girl whom he had never seen, since she had a thing that he wanted.

*I did not know to teach you that.*

It seemed that somebody had taught him to ask.

There were feet in the lane. There were a good many, and they were small.

She put the card away.

---

They had sent the juniors. There were fourteen of them, in their grey smocks, in a huddle by the first shed, and with them a bursar's clerk holding a proof sheet as though it might go off.

Kiva had come by the side gate with Anwen. She had not known that there would be children.

"The tenth edition," said Orla. She did not get up. "They've set the lane. Seven columns. They've put the seventh back, in the same type as the other six, and I'm told that it cost the printer a night's sleep to make it fit." She took the sheet from the clerk and held it out. "The bursar wants to know whether anything should be added. He asked me. I said that I wasn't the one who'd found it out twice."

Kiva took it in her right hand. Anwen, without being asked, held the lamp.

It was all there. *The lane with the crooked posts. At Meridian, under the south wall, on the line of the hill drain.* It had the nine posts, and the sluice, and the bar. *Call the turn before the eighth, aloud, and say who has the bar.* It had a collarbone, and a cellar in the Hythe.

And at the right, in print, with two initials that nobody in Meridian could put a face to:

*Why: because the road isn't straight. S.V.*

She had thought about that line a good deal in ten days. She had thought that when she understood it she would have something to say.

She did. It was about a plan that had been tidied by a helpful clerk, and a post that had been aimed and called crooked, and a girl who had gone east in the Pit and had nearly gone east again. It was about there being room on a bar for two. It had a shine on it that one could have shaved by.

She did not write any of it.

"There's a pencil in my coat," she said. "The left pocket. I can't get it out with this hand; the pocket's got a button."

"I know which pocket," said Anwen. She got it out.

Orla made room. Kiva laid the proof on the crate beside her, and held it flat with the heel of her left hand, and the last two fingers. The first two lay on the paper, and told her that there was paper.

She did not touch the last column. It had been right for five hundred years. It had only wanted the other six.

She wrote in the fourth, where it said *Went wrong,* under the two who had come to the bar unnamed.

*2680. The mark was called east, since east had served once before. East was the drain. The step was tried first, and it said so.*

She passed over the fifth, where the collarbone was. And in the sixth, under *Second try:*

*Called north, aloud, before. Said back by two. Three others had the corner after six. Two had the bar. One had the count, from a chair. K.R.*

She read it over. It did not look like wisdom. It looked like a meter reading.

"You've not put what it cost," said Orla.

"It's in Vane's ledger. And Aske's card. And the green book." She gave the pencil back to Anwen. "I'd be putting it in a fourth place so as to have it where students could see."

"That's a reason not to."

"It's the reason I've got."

Orla looked at the two lines for some time. Her crooked face did something complicated, and settled.

"I asked you in the spring to show me six," she said. "I was rather pleased with it. I'd no notion what I was asking for." She folded the proof along its crease and gave it to the clerk. "That goes as it is. Tell the bursar that there's a hand in two of his columns that isn't a founder's, and that if he tidies it, I shall come up his stair."

The clerk fled.

The juniors had been edging down the lane. They had got as far as the fourth post, and Kiva went down the lane to them. The sandy one with the plait was in front, and she had her eyes on the bar.

"Miss Dane says that we're to run it," she said. "Dark. From the sheds. She says that it's in the manual again." She looked at Kiva. "You said that you didn't know why it was crooked. I asked you. You said that you'd wondered for four years."

"I had."

"Do you know now?"

It was the plainest question that Kiva had been asked since a watch officer had held up a slate.

She could have told her. It would have taken about a minute. The child would have nodded, and gone round the post by her little sideways step, exactly as before, with a sentence in her head in place of a blank.

*Then they should go and find out,* somebody had once said, of students who laughed. Kiva had never heard it. She had only been walking toward it since she was thirteen.

"I know what it's for," she said. "I'd sooner you told me. Run it. From the sheds, in the dark. There are two things to say aloud before you reach the eighth post, and I'll give you the first. *Turn.* And which way."

"What's the second?"

"Who has the bar."

"*I* haven't got the bar."

"No. So you'll have to say who has. Before you get there. That's the whole of it."

The child thought about that. She looked along the fourteen of them.

"There's got to be two," she said slowly. "It's too long for one. Somebody'd have to be at the other end." Her face cleared. "Bettony. You're tall. You're the bar with me. Say it back."

"I'm the bar with you," said a long thin girl, in a voice of doom.

They went up to the sheds in a body, arguing about it.

Kiva walked back along the lane to the first shed, and stood under the lamp, where Orla had sat for twenty-one years. Anwen came with her. Neither of them stood at the post.

"You could have told her," said Anwen.

"I had it ready."

"I know. I watched you put it in your pocket." She was not smiling. She had the look that she wore when a wall had been gone over and had no thin place in it. She put the pencil back in the left pocket, and did up the button, without comment, as one does up a child's. "I told Mace on Thursday. About coming off a join. He'd it on his board already. He'd written, *Said so as she went.* He's left my name where it was."

"I knew that he would."

"You didn't. You hoped. They aren't the same."

"No."

The lamp hissed. At the far end of the lane, somebody small was counting three, aloud, for courage.

Kiva looked down at her left wrist. The cuff had come undone again. A button wanted an edge found, and a hole, and one thread told from two.

"Anwen," she said. "My cuff."

She said it before she had tried. It was not hard to say. That was what surprised her. She had hated it for a fortnight, and Pike had promised her a year.

"Give it here," said Anwen.

They came.

They came out of the dark at the sheds' end in a pounding, uneven line, fourteen pairs of boots on packed dirt, with nothing lit. They went by the lamp like a flock of starlings. A small, breathless, sandy voice went up ahead of them.

"*Turn! Left! Bar's me and Bettony!*"

"*Bar's me!*" That was the voice of doom. "*Say it back!*"

"*Turn left! Bar's them!*" said twelve others, more or less together.

Not one of them struck the post.

Two of them reached up to the wall. Kiva heard the ash pole come off its hooks, and a great deal of advice. She heard it go home in the socket. She did not hear it turn. They had been told that a gate was not to be shut on a dry night, and for once in the history of that School a junior had believed a thing because she had been given the reason with it.

"That's the first time that lane's been run to the bar since 2390," said Orla. She had her palm flat on the dirt beside her crate, as a woman feels for a tram. "I've sat here one-and-twenty years. I never thought to hear it."

Kiva did not answer.

She was looking over the bottom wall. It was a clear night, with a frost coming. Beyond thirty feet of brick the long hill went down, straight as a rule, with a lamp at every twentieth pace, to the Brickfields road and the river and the dark.

There was a card in her coat from a company, with *either of us* on the front of it and four names on the back. There was a line on a board about a Measure at Saltmere, in the third month. There was a pump-fitter on the south coast, though she did not yet know it, who knew a thing by his bad hand, and wanted to read what she had written down. Somewhere beyond all of them, a keeper whom she had never heard of had a book with a remarks column.

It was two hundred miles to the coast. She had seen the map on the wall of the watch room. The road went round the Sag, and doubled at a ferry, and followed a river that had never in its life gone where a clerk would have drawn it.

Down the lane the juniors had found that the bar would lift two of them off the ground if four more sat on the other end.

"Your cuff's done," said Anwen. "You may have your arm back."

Kiva took it back.

She looked once at the post. It stood a pace out from its row, black against the brick, with a bar through it and six children hanging from the bar. It leaned to the left, as it always had.

It was not listening for anything. She could see that now. It had only ever been pointing at the place where the road turned, for as long as there had been somebody to say so aloud.
