# Chapter 39 — Kiva: Price First

"Say that so a foreman can use it," said Hollis Vane.

He had come down the east wall at a walk. He had his chalk in his hand, and he did not look at the square in the middle of the floor. He looked at Kiva.

"It's a sheet stood on end," she said. "The white line's the cord through the hem. There were four corners holding it up. She cut one, and the cord came off and fell on her, and the other three hauled the neck in. They're still at it. It tightens, and lets off, and tightens." She pointed, with her right hand. "She's what's keeping that corner where it is. If she's dragged out from under, there's nothing in it. The cord goes straight. Corner to corner, across the middle."

"Through the tear."

"Through the tear, and through whatever's coming up out of it. And through whoever's got her by the arms."

"That's it," said Lowen Brask. "That's what a hold does when a holder's lost. The other three don't stand there with a hole. They close." He had not taken his eyes off the cord. "It shuts like a gate. I told her so yesterday. I didn't think I'd see one do it with a girl in the hinge."

"Then she can't be pulled," said Vane. "Can it be cut?"

"It's a hem under load." That was Lowen again. "Cut a cord that three are hauling on, and both ends fly. One of them's across her."

"It'd turn a blade besides," said a level voice from the west. Rhea Sorn had not moved from behind her string, and she did not turn her head. "That's skin. I've had my edge turned on the flat of one this spring. I'd want a full second on it, lit, an arm's length from her, and I couldn't swear which way it went."

"A ward under it, then. To take the weight."

"*No.*"

It came from the gate. It was a hoarse, carrying voice, and the woman it belonged to had not left her place in the wall or dropped her hands.

"Greta Stroud. I put a ward on one of those in the spring. It didn't push my ward. It took it. It came up my arms like open ash taking a print, and I had my hands in linen for six weeks." She was looking straight ahead, at a Flask's blunt head. "Don't let anybody lay a wall on that who means to keep hold of it."

"Still-doors," said Perrin. He had gone forward a pace. "Foreman. Three of us lifted a leg off a man on the Weighbridge. I'm passed to the shoulder."

"You'd not get hold of it," said Lowen. "It's not a weight. It's a wall. Your hands go into it, and then you're Stroud."

"And if the three posts are cut?" said Vane. "All together. Drop the whole of it."

"Then the hem comes down. All of it. And she's lying under the one place where it's already on the ground."

There was a silence. In the middle of the square, behind the frost, the blue stroke had opened a little wider. Two grey knots stood above the gratings now, one on the other, and a third was coming.

"A knot about every minute and a half," said Vane. "Eight to a string. When the legs are through, it walks. Ten minutes, perhaps."

Kiva had not been asked. She had been asked what it was, and she had said.

She looked at her left hand. It hung at her side. Two fingers of it had told her that morning about a healer's thumb.

*What would take that off her?*

She knew. She had known since she said the word *hem.* Lifting was no good to it, and holding was no good. It wanted what any loose thing under load wanted, on any floor that she had ever knelt on. It wanted a pan put under it at the instant that it moved, and then it wanted carrying off, and setting down somewhere that it could do no harm.

There was nobody else in the Pit who had all three of those. There was hardly anybody in Meridian who had two.

The answer was standing ready. For once it was not a reason. It was a price, and it was hers.

"It wants catching," she said.

Vane's head came round.

"Rowan. Seven. Catcher." Her mouth was dry. "It's lying across her leg, on the north side, where it comes up toward that post. If a ward went under it there, for one tap, it'd come off her. It's under load, so it'd throw whatever caught it. I'd let it. A curved ward turns a shove, and I'd bend mine to turn that one east. I'd shut the ward before it could take hold, and go with the throw, four paces out, that way." She pointed east, along the line of the hold's south side, across empty cinders. "And put it in the ground. Then I'm the corner, and she isn't. The south wall stays where it stands, and she's on the open side of it."

"That's three doors."

"Hand, Ember, still. One after another, with nothing open between. It isn't on my paper." She made herself say the rest in the same voice, as she had said things to him once across forty paces of a spring morning. "I've done it once. In a yard, with a sandbag, on the twenty-ninth of last month. I've missed it three times. It costs six-tenths of a dram; I've a whole one, I've spent nothing. And it's two gaps. I had one yesterday that didn't land. My healer says no go before the twenty-third."

"Sponsor," said Vane. He did not raise his voice. "What's she passed for?"

Orla was at the east rail, above them. She had got up out of her seat when the cord fell. She stood with both hands on the head of her stick.

"Recovery. Dark hands. One crossing, Ember to still, on a week's rest, and she spent that yesterday in a lane." It came down flat and clear, every word of it, like something that had been said over beforehand to a post. "The other I have seen once. I have seen it missed three times, and the last miss she called before her reader did. It is not on her paper and I shall not put it there. Cade can stop it. So can I. So can she." The stick shifted. "And I'll say what I didn't say on a floor twenty years ago. I don't know that it will work."

"Healer."

Aske had come to the west gate. He had his coat off. He did not come onto the cinders; he stood at the edge of them and spoke across.

"I saw the hand at seven. She'd one gap yesterday. If she's given two today, those two fingers won't come back. I'm not guessing this time." He was not a man who shouted, and his voice was thin with the effort of carrying. "I can't speak for the rest of the hand. It may be the wrist. I'd not be surprised."

"Chair."

There was a tall grey figure at the rail beside the mast, high on the east rim.

"It is struck, Officer," said Ysra Crest. "It is yours. Nothing done under your direction is an incident of mine. My reader has two flags, and he will use them."

Vane put his chalk away.

He stood for what seemed a long time with his hands at his sides. A third knot had come clear while the three of them answered him, and the fourth stood whole behind the frost before he spoke, and when he did it was in the flat field voice that he used for a casualty, so that the whole Pit had to be still to hear it.

"Direction to Kiva Rowan, unplaced. Three doors in sequence, hand, Ember, still, declared. Cost, as her healer said it before: the first two fingers of the left hand, not to return. As she said it herself: two gaps on a hand that had one yesterday. For: a blade of Twenty-Eight under a hold's hem, and no other hand on this floor that can take it off her." He looked at her, and his voice did not change. "I have watched her miss. Do you take it?"

It was the plainest question that she had ever been asked.

She looked up, past the square, into the east terrace, two rows above the rail. Mara had stood. She had not come down. She had said that she would not, and she was keeping her word; she had her right hand pressed flat against the front of her good coat, the scarred one, as a person holds a thing that has been knocked.

*It can be done,* Mara had said. *I'd not wish it on you for all the doors in Meridian.*

"I'm frightened," said Kiva.

"So am I," said Vane. "Do you take it?"

"Yes."

---

It took two minutes to set.

"House four, off the floor."

That was a square man in black, with a foreman's slate of his own. He had come along the wall from the Halden end.

"Birch, chartered, the Halden Works' license yard. It's struck. The Works' license puts no student of ours under a Home Line direction, and I'll not pretend it does to be liked." He jerked his head. "Pettit. Yarrow. Rail. Hesketh, a gate's the Circuit's rule and no man's direction; you may stand one if you choose. Renn."

"No, sir," said Kellan.

He had stepped out of the line. His face was white, and perfectly composed.

"Rule Nineteen. A house entry for the Regional Gates is not a transfer. I asked the clerk in the sixth month. The Regional's struck. I'm Team Seven's." He turned his back on a good black jacket and faced the foreman. "Renn. Seven. Kill."

Birch looked at him for the space of a breath.

"Then you're not mine to order," he said. "I never thought you were. Mind your feet." He turned on his heel. "Sorn. I'm chartered and I've an edge. You'll want a second blade on that string, and there's nothing in my license against cutting where I stand."

"West side," said Rhea. "I'll be glad of you."

"Three posts," said Vane. "Three blades. Renn, the north-east. Talley, north-west. Gower, south-west. You come at them from outside, dark. They've their backs to you; the skin's turned in. You light after the foot, and you cut on one word, and the word's hers."

"What word?" said Jude.

"*Back,*" said Kiva. "When she's clear. I say *back,* and I give the corner to the ground and shut. If you cut then, there are four corners gone at once and nothing left to haul."

"It comes down like a sheet off a line," said Lowen. "I've seen ours dropped so, on a word. It's the only way it ever comes down kindly."

"Pike."

"Three cloths," said the square catcher. He had them under his arm already. "Behind three posts. I'll call *dark* for the lot, and I'll bag what I can reach. If two of them lie, they lie."

"Cade. You're the Carry."

"With Marten," said Perrin. "She's his. I'd not take her off him."

"Pryce."

"A gate," said Anwen. "For what's coming up. When that wall's gone it'll read, and I'd sooner it read us than a terrace." She was already looking for the ground. "Head of the sixth sheet. Fifteen paces south-west of that corner. Four wards: mine, Edda's, Hesketh's, and Twenty-Eight's. I call the step."

"It's a string. You've held a rung's worth."

"I've held the end of a wall for four weeks with nothing in front of me. I know what I'm asking for." She turned her head, and came the two paces to Kiva, and stood square in front of her. "What's it for?"

There was no time for it. She asked it anyway.

"To take a hold's corner off Dray's leg," said Kiva.

"What's it cost?"

"Two fingers. For good. Perhaps more."

Anwen looked at her a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and turned for the sixth sheet.

"Anwen," said Kiva. Her eyes had stung. "The gate. On *back.*"

"I know where it is," said Anwen, without looking round.

"Brask," said Vane. "You know that thing's breath."

"It pulls, and then it eases. Slow, and the same each time. I've been watching the cord." Lowen had moved to where he could see the south-east corner. "I'll call it. *Out* when it pulls, *in* when it gives. She goes on the *in.* So do the blades."

"Call it," said Kiva. "If I feel it give before you say it, I'll go on the feel and not wait for you."

"Then I'll be late," said Lowen. "Go on the feel."

"Then go," said Vane.

They went.

Kiva walked out across the cinders with Perrin on her left and a thin boy in Wren House green on her right. It was eighteen paces. She laid two fingers of her right hand on her breastbone as she walked.

"Cold," she said, to nobody. There was no white. She had looked.

Selka Dray was lying on her back with her red head to the south, on the open cinders, her right leg south of the line with the rest of her, and her left leg gone under a white line as thick as a wrist. The wall rose from it. It was so thin that Kiva could see through it, the blue stroke, and five grey knots now, and the sixth all but clear.

Dray's face was the color of lard. Her eyes came round.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"Don't make a speech."

"I haven't got one." Kiva stopped a pace from her hip, on the east side, facing the way that she meant to go, and set her feet. "You're being rescued. That's Cade's line; he lends it. I take that off your leg, there, where it comes up. It throws me that way. I put it down four paces off and stand on it. When I say *planted,* they pull you. Not before."

"And if you miss?"

"Then I call *off,* and it comes back down where it was. And I'm no use to you, and I've lost the fingers for nothing."

"*Fingers?*"

"It's what it costs. He said so."

"I couldn't hear. I've had a thing on my leg." Dray shut her eyes for a moment. "It bites when it pulls. I've been lying here feeling it. It's the only thing I can do." They opened again. "I objected to you twice."

"I know. I was in the room both times."

"I'd do it again. You oughtn't to be on a floor without a needle on you." A breath, held, through a bad one. "There's a needle on you. I looked. He's got his flag out. Get on with it."

Perrin had knelt at her head, dark, and got his hands under her arms. Marten had her right shoulder. They were both south of the white line, on the open side, where the wall would not cross them when its corner went east. Neither of them said a word.

"*Out,*" said Lowen Brask, from the wall. His voice was as even as a pump.

Kiva put up her hands.

They came up low, at her left hip, a tray's width apart. Two fingers stood clear, straight and stiff, with nothing to do. She bent the ward into the bowl of a spoon and held it a hand's breadth above a girl's thigh, under the place where a white cord left the ground.

It was cold. She could feel the cold through the silver.

"Hand, to Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on the throw, and on the landing."

The cord came taut. Dray made a sound in her teeth.

Kiva felt it reach the top of its haul and hang there. She felt the hang begin to give, a hair before the word came.

"*Hand,*" she said, and slid the pan under.

"*In*—" said Lowen, a breath behind her.

For the length of the give the cord was slack. It lay in the curve of her ward, light as a clothesline, humming a little. She had it. She had the whole corner of a thing that nobody had ordered, lying in a tray between her palms.

"*Out,*" said Lowen.

They hauled.

Three grey shapes leaned back together, and the cord came up out of her ward like a hawser taking the weight of a barge. It did not push. It lifted. It came off a girl's leg and found a spoon under it, and the whole pull of three posts went round the curve and into Kiva's back, and flung her.

It was not Gerald. It was not anything like Gerald. Gerald had been a hundredweight of sand with no judgment. This had a beat in it. She felt it for the length of one tap, slow and even and many, like the hum of the sheds coming down a street.

*I don't like its time,* she thought, and did not know why.

"*Ember.*"

She folded. Far edge. Near edge. A penny, in her palms. Shut.

Nobody.

She was nobody, going sideways across the Pit with a thing in her spine that was heavier than she was. The ash went, like a breath. And the beat went with it. She felt that, and it was the one thing all afternoon that she had not expected. The shove was still in her. The rhythm was not. It had been left on the other side of a shut door, as mud is left on a step.

The struts caught her. Low, and long. She was over open cinders with her left hand gone cold to the wrist, and she shut the Ember at the top and did not listen for it. She waited. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs and arrive.

Nobody, again.

It came out of a place that had already been emptied once that minute and once the day before. There was a moment, in the air, when she thought quite calmly that there was nothing left to pay it with. Something paid it. It went out through her left arm like water out of a cut hose. She felt it go past the wrist.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the pan. Wide. By its corners.

"*Still!*"

Her right foot came down on the cinders of the Pit, and the floor took her.

Then the corner arrived.

It came in at her heel, slow and enormous, and it did not stop coming. A sandbag had been one swing. This was three things leaning back on a line, and they went on leaning. It went down through her into the packed clay under the cinders and spread there, and she felt the whole oval of the old pit take it, as a wharf takes a mooring.

There was a white cord running down to the ground a foot from her right boot. It had nothing under it but cinders. It stood there, taut as a stay, fastened to the place where she was.

"*Planted!*" said Kiva.

Behind her she heard Perrin set his knees in the cinders.

---

Perrin Cade had never in his life wanted so much to look at something and been so entirely forbidden.

*You don't look up,* a Torch had told three shed hands on the Weighbridge, and one of them had been his father. He had thought at the time that it was an easy thing to be told.

He had a girl under the arms. She was light, a blade and built like one, all cord and temper. The trouble was that one of her legs had been under something for the best part of a quarter of an hour and had come out the wrong shape.

"*Planted!*"

"That's us," said Perrin. "Marten. On my word, and along the ground, straight back from the wall; we don't lift till she's well off it. Ready."

"I'm not deaf," said Selka Dray, through her teeth.

"I know. I'm only telling you. *Now.*"

They pulled.

She came. There was nothing on her. Where the cord had been there was a stripe across her breeches as white as salt, and the cloth cracked when it bent. She made one noise, a short one, and then she shut her mouth on it so hard that he heard her teeth.

He went backward on his knees and then on his feet, bent double. Each step he waited for his ribs to tell him that they had begun again. They said nothing. He had been strapped from the armpits to the hips for the whole of a spring and a summer, and had held a corner and a pole and a sandbag. He had said *I can't* in front of a Torch, and his father had called him a good lad for it.

He was dragging a person. He was passed for it.

"She's not on it," said the girl on the ground. Her eyes were open, and fixed on something behind him and to his left. "Brask's calling it. She's not on it."

"Don't watch her. Watch where we're going."

"I can't see where we're going. I'm going backward."

He could hear it. That was the worst of having his back to it. He could hear Brask's voice going on, *out* and *in*, as steady as a pump, and under that he could hear something else, a girl's voice, quite quiet, in a different time altogether.

*Now.* And a while after, *now.* And then, sooner than he would have thought, *now* again.

Not on Brask's word. Perrin did not know what it was for. He knew that it was being done on purpose, and he knew the sound of it. It was what she did when a thing was trying to come in. He had heard her call the thread cold aloud in the dark for six months, on her knees, on the tram, under her breath in the Old Yard, and he had thought it was nerves. He heard now what it was: a girl keeping a door shut by saying so, and keeping a thing from getting hold of her the same way.

"That's far enough," said Marten. His voice was shaking. "That's off it."

"Then up. I've her shoulders. You take the good leg and leave the other to hang, and you say *sorry* afterward and not during." He got his arm under her. "To the shoulder. I'm told that I'm allowed."

He stood up with her.

She was the first person that he had lifted since the twenty-ninth of the fifth month. Gerald was not a person, and nor was a dummy on a ladder. The weight of her went onto his shoulder, and down his back, and into his boots, and the cinders had it. Nothing gave. He stood a moment in the middle of the Pit with a girl's red head against his ear, and he could have sung.

"*Clear!*" he shouted. He did not turn round. "*She's clear! Kiva! She's off it!*"

"You've a voice like a tram," said Selka Dray, faintly.

"I've been saving it."

He walked. The west gate was forty paces, and a bald man with a board was already running out from the cart. There was a long-legged fellow on a camp stool beside it, who had got up on his stick.

Behind Perrin the two voices went on. *Out,* and *in.* And *now,* in a time of its own.

Then there was one word.

"*Back!*"

He kept his eyes on the gate, and saw it all the same. He saw it on the terraces in front of him, on a whole tier of faces at once, three flashes laid across them like lightning on a wall: one blue-white and short, and two more a good deal longer. He heard something come down behind him with a long sigh, the whole length of it, like a wet sheet let go from a line.

"*Dark!*" That was a square, flat voice.

And a moment after, from the south-west, a girl's voice that he had known since he was a first-year with purple hands. It was quiet, and perfectly level.

"*Mine.*"

"Is that your Hold?" said the girl on his shoulder.

"That's Pryce."

"She's got a string on her."

"I expect she has." Perrin kept walking. His eyes had gone wet, which was the cinders. "I mind, you know. I've carried you forty paces with my back to the best thing I'm ever going to be near."

"Then don't drop me," said Selka Dray, "and you can mind all you like."

---

Kiva had said *back,* and given the ground its weight, and shut the door.

She had said it on Lowen's *in.* It was the one time, from *planted* to the end, that she had let herself be on its time, because the blades had to cut on the slack and she had told them so. She had felt the cord go slack at her heel, and had let the whole corner down into the clay as one sets down a full pan, and there had been nothing left in the doorway when she closed it.

The rest of the while she could not have said what she had done. It had felt like the time it takes to cross a city. The corner had sat at her heel with a time in it, slow and patient, and she had not liked it, and had not known why. Whenever it seemed to settle on her she had shifted her weight and said *now,* aloud, anywhere, as she said *cold* to a thread that wanted warming, because it was the only thing she had. There was nothing clever in it, and it jarred. Each time, the corner came down on her heel like a dropped sack, through the ankle and up the shin into the knee that Aske had passed, and by the end her right leg was shaking in its boot.

After that she was not certain of very much.

There had been light. Kellan's she knew, a line as long as a breath, away to the north. There had been two more. Then the white cord beside her boot had simply lain down, loose from end to end, all four sides of it at once, and the frost that hung from it came down after, slowly, folding as it fell.

In that same breath every lamp on the rim of the Pit had shown a point of light, once, and gone out. She had caught it at the edge of her eye. It was the middle of the afternoon. The branch was dead; she had heard the handle go. There were forty of them, round the whole oval, and for the space of a blink each had a spark in it, like forty eyes opening and closing.

The frost lay on the cinders in a ring, eight paces across, pale and thin. It looked like the mark that a tub leaves on a floor.

"*Dark!*"

Pike. Somewhere behind three heaps of ash. She was dark. She had nothing to be anything else with.

She was sitting down. She had not decided to. The cinders were under her, and Pru Finch had her by the shoulders from behind and was saying something very fast, of which Kiva caught only the word *narrating.*

In the middle of the ring, with nothing round it any longer, stood a string.

It had seven knots. The eighth was coming up between its legs, out of a thin blue stroke that was already narrower than it had been. There was frost on it from crown to knee. Its blunt top turned slowly, a quarter of a circle, as a weathercock turns.

It had every right to come to her. She was the nearest thing in the Pit that had been worked.

It went past her. It went to the south-west, where four people stood in a row at the head of the sixth sheet with a wall of silver between their hands, and a girl in the middle of them was calling the step.

"Step," said Anwen. "Step. Edda, you're a hand short on the outside. That's it. Step."

The Flask put its blunt head against her wall and leaned.

It was a thing that Graywater did with four Torches. Kiva saw the wall take it. She saw it dip, at the south end, by the width of a hand; and she saw Anwen feel that, without turning her head, and say one word, and saw it come level.

"You're not to watch that," said Pru. "You're to show me your hand. Orla's shouting it from the rail. *Hand, Rowan.* She's said it three times."

Kiva looked for her hand.

It was at the end of her arm. She lifted the arm, with her shoulder, and the hand came up with it. It was the color of a candle to a place two inches above the wrist. She could not feel it, not even the cold. She could only see it.

"That's not pink," said Pru, in a small voice.

"No."

She put her right hand to her collar instead. That one still worked. The disk was where it had always been.

"Cold," said Kiva.

She had known that it would be.

There was a white flag at the east rim. He had it up as high as his arm would reach, and he was holding the rail with his other hand as if the terrace had moved under him. Beside him a tall woman in a grey coat had come along the rim to the mast and was bending over the drum, and behind her a man in a black coat had got to his feet.

There was a noise in the terraces like wind in a wood. It had begun when the lamps winked, and it had not stopped.

"Keeper," said Vane's voice, quite steadily. He had a string on a gate now, and breath to ask. "The rim. As she said *back.* Did I see that?"

"You did." Dunmore was at the load board with both hands on the case. He sounded like a man who has been told the same joke twice. "They're dead. They've been dead since I dropped the race. That's the second time in my life I've seen a dead ring jump, and it was on this floor, with her on it." He spat, and took a stub of chalk from his waistcoat, and put *T-0* on the case under his hand, since that was what a keeper put. "I'd as soon somebody told me why."

Nobody told him.

For a long minute nothing crossed the ring. Pike had seen to that. The stuff lay open, and it was not ash, and nobody in Meridian had a rule for it.

Then six people and a handcart came out from the lattice house.

Kiva knew the walk of the woman in front. She had last seen her on a bank on the Sag Line, weighing tins. The closing crew went in over the pale ring without looking at it, as a carter steps over a hose, and gathered round a blue stroke that was no wider now than a hand.

Whatever they did there, they did with their backs to the Pit. It was not student work. It had never been shown to her, and she did not ask.

"*Closed!*" called Bettan. Her cracked voice went up into the terraces. "It's shut! It's done!"

---

There were two strings on the floor of the Pit, and the Regional Gates had been struck, and it was a call.

It was worked as a call. Kiva saw that much from where she sat, with her back against the tinning table and her left arm lying across her knees. Nobody ran. Rhea Sorn took the old string down the west side, rung by rung, on her own gate, and a square man in Halden black took the new one, on Anwen's. They cut turn about. Each blade asked a catcher before it lit.

The top four rungs of the new string were student work, and Vane said so.

"You're second step, the lot of you. One rung, under a foreman, held and cut and bagged. I'm the foreman. I see no reason why it shouldn't be done because it's real." He looked along what was left of his first company. "Old Four. Twenty-Eight, with a reserve blade; you've a yellow card somewhere in that line. Seven. And Halden, if Halden's foreman will lend me his four for a rung under my word and his own eye."

"He will," said Birch, without looking round. "That's a rung. That's in my license."

So it was that Seven's rung was called at about half past three, and that Kiva did the only part of it which she had left.

She set the mark. She walked the seventh sheet with Pru's arm through hers, and read the ground and the air, and what was behind them, the whole circle round. She put her right palm on the canvas.

"Here. A pace north. Jude takes a full second; he'll want the room."

"And the cord?" said Pru.

"Yours. From the first turn. Not at the third. All of it." She had thought that it would be hard to say. "I'll call the mark and I'll call the check. You've the hands."

"I cross it right four times in five."

"I know. Pryce told me. I'm to let you find the fifth yourself."

Jude went up the back of a string dark, and hung there, and asked.

"Not yet," said Kiva. "It's a foot short. Anwen. One more."

"Step," said Anwen.

"Now."

He lit for a full second, and came down heavy, and sixty drams fell like sand.

"Dark!"

Perrin had a corner. Kellan had a corner; he had come without being asked, in a black jacket with the cord torn off one cuff, and knelt on the lead. Pru drew the neck in at her elbow, as she had been shown by a girl who was now holding a Flask's face, and at the third turn she stopped.

"It's wrong," she said. "It's under and then over. I've done the fifth one." Her round face was scarlet. "Wait. Don't tell me. Over. Then under, toward the catcher."

"Cord check," said Kiva.

"Crossed right."

"Sealed."

It was slow. It was slower than a street at half past two in the morning, when everything had been fine. Nothing had happened.

Pike held the comb over the bags afterward, at the table, and she watched his face.

He did not need to tell her. She had laid her right palm on the neck of each as it came off the pole. Rhea Sorn's rungs had the hair in them, the bend in the grain that all of that year's ash had carried, and nothing else.

The other string's were loud.

Every one of them. It came up through the canvas into the heel of her good hand, and it was the same beat that she had left on the far side of a shut door.

"That one keeps time," she said. "And that. All of his. All four of ours. I can't weigh it. It's only what comes through a neck."

"No. You can't." Pike held the brass over Seven's bag. The third reed sang. The seventh rapped, hard, like a knuckle on a door. "But there's a rule now that says you may stop a bag on it, on your word. I heard the Chair make it. I thought at the time it was a clerk's notion." He took a red tag from his roll and laid it in front of her right hand. "It's your word. Put it on. I've never been a catcher of any step whose word was good for that. You are."

She tied it on. It took a long time, since she had nothing to hold the neck still with, and in the end Pike put one broad finger on the knot.

"There's a thing on my glass that the keeper ought to hear."

It was a pleasant voice. Mr. Adlam had come down from the rim. He had his hat in his hand, and he was not looking at the bags.

"Mr. Dunmore. The Municipal Works has a bank of four-and-twenty across the road, in the Ferren pump house, that keeps a district dry. I had it on the step glass a little after two, running as it ought, every cell on its own time." He spoke with great care. "It fell in at the instant that square stood up. All of it. I assumed that it would come out when you dropped the race. It did in the spring; I watched it."

"It's off the bus," said Dunmore. "There's a foot of air in that link."

"I know that there is. I watched you open it. It is still in step." Adlam turned his hat round by the brim. "It's beating as one, with nothing joined to it. My house has spent three hundred years learning how to put a bank into step. I find that I don't know that anybody, in all that time, has ever tried to take one out."

"And?"

"Nobody ever wanted to."

Kiva heard that. It went by her like something said in the next room.

She had her arm across her knees. Somebody had put a blanket round her. The Wren House cart had been brought in at the west gate, and Healer Aske had come across the cinders to her, since she had not come to him.

He did not take out the pin. He took the hand in both of his, and turned it over, and pressed the two fingertips, and then the palm, and then the wrist. He was a long time over it.

"Can you ease it?" said Pru.

"With what?" He did not say it unkindly. "Ward-trace ash eases a nerve that's starved. It gives it something to answer. This isn't starved. It's been turned out. There's nothing in there for the ash to speak to." He laid the hand back on her knee, quite gently, like something returned. "The wrist will come. I think the palm will come. I'll tell you about the rest when I've a week to tell you with."

"You said. At seven."

"I said it at seven and I said it across the cinders. You'd an answer for me both times." He stood up. "It was the same answer. I notice that. Most people have a different one in the afternoon."

They had put a stretcher on the cart's tailboard. There was a red head on the pillow, and a leg in a board, and a thin boy sitting on the step below with his face in his hands.

"You kept moving," said Selka Dray.

Kiva turned her head.

"From *planted* to *back.* Brask called it the whole while, and you were never where he called. Cade told me to watch where we were going, so I watched you instead." The grey eyes were sunk, and exact. "I thought you'd lost your head."

"I didn't like its time."

"Nor did I. I'd had it on my leg." Dray looked up at the yellow brick of the west wall. "I still think what I thought. You oughtn't to be seated without a needle. I'm sending to the Chair from whatever bed they put me in, to say that it should stand for anybody like you, ever, and that it worked." Her jaw moved. "I'd be under a string if they'd unseated you in the spring. I can hold both. I've been practicing."

"So have I."

"Then don't thank me, and I won't thank you. It would only be a speech."

Kiva looked at her left hand. It lay on the blanket, pale and neat, like something that she was minding for a stranger.

She had been told what it would cost, by a healer across the cinders and by a watch officer in the voice he kept for casualties, and both of them had said it before anybody asked her to choose. Then she had been asked. Nobody had gone round her.

She found that it did not make the hand feel like anything. She had rather thought that it might.
