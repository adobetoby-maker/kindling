# Chapter 33 — Kiva: Not Recalled

The foot of the oak table seemed nearer the door than it had on Thursday, and there was still no chair at it.

She had come up the hall stair at a quarter to three, with Orla behind her on the stick, and the stair had creaked on the same tread that it had creaked on going up on Thursday. She had decided on the tram that she would not mind about that. She minded.

She stood. It was Sunday afternoon, and the window behind the seven was white with river light, and the black box lay open in the middle of the table with nothing in it. The disk was on its cord under her collar. The Ferren had said that she should bring it in on her neck, as the bearer, and not in her hand, as a thing to be looked at.

Orla was on the bench in the passage again. Vane sat at the near end of the table, on Kiva's right, in the place that the Sag took. He had nodded to her when she came in, and had not looked at her since. She had understood by the third minute that this was a kindness.

"The River Flats gave notice of a motion on Thursday," said the Ferren. "He'll say his cause. The bearer's here to hear it, as is our custom. Then anybody may answer. Then we vote."

The River Flats was a broad, careful man, the youngest of them but Vane, and he had plainly said it over to himself on the way in. He stood up to say it, which nobody had asked him to do, and he said it to Kiva and not to the table.

"Recall. Until the season's out. Here's the cause." He kept one hand flat on the oak. "You're seventeen. You're a student, provisional, on a sponsor's line. You're on a Circuit tape that opened to chartered parties this morning, and there was a Halden man in the Pit's mast room this morning before the keeper had his boots on. There's a house claiming a bag you tagged, and an Order that's been to your aunt's door. And now the plate's done something." He took a breath. "I don't say you're unfit. I said that on Thursday and I'll say it again. I say that plate's never been carried by anybody with so many people looking at her, and if it's in that box, nobody can ask to borrow it off your neck. When the season's out you have it back, and welcome."

He sat down. It was a fair cause. Kiva found that she did not dislike him for it, which surprised her.

"The bearer may answer," said the Ferren. "She needn't."

Kiva had an answer. It was about the warmth being the plate's business and not hers, and it would have been too long.

"I'd sooner be asked to borrow it and say no," she said, "than have it in a box so nobody has to ask."

The River Flats looked at her for a moment, and nodded, as one does to a point that has landed and changed nothing.

"May I ask her one thing?" he said.

"Ask," said the Ferren.

"If a man came up to you tomorrow, in a street, and asked you for it. Civilly. In a good coat."

"I'd say it's held of the Line," said Kiva, "and he'd best come and ask you."

"And if he didn't ask?"

She thought about it, since he had asked properly.

"Then I'd have my hand on my collar and a long way to run," she said. "I'm a catcher. I'm quick on my knees. I'm not much on my feet."

The Weir laughed, shortly, and turned it into a cough.

"The Sag," said the Ferren.

"I gave that hand a field direction," said Vane, "and I'd give it again. That's the part that's mine to say." He did not look at Kiva. "The rest is the custom. *Released to a bearer.* It doesn't say released to a bearer nobody's looking at. And she stood here on Thursday and told us, when she might have said nothing. If we box it for that, the next one who feels it warm has a very good reason to keep quiet."

"I'll say why I'm going his way," said the Lantern. She was a cautious woman, and she looked unhappy. "It isn't the girl. It's the house in the black coat. A thing in a box can't be had off a girl in an alley."

"And a thing in a box isn't carried," said the Weir. "It wasn't made to lie in a box. None of us knows what it was made for, but I'll lay a week's pay it wasn't that." He rubbed his chin. "And I've sat on the Sag in a bad year. A season's never out when you say it'll be. It's out when the river says, and the river's not on this watch."

They voted as the watch had always voted, with a hand laid flat on the oak for *aye* and a fist for *no.* Kiva stood with her own hands at her sides and found that she could feel the cord on the back of her neck, which she had not felt in years. She watched the hands go down, since she could not help it. The River Flats laid his flat. The Lantern laid hers flat after a moment, and looked sorry about it. The Weir's was a fist before he had finished putting it down. Then Vane's, and two more, and last the Ferren's, a small brown fist, set down without any hurry at all.

Two flat. Five shut.

Kiva let her breath go. She had been holding it since the River Flats sat down, and the Lantern, who was looking at her, had seen her do it and looked away.

"Not recalled," said the Ferren. She unfolded her hand. "And since a plate that does something isn't the plate we've been sitting over, I'll not have every clerk in the Compact deciding for himself what it is. It's held of the Line, and borne by you. It isn't equipment. It isn't an instrument, and it isn't evidence. Nobody's to lay a hand on it but you, to test it or weigh it or show it, without the seven of us in this room."

"And if it does something again?" said the Lantern.

"Then she'll come and tell us, as she did. I'd not make a rule of it. She managed without one." The old eyes went to a letter on thick cream paper that lay beside the empty box. "The Crooked Line's bursar has asked whether he might have the loan of it for the anniversary case."

"Tell him it's on a girl," said the Weir.

"Tell him that." The Ferren looked down the table at Kiva. "You'll tell your aunt yourself, I expect. You've a better right than any of us, and she's had enough of us at her door this week."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then go and do it before the tram fills up."

Orla got up off the bench when the door opened, and looked at her face, and sat down again.

"Not recalled," she said. "You've the look. I heard the Weir through the door; he's a voice for a levee." She took up her stick. "Did you answer?"

"Once. Shortly."

"Good. They like it short in there. They've had forty years of people being long." Orla got to her feet properly this time. "Go home and tell your aunt. I'll go down by the other stair, so you needn't walk at my pace." 

---

Mara was at the bench when she got in, with her left palm on a lamp-shop cell that a man had brought in that afternoon swearing it was fine. She did not take her hand off it.

"Well?" she said.

"Five fists," said Kiva. "Two flat. The River Flats and the Lantern. The Lantern looked sorry."

Mara's palm stayed where it was a moment longer, as if the cell had said something she meant to hear to the end. Then she took it away and wiped it on her apron.

"What did you say? When they let you answer."

Kiva told her.

"That will have annoyed the River Flats," said Mara, "being fair to him." She did not smile. "And?"

"It's held of the Line and it's mine to carry, and it isn't equipment, and nobody's to put a hand on it but me without all seven of them sitting." Kiva found that her own hand had gone to her collar again, and took it down. "The School asked to borrow it. For a glass case. For the anniversary."

"Did they?" Something happened at the corner of Mara's mouth. "And I thought I'd been ill-used." She looked at the cell. "This one's lying to its owner. It's a fault in the can, not the tune; he's been topping it with sweepings." She set it aside. "I'm not going to say I'm glad. I'm not sure what I am. I'll know by Tuesday."

Perrin had been in while she was at the hall. He had come back on the morning runner from the Sag, with mud to his knees and a great deal to say, and he had said most of it to Mara at the shop counter, because Kiva was out.

"He saw her," said Mara. "On the bank, at the change of shift. He told her about Kellan and the Halden yard, and about Finch on the hill. He says she listened to all of it without interrupting him once, which he found very upsetting."

"What did she say?"

"About Kellan, *Good.* He says she meant it, and that it took her about a minute to." Mara took the kettle off the hob. "About Finch, that Finch should keep the card, and that she'd said so in the spring. And that she's had the end of a wall since Tuesday, behind nobody, and hasn't dipped. She told him he could tell you that."

Kiva sat down at the table. She had not taken off her coat.

"He'll have told it all to her twice," she said. "And then told her what I'd say, and what you'd say, and what Orla would say if she heard him."

"I gathered he'd done something of the kind. He was very pleased with himself. He had a cut on his shin from the runner's step and he showed it me." Mara set a cup in front of her. "He asked whether Finch might come to the yard on Tuesdays and watch, since she's keeping the card. I said it was nothing to do with me, and he said that was what he'd thought, and he'd only wanted to hear somebody say it."

"Nothing else? From Anwen?"

"Nothing else." Mara poured. "You'd a deal sooner she'd been cross. So should I. It isn't ours to order."

---

Selka Dray had not brought anything.

That was the first thing Kiva noticed on Monday morning, in the Placement Chair's office, at eight. She had expected the pressed tunic and the red head held up, and a sheaf of something. There was only Dray, with her hands empty at her sides.

"I asked your reader," said Dray. She spoke to the long plain face behind the desk, as she had in the spring, and not once to the chair where Kiva sat. "I stopped him on the Stonehand stair on Friday and asked him straight what was on the eighteenth. He told me. He doesn't know how not to."

"I know what's on the eighteenth, Miss Dray," said Ysra Crest. "I've had the travelling mast's tapes since the week they were made."

"Then you know. Two doors open together, on a sanctioned instrument. She landed it. It was soft. It cost a third of what the others cost. Her reader called it a miss." Dray's voice was quite steady. "Her sponsor was a yard off with a stick on her shoulder and felt nothing wrong. Bexley says she'd have passed it. In the spring I said she'd fouled a cell with her hand off the drum, and didn't know she had. I was told that was one day and one cell. Here's a second. She did the thing that ended Jessamy Hart's hand at the Regional. Marten, my catcher, had that story from the Wren House; they tell it to anybody who's to stand a Bench. And it felt like nothing to her, and nobody in that yard could tell but a first-year with a pen."

Orla was sitting at the back, by the door, with her stick. She did not stir.

"I'm not saying take her off the roll," said Dray. "I said that in the spring, and I've stopped. I've stood on a shelf with her." It was the nearest that she had yet come to looking round. "I'm saying that the Regional has a Bench with live cells on it, and a floor with a rift at one end. I'm saying she can do that soft thing without knowing, and her sponsor can't see it, and the only thing in Meridian that can is a pen on a mast. And there isn't a pen on her in a lane."

Ysra turned her eyes on Kiva.

"Miss Rowan."

Kiva had an answer.

She had made it on the tram. It was about a called miss, and a reader who had stopped it, and a sponsor who had walked into this office on the Monday and said so herself. It was all true. It would have sounded very well in that room.

"She's right," said Kiva.

Dray's head came round at last.

"I didn't know I'd done it. I thought it was only that I was pleased. I'd have gone home thinking it was the best I'd ever made." Kiva kept her hands in her lap, the right over the left. "I've done one since that was the dear kind, and I chose it with the cheap one in plain sight. I can tell them apart now, in the air. I couldn't prove that to you. So I'd ask for what she's asking for. Put a pen on me. Every floor."

There was a short silence.

"That is not, I think, what you came in here expecting to say," said Ysra.

"No, ma'am."

"No." The Chair looked at her a moment longer, and then folded her hands on the desk.

"The objection is upheld in one part. Miss Rowan opens no second door on any Circuit floor save by a crossing declared aloud before she goes, with a sanctioned mast on her and a reader of this office at it. A stack so read is an incident. She does not stand at the Bench. On any floor with a contained rift she is seated in the recovery role only, as her certificate has it. I see her sponsor and her reader tomorrow, and that will settle who holds the pen." Ysra's gaze went to Dray. "The seat stands, Miss Dray. I told you in the spring that I'd have no roster left if I unseated on unease. I have rather less unease before me this morning than I had, and you're the one who brought me the reason."

"That's what I asked for in the spring," said Dray. "There wasn't a mast then."

"There was not. I'm aware of who paid for one."

They went down the Placement Hall stair at the same time, since there was only the one. Dray went first, quickly. At the turn she stopped, and spoke to the wall.

"We got our second step. Twenty-Eight. On the river flats, on the twenty-seventh. Fifty-three drams in the bag."

"I heard you had the seat."

"I lit after the cloth." It came out stiffly, like something that had been practiced and was not enjoyed. "Marten said *not yet* twice. I'd have had it dead on the first, and it would have gone in the river. I stood there with my blade out like a lamppost until he said. I've never been so angry in my life." Her jaw worked. "It graded First on the test tune."

"That's First."

"I know what it is." She went down three more steps. "I don't want you off the floor any longer, Rowan. I want you where I can see the needle. It isn't the same thing as being pleased you're there, and I'd not have you think so."

"I don't."

"Good," said Selka Dray, and went on down.

---

Noa told her about the Halden man that evening, in the lane, while he was threading the tape.

He had been at the Pit's mast room at eight on Sunday. Mr. Adlam had come an hour after, with a glass and a pair of dividers in a morocco case, and had wished him good morning as if they had arranged it.

"He put his glass on the cliff," said Noa. "And on the notch, and on the space between them, for a long while. He said, *You've the right of it, Mr. Bexley.* Then he asked the keeper which way the lamp ring was wired." He pulled the tape through with his teeth. "He didn't ask me one thing about you. I'd rather he had. I've nothing to refuse him now."

"What does he want?"

"I don't know. He's asked the Chair for leave to seat a glass of Halden's own at the Regional, beside the Circuit's mast. It's a thing they use in the refinery yards. It doesn't draw what a fighter spends. It shows whether anything on a floor is keeping step with anything else."

Kellan had come in at the gate behind them. He had heard the last of it, and stood still.

"A step glass," he said. "My mother has one on her bench. It's for finding the cell in a bank that's fallen out." He frowned, the way he frowned at a cut he did not like. "They'd not carry that across the river to look at a lane."

"No," said Noa. "I thought that as well."

It was a quarter to eight, and the thread was cold, and the step behind her was clean. Kiva had a week's ration in her. A crossing would take thirty-one hundredths out of the practice glass that four people had said yes to, and there would be ninety-four left when that was gone. She was standing with her toes on the scraped line when the others turned their heads.

Somebody was coming down the lane. It was a tall, upright figure in a grey coat, walking without hurry, and behind it came a clerk with a folding stool.

"Sit down, Ysra," said Orla, from her crate. She did not get up. "You'll find the spare one's got a nail in it."

"Thank you. I've brought my own." The Placement Chair had the stool set three paces from the mast, and sat on it, and folded her hands. "I've had four of these described to me. I thought that I should see one before I ruled on what can be seen. You'll go on exactly as you would have gone. I'm not here."

"You're as much here as that post," said Orla. "Mr. Cade. You've the word still."

"I've the word," said Perrin. He had been turned off the spare crate and was standing by the shed with his arms out from his ribs. He looked at the Chair of the Placement Board, and then at Kiva, and went rather red. "I'm not nervous," he told the shed.

Kiva was. It sat under her breastbone like a swallowed stone. The woman on the stool had put a hand on a master stop in the spring and not pulled it. She could end a season with four sentences, and had said three of them that morning.

*That's the pen again,* she thought. *It's only a larger one.*

"One thing," said Ysra. "Before she goes. Orla, you'll oblige me by saying, when she's landed, what you think it was. A crossing, or the other. Before the boy reads. I shall do the same."

Orla looked at her for a long moment.

"That's fair," she said. "I'd have asked it in your place."

"Rowan, changing at the top," said Kiva. "Ember, to still."

She left the line.

It was the fifth time over that strip of dirt, and her body knew the road now, both roads, the dear and the cheap. The cheap one was there at the top, as it always was, like a known step on a dark stair. She shut the Ember. She waited for the last thread of metal to run home along her ribs and to arrive.

Nothing. A girl in the air with no doors. The ash went out of her all at once, like a breath, a quarter of a dram, and a woman on a folding stool was watching it go.

She set the pan.

"*Still.*"

The cross. No jar. The whole of the Old Yard came up under one heel and took her. Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder, and the weight behind it, and went through her into the ground, and she held it there while the yard held her.

"Give it back."

She gave the ground its weight, and shut the still door with nothing left in the doorway. Then she turned, and held out her left hand before she showed anybody her face.

"Now," said Ysra.

"Crossing," said Orla. "She waited at the top. I watched her do it."

"I cannot tell," said the Chair of the Placement Board.

She said it in the level voice that Kiva knew from the foot of a notice board, and she did not seem to mind that Perrin had heard her.

"Mr. Bexley."

"Two hundredths," said Noa. His voice was not quite steady. "Then twenty-six, straight up. It's a cliff. Then three for the stance. Thirty-one."

"Nor could I have told, a fortnight ago," said Orla. "I said it because I watched her wait. I'd not swear to it against the tape."

"No. Nor shall you be asked to." Ysra did not look displeased, or pleased. She was looking at Kiva's hand.

It lay in Orla's two palms under the lamp. The first two fingers were the color of candle wax, to the second joint.

Nobody spoke. Kiva watched them as she had watched them on the twentieth, two things that somebody had set down beside her other fingers and forgotten. On the twentieth they had come back before Orla had her gloves off. Tonight Perrin had time to fold the mast's cover, and put it on crooked, and take it off and put it on again. A flush crept along the first nail at last, as slowly as damp comes up a wall.

"It was quicker last time," said Kiva.

"It was." Orla did not let go of the hand at once.

"Who sees that hand?" said Ysra. "Besides you."

"Healer Aske," said Kiva. "In the morning, before your room."

"Then from tonight I'll hear what he says of it as well. I've had a boy lose one at the Regional. I was a ward-holder on that Bench. I held the wall that the two others were dragged behind." She rose, and the clerk folded the stool. "Tomorrow morning, Orla. Bring the boy."

She went back up the lane as she had come, without hurry.

"Ninety-four in the glass," said Kellan, into the quiet. "Enough for three more." He was looking after the grey coat. "I didn't know she'd been on that Bench."

"No," said Orla. "She doesn't tell it. She was twenty years on a Wren House ward line before she ever sat behind a desk, and she spent ten of them not speaking to me." She took up her stick. "I thought for a long while that it was because she blamed me. It was a good deal later that I learned she'd been asked to pass Jessamy Hart for that Bench, and had."

---

Healer Aske had the pin out at seven on Tuesday, and the towel.

"Eyes shut. Say when."

She said it for the little finger and the next. Then she waited. She waited through what felt like a slow walk across a room.

"When."

"Three breaths. It was two, a month ago." The cold thumb moved. "And here?"

She did not feel the pin at all. She felt, some time after, that something had pressed.

"That isn't the pin you're feeling. That's my thumb." He put the pin in his lapel. "How long, last night?"

"Longer than the time before."

"And the time before was longer than the one before that."

"Yes."

He sat looking at the hand, and not at anything else. He was not a man who liked to be hurried to a conclusion.

"What do you reckon the next one is?" he said.

"Longer."

"So do I. And I'd be guessing, and so would you, and we've both said it." He let go of her wrist. "They've come back. They've come back to where they were, and I'd not say a hair past it. You may have your go on Monday. I want to see that hand the morning after, whatever it's doing."

"If I rested it," said Kiva. "A long while. Would it fill?"

"Rest is the only physic I've ever trusted." He stood. "I can tell you what a week buys, because I've watched it buy less each time. I couldn't tell you what a month buys. Nobody's ever given me one to look at." He glanced at the clock. "You're wanted across the square, I'm told, with half the district. Go on. Don't let them keep you standing."

She went out by the infirmary stair.

It was a clear morning, and already warm. Across the square the doors of the Placement Hall stood open, and people were going up the steps by ones and twos. She saw a black coat with gold at the cuff. She saw a very small woman in a man's overcoat being helped up by a wide man with a grey mustache who had plainly never been inside the building in his life.

There was a bag in a cold room with a hundred and thirteen drams in it, and her red tag on its neck.

She put her hands in her pockets, the left one carefully, and crossed the square.
