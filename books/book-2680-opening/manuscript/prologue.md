# Prologue — What Somebody Wrote Down

## 2080 — the night of the Fall

June Salo was at the kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold when the window turned white.

It was not a flash. It was a whiteness, as if someone outside had pulled a sheet tight across the glass and lit it from behind. It lasted one long breath. There was no sound at all.

"Mum?" said Bram from the couch.

Then the house died.

The ceiling light went out. The wall screen, which had been playing a quiz show with the volume low, went black in the middle of a contestant's laugh. The refrigerator stopped humming, and June heard for the first time how loud it had always been. The little green light on the oven clock vanished. Her phone lay face-up beside her cup, and its screen was dark, and when she pressed the side of it nothing happened.

Out in the street a car rolled past with no engine noise and no lights. It drifted slowly into the curb and stopped. The driver's door opened, and a man got out and stood in the road looking up.

June looked up too.

The sky to the south was moving. Curtains of red and green hung over the lake and rippled like cloth in a wind. While she watched, another whiteness bloomed far beyond them, very high, and then another further east. They made no noise. They were like someone taking photographs in a distant room.

"Teo," she said.

Her husband was already in the doorway with a flashlight in his hand, shaking it. It did not come on. He swore quietly and put it down.

"Get away from the window," he said.

"Why? What is it?"

"Just get away from it."

Her father came out of the back bedroom in his robe, holding the wall. He was seventy-eight and did not like the dark. "Power's out," he said, as if he were the first to notice.

"Everything's out," said Teo. "Not just power. Everything."

Bram had climbed onto the arm of the couch to see. He was nine. His face was green and red in the light from the sky. "The stars broke," he said.

Nobody told him he was wrong.

They found candles in the drawer under the sink, left over from a birthday. Teo had a box of kitchen matches. In the yellow light the room looked like a room from an old film. June's father sat in his chair and held his own wrist as if he were taking his pulse.

"It's the sun," he said. "There was a storm on the sun. They talked about it in the eighties. The old eighties. It knocks out the satellites."

"It's not the sun," said Teo.

"How do you know?"

"Because I saw it go off." Teo sat down on the floor with his back to the cupboards. He worked on the signal side of the rail authority, and he had done the hardening course twice, the one nobody took seriously. "You put one up high enough, above the weather, and you don't get a blast down here. You don't hear anything. You get a pulse. Every wire turns into an antenna for half a second, and everything on the end of a wire burns out."

"One what?" said June, though she knew.

Teo did not answer her. He looked at Bram.

"Who would do that?" said her father.

"Anyone who wanted everybody else to stop," said Teo. "Anybody. Everybody. I don't know."

June went to the kitchen drawer where her grandmother's things were kept. She did not know why she went there until her hand closed on the watch. It was an old wind-up thing in a steel case, with a cracked crystal, and she had kept it because her grandmother had worn it every day for fifty years. She held it to her ear.

It was ticking.

She stood very still and listened to it. Somewhere downstairs a woman was calling a name. In the stairwell somebody struck a lighter, and the flame showed through the gap under the door. The elevator was silent between floors. Later they would learn that a man had been inside it for nine hours.

June took Bram's school notebook off the counter. It was a paper one, for handwriting practice; his teacher was old-fashioned. She tore out the pages of his careful letters and folded them into the back. Then she sat down at the table by the candle and opened it to the first clean page.

"What are you doing?" said Teo.

"Somebody should write down what happened."

"Who's going to read it?"

"I don't know." She looked at the watch and wrote the time. *11:52.* Her hand shook, so she pressed harder. "Somebody. Us. Later."

She wrote what she had seen: the white window, the car rolling into the curb, the coloured sky over the lake, the second and third flashes going east. She wrote that there had been no sound. She wrote that the phone was dead and the flashlight was dead and her grandmother's watch was not.

Then she stopped, and read it over, and added a line underneath.

*Teo says a bomb, very high. Dad says the sun. Bram says the stars broke. I don't know which. Nobody here knows who.*

Outside, the man from the car was still standing in the road. He had his phone in his hand and kept pressing it. After a while he put it in his pocket and began to walk north, the way he had been driving, as if home were still exactly where he had left it.

June wrote that down too.

## 2085 — five years after the Fall

The line at the east gate of the Ostrander Imaging Wing started forming before dawn, and by the time Lio Brandt came up from the generator vault it went past the old bus shelter and around the corner.

He did not look at it for long. Looking did not help.

The wing had survived because of an accident of design. Thirty years before the Fall, somebody had built its scanner suites inside copper-lined rooms so the machines would not pick up stray signals. Somebody else had buried the generator vault under eight feet of concrete to keep its hum away from the imaging. Neither of them had been thinking about a war in the sky. When the pulse came, every other building in the district went dark, and the Ostrander Wing went dark too, for eleven seconds, until the shielded generator caught and the shielded rooms woke up as if nothing had happened.

There were maybe three places like it in the whole region. People walked for days to stand in its line.

"How many today?" said Lio.

The gate officer was a tired woman named Hedda Arkwright who had been a radiology nurse before there was no such thing. She had a clipboard and a stub of pencil. "Fuel for six scans," she said. "Eight if the north convoy comes in."

"Will it?"

"The board says the river plant's coming back up by spring, so they'll have diesel to spare." She did not smile. "The board said that last spring."

Everybody knew about the board. The restoration board met in the old courthouse every month and announced dates. The grid would be back by summer. The rail line would reopen by the first snow. The water plant needed one part, then four parts, then a factory that could make the parts, and the factory needed power. Every date came and went. Lio had stopped listening to the announcements somewhere in the third year.

He went down the line with Hedda, the way they did every morning.

A man with a convoy pass and a yellow armband went straight through; his people had brought salvaged diesel up from the hill district, and diesel bought a place at the front. A woman with a hacking cough was turned toward the church clinic two streets over, where there was no scanner but there were blankets. A boy of about fifteen with a crushed hand stood very straight and said he could work, he could still carry, he only needed someone to look at the bones. Hedda wrote his name down and nodded him through.

Near the bus shelter was a woman holding a small girl on her hip. The girl's left leg was swollen from the knee to the ankle and hot enough that Lio could feel it from a foot away.

"She fell in the canal," said the woman. "Two weeks ago. It isn't getting better."

Hedda looked at the leg. She looked at the girl's face, which was grey and patient. She looked at the list.

"I'm sorry," she said. "Fuel."

The woman did not argue. That was the worst part, Lio thought later. Nobody argued anymore. She shifted the girl to her other hip and turned and walked back down the line, and the people behind her stepped forward to close the space.

At the end of his shift Lio sat on a crate in the corridor outside the vault, where the light was steady and nobody came. He took a notebook out of his coat. It was a ledger from the hospital stores, the kind they used to count gauze, and he had been writing in it for a month.

The hospital kept its own records. Somebody upstairs wrote down every scan, every patient, every litre of fuel, and filed it. Those records said the wing was saving lives, and it was.

They did not say anything about the line.

Lio found his place and wrote the date. Underneath, he wrote: *Girl, about six. Left leg, infection after a fall. Turned away at the east gate.* He drew a short line beside it and stopped.

*Reason given: fuel.*

He sat with his pencil over the page for a long time. Then he wrote the rest.

*We had the fuel. We did not have enough of it to spend it on her. That is not the same thing. Somebody should write down the difference.*

He closed the book and went back down into the warm, loud dark of the vault to check the generator, because it was the only machine in the city that still did exactly what it had been built to do.

## 2095 — fifteen years after the Fall

Lark Ostby was thirteen and up to her knees in the drain cut, pulling cress, when the air above the far bank puckered.

That was the only word she had for it afterward. It puckered, like skin around a stitch.

Her cousin Pip was sitting on the bank with the basket between his feet, singing a song about a goat. He was seven. He did not see it at first. Lark saw it because the light went wrong. A strip of air about as tall as a door, just above the bank, had started to bend the hedge behind it, the way heat bends a road in summer, except that this was cold. She could feel the cold on her wet arms from twenty feet away.

Then it made a sound like a wet sheet tearing down the middle.

"Pip," she said. "Come here."

"I'm not finished."

"Pip. Now."

Something came out of the tear.

It was about the size of a dog and the colour of old ash, and it was wrong in every way she had words for and some she didn't. It had no eyes. It had too many joints in its front legs, or not enough. Its body seemed to be held together by nothing but how much it wanted to move. It landed on the bank in a crouch, and the grass where it landed went grey.

It turned toward the singing.

Lark did not think. There was nothing to think with. She came up out of the water and threw herself in front of the basket, and her arms went up in front of her face the way anybody's would, the way you put your arms up against a kicking horse.

The air in front of her hands went hard.

She felt it happen. It was like closing a fist she had never known she had. The air between her palms and the thing went solid and slightly warm, and the thing hit it at full speed and stopped as if it had run into a wall. It slid. It scrabbled at nothing, its grey claws squealing on the empty air a hand's width from her fingers.

Behind her Pip screamed and ran. She heard his feet on the path. Good. Run.

The thing drew back and came again. The hardness held. She did not know how she was holding it, only that she was, and that it cost something, and that the something was coming out of her chest in a thin hot stream.

She pushed.

The hard air moved forward with her hands. It was clumsy and slow, like pushing a door through deep water, but it moved, and the thing was caught against the stone face of the cut with nowhere to go. She pushed harder. She heard herself shouting, though she could not hear what. The thing's shape crumpled along one side.

Then it came apart.

It did not die the way animals die. It simply stopped being anything. One moment there was a crouched grey shape against the stone, and the next there was only ash, soft and fine, sliding down the rock and drifting on the wind across the water.

Some of it settled on her arms.

It was warm. She felt it go into her skin, or under it, or through it. The thin hot stream that had been running out of her chest stopped, and something poured back the other way, and the shaking in her arms eased. She stood in the cut with her hands still up, breathing hard, and she felt less tired than she had a minute before.

Behind the bank, the pucker in the air closed like a mouth.

When her aunt came running down the path with Pip and a hayfork and two of the men from the barn, Lark was sitting on the bank with the cress basket in her lap, staring at her palms.

That night her aunt took down the settlement book from the shelf over the stove. It was a big book with a cracked spine, and in it, for eleven years, she had written every birth, every death, every harvest, every traveller, and every trade. She opened it to a new page and wrote slowly, with Lark watching over her shoulder.

*Seventh day of the hay month. A tear opened over the drain cut and something came through it. Grey, no eyes. It went for Pip. Lark's hands made the air hard, and she held it, and pushed it into the stone, and it turned to ash. The ash went into her, she says, and she felt better after. Pip saw it. I saw the ash on the water.*

*We have no word for this. I have written it down so the next one knows it happened here first. Or here too.*

Two months later, she added a line underneath in a different ink.

*The coast radio says children in other places. Hands like Lark's, and other things. They say it is something brought back from Mars that woke up. They say a great many things on the coast radio. I have written that down as well, as heard, not seen.*

## 2151 — seventy-one years after the Fall

Odile Brack had kept the records house at Corran Ford for twenty-two years, and in all that time she had learned to sort what came through the door into two piles.

Seen, and heard.

On the morning the coast carrier came, she was sixty and her eyes were going, so she read the letters at the window with a lens. There were two that mattered.

The first was from an old correspondent upriver, a trader in instruments who had sold glass and copper wire to every research post between the mountains and the sea. His hand had grown shaky. He wrote that he was getting old, that his knees were bad, and that he wanted to set something down before he forgot it.

*You asked me once about Homura. I did business at its gate twice, years ago, before the burning. Everyone who would really know is dead or not saying. What I have is what the gate clerk told me over a bad lunch. Vell's people paired children from nine traditions and took the same readings from all of them for years. The clerk said the readings kept coming out the same. Ember child, hand child, whatever kind of child. Same pattern underneath. One thing behind every door, he said, and they were close to proving it.*

*He said something else, and I've never known what to make of it. One of their theorists wrote that the sky in '80 might have cracked more than our machines. Something under the world, he called it a filter, a thing that had kept the doors shut. He said the tears were leaking through the crack. The clerk said the theorist called it a theory. He said it twice, so I will too. It is a theory.*

Odile read the letter twice. Then she took her pen and wrote at the top of the first page, in the margin, in small square letters: *HEARD. Not seen. Not proved.*

She put it on the heard pile.

The second item was not a letter at all. It was a single page folded in four, wrapped in oilcloth against the weather. The carrier said it had come down the coast road from Meridian, copied there in a hand nobody at Corran Ford knew, and that the person who gave it to him had said only that somebody there wanted it copied.

Odile unfolded it on the table and weighed the corners down with stones.

It was a page of ruled columns. Across the top, in careful print, someone had written *North yard. Home Line.* Below that ran the headings.

*Exercise. What went wrong. Who was hurt. What it cost. How long to come back. Second go.*

Then, squeezed in at the right-hand edge as if it had been ruled in later, a seventh column with a single word over it.

*Why.*

The entries under the first six columns were brief and plain. A drill. A fall. A name, an injury, a number of days. Under the seventh, in a different and more slanting hand, there were short lines of explanation, and the one at the bottom caught her eye and made her read it again.

*The lane with the crooked posts. Why: because the road isn't straight. S. V.*

Odile did not know what lane it meant, or what road, or who S. V. had been. She sat for a while with her lens in her hand, looking at it.

It seemed to her a very sensible thing to keep. The first six columns said what had happened. The seventh said why anybody had bothered.

That afternoon she took down the records house's own big book, where she had always written arrivals and departures and the prices of salt. She took a ruler and a fresh pen. On the right-hand edge of the next clean page, she ruled a new column, and at the top of it she wrote the same word.

*Why.*

Then she filed the Meridian page on the seen pile, because she could see it, and it was real, and she had no reason to doubt that someone had meant every word.

## 2431 — three hundred and fifty-one years after the Fall

Clemence Ardo had three copies of the north-yard ledger open on her desk, and no two of them agreed.

It was the ninth week of the work, and the work was supposed to be finished by the end of the tenth. The Meridian Compact had three schools and a field line now, and nine hundred students. It had a seasonal competition. It had a charter with a seal on it. What it did not have was a single book that told every lane warden in every school how to run a drill the same way, and the Compact's council had decided that it needed one.

Clemence was the assistant registrar for lanes. The job of writing that book had come down to her.

She rubbed her eyes and looked at the copies again.

The oldest was in the Compact's own archive, bound in cracked leather, and its seventh column was mostly gone. Somebody, a century or two ago, had left it under a leaking roof. The right-hand edge of every page had swollen and bled until the ink was only a brown shadow.

The second copy had come back from a river town in the south, sent as a gift when the Compact signed its trade terms. It was cleaner. Someone had recopied it there at least once, maybe twice, and had spelled some names differently and dropped some entries entirely.

The third was a schoolroom copy, with notes in the margins in three different hands, arguing with each other.

They all had the line. That was the strange thing. Whatever else had been lost, every copy had kept the same short entry in the same column, as if it mattered.

*The lane with the crooked posts. Why: because the road isn't straight. S. V.*

The river copy said *post*, not *posts*. The schoolroom copy had a pencil note under it that said *who?* and another under that, in a different hand, that said *a founder?* and nothing else.

Clemence set down her pen.

There were four crooked posts left in the old lanes. She had walked them that morning with a measuring line. They leaned a hand's width or more off true, every one in a different direction, and the new lane wardens hated them because a straight post gave a clean mark and a crooked one did not. Last spring a Stonehand boy had caught his wrist on one in a fall and broken it in two places. His family had written to the council.

*The road isn't straight.*

She looked at it for a long time, trying to make it mean something. Lanes were not roads. The schools trained students to hit a mark, and a mark was easier to hit if you knew where it was.

It might have been a joke. Founders made jokes, and later people carved them into stone.

There was a knock, and the old lane warden of the Crooked Line School came in without waiting. He was a stooped man of eighty who had taught in the old lanes since before Clemence was born, and he had heard about the posts.

"You can't straighten them all," he said.

"Why not?"

He opened his mouth and closed it again. "Because my teacher told me not to," he said finally. "And his teacher told him."

"Did either of them tell you why?"

The old man looked at the ledgers on her desk, the brown stains on the oldest one, the pencil arguing in the margins of the third. "No," he said. "I think they thought it was obvious."

Clemence was tired. The book was due in a week. She did not want to be the registrar who ripped out the founders' posts, and she did not want to be the one who left a hazard in a lane where children fell.

"One," she said. "We keep one. In the old lane. For the anniversary."

The warden thought about it, and nodded slowly, as if he had been given less than he wanted and more than he expected.

When he had gone, Clemence pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began to draft the section on lane fittings. Lane posts to be set plumb, to the line. One post in the old lane of the Crooked Line School to be retained in its present position. She had to put something in the column beside it. The new manual had a column for reasons, a narrow one, for the sake of form.

She looked at the old line once more. *Because the road isn't straight.*

Then she wrote *custom*, and moved on to the section on frame weights. Later that winter, when the council asked whether the seventh column needed to appear in the printed manual at all, she said she supposed not, for clarity, and they took it out.

## 2680 — six hundred years after the Fall

On the morning of the anniversary, Mara Rowan is at the bench before the bells.

Kiva hears her from the stairs: the scrape of the toolbag across the boards, the click of the case that holds the spare needles, the small dissatisfied sound Mara makes when a spring is not where she put it. The bench runs the whole width of the front room of the house on Gauge Row. On it are three lamp regulators waiting for parts, a pressure gauge from the canal locks with its glass off, and the long brass card box where every instrument in the north district has a card.

"You're up," says Mara, without turning around.

"You're up," says Kiva.

"I'm on call." Mara holds a needle up to the window, squints along it, and puts it in the case. "The Assay frame has to run nine hours today, and I rebuilt the left coil on Thursday, and if it smokes in front of the whole Compact, they'll have my name on it by lunch."

"It won't smoke."

"I know it won't. That's why I'm checking."

Kiva pulls the frame's card out of the box without being asked. It is soft at the corners from handling. Down the left side runs the instrument column, in Mara's small flat figures: the coil readings, the needle tests, the dates. Down the right runs the paper column, which is what the records office says the frame ought to read. At the far edge there is a narrow strip for remarks. Most cards have nothing there. This one has three words in Mara's hand from Thursday.

*Left coil rebuilt. Watch.*

"It agrees," Kiva says. She runs her finger down the two columns. "All of it. Thursday and Friday."

"Then it agrees." Mara snaps the toolbag shut and looks at her at last. Her eyes go to Kiva's collar, to the thin braided cord that disappears under her shirt. "You're wearing it."

"It's the anniversary."

"You're thirteen. It's your Kindling. That's not the same thing as an anniversary."

"It's both," says Kiva, and Mara almost smiles.

Under her shirt, against her breastbone, the disk is cold. It is always cold. It is near-black and two thumbs wide, with nine fine grooves cut into each face, and it came to the house on Gauge Row four years ago in a sealed packet with a Home Line stamp. Kiva was nine. She remembers the courier on the step, rain on his hood, asking for Mara's signature. She remembers the one line on the outside of the packet, in someone's careful hand.

*Released to bearer: Kiva Rowan.*

She does not know who released it. The rest of the appointment was sealed, and the Home Line keeps its seals. She knows the disk is old. She knows somebody chose her. She has been turning that over for four years like a stone in a pocket.

The bells start at the Compact tower, nine of them, slow.

Mara puts on her coat. Kiva ties her boots. They go out together into Gauge Row, where every door has a ribbon on it for the six hundredth year, and walk north with the crowd toward the Founders' Yard.

The yard is bigger than she remembers from last year, or she is smaller in it today. Banners hang from the lamp-lines. The Assay frame stands at the south end under its canvas, a ring of brass and dark iron as tall as a cart, and Mara peels away from her toward it with a hand lifted in something halfway between a wave and a warning. Beyond the frame, along the east wall, are the school lanes, painted fresh, straight as rules, their posts standing at their far ends like soldiers.

All but one.

In the oldest lane, the one nobody uses except on the anniversary, the post at the end leans. It is grey iron, as thick as a wrist, set in a collar of old lead, and it tilts a hand's width off true toward the east as if it were listening for something. A small brass plate is fixed to its foot. Kiva has read it every year since she could read.

*Retained. Ceremonial. Custom.*

She stops in the moving crowd and looks at it, the one crooked thing in a yard full of straight ones, while the ninth bell fades and people push past her toward the ring.
