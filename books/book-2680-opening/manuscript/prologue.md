# Prologue — The Leaning Post

## 1. The Night of the Fall

*2080*

Ruth Achterberg was rinsing the last plate when the kitchen went white.

It was not a flash, exactly, because a flash would have hurt. This was a slow, clean light that came in through the balcony door and lay across the floor tiles as if someone had opened a refrigerator the size of the sky. Then it faded, and the ordinary night came back, and the tap was still running. Ruth turned it off.

"Mum," said Mina from the balcony. "Mum, come and look at this."

The apartment was on the ninth floor, and from the balcony you could see the river, the bridges, and the long lit spine of the ring road going south. Joel was already out there with his hand on the rail, and Saffi was beside him in her socks. Ben was asleep, or meant to be.

"Up there," said Joel, and he pointed south and high.

There was a point of light in the sky, far above where any aircraft flew and much too bright for a star at that height. It was not getting bigger so much as spreading. As Ruth watched, it opened into a soft white blur, and then the edges of the blur began to turn colors, red first and then a long, rippling green, like oil on water, that crawled across the south of the sky in folds.

There was no sound at all.

"Is that the northern lights?" said Saffi. "They said there might be a storm. On the news, they said."

"That's not the north," said Mina.

Then the lights went out.

They did not flicker or dim. Every light in the city went out at once, from the bridges to the ring road to the window across the courtyard where the Dabrowskis were watching their enormous television. The hum went out of the building, and Ruth had not known the building hummed until it stopped. The refrigerator behind her fell silent, and the little green eye on the oven clock was gone.

Down in the street, a car rolled to a stop without its headlights, and another one coasted past it and bumped gently into the curb. Nobody honked. The horns had died with everything else.

"Joel," said Mina.

"I know."

"Joel, what is it?"

He was quiet for a moment, and Ruth could see his face only because the sky was still glowing. He was an electrician who had spent twenty years on the city's substations, and he did not look surprised. He looked like a man doing sums he did not like.

"That was a bomb," he said. "A nuclear bomb. High up. Way above the weather."

Saffi took a sharp breath.

"Not here," said Joel quickly. "There's no blast, see? Nothing's on fire. It went off way up, and when it does that, it throws out a pulse. Like lightning hitting every wire at once. Anything with a chip in it, it cooks."

"Everything?" said Mina.

He held up his phone, and the screen was black. He pressed the button and nothing happened.

"Everything with a plug or a battery," he said. "Pretty much."

The colors in the south were fading now, folding down into a dull red smear. Ruth stared out at the dark city and could not make it into a shape she knew. Candles were appearing in some windows already, tiny and yellow, and a dog was barking somewhere, and down the hall someone had begun to pound on the elevator doors.

"Who would do that?" said Saffi.

"Them," said Joel. "Who else? It's been coming for years."

"You don't know that," said Mina.

"Somebody launched it. It didn't launch itself."

Across the courtyard a window scraped open, and Mr. Dabrowski leaned out with a flashlight that did not work. He shook it, cursed, and put it down.

"It's the sun!" he called across to them. "They said! A solar storm. It'll be back on by morning."

"It's not the sun, Piotr," said Joel.

"How do you know?"

Joel did not answer. Ruth thought that was because he did know, and because knowing did not help anyone.

She went back inside. In the dark kitchen she found the drawer by feel, took out the box of matches she kept for birthday cakes, and lit a candle. Then she held her wrist up to it.

Her father's watch was still running. It was an old wind-up thing with a cracked strap, and she had worn it every day since his funeral because she missed him, not because it was useful. Now the second hand was moving in the candlelight when nothing else in the city was.

Quarter to twelve.

When she came out of the kitchen with the candle, Joel was in the hall with the cupboard open. He had his work boots on and his tool roll under his arm, and he was holding the big rubber flashlight from the top shelf, which did not work either. He stood there with it as if he had forgotten what it was for.

"Where are you going?" said Mina.

"Linden Road. The substation." He did not look at any of them. "It's mine. Twenty years it's been mine. There'll be men down there by now trying to get it going in the dark, and they'll put their hands on the old breakers, and the old breakers will kill them."

"You said everything's dead."

"The breakers haven't got chips in them. That's the trouble."

"It's four miles," said Ruth. "In the dark. With no streetlights and everybody frightened."

"I know how far it is."

"Are you coming back?" said Saffi.

Ben came out of the bedroom then, rubbing his eyes. "The fan stopped," he said.

"I know, love," said Ruth.

"Why did it stop?"

Nobody answered him. Down the hall, whoever was at the elevator had stopped pounding and begun to call a name, over and over, a woman's name, in a voice that was trying to stay polite.

Joel looked at the door. Then he looked at Ben, in his pajamas, and at Saffi in her socks, and at the one candle.

He put the flashlight down on the hall table.

"In the morning," he said. "When it's light. There'll be somebody at Linden Road in the morning, and I'll be one of them." He sat down on the floor with his back against the front door and his boots still on, and he did not take them off.

Ruth picked Ben up, even though he was eight and too heavy, and carried him to the window so that he could see the last of the red going out of the sky.

"We don't know yet," she said.

On her wrist, under the candle, the second hand went round.

## 2. The Annex

*2085 — five years after the Fall*

The queue at the Saint Brigid annex started at the inner fence and went all the way down the hill to the old bus shelter.

Teo Barrasa could see it from the generator room window, though he did not look at it often, because looking at it did not shorten it.

The annex had been built twelve years before the Fall to hold a magnetic imaging machine. The machine needed a room that kept every stray signal out, so the builders had lined the walls with copper mesh and set the whole suite on its own buried power plant. Nobody had been thinking about bombs. They had been thinking about clean pictures of people's insides, and so when the pulse came through on that first night, it went around the annex the way water goes around a stone.

So the annex still had lights. It had a sterilizer, a refrigerator for insulin, three working dialysis machines, and one generator that ran on fuel nobody could make anymore, and across sixty miles of dead country that made it the only real hospital left.

The Restoration Board owned the fence. The Board owned the fuel. The Board decided who stood in which line.

The second winter had been the worst, because that was when the power station up the river had gone. Its reactor had sat there without outside power for most of a year, kept cool by backup generators until the last of their diesel ran out, and after that nobody could keep it cool, and nobody had been there to try. The steam had gone up in the spring, and the wind had gone east, and the people who came down the river road that summer had their hair coming out in their hands. Teo had run the machines for them until there was no point in running them.

"Teo." It was Marguerite, the night nurse, leaning in the door. "There's a girl at the fence."

"There's always a girl at the fence."

"This one's swelling up. Her ankles are like bread, and her mother says she hasn't passed water in four days." Marguerite studied the floor. "No card."

Teo checked the board on the wall. Machine two was booked at six for a man from the Board's line crew. The line crews spent their days stringing new wire between dead towers, and the Board said they were rebuilding the grid. Old Farrow, who had been an electrician before the Fall and came to the annex for his heart pills, said every visit that you could not rebuild a grid when every transformer in the country had burned out on the same night. "We're stringing wire to nothing," he said. "But it gives the young ones something to do."

It was four o'clock. Machine two was free for two hours.

"What's her name?" said Teo.

"Lina."

He went out to the fence himself. The girl was about ten, and she was sitting on her mother's coat with her face puffy and grey. The mother looked at Teo the way people looked at the fence, like someone who had stopped hoping it would open.

"I can give her one session," he said. "Now. Before the six o'clock."

"Will one help?"

"It'll help today."

The mother did not ask what would happen tomorrow, and Teo was grateful for that.

He ran the machine for an hour and fifty minutes, and the girl slept through most of it. When she woke up, her color was better, and she asked for water, and her mother cried in the corridor where the girl could not see her. At ten to six Teo unhooked the lines and cleaned the station. The man from crew three came in at six exactly and sat down without noticing anything.

The Board's fuel man noticed.

His name was Hollin, and he came down from the office at half past six with the generator gauge in his head, because reading the gauge was his whole job and he was good at it. He was not a cruel man. He stood in the doorway of the dialysis room in his good Board coat and looked at Teo, and then at machine two, and then at Teo again.

"Two hours of fuel off the tank between four and six," he said. "And nobody booked on that machine till six."

"A girl from the fence," said Teo. "Lina. About ten. No card."

"I know what no card means."

"She was here. The machine was free. Nobody else was waiting for it."

Hollin did not raise his voice. "That fuel was crew three's. Every liter in that tank is somebody's. That's two hours a man on the line crew won't get on Thursday, and he's got a card, and he's been waiting for it." He let that sit. "So you tell me which man. Because I have to tell him."

Teo had known it would come to this, and he had thought, running the machine, that he would have something to say. He did not.

"Me," he said at last. "Tell him it was me. Use my name. And tell him the girl's name."

"They'll have your card for this."

"Maybe."

"Then why?"

Marguerite had come up behind Hollin in the corridor and stopped there, with a tray of clean lines in her hands.

Teo looked past both of them, out of the window. The queue went down the hill in the dusk, and a few people at the bottom had lit a fire in the bus shelter.

"Because she was at the fence," he said. "And I was the one standing here."

Hollin was quiet for a while. Then he went back up to his office, and Teo heard him on the stairs, slowly, a man going to do a thing he did not like.

## 3. The Tear

*2096 — sixteen years after the Fall*

Pell Garrow was thirteen, and she was digging potatoes in the low field when the air tore open.

She heard it before she saw it. It was the sound of a bedsheet being ripped, except that it was too big and it came from the wrong place, from the middle of the field about thirty paces away, where there was nothing at all.

She stood up with the fork in her hands.

There was a line in the air. It hung there, a little taller than her father, and it was the color of nothing, which was not the same as black, because black was a color. The line was like the place your eyes go when you rub them too hard. While she watched, it widened into a long, ragged slit, and the air around it shivered like the air over a road in August.

"Nico," she said. She did not shout, and she did not know why she did not shout. "Nico, come here."

Her brother was seven. He was on his knees at the end of the row with his hands in the dirt and his tongue between his teeth, and he looked up at her and then past her, and his mouth fell open.

Something came out of the tear.

It did not climb out. It poured, the way wet sand pours out of a bucket, and slid onto the ground in a grey heap the size of a calf. Then the heap pulled itself up into a shape that had four thick legs, or something that worked like legs, and a front end that was lower than its back end. It did not have a face. It had a place where a face should go, and the place was turned toward Nico.

Pell's father was at the top of the field with the cart. She heard him shout her name, and she heard him start to run.

The grey thing went for Nico.

It was not fast. It moved like a man carrying something heavy, lurching and sure, but Nico was frozen on his knees, and Pell was eight rows away, and her father was a hundred paces off.

Pell dropped the fork and ran.

She did not decide to do it. Afterward she tried to find the moment when she had decided, and she could not, and all she had was her legs moving and the dirt flying up and her own breath very loud. She reached Nico two steps before the thing did and got in front of him, and she threw her arms up in front of her face, because that was what you did when something was going to hit you.

The thing hit nothing.

There was a sound like a hand slapping a wet window, and the grey thing stopped in the air an arm's length from her. It did not bounce. It just stopped, the way a bird stops against glass, and slid down onto the dirt.

Pell's hands were hot, and her palms burned as if she had grabbed a pot handle. Between her hands and the grey thing the air had gone thick and bright, and though she could not see anything there, she could feel it, like pushing against the bottom of a full bucket of water.

The thing pulled itself up and hit the nothing again. Pell felt it in her shoulders, in her teeth, in the bottoms of her feet. She dug her heels into the soft dirt and did not move.

"Pell!" Her father was there with the long-handled shovel from the cart. He did not understand what he was seeing, and he did not stop to understand it. He swung the shovel flat into the side of the grey thing as hard as he could.

It came apart.

That was the only way to say it. It did not bleed or scream. The shovel went into it, and the shape it had been wearing simply let go and fell into a heap of fine grey powder, like the ash at the bottom of the stove, and lay there on the dirt, and did not move again.

Behind it, the tear in the air was closing. It shrank to a line, and the line shrank to nothing, and the ripping sound stopped as if somebody had shut a door on it.

Nico started to cry.

Pell dropped her arms, and the bright thick feeling went out of the air at once. Her palms were red. Her legs gave out, and she sat down hard in the potato row beside her brother.

Her father stood over the grey heap with the shovel, breathing like a man who had run up a hill. He stared at the powder, and then at the empty air where the tear had been. Then he turned to Pell, and to her hands, and she saw that he was afraid of something, and she did not know if it was the grey thing or her.

"What did you do?" he said.

"I don't know."

"You stopped it."

"I don't know how."

That night the grey powder was still warm. Her father had shoveled it into a feed sack because he did not know what else to do with it, and Pell lay awake with the sack beside her bed, because her mother had said to keep it away from the animals and there was nowhere else to put it. Some time after midnight she put her hand on the sack, and the warmth went into her palm and up her arm like something hot to drink on a cold day. The burned feeling in her hands went away. She slept until noon.

---

By the end of the week everybody at Garrow's Ford had an opinion, and most of them brought it to the mill.

Pell had to go, because the meal was owed and her father would not leave her at home. She sat on the sacks inside the mill door with her red hands in her lap while the stones went round behind the wall, and the grown-ups talked over her head as if she were furniture.

"Three by the mill clock," said Aud Fenn, the miller's wife, to anybody who came in. "I heard it from here. Like a sheet tearing. I thought it was the sails."

Old Sefton had come down from the crossroads with his hardened radio in a box on his back, the only radio for thirty miles that still made a sound. He set it on the meal bin and took his hat off.

"It's not only here," he said. "There was a tear by the coast road. Two up north. There's a boy over at Ashby who stood in the river in flood all summer and couldn't be moved off his feet, and a girl down the valley who puts her hand on a sick cow and tells you where it hurts." He looked at Pell. "All of them about thirteen."

"That's talk," said somebody.

"It's the radio," said Sefton. "Before the Fall some survey brought a thing back from Mars. A disk. It's been in a vault in the south all this time, and it woke up this spring, and that's what's tearing holes in the air." He put his hat back on. "Mind you, the same man on the radio says nobody ever went to the moon. So."

The minister had come for his flour and stayed for the talk. "It is a judgment," he said. "I don't say whose. But it's a judgment."

"It's a wolf," said Pell's father. He was standing by the door with his arms folded, and he had not said much all week. "A new kind. It came at my boy, and I hit it, and it fell down. That's a wolf. You don't need a sermon for a wolf."

"And the girl?" said the minister. "What's she, Henrik?"

They all turned to look at her.

Pell had been asked what she did every day for six days, and she had said she didn't know every day for six days, and it had not got any better for being said. Her palms still felt hot when she thought about them. The sack of grey powder was still beside her bed, still warm, and she still slept better for it, and she had not told anybody that, because they would want to know why.

"I don't know what I am," she said. "I don't know what I did. I'd like everybody to stop asking."

Aud Fenn came over and put a hand on her hair, as if she were a much smaller girl. "There," she said. "There. Let the child alone, all of you."

Nobody let her alone. But they lowered their voices, and behind the wall the stones went round.

## 4. The South Lane

*2152 — seventy-two years after the Fall*

The director put Oriel Hask on the upriver barge with a purse of ash, a letter of introduction, and one instruction.

"Learn the draw," he said, "and come home. Don't let them keep you for the slow part."

Oriel was nineteen and the junior orderly at the Lowmarket infirmary, ten days down the river from Meridian. Meridian had walls and hardened lamps and medicine. Lowmarket had a mud wharf, two churches, and one healer.

The healer was a hand-door Kindled named Brother Aldous, who could put his palm on a fever and draw it down like water out of a well. Two months ago a scavenger crew had come back down the river with their skin peeling, after a season picking through the empty towns across from the dead power station at Corbel Reach. Brother Aldous had tried to help them without knowing how, and now he was in a bed beside them, and his hands shook when he held a cup.

Meridian's Home Line knew how. So the director had paid for the barge, and Oriel had gone, because she was the one he could spare.

---

The trainer who met her at the south wall did not look at the letter.

She was a lean woman of about forty with a burn scar across one cheek, and she led Oriel along the foot of the wall to a strip of packed earth between the wall and a row of sheds. Down the middle of it stood nine posts, thick and black and taller than a man. They were not in a straight row. They wandered a little, left and then right, the way a path wanders across a field. The last one stood out from the wall at a lean, with an iron bar run through its top.

"Recall," said Oriel. "I've come to learn recall. For burns. The bad kind."

"Everybody who comes up the river comes for that." The trainer nodded at the posts. "First you run the lane."

"I'm not a fighter."

"Nobody here is, the first week." The trainer handed her a strip of black cloth. "Tie that on. You run it dark. Keep your left hand out and touch each post as you pass. Before the eighth, you call the turn, so the one behind you turns with you. At the last post you put your hands on the bar and say who has it."

"Who has it?"

"You, the first time."

Oriel tied the cloth on.

She hit the third post with her shoulder and the fifth with her face. She forgot to call the turn, and the boy behind her walked straight into her back. At the last post she found the iron bar by falling against it.

"Hask," she said, into the dark. "Hask has the bar."

"Good," said the trainer. "Again."

---

On the third day the trainer let her near a patient.

It was not a burned man. It was a Home Line hand whose cut foot had gone hot and swollen, and the trainer sat Oriel beside him in a shed with the shutters closed.

"Hands on him. Eyes shut. You read with your hands, in the dark, the way you found those posts. Tell me where you are as you go, so that if you go down, I know where to pick it up."

Oriel put her hands on the man's shin and shut her eyes.

It was like the lane. She found nothing, and nothing, and then something: a heat that was not the heat of his leg, sitting low, like a coal in ash.

"There. Above the ankle. It's small."

"Good. Now don't do anything about it. Read the rest of him."

"Why?"

"Because the one you didn't read is the one that kills you."

She read him for an hour. She was not allowed to draw anything.

On the fifth day a bargeman found her at the wall with word from Lowmarket. The crew was worse. One of them, Carrick, had stopped eating. She was to come home on Thursday's barge with whatever she had.

Oriel told the trainer.

"Then go on Thursday," said the trainer. "You can draw. You'll get the worst of it out of them."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is the slow part. Read it, draw it, spend the ash to pull it off you, vent it in the ground before it lodges in your own hands. Stop when the ash is out, or you're next." She said it flatly, as if she had said it a great many times. "You've got the draw. You haven't got the reading, and you haven't got the stopping."

"He said not to let you keep me."

"I'm not keeping you. The barge is on Thursday." The trainer turned back to the lane, where two boys in blindfolds were coming down it at a jog with their hands out. "Turn," said the first one, before the eighth post, and the second one turned with him. The second boy reached the end first and put both hands on the bar.

"Dunn has the bar," he said.

"Why does that one lean?" said Oriel.

She had wanted to ask since the first day. It was the only post that stood out from the wall, black and split with age and bound with iron.

The trainer did not look round.

"Because the road isn't straight," she said.

Oriel waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it. The trainer was already calling the boys back to the start.

That night by the long hall's fire, a girl from up the valley country said that her grandmother had told her the sky had a lid on it, and that the bombs in the Fall had cracked it, and that was why the tears came.

"Your grandmother says a great deal," said the trainer, from the bench by the door. She did not say that it was wrong.

Oriel did not go home on Thursday.

---

She came home four days late.

The director met her on the wharf. He was a big, tidy man who liked the infirmary to run like a clock, and he did not shout, but he walked very fast beside her up the hill.

"Aldous can't lift a spoon," he said. "Carrick—"

"I know," said Oriel. "I'm here now."

She did it in the long ward with the shutters closed, so that it was dark, which was how she had learned it. She put her hands on Carrick first, because he was the worst. She read him from the crown of his head to his feet before she drew anything, while the director stood in the doorway with his arms folded.

"Chest. Long bones. It's gritty. It's in his thighs, both. Drawing now."

The ash in her left palm went from pale to dark. She knelt at the clay pit she had made them dig behind the ward and put both hands into the wet clay until they were clean.

"Three drams," she said, when she came back in. "The others will be less. Aldous last, because he's the least, and because he'd want to be."

"That's most of what you took with you," said the director.

"That's what it costs."

Carrick's hands did not come back. The skin of them stayed thin and shiny for the rest of his life, because what had already been done stayed done. But on the second morning he asked for bread, and on the fourth he ate it.

That evening the director came to the ward and stood at the foot of Brother Aldous's bed, and watched him drink a cup of water with both hands and not spill any. Then he went and fetched a second stool from the corridor, and set it beside Oriel's, and sat down on it, and did not go back to his office until the lamps were out.

## 5. Rotation

*2170 — ninety years after the Fall*

The control building at Corbel Reach still had its clocks. They had all stopped at the same minute, almost ninety years ago, and they had stayed stopped.

Ines Coyle could see one through the gap in the blast door, hanging over a long grey desk covered in dead switches. Her eyes went to it every time the winch pulled her through, and she could not help it.

"Twelve minutes," said Abel Strand behind her. "Not thirteen."

"I know."

"You said that last time, and you came out at thirteen and a half."

"I was holding a binder."

"The binder doesn't care about your marrow," said Abel. "I do."

He was a short, square man with grey in his beard and a healer's soft hands, and he wore the same lead-lined apron she did. It did not do much, and it was not meant to, because all the apron did was keep the dust off. The real protection was distance, time, and him.

They stood at the yellow line painted across the old car park, two hundred paces from the reactor hall. Behind them was the camp, which was four tents, a winch on a sledge, a cart on a cable, and a crate of ash tins that Abel guarded the way other men guarded money. In front of them was the plant, where the concrete was streaked black, the cooling tower had a crack down one side like a split egg, and weeds grew out of the roof of the turbine hall.

When Ines was a girl, everyone in the river towns had known the story of Corbel Reach: how the backup generators ran out of diesel in the second winter after the Fall, how the pumps stopped, and how the core got hotter and hotter with nobody to cool it, until the steam came up and the wind went east. For nearly ninety years the only people who had gone closer than the far bank were scavengers, and the ones who came home had come home sick.

Now Meridian's Home Line had sent four people to go inside.

"Dosimeter," said Abel.

Ines held up the hardened meter on its strap. It was an old design, a chamber of gas and a needle with no chip anywhere inside it, and the needle sat near the bottom of its arc.

"Six," she said.

"Six," said Abel. "Go. Twelve minutes."

The winchman, Dov, threw the lever, and the cable took up slack. Ines held the cart rail and walked beside it, past the yellow line and the second line and across the cracked yard to the blast door, while the meter on her chest ticked faintly and she tried not to listen to it.

Inside, the air was cold and smelled of wet concrete, and her lamp lit the long room in pieces. Desk. Panel. A chair still pushed back from the desk, as if someone had stood up quickly. The binders were on a shelf behind the shift supervisor's station, forty of them, in grey plastic covers gone soft with damp. There were operating procedures and emergency procedures and maintenance logs, everything the old world had known about how to keep a reactor from doing what this one had done.

She took three binders and put them in the cart, and then three more. She checked the dead clock out of habit, and then her own watch. Half her time was gone.

On the way back she stopped at the instrument panel for the core. Behind it, through a small window of thick yellow glass, she could see a gauge that still had its needle. She wanted that panel. She wanted to know what the core had been doing in its last hour, and she had wanted it since she was fourteen and found her first pump valve in a box of scrap.

She put her hand on the panel cover. Then she read the meter on her chest.

She took her hand off the cover and pulled the cart out with ten seconds in hand.

---

Abel was waiting at the yellow line with his sleeves rolled up.

"Sit," he said.

She sat on the upturned crate. He knelt in front of her and put his palms flat on her collarbones, then on her ribs, then on her thighs above the knee, and closed his eyes. His face went still and listening, the way her grandmother's face had gone when she listened for a kettle.

"There," he said. "It's in your long bones. It's gritty. Like sand in a blanket."

"Can you get it?"

"Some." He opened one of the ash tins with his thumbnail and tipped a pinch of grey powder into his left palm, where it lay fine and pale and faintly warm until he closed his hand around it. "Hold still."

Ines had seen him do this twenty times in two days, and she still did not understand it. Abel was hand-door Kindled, Fire rank, trained at Meridian, and he had told her that recall was not healing. It found the part of the poison that was still burning inside a living body and pulled it out before it did more damage. What was already done stayed done.

She felt it as a drawing ache, deep in her thighs and hips, as if something were being pulled through her by a thread, and then it was gone. Abel's left fist tightened. The ash in it went from pale grey to dark, almost black, and he gasped a little.

He got up, walked to the vent pit, and put both hands down into the wet clay.

The vent pit was a hole dug at the edge of the camp, ringed with orange cord and lined with clay from the river. Abel knelt there with his eyes shut for half a minute, and when he took his hands out, they were clean, and he was pale. The spent ash lay in the clay like soot.

"Don't touch that," he said. "Ever. We'll seal it when we go."

He came back and opened the ash crate and looked into it for a long time.

"How much is left?" said Ines.

"Enough for you once more. If I clear Linnet after her next pull, there's nothing for me." He did not look up. "Linnet's at her line already. She's done."

Linnet was their second healer, and she was lying in the tent with a wet cloth over her face, because she had cleared Dov twice that morning when the cable jammed and he had to go out to free it.

"Then you clear me once more," said Ines. "And I get the core panel."

Abel shut the crate.

"No," he said.

"Abel—"

"You get the core panel, you come out with that needle twice as high. More, if the panel sticks. I clear you, and the tin's empty. And then I've got whatever I couldn't separate from you sitting in my own hands, and nothing to spend getting it out." He held up his palms, which were trembling. "Recall's not free. It's paid for. When the ash runs out, it gets paid for out of me, and if I keep going after that, I'm the next one on the cot."

"It's three more minutes."

"It's the line." He tapped the crate. "You can see what's in there same as I can. A ward won't help you. Linnet can't push this stuff off you, and nobody else can either, because it's not a thing a ward moves. All we've got is time, distance, ash, and the line. And we're at the line."

Ines turned toward the plant, where the crack in the cooling tower ran down from the lip like a river on a map. She thought about the binders in the cart, the shift logs, the emergency procedures, the chair pushed back from the desk.

"You've done this before," she said.

"I've done it at Meridian. On scavengers." He met her eyes then. "I've also watched a healer go past the line. She cleared three men out of the Ridley mine, and she went in for a fourth. We buried her in the spring."

Then he stood up and turned toward the winch, and called it, loud enough for the whole camp to hear.

"Abort! Rotation five is off! The ash is out and the healer's at his line!"

At the winch, Dov straightened. "Abort, rotation five," he called back. "Heard."

In the tent, Linnet lifted the cloth off her face, looked at the canvas over her for a moment, and put the cloth back.

"That's not a failure," said Abel, more quietly. "That's the job. Six binders out. We went in and we came out, and now everybody knows we can. We come back in the autumn with forty tins and two more healers."

Ines looked at the plant for a long moment. Then she nodded.

In the evening she sat by the lamp in the tent with the six binders stacked beside her and did not open them. She was thinking about the thing every child in the river towns knew, which the old world had known too and had not been able to do anything about. The core did not need a person to stay hot. It needed a person to stay cool.

Abel was asleep on the next cot with his hands open on the blanket, palms up, as though he were still waiting for something to be put into them. Ines turned the lamp down and went to the tent flap. They had sealed the vent pit that afternoon with a lid of river clay, and the orange stakes stood around it in the last of the light. Beyond the stakes and the yellow line, the plant sat dark against the sky, with all its clocks stopped at the same minute and one chair pushed back from a desk.

Three minutes from that chair there was a panel she had not opened. It would still be there in the autumn.

## 6. The Weir

*2182 — one hundred two years after the Fall*

Gideon Sayle had not slept properly in four days, and the stranger in the doorway was eating his bread.

"Sorry," said the stranger. He did not look sorry. He looked hungry. "The woman at the gate said I could have it. She said you weren't going to eat it anyway."

"Who are you?"

"Tamar Vey." He finished the crust and dusted his hands on his coat. "I teach letters. And anything else anyone asks me, which is mostly nothing. The Home Line sends me around the work camps in winter, and I came to see if anyone here wants to learn to read."

"Everyone here can read," said Gideon.

"Then I'll sit by your stove and be useless," said Tamar. "I'm good at it."

He was a large, slow, untidy man of about fifty, with a limp in his left leg and a beard that had been cut by someone in a hurry. His coat was patched at the elbows with two different colors, and the satchel over his shoulder clanked when he put it down. He lowered himself onto the bench by the workshop stove with a long sigh and stretched his bad leg out toward the heat.

Gideon went back to his table.

The workshop at Corbel Reach was a long tin shed against the old turbine hall. The plant's own drawings were pinned along one wall, and along another were the parts, the valves and pump housings and lengths of pipe and the pressure gauges with their glass cleaned. In the middle of the floor, on a test stand, sat the reason Gideon had not slept.

It was an ash cell, a sealed steel can the size of a bread crock, with a pinch of refined ash inside and a coil of copper wound around its waist. When a hand-door Kindled held a pattern on it, the ash gave up its warmth slowly and the copper drew off a thin, steady current, which was enough to run a small pump.

For about half a day.

Then the ash went to dead grey dust, and the pump stopped.

"That's the problem," said Gideon, to nobody. He had been saying it to nobody for a week. "That's the whole problem."

"What is?" said Tamar from the stove.

Gideon did not want to explain, because he was too tired. But the shed was quiet, and the two welders had gone to supper, and the man by the stove was watching him with plain, patient interest, so he explained.

"A reactor keeps making heat for weeks after you shut it down," he said. "The old one here melted because its pumps stopped and its heat didn't. We can build a new one, small. But the first thing anyone at Meridian asks is, what happens when the pumps stop? There's no grid now. There's nobody to call."

"So you want the pumps to run on ash."

"Ash is the only power a person can carry in a can. But it runs out. Half a day." Gideon pushed his hands through his hair. "If I could make it burn for a season, the reactor would be safe. I've tried thinner patterns. Colder patterns. Purer ash. I've had three Kindled holding one cell at once until they nearly fainted. Half a day. A little more, once."

Tamar was quiet for a while. Then he said, "My uncle had a mill."

Gideon closed his eyes.

"I'm not changing the subject," said Tamar. "I'm going the long way round. I always go the long way round. It's the leg." He shifted on the bench. "The mill was on the Tarrow, below the ford. There was a millrace off the river. You know what a millrace is?"

"A channel. To the wheel."

"Right. Now, the river had all the strength in the valley. More water went past that mill every hour than my uncle could have carried in his whole life, and he never added a drop to it. Couldn't. What he did, every spring, was go out in his boots and set the stones in the weir. Big flat stones, set just so. The stones told the river where to go. Most of it went on down the valley like always, and a little of it went into the race, and turned the wheel, and ground the corn."

"I'm not a miller."

"No. Listen. Here's the part I liked when I was a boy." Tamar held up one finger. "My uncle went home for supper. And the stones kept telling the river where to go. All night. All summer. He wasn't standing there holding them. He'd set them, and they held."

Gideon opened his eyes.

"You keep trying to make the ash be more river," said Tamar. "Maybe it's better at being stones."

"Ash isn't stone. It burns."

"Does it?" Tamar reached for his satchel. "Can I show you something? It's small. It might not work. It didn't, the first time I tried it, and I burned my thumb."

He took out a tin bowl, a short length of copper wire bent into a hook at each end, a twist of paper, and a smooth river stone the size of an egg. He put the stone on the stove top, and while it warmed, he filled the bowl with water from the bucket by the door. He hung the copper wire across the bowl so that one hooked end rested on the rim beside the stone and the other end hung over the far rim into a tin cup. Then he went outside, came back with a cupful of snow, and set it under the far end of the wire.

"Hand-door," he said, touching his own chest. "Ward, mostly. I held a line on the Home Line for eight years before my leg went. I'm not much good anymore, but I can still do a little fence."

He took the stone off the stove with his sleeve and set it in the water at the near edge of the bowl, where steam lifted off it. Then he opened the twist of paper and tipped a pinch of ash onto the copper where it touched the stone.

He put his hands around the bowl without touching it and closed his eyes.

Gideon felt nothing and saw nothing. For a moment, it looked like an old man warming his hands.

Then the steam stopped rising from the water.

The stone sat in the bowl, still hot, and the water around it did not warm. Instead, a thin line of frost began to vanish from the copper wire, from the near end to the far end, and over the tin cup a drop of water fell off the wire into the snow. Then another. The snow began to sink around the wire's tip.

"I'm putting a fence round the stone's heat," said Tamar quietly. "A little ward. It can't go into the water. It can only go one way. Along the copper."

"You're holding it."

"Yes. Now watch."

He took his hands away, put them in his armpits, and leaned back on the bench.

The fence held.

The water around the hot stone stayed cool, and the snow in the tin cup went on melting, drop by drop, from the copper's tip. Gideon found that he was holding his breath, and let it out, and took another. Tamar sat with his hands in his armpits and his eyes open and did nothing at all.

Then the pinch of ash on the copper went from pale grey to dull, dead grey, and the water around the stone began to steam again. The dripping stopped.

"There," said Tamar. "The ash ran out."

Gideon stared at the bowl.

"It didn't burn," he said slowly.

"No."

"It didn't give off heat. It wasn't the fuel. The stone was the fuel." He stood up, and his chair fell over behind him, and he did not notice. "It held what you told it. After you let go."

"For about fifty breaths," said Tamar. "With a pinch. I'm not very good." He leaned forward and peered into the bowl, as interested as if he had never seen it before. "I don't know anything about reactors. I couldn't tell you what's inside one. But it seems to me your reactor already knows how to make heat. It's very good at heat. It's too good. What it doesn't have is somebody standing there all night setting the stones."

Gideon turned to the ash cell on the test stand, and thought of half a day of current, and of a week spent trying to make ash into more river.

"You want the ash to hold the rules," he said. "Around the heat. Not be the heat."

"I don't want anything," said Tamar. "I'm a schoolteacher. I'm asking."

Gideon picked up his chair and sat down in it. Then he got up again and went to the shelf and took down a binder and opened it and put it back, because he did not know where to start. He knew already that it would take years, and that the pattern would have to be far more complicated than a little fence around a stone. Somebody would have to find out how big a pinch could hold how complicated a rule, and for how long, and what happened when it ran out halfway, and somebody would get burned finding out, or worse. Nothing about the bowl told him how to build anything.

But it told him what to try.

Tamar stayed three days. He taught the younger welder to read the long words in the binders, and he told stories at supper that were mostly about his uncle's mill and a goat that had once eaten a bishop's hat, and he fell asleep by the stove every night with his mouth open. On the fourth morning he packed his copper and his twist of paper, left his tin bowl on the stove shelf, and went on down the river road toward the next camp, limping, with Gideon's last loaf of bread under his arm.

Gideon never saw him again.

That same morning he called the two welders and the three pattern Kindled to the bench, before the stove was even lit, and put the tin bowl in the middle of it, because Tamar had left it behind. Whether he had meant to was anybody's guess.

"Here's the question," said Gideon. "Can ash hold the rules around the heat, instead of being the heat?"

They looked at the bowl. Then they looked at him.

"I don't know," he said. "Nobody does. We start today."

He sat down at the bench, and after a moment, so did they.

## 7. Commissioning

*2195 — one hundred fifteen years after the Fall*

By the time they lit Unit One, there were three hundred people on the hill above Corbel Reach, and most of them had brought lunch.

Gideon Sayle watched them from the control room window. He was sixty-one now, and his knees hurt in the cold. There were families on blankets and children sliding down the frosted grass on tin trays, and there was a man from the Meridian news sheet in a borrowed coat, eating somebody else's sandwiches. Somebody had brought a fiddle.

"They think it's a festival," said Pia Morrow beside him.

"It is a festival," said Gideon. "They're just early."

Pia was the operator. She was thirty, with short hair and steady hands, and she had trained for six years on a mock control desk built out of wood and painted cardboard before she ever sat at a real one. Every needle on this desk was hardened, and every switch had a mechanical backup. In front of her, under a hinged cover painted red, was the scram lever, and if she pulled it, the control rods would drop into the core on their own weight, with no power needed and no pattern needed. Just gravity.

"Board's clear," she said. "Lattice crew?"

"Lattice holding," said the speaking tube from the core hall.

Unit One was small, which was the first thing everyone said about it. It would make enough power for the town of Corbel, the workshops, and the hospital at Lowmarket by wire down the river. The old reactor had been forty times bigger, and nobody wanted to build one that big again, not yet.

The second thing everyone said was the lattice.

It sat around the core like a cage, forty-eight steel cartridges, each the size of a man's forearm and each packed with graded ash. Over thirteen years, Gideon's people had learned to set patterns in them the way Tamar had set a fence around a stone, except that this was not one fence but hundreds of small rules woven together. *Heat may go this way. Not that way. If it builds here, spread it there.* Six Kindled had spent a month impressing the master pattern, and a Blaze-rank ward master from Meridian had checked every cartridge by hand.

Once it was set, nobody had to stand beside it.

The water still carried the heat to the turbine, and the pumps still ran. But if the pumps stopped, the lattice would hold the core's heat in a safe shape and spread it into the great sink of cold water under the floor, and it would go on holding for weeks, while the ash lasted, which was long enough for people to come.

That was the idea.

"Withdrawing rods," said Pia. "Ten percent."

The needles moved.

On the hill, the fiddle stopped, and people stood up on their blankets. There was nothing for them to see, no light or sound or smoke, only the great grey building sitting in the snow the same as before. But they stood up anyway, and some of them took off their hats.

Twenty percent. Thirty. The temperature needles climbed in slow, even steps as steam went to the turbine, and in the turbine hall the big shaft began to turn.

At forty percent, Pia closed the main breaker.

Down the valley, along the one street of Corbel that had been strung with new wire, the lamps came on.

Gideon heard the cheer from the hill through the thick glass. He saw children jumping, and the man from the news sheet throwing his hat in the air, and he let out a breath he had been holding, it felt like, for thirteen years.

"Sixty percent," said Pia. "Following load."

It held at sixty for twenty minutes, and everything was even. The lattice cartridges each had a small gauge on the outside of the core hall, forty-eight needles in a ring, and every one of them sat in the green.

Then cartridge thirty-one began to drop.

Gideon saw it before Pia did. It was on the far side of the ring, nearest the hot channel where the coolant came in, and its needle slid from green into yellow while the ones on either side of it did not move.

"Thirty-one," he said.

Pia looked. "It's spending faster than the others."

"Why?"

"I don't know. It's closest to the hot channel. It's doing more work." Her voice was very calm. "Thirty and thirty-two are steady."

The needle on thirty-one kept falling. Yellow. Low yellow.

On the core temperature board, one needle twitched.

It was a single thermocouple on the hot-channel side, and it went up two degrees and then five, while the others stayed where they were.

"It's leaning," said Gideon. He could feel his heart in his throat. "When thirty-one runs out, that part of the fence forgets. And the heat leans into the gap."

"Lattice crew," said Pia into the tube. "Can you hold thirty-one?"

"Trying." The voice from the core hall was strained. "It's deep in the ring. Rao's reaching for it. It's drinking faster than he can—"

The needle on thirty-one touched red.

The hot-channel thermocouple jumped twelve degrees in a breath.

Pia lifted the red cover and pulled the scram lever.

There was a heavy, rattling thud from the core hall, felt through the floor more than heard, as the control rods fell. The needles on the board shuddered and began, all together, to drop. On the hill, the cheering faltered, and down the valley the lamps of Corbel went dark.

For a long moment, nobody in the control room spoke.

Then the hot-channel needle peaked, paused, and began to come down.

The lattice held around the shut core, with forty-seven cartridges in the green and one in the red, and the heat spreading slowly and safely into the cold water under the floor, exactly the way it was meant to and exactly the way the old reactor never had.

Gideon sat down in the chair beside the operator's desk because his legs would not hold him.

"Well," said Pia. Her hands were shaking now that it was over. "That's not in the binders."

"No."

"It wasn't the pattern. The pattern was fine. It was the fuel. One cartridge doing more work than the others and running out first, and once one runs out, its neighbors have to carry its share, and then they run out faster." She studied the ring of needles. "If we'd been at a hundred percent, we'd have lost three before I saw it."

"Then nobody runs at a hundred," said Gideon. "And nobody lets one cartridge get past half before it's changed. And nobody runs a cartridge without a pair on the other side of it. Every cartridge gets its own meter on the desk. Not out in the hall. Here."

He leaned to the speaking tube, so that the lattice crew would hear it too, and Rao, who had been reaching for thirty-one.

"Ash is finite," he said, "and it doesn't spend evenly. A fence with one weak stone is not a fence. Check the pairs."

"Check the pairs," said the tube, in a tired voice.

Outside, the people on the hill had not gone home. They were standing in the snow, waiting to be told whether to be afraid. Gideon put on his coat and went out to tell them the truth, which was that the new reactor had found a way to fail on its first day, and had failed safely, and that this was the best possible thing that could have happened, because now they knew.

Some of them believed him.

Six weeks later, they lit it again. It ran for nineteen years.

Every shutdown code after that, in every plant from Corbel Reach to the coast, began with the same three words.

## 8. First Light on Weighbridge Street

*2247 — one hundred sixty-seven years after the Fall*

The core came on a flat wagon pulled by a lift-truck, and the whole of Weighbridge Street came out to watch it go by.

Femi Oduya rode on the back with it. It was the size of a wardrobe and painted the dull green of everything the Halden Works made, with a brass plate on the side. *Compact Core, Domestic, Class C. Pattern impressed under Halden license. Do not open. Do not move while running. CHECK THE PAIRS.*

"Is that it?" said a boy on the curb. "Is that the reactor?"

"That's it," said Femi.

"It's small."

"It only has to do one building."

The boy looked unconvinced, and Femi did not blame him. Across the canal, in the Lantern district, the big houses had had cores in their cellars for twenty years, and you could see the Lantern from the Weighbridge at night, a long low wash of steady white light along the far bank. On this side, people still lit their rooms with oil, and with the old solar panels on the roofs, and with whatever ash lamps they could afford to have refilled. The last coal plant upriver had closed in Femi's grandfather's time. The big solar fields outside the city were still there, tilted at the sky, but these days they were mostly backup, because everything worth having ran on cores and cells now.

Everything on the far side of the canal, anyway.

The Pellow Building was five stories of yellow brick with a crack over the door. Its landlord, Mr. Pask, met them on the step in a good coat.

"About time," he said. "I've been paying for that license since spring."

"The core's the core," said Femi. "It comes when the Works says it comes."

It took him and his apprentice all morning to set it in the cellar. They bolted it to the floor and ran its heavy cables to the induction board on the wall, and from the board up through the building to a small steel cell box in every flat. The core would make the heat. Its pattern, impressed in the Halden yards by a Glory master Femi had never seen and never would, would hold the fission steady and spread its heat to the induction ring, and the ring would turn heat into current. The cell boxes in the flats would take that current and store it and give it out, and each one had its own meter and its own red handle.

It was not magic in the old sense, the sense that meant a woman with her hands on a sick cow. It was a hundred and fifty years of hard, careful work, and a license fee, and a Works inspector willing to put his name to it.

That was Femi's job.

At noon he went up to the second floor to fit the first cell box. Flat 2C belonged to a woman named Hester Quayle, who opened the door with a wooden spoon in her hand and a small boy hanging off her skirt.

"You're the power man," she said.

"I'm the install man. The power comes later."

"How much later?"

"Today, if everything passes."

She let out her breath. Femi noticed then that the flat was very cold. There was a little oil stove in the corner, not lit, and on the windowsill, packed in a bowl of snow, were four small glass vials.

"Insulin," said Hester, when she saw where his eyes had gone. "For Jory. The chemist on Mill Lane keeps it cold for me, but he charges, and in summer I can't—" She stopped. "The cell box. It'll run a cold-cupboard?"

"It'll run a cold-cupboard."

"All the time? Even at night?"

"All the time," said Femi. "That's what it's for."

He fitted the box on the wall beside her stove. Then he went down to the cellar to fetch the building's cells.

The cells were the last piece. Each was a sealed can of refined ash that slotted into a flat's cell box and held the building's reserve, so that if the core shut down, the lights and the cold-cupboards would run for a few days more. Mr. Pask had bought the cells himself, and they were stacked in a crate by the cellar door, twelve of them, each stamped on the lid. *Grade First. Clean.*

Femi picked one up.

He was hand-door Kindled, Flame rank, which was not much. But every installer who carried a Works license had to pass a grading test, and Femi had passed his on the first try, so he did not really need the meter, though he would use it anyway. He closed his hand around the can and felt for the ash inside.

It was wrong.

Clean ash felt like warm sand, smooth and even all the way through, and this felt like sand with gravel in it, lumpy and dull in places. Somebody had cut the good ash with something else, ordinary stove ash or soot, and then stamped the can *First*.

He tried a second can and found the same thing, and then a third.

He put the meter on the fourth to be sure. The needle stopped well short of the line.

"Mr. Pask," he said.

The landlord came down the cellar steps. "Problem?"

"Your cells are cut." Femi held one out. "They're stamped First. They grade at Third, maybe lower. There's filler in them."

"Nonsense. I paid First for them."

"Then you were cheated. I'm sorry." Femi set the can down. "I can't pass them into the building."

Mr. Pask's face went red. "Do you know what those cost me?"

"I know what they'll cost the people upstairs." Femi kept his voice level. "Cut ash doesn't hold a pattern evenly. A cell with filler in it can run hot on one side. If the core trips some night and the building goes over to cells, one of these could cook its box. Or start a fire. Or just die at two in the morning when somebody's depending on it."

"Then fit them and I'll replace them later."

"I can't." Femi put his hand flat on the crate. "If I pass it, it's my name on this building. If I don't pass it, the building doesn't pass. That's how it works."

"The building doesn't pass, nobody gets power."

"Nobody gets power until you bring clean cells. A week, maybe. The Works will sell you graded stock."

Mr. Pask said several things then that Femi did not repeat to anybody afterward. Then he went back up the steps and slammed the cellar door hard enough to shake dust out of the ceiling.

Femi stood in the cellar for a while with his hands on the crate, thinking about the four little vials in the bowl of snow.

Then he went out to his wagon and opened his own stock box. Installers carried two clean spare cells, Works graded, for testing boards, and they were not for selling and not for giving away. He took one out, carried it up to the second floor, and knocked on 2C.

"The building doesn't pass today," he said, when Hester opened the door. He saw her face fall, and kept going. "But I can wire your cold-cupboard to a test cell. It's not on the core, and it won't run your lights, but it'll keep the cupboard cold for about two weeks. By then the building should be on."

She eyed the cell in his hands. "What does that cost?"

"It's a test cell. I'm testing your cupboard."

She did not believe him, and he could see that. But she stepped back and let him in.

He fitted the cell and wired it to the old cold-cupboard in the corner, which had been dead since before Jory was born. He checked the meter, and he showed Hester the red handle and where the shutdown was and how to read the needle. Then he closed the switch.

The cupboard shuddered, and somewhere inside it a small motor began to hum.

Jory let go of his mother's skirt. He walked across the floor and put his ear against the cupboard door.

"It's making a noise," he said.

"That's it working," said Femi.

The boy opened the door, and a small yellow light came on inside. He stared at it as if it were the most wonderful thing he had ever seen, and Femi, who had seen a thousand of them, found that he could not turn away from it either.

Hester moved the four little vials from the windowsill into the cupboard, one at a time, and shut the door.

Downstairs, in the cellar, Femi stood over the crate of cut cells for a while.

Mr. Pask would be at the Works by morning, and the Works would want its test cell back or its price out of Femi's wages, and somebody in a good coat would ask him what he thought he was doing. He found that he was not worried about what he would tell them. A child's medicine was in that cupboard. The ash said *First* and was not. He picked up one of the cut cans, weighed it in his palm a last time, and put it back in the crate with the others, lid down.

It was dusk by the time he had the wagon loaded. Across the canal, the Lantern had come on, a long white wash of light along the far bank, the same as every evening of his life. Weighbridge Street was dark behind him, with oil lamps showing in a window here and there and the Pellow Building standing unlit over its new green core. He looked up to the second floor anyway. He could not see it from the street, but he knew it was there, behind a cupboard door in 2C: one small yellow light, on this side of the water.

## 9. The Haul

*2362 — two hundred eighty-two years after the Fall*

The ash lay on the gravel bar in a long grey drift, and Tova Reinholt could see the river coming for it.

"That's a season," said Hob Lanyard over the speaking wire. He was the crew chief, and his voice crackled in her ear above the whine of the lift engines. "That's the whole season, Tova. That's two new lifters and every wage on the boat paid through the winter."

"I can see it."

"Then let's get it."

She hung in her harness below the belly of the lift craft, ten meters over the flooded valley, and looked down. The craft was a long, flat-bottomed thing with four lift rings, one at each corner, humming with the steady blue-white of ash induction, and under it the Ferren valley had turned into a brown lake overnight.

The dam at the head of the valley was still standing, which was the good news. The bad news was the spillway. A rift had opened in the spillway channel at two in the morning, and something had come through it that the sensing masts at Saltmere had called a Barrel yield before it was all the way out. It had torn the spillway gates half off their hinges, and the water behind the dam had been pouring through the gap ever since.

Tova's crew had killed it at dawn.

She was still shaking from that. It had taken all six of them, two hours, and most of their ash, because the thing had been the size of a barn and had moved like a landslide. When Kaspar finally put his spear into whatever it used for a heart, it had come apart all at once into the biggest drift of clean ash anyone on the boat had ever seen.

Now that ash lay on a gravel bar in the middle of the valley with the water rising around it, and in twenty minutes, maybe less, it would be under the flood. Ash that went into moving water spread out and settled in the mud, and it was gone. This drift was worth more than the lift craft. It was worth more than the lift craft twice.

"Fuel?" she said.

"Thirty-one on the ring meter," said Ansel, the pilot. "We can take the ash. It's heavy. Two lifts to the hill and then home on fumes. Or one lift of the ash and some of the rest scraped on the next pass."

"And the rift?"

"Closing," said the sensing operator, Mags, from the back. "Slowly. Masts say it's down to a crack. Another two hours, maybe, and it's shut."

Tova nodded to nobody. She turned in the harness and looked down the valley.

Well below the gravel bar, the flood had reached the edge of Ferren village. Most of the people had got out in the night, and she had seen them on the hills at dawn, wrapped in blankets beside their carts. But on the roof of a farmhouse at the far end of the village, there were three people and a dog, two adults and one child, and one of the adults was waving a red cloth.

The water was at the farmhouse's upper windows.

"Hob," she said.

"I see them."

"They weren't there an hour ago."

"They were inside. Now they're on the roof." His voice was flat. "The Vellen company boat is coming up from Lisk. It'll be here in forty minutes."

"The water will be at the roof in thirty."

There was a silence on the wire.

"Tova," said Hob. "Listen to me. I know the rule. I know. Lives first. But the rule says clean ash too, and that's a whole Barrel of clean ash going into the mud. That's power for half of Saltmere for a winter. That's people too. You just don't see their faces."

She knew he was right, and that was the worst part. Every ash cell in Saltmere ran on what companies like theirs brought home, and that meant every hospital, every cold-cupboard, every pump. The company standings rewarded clean ash for a reason, and the people who drew up the standings had not been fools.

She checked the meter on the ring. Thirty-one.

"Ansel. If we go for the roof, what's the fuel after?"

"Down to twelve, maybe. Fourteen. Enough to get the family to the hill and the boat home. Not enough to come back for the ash."

"And if I go on the harness? Not the boat."

"Your harness is at eight minutes."

Eight minutes of lift. The personal rigs were not made for long flights. They were made for short, hard jumps by people trained to use them, and Tova had trained for six years to join the fewer than one fighter in twenty in the companies who were licensed to wear one. On a good day she could hold a straight line in a crosswind for eight minutes, set down soft, and lift again. She had never carried a child on one.

"I'll go to the roof on the harness," she said. "You bring the boat down over the house. I'll put them on the ladder one at a time. That's less fuel than the boat landing in the water."

"And the ash?" said Hob.

Brown water was already lapping at the edges of the grey drift on the gravel bar.

"The ash goes in the river," she said.

Hob did not answer. Then the wire clicked.

"All right," he said. "All right. Go. I'll answer for it."

She went.

The harness was a frame of struts across her back and down both legs, with two small lift rings at her shoulders. She thumbed the throttle, felt the ash in the cells at her hips warm against her skin, and dropped out from under the boat. The wind caught her at once, and she leaned into it, found her line, and flew.

It was the better part of two kilometers. She did it in a little under three minutes, low over the water, with the spray coming up at her and the cell heat building at her hips, while the farmhouse grew larger and the people on it came clear: a man in a wet shirt, a woman with a little girl of about five in her arms, and a brown dog with its tail between its legs.

She set down on the roof ridge harder than she meant to, and her knee hit the tiles. The man grabbed her arm before she slid.

"Company," she said. "Saltmere. There's a boat coming. You're going up a ladder."

"The baby first," said the woman. "Please. Her first."

The lift craft came down the valley behind her, low and loud, and its downdraft tore at the water and sent spray across the roof. Ansel brought it in over the house as gently as Tova had ever seen him fly, and the rope ladder came down and slapped against the tiles.

Tova took the child, who screamed and grabbed her hair. She held the little girl against her chest, got one boot on the ladder rung, and climbed one-handed with the harness throttled up just enough to take half her weight, until Kaspar's arms came down and took the child at the top.

She went back for the woman. Then the man. Then, because the man would not climb without it, the dog.

The harness meter was at two minutes when she pulled herself over the edge of the boat's deck. She lay there on her back with the downdraft roaring and the little girl crying somewhere behind her and every muscle in her body shaking.

Ansel lifted them away.

As they turned toward the hill, Tova rolled over and looked up the valley.

The gravel bar was gone. There was only brown water where it had been, and a faint grey sheen spreading out on the current like smoke.

Hob came and crouched beside her. He did not say anything for a while. He looked at the brown water where the gravel bar had been, and then he rubbed his face with both hands.

"The board will dock us for the ash," he said. "We'll be fourth this season. Maybe fifth."

"I know."

"The Vellen boat would have been here in forty minutes."

"The water would have been at the roof in thirty."

He held her eyes for a moment. Then he took off his own jacket, which was dry, and went and put it round the shoulders of the woman from the roof, and came back and sat down by Tova on the wet deck without a word.

Behind them, the crying had stopped. The little girl was sitting against the deck rail in somebody's company jacket, with both arms around the wet brown dog, and the two of them were watching the roof of their house get smaller behind the boat.

## 10. The Stair

*2603 — five hundred twenty-three years after the Fall*

The lane-drill committee of the Crooked Line School met on the top floor of the old lecture house, in a room with tall windows that looked straight down into the Old Yard. Marisol Quenby sat at the far end of the table, on a hard chair against the wall, because she was there to answer questions and not to vote.

She was twenty-six. She had run the School's lane for first-years for three years, and for three years nobody had asked her anything at all.

Last Thursday, a first-year named Davey Mott had run the lane in the dark and hit the leaning post at the end of it at full stride, and broken his collarbone in two places.

"We are not here to blame anybody," said the chair.

He was a pleasant man named Teague Orme, with neat silver hair and a Halden Works pin on his lapel. House Halden had paid for the committee, and for the School's new lecture hall, and for the row of tall induction lamps that workmen were bolting to the wall over the Old Yard that very morning. Marisol could hear them through the window, clanking. Nobody in the room mentioned the lamps, and everybody knew about them.

"We are here," said Orme, "because a boy of fourteen is in the infirmary, and his mother would like to know why the Crooked Line School sends children down a lane of posts with their eyes covered."

"It's a lane drill," said a lecturer from Stonehand. "Every school does it."

"Lit," said Orme. "Every other school does it lit." He turned. "Miss Quenby. You were at the lane."

Marisol stood up, because she did not know whether she was supposed to.

"He ran it straight," she said. "From the sheds to the end. He didn't call the turn before the eighth, and he didn't touch the posts, and he didn't say who had the bar. He just ran." She made herself look at the chair and not at the floor. "He ran it straight and silent because I hadn't made him call. He'd been quiet all term, and I let him be quiet. If he'd called the turn, he'd have turned. That was mine."

There was a small silence.

"That's very honest of you," said Orme kindly. "And I think it rather makes the point. A drill that breaks a child's bones if the instructor has a soft week is not a drill we can defend to his mother."

"It's the call that keeps them safe," said Marisol. "Not the dark. If you drop the call—"

"Thank you, Miss Quenby."

She sat down.

The Warden of the Order of the Even Hand was at the middle of the table, in grey, with her hands folded in front of her. Her name was Ottilie Sand, and Marisol had heard that the Order's people trained in one door for their whole lives and sometimes did not speak for a year at a time. She did not speak now. She was watching Marisol.

Across from her sat a heavy man in a Home Line field coat, Foreman Absalom Rudd, who had his arms folded and looked as if he had been in better rooms.

"There's one more thing," said Orme. He smiled down the table at a senior student who had been put on the committee to speak for the students, a tall cheerful boy named Jasper Lyle. "Mr. Lyle. You've run that lane for four years. Why does the last post lean?"

Lyle grinned. He knew the answer, and so did everyone else.

"Because the road isn't straight," he said, and laughed.

Half the table laughed with him. Orme laughed. Even the lecturer from Stonehand smiled.

"Quite," said Orme. "I think that's our answer. I move that the School run its lane lit from the start of next term, and that the call be dropped from the drill, since with the lamps on, it serves no purpose."

They voted. It was not close.

The Warden's hand went up against it, and Foreman Rudd's, and two lecturers' from the Home Line side. That was all. Marisol, who did not have a vote, watched the hands go up and down.

"Carried," said Orme.

Foreman Rudd unfolded his arms.

"The Line will go on running its own lanes dark," he said. "With the call."

"That is the Line's business," said Orme pleasantly. "Item four."

---

Afterward, on the stair, the Warden caught her up.

The stair went down the outside of the lecture house in a long stone spiral, with a window at every turn, and from the third window Marisol could see the workmen in the Old Yard below. They had six of the new lamps up already. They were tall and white and very bright, even in daylight, and they made the old black posts look small.

"Miss Quenby," said Warden Sand.

Marisol stopped. "Warden."

"You said that was yours. In a room that was trying very hard to give it to somebody else." The Warden came down two more steps, so that they were level. She was older than Marisol had thought, close to, with a lined, quiet face. "I'd like to offer you a place."

"A place?"

"At our house. In the hills." Sand put it simply, as if it were an ordinary thing to say to a stranger on a stair. "The Order would train you in your one door. Ward, I think. You'd give up the others. You'd give up most things. And you'd stay."

Marisol did not know what to say, so she said the true thing. "Why would I do that?"

"Most people ask why we do it." The Warden looked out of the window, down at the lamps. "Do you know who Hesper Annick was?"

"The woman on the flood-wall. In bronze."

"She was ours. Our master. She reached Glory, which almost nobody does, and she held the Eastbank wall through the whole of a flood. And she had a dream, when she was fevered, the same dream, more than once." Sand's voice did not change. She might have been giving directions to the tram. "She saw the sky come off. Not torn. Gone. Like the roof off a house in the night." She stopped there, as if that were as far as anybody went on a stair. "There was more. She didn't tell it to strangers. Neither do I."

The workmen in the yard had stopped for their lunch. It was very quiet on the stair.

"That's what you believe?" said Marisol.

"That's what we believe. That the sky will come off. That when it does, somebody had better be standing there who has spent a whole life on one door and knows exactly what it can hold." The Warden turned back to her. "We don't know when. We don't know what comes after. We train so that we're there."

"And you want me."

"I want somebody who says *that was mine* in a room like that one." Sand almost smiled. "We don't get many."

Marisol looked down at the yard, at the new lamps and the old posts and the long strip of packed earth where she had run first-years in the dark for three years.

"I run a lane," she said.

"You ran one," said the Warden. "They've lit it." She went on down the stair. At the next turn she said, without looking back, "The offer's on the latch. It doesn't go stale."

## 11. Three Doors

*2676 — five hundred ninety-six years after the Fall*

The Home Line courier came at seven in the morning, while Kiva Rowan was still trying to make her hair lie flat.

She heard the shop bell downstairs, and then Mara's voice, low and surprised, and then a long silence. Kiva put down the comb and went to the top of the stairs.

Her aunt was standing in the shop doorway in her work apron, holding a small grey case with a seal on it, while a woman in a Home Line field coat walked away down Coper Street toward the lift-tram stop. The morning was bright and cold. Above the rooftops, a pair of lift craft slid along the eastern flight lane toward the station with their rings glinting.

"Who was that?" said Kiva.

Mara did not answer at once. She shut the door and turned the shop sign to *Closed*, though they were not open yet anyway. Then she set the case on the counter between a half-repaired pressure gauge and a tray of meter needles and regarded it as if it might bite.

"It's from Ilse," she said.

Kiva came down the stairs slowly. "Ilse's dead."

"I know she's dead." Mara put her hand flat on the case. "She left instructions with the Home Line. A sealed appointment. It was to come to you on the day of your Kindling and not before." She let out her breath. "She asked me, you know. Years ago. When your mother died. She said she wanted you to have it one day. I told her no."

"Have what?"

Mara broke the seal.

Inside, on a bed of faded green cloth, lay a disk about the width of Kiva's palm. It was dark, almost black, and smooth as river glass, with nine grooves cut around it in rings, each finer than the last, and a cord through a small hole near the edge.

Kiva did not touch it at first.

Kiva had a picture of Ilse Corran in her head, a tall woman with cropped grey hair and a long scar on her jaw who had come to the flat sometimes when Kiva's mother was alive and laughed very loudly in the kitchen. After the Tallow fire, she had come once more and sat with Kiva on the stairs for an hour without saying much, and then she had gone back out to the field. Four years later, the Home Line had sent word that she was dead.

"What is it?" said Kiva.

"It's old," said Mara. "Older than the Compact, Ilse said. The Home Line gives it from one bearer to the next. Not in families. By appointment. The one who has it chooses who gets it." Her mouth tightened. "Ilse chose you."

"Why?"

"She didn't say. She didn't say much about it at all. Only that it was entrusted, and that it was yours to keep and not yours to sell, and that you'd understand it better when you were older." Mara frowned at the disk. "I'm not sure she understood it herself."

Kiva picked it up.

It was cold, and not with the cold of metal in a cold room. It was colder than that, like a stone pulled out of a stream, and the grooves were very fine under her thumb.

"You don't have to wear it today," said Mara.

"I want to."

Mara studied her for a long moment. Then she took the cord and put it over Kiva's head herself, and tucked the disk inside her shirt, and pressed her palm flat against it for a second over the cloth. Her hand was rough and warm and smelled of oil.

"Right," she said. "Shoes. The registry line opens at eight, and we'll miss the tram."

---

The Kindling for the Eastbank wards was held in the Old Yard of the Crooked Line School, which was the oldest part of the Meridian Compact and looked it.

Kiva had walked past it a hundred times. The rest of the Compact was glass and white stone and induction lamps, with flight lanes over the roofs and practice arenas you could hear cheering from three streets away, but the Old Yard was a long walled strip of packed earth, with sheds down one side and a line of black practice posts down the middle, and tall white lamps along the wall above them.

The morning went to the registry line, the healer's check, and a long cold wait on a bench, and it was well past two in the afternoon before Kiva's group was called into the yard. By then there were braziers burning and benches full of families, and a long table where three assessors in Compact grey sat with their instrument boxes. Behind the table stood the lit frame, an open cage of fine wires taller than a man, and beside it was a projection board, a sheet of pale glass on a stand where the readings would show.

There were thirty-one children in Kiva's group, all of them thirteen, and all of them trying to look as if they did not care.

The three stations stood in a row in the middle of the yard.

The first was the brazier. A bed of coals glowed in an iron bowl, and in the coals lay an Ember slab from the Compact's store, flat and water-grey, about the size of a hand. If the Ember door opened for you, the slab took your color when you held it, and then it was yours.

The second was the bowl, which was wide and shallow and full of water drawn from the rift-pool under the Wren House. If the hand door opened for you, the water answered your palms. Sometimes it rippled. Sometimes it rose.

The third was the stance stone, a flat grey slab worn smooth in the middle by five hundred years of feet. You stood on it while an assessor put a hand on your chest and pushed, and if the still door opened for you, you did not move.

Most people opened at one station. That was how it worked. Your first door was whatever answered first, and that was who you would be: Ember-door, hand-door, or still-door. Some people opened at none and had to come back the next year.

Kiva's name was called before she was ready.

She walked to the brazier, where the assessor, a broad man with burn scars up both forearms, handed her a pair of tongs and nodded at the slab.

"Lift it out. Then hold it in your bare hands. It won't burn you if it's yours."

"And if it's not mine?"

"Then drop it fast." He smiled a little. "Go on."

She lifted it, and it was heavier than it looked. She set the tongs down and took it in both hands.

It did not burn.

It was hot, deeply and evenly hot, like bread just out of the oven, and then it changed. The water-grey went out of it from the middle, and a color came in that was dull copper, the color of an old pan, and spread to the edges of the slab and stayed.

The assessor nodded at the slab. "Ember door," he said. "Congratulations. Step to the table."

Kiva stepped back from the brazier with the slab in her hands. To reach the table, she had to pass the bowl.

As she went by it, the water stood up.

It rose out of the bowl in a thin ring, a hand high, and leaned toward her like a plant toward a window. It was not touching her, and her hands were full of the hot copper slab, nowhere near the water.

Someone on the benches gasped.

Kiva stopped. The water ring hung there, trembling, and then, slowly, it sank back into the bowl.

The assessor at the bowl, a thin old woman, stood up very slowly from her stool.

"Put your hands over it," she said. "Just over. Don't touch."

Kiva did not want to. She glanced back at the benches, where Mara was on her feet in the third row, very still.

She set the slab down on the edge of the brazier table and held her palms over the water.

It rose to meet them. It stood up out of the bowl in a column as thick as her wrist and stopped a finger's width below her hands, and stayed there, and she could feel it the way you feel heat off a stove, except that it was cool and close and waiting.

"Hand door," said the old woman. Her voice was not steady. "Hand door as well."

The yard had gone quiet, and the other children were staring. The broad man from the brazier had come over, and so had the third assessor, a young man who had not let go of his instrument box.

"That's not possible," said the young man.

"Look at it," said the old woman. "It's possible."

"The stone," said the broad man. He was watching Kiva the way her mother had once watched a cell that was humming the wrong note. "Just to be sure. Get on the stone, girl."

Kiva stepped onto the stance stone. The middle of it was worn into a shallow cup, and her feet fit into it as if it had been made for them.

The broad man put his palm flat on her breastbone. "Brace," he said. And he pushed.

He was twice her size, and he pushed the way you would push a door that was stuck. She felt it go through her chest and down into her legs.

And something under her answered.

It was not strength, because she had never been strong. It was more like the moment on a moving tram when you stop holding the rail and find you have not fallen. Her feet went heavy, and her knees went soft and certain, and the push went down through her into the stone and stopped there, as if the stone and the ground under it had agreed to take it for her.

She did not move.

The broad man pushed harder, until his boots slid on the packed earth, and Kiva stood in the cup of the stone and did not move at all.

He took his hand away and stared at it.

"Still door," he said quietly. "Mother's mercy. All three."

---

They put her in the lit frame.

She stood inside the cage of wires while the young assessor worked the instrument box. The projection board lit up, and bands of pale light rose on the glass, one for each door, the way they did for everyone.

Except that they would not hold still.

The Ember band rose to a quarter and dropped. The hand band jumped up past it and shivered, and the still band sat low and steady for a breath and then leapt, and they kept trading places while the young assessor tapped the box and swore under his breath. The total at the bottom of the board read a number, then a different number, then a row of dashes.

"It's the rig," he said. "It must be the rig."

"It's not the rig," said the old woman.

Inside the frame, Kiva had begun to hurt.

It started in the middle of her chest, under the disk, and it felt like three people pulling on one blanket from three different sides. The pull went up and out toward her left shoulder in three thin, burning lines, as if somebody were drawing on her from the inside with a hot wire. She put her hand to her chest, and her breath went short.

"Get her out," said Mara's voice, from somewhere. "Get her out of that thing."

Kiva stepped out of the frame. Or she tried to.

The disk against her breastbone went cold.

It went colder than it had been, colder than anything, so cold that it felt like a hole in her chest, and through that hole, something came in.

---

She was standing somewhere very high, and the people were coming.

They came from every direction at once. She could not see their faces. Each one was carrying light, and the lights were different, one white like a lamp, one red-gold like the coals in the brazier, one a color like deep water. They walked toward her, and toward each other, from every road and every door there was. She did not know them. She knew what they were, because her body knew. They were very old and very bright and she could not look at any of them for long.

When they came close, their lights touched.

There was no sound. There was only the moment when one light met another and neither of them stopped.

Then she looked up, and the sky was gone.

It did not break or tear. She had heard about rifts all her life and this was not a rift. There was simply no sky there anymore, no blue, no lid, nothing between her and what was above. And what was above came down.

It was not a weight so much as a fullness, and it pressed into everything, into the air and the ground and her skin and the spaces between her thoughts. It was enormous. It was paying attention. It did not speak. She did not think it could have been held back by anyone.

Below her, the whole city answered at once.

Every lamp. Every cell in every wall. The great cores sleeping under the districts, and the rift-pool under the Wren House, and every Kindled person in Meridian, every open door, all of it rang like a struck bell. Some lights went out. Some went white. Some went a color she had no word for.

She did not know if the bright people had come to hold it shut, or to tear it open, or to take it for themselves, or only to stand together when it happened.

She tried to see their faces.

She woke up on the ground.

---

The packed earth was cold against her cheek, and somebody's coat was under her head. Mara was kneeling over her with one hand on her face and the other flat on her chest, over the disk, and Mara's face was white.

"Kiva. Kiva, look at me."

"I'm here."

"Can you sit up?"

She could. The yard tilted and came back. All around them people were standing, and the assessors were arguing at the table, and on the projection board the bands had gone dark.

"The lamps went out," somebody was saying. "All the lamps in the yard. Just for a second."

"It was the rig. The rig shorted."

"The rig doesn't run the lamps."

Kiva pulled at the neck of her shirt. On the skin of her chest, from the middle of her breastbone up toward her left shoulder, there were three thin red lines that were neither straight nor even, like three streaks of heat drawn on her by a careless hand. When she touched them, they were tender.

The disk lay in the middle of them. It was cold, but only ordinary cold now, like stone.

"What did you see?" said Mara, very low.

"People," said Kiva. "Coming. And the sky—" She stopped, because she did not know how to say the rest. Already it was going, the way dreams go, leaving only pieces. Light touching light. A hole where the sky should be. Every lamp in the city ringing.

"It's all right," said Mara. "You don't have to say."

The old woman from the bowl came over to them. She crouched slowly, with a hand on her knee, and considered Kiva for a long time.

Then she turned to Mara.

"All three," she said. "Classification deferred. I'm sorry. I've been reading children on that bench for thirty years, and I have no name for what that was."

---

They walked out the far end of the Old Yard because it was closer to the tram.

Kiva carried her copper Ember slab in both hands, and Mara walked beside her with one arm around her shoulders, which she had not done in public since Kiva was ten. Neither of them spoke. Behind them, the next child was walking to the brazier.

At the end of the yard, the last practice post leaned out of the line.

It was older than the others, or looked it, black and split with age and bound with two iron bands. All the other posts stood straight. This one tilted to the left, a little, as if it were listening for something down the lane.

Kiva stopped beside it.

"Why is it crooked?" she said.

Mara glanced at it, and then back at the yard, at the assessors still bent over the projection board, at the lit frame and the braziers and the bowl.

"I don't know," she said. "Nobody knows. It's always been like that." She tightened her arm. "Come home. We'll sort the rest out at home."

Kiva did not move for a moment.

She put her hand on the post. The wood was rough and cold. Under her shirt, the three lines on her chest throbbed slowly, in time with her heart, and the disk lay still between them.
