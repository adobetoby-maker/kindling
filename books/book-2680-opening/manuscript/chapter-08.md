# Chapter 8 — Kiva: The Lowest Defensible Place

Ysra Crest's office was at the top of the Placement Hall, at the end of a long corridor with tall windows that looked down over the plaza. Kiva walked along it at five to ten with her arm in its sling, past the closed doors of the assessors' rooms, and did not look out.

Her fingers were coming back. On the tram she had pressed the tips of the first two, one at a time, with her thumb, the way Mara had all night, and she could feel it now. It was duller than the third finger, which was sharp and close, as if the first two were wearing thin gloves, but it was more than she had felt at midnight, and more than at dawn.

The door was open. Ysra was sitting behind her desk with a cup of tea and a stack of paper, and she was not alone. Orla Dane sat in a chair by the window with her bad leg stretched out and her stick across her lap, looking as if she had not slept either.

"Sit, Miss Rowan," said Ysra.

Kiva sat.

Ysra did not say anything else for a while. She looked at the sling, and the salve showing at Kiva's collar, and the way Kiva held her left hand in her lap with the first two fingers curled, and she took her time over all of it, the way Mara took her time over a gauge she had not yet decided about.

Then she folded her hands on the desk.

"Tell me about last night," she said.

Kiva told her about the fever, and the dream, though she did not say what was in it, only that it came. She told her about waking in the hallway with her hand on the panel, and the core tripping, and Mara's quench. She told her what Examiner Rusk had said to Mara in the winter, on the stool in the shop.

"The Works will have it by now," she said. "My aunt's gone to tell Examiner Rusk herself. Her license goes to review."

Ysra was quiet.

"That is outside my office," she said at last. "It belongs to the Works, and I have no standing there." She regarded Kiva across the desk. "But I'm glad you told me. I'd have heard by Monday, and I'd have wondered why you didn't."

"I didn't want you to wonder."

"No." Ysra picked up her tea and drank some. "Now. The run. I have the board and four hundred witnesses for what happened. I don't need it told to me again. I need two things the board can't give me. When the husk came up the stair and the other doors came in, did you know that could happen?"

Kiva thought about it.

"I knew it could," she said. "I told Kellan it could, in the corridor. I thought I could keep them out." Her eyes went to her own hands in her lap. "I could, until I was frightened. I don't know how to keep them out when I'm frightened. I haven't learned that yet."

Ysra nodded slowly.

"And the rail. You declared the first change and not the second. If it were now, would you say the two words?"

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"I don't," said Kiva. "I know there was time, and I didn't use it. I know I'll try. That's all I know."

---

Ysra put her tea down. She leaned back in her chair and gazed out of the window for a moment, at the roofs of the Compact Quarter and the flight lanes over them, before she came back to Kiva.

"I had my hand on the master stop," she said. "From the moment the needle crossed eight. Did you see?"

"After. I saw your hand on it after."

"I've run the Cellhouse for nineteen years. I've pulled that lever perhaps a dozen times. Every time, it was because I thought a team had lost control of something they couldn't get back." She tapped the desk. "I didn't pull it on Friday. Do you want to know why?"

Kiva nodded.

"Because I wanted to see whether your team could stop what you'd started." Ysra's voice was dry and level. "If I'd pulled it, the cell would have quenched and the run would have ended, and I'd have learned exactly what I already knew, which is that you can't hold three doors, and that they fight, and that a cell keeps what you leave in it. I've had all that on a board in front of me every spring for four years." She paused. "What I didn't know was what happened next. Whether you'd see it. Whether you'd call it. Whether anyone would listen."

"Kellan listened."

"Renn listened. Because, he told the booth afterward, your numbers had been right all run." Ysra almost smiled. "That's the highest praise I've ever heard that young man give anyone."

Kiva did not know what to say.

"The run failed," said Ysra. "I want that understood between us. It failed on the board and it failed in fact. You broke your own declared role. You put the cell into a state no one in the Compact has seen before. You took an undeclared violation. You hurt yourself, and you could have hurt Cade and Pryce badly. On a real substation, people would have lost power at the worst possible moment."

"I know."

"There is also a condition, with my stamp under it. On Classification Day I told you that if you woke more than one door in that trial and anything went wrong, you would be withdrawn and out for the season." She let that sit. "You did, and it did. That condition is mine to enforce and mine to set aside, and if I set it aside, I answer for it."

Kiva's mouth had gone dry.

"So here is why." Ysra laid her palm flat on the desk. "For four years, I've had a board that can't read you, with bands that won't hold still and a Rank that reads Ember, then Flame, then nothing. Every spring I've deferred, because I had no way of knowing what you could and couldn't do, and no ruling I could have defended in public if something went wrong."

She lifted her hand.

"On Friday I watched you run. I watched you call a clock that was right to the tenth. I watched you break your own call, and then I watched you tell a boy on a stair the one thing that would save it, from under a husk." Ysra held her eyes steadily. "It is not a classification. The board still can't name you, and neither can I. But I saw what you do when it goes wrong, and that I can rule on. It's all my office has ever needed."

Kiva sat very still.

"I'm prepared to post you," said Ysra. "Provisional. Third Division, Crooked Line School, the third team. It's the lowest place on the Circuit, and the lowest place I can defend. It carries tuition and no stipend. The conditions are these. No live-cell events until your sponsor certifies you. Before every sanctioned event, you declare to the referee which single door you'll use, and any change, you call before you make it. Your sponsor sees you work every week, in a lane, with her own eyes. You're reviewed after your first sanctioned meet. And if there's one incident in a sanctioned event, you're off the roster for the season."

"My sponsor," said Kiva.

"Your sponsor," said Ysra, and she turned her head toward Orla. "A provisional placement for an unclassified candidate whose household license is under review requires an accountable sponsor. That means someone whose name goes on the roster beside the candidate's, and whose hand is on it if it goes wrong." She paused. "That's the condition. I can't waive it. I wouldn't if I could."

---

Orla had not said anything. She was sitting by the window with her hands on her stick, and she was watching Kiva, not Ysra.

"Four years," said Ysra quietly.

"Twenty-one," said Orla.

Ysra did not answer that. She only waited.

Orla turned to Kiva.

"I told your aunt I'd put my name next to a student's once," she said. "You were on the stairs. You heard."

"I heard."

"I've told a hundred stories about the field, and I'm the fool in most of them. This is the one I never told." Orla took a breath, and then she said it without stopping, the way you pull a splinter. "His name was Jessamy Hart. Twin-kindled, Ember and hand. I put him in an ashcraft trial at the Regional Gates in his second year, because I thought I understood him and I wanted the board to see what I saw. His doors fought on a live cell in front of four thousand people. The cell ran away, and the backwash went up his arms before the quench took. He lost a hand. Two other students were burned pulling him clear. The board closed his place, and he left Meridian at nineteen with one hand and no category. I heard, years after, that he was fixing pumps somewhere on the south coast. I never went to find him." She studied the head of her stick. "They took my sponsoring standing for ten years, and when they gave it back I never used it. That's why the line on your form was empty."

Kiva did not know what to say. So she said the thing that was in front of her.

"I'm not him."

"No," said Orla. "You're not. He never called the door when the line warmed. He never stood in a yard at ten o'clock at night doing the same drill again and again because it was not yet clean. And he never sat in front of a board and told it what he couldn't do." She raised her head. "I didn't know how to teach him that. I didn't know it needed teaching. I learned it from losing him."

Ysra had taken a roster form from her drawer while Orla talked. She laid it on the desk, turned so that Orla could see the line near the bottom, and uncapped her stamp. She did not say anything. On Classification Day she had told Orla what it took. One word, and the stamp.

"You don't have to," said Kiva.

"I know," said Orla.

She turned from Kiva to the desk, and her hands closed on the head of her stick, and Kiva saw the knuckles go white.

"Sponsor," said Orla Dane. "Dane."

Ysra wrote it on the line herself, *O. Dane, Senior Lecturer, Crooked Line School*, in a hand like iron railings. Then she pressed the stamp into a pad of red ink and brought it down on the page, very firmly, once.

"If she has an incident in a sanctioned event," said Ysra, "your sponsoring standing goes. And the board will review your teaching contract. You know that."

"I know that."

"Then it's done." Ysra slid the page into a folder. Then she turned to Kiva, and for the first time, her long plain face did something that might have been warm. "Provisional, Third Division. It'll post on Monday with the others. Congratulations, Miss Rowan. You've earned the lowest defensible place in the Collegiate Circuit. I hope you hate it."

"Why?"

"Because people who hate the bottom work hardest to leave it," said Ysra. She picked up her tea. "One more thing. Graywater sent a man up to the booth last night to ask who sponsors you. My clerk told him nobody." She drank. "I shall enjoy correcting that on Monday. Don't let it go to your head. Graywater asks about a great many people."

---

Mara was sitting on the bench outside the Municipal Works office on Hanley Street, in her good coat, with her bag on her knees and her hands folded on top of it.

Kiva found her there a little before noon. The Works was a long soot-dark building with a clock over the door and the city's arms cut into the stone, and the bench stood against its front wall where people sat to wait for the clerks. Mara had nothing to wait for. She sat the way she sat at the bench at home when a meter had told her something and she had not yet decided what to do about it.

"Rusk," said Kiva.

"Rusk." Mara moved her bag so that Kiva could sit. "He had the trip off the wire already. I told him the rest. The fever, and the panel, and that you were asleep." She looked across the street at nothing. "He listened to all of it. He says the review notice will come by the end of the week, and that he's sorry, and I think he is."

"I'm sorry," said Kiva.

"I know you are. Sit down."

Kiva sat. The stone of the wall was cold through her coat. Down the street a tram went by with its rings singing, and a boy on a lift-board came after it, a hand's height off the paving, shouting at someone.

"Last night," said Kiva. "You said it wasn't the rig. The lamps in the Old Yard."

Mara was quiet for a moment.

"I've never told you this," she said. "I didn't think it was mine to tell. I thought it would frighten you, and I didn't know what it meant, and I still don't." She turned her scarred hand over on top of the bag. "When you went down, I was on my knees by you. The lamp over the shed went out, and somebody shouted, and I looked up. The yard gate was open behind the braziers. You can see down the hill from there, over the river to the Eastbank."

Kiva waited.

"Every light on the Eastbank went out," said Mara. "The whole far side of the river. The trams on the bridge stopped where they were. The signs along the embankment. The windows. Every lamp I could see, at once." She was quiet a moment. "And then they came back, all of them, before I'd finished breathing in. Like a blink."

"The whole Eastbank."

"I read banks for a living. One bank can't do that, not on its own. A whole side of a river of them can't do it together." Mara's thumb moved across her palm. "The Works asked me that week what I'd seen, because I'd been there. I told them. Nobody could say why. They put it down to the rig at the Old Yard, in the end, because they had to put it down to something." She shook her head. "The rig doesn't run the Eastbank."

Kiva sat very still. *Below her, every lamp answered.*

"So it was me," she said. "My Kindling. Three doors opening."

"Maybe," said Mara. "That's one story. It could be your Kindling. It could be the dream. It could be something else that happened at the same moment, that has nothing to do with you at all. A rift. The filter. The six-hundredth year." She shrugged. "Three stories. I've got one thing I saw with my own eyes. I know which I trust."

"What do you trust?"

"That it happened," said Mara. "In the afternoon, in daylight, stone sober, with my hand on your face. It isn't a fever."

Kiva thought about the bright people coming from every road. She thought about the sky with nothing in it, and the whole city ringing like a bell. She did not know whether the lamps on the Eastbank had anything to do with a dream at all, hers or anybody's. She only knew that Mara had been kneeling in the dirt with her hand on Kiva's face, and had looked up, and had seen it.

She took the roster consent form out of her coat pocket, the one Ysra Crest's clerk had given her on the way out, and held it out.

Mara took it, and read it, and put it in her apron pocket under her coat without a word.

---

They walked home along the river because Kiva could not face the tram.

It was Saturday and the embankment was busy, with families walking, and kids on lift-boards, the small, cheap kind that only rose a hand's height off the paving and ran for ten minutes on a thumb-sized cell, and a long Halden barge going downstream with its induction rings humming. On the far bank, the white arenas of the Compact Quarter caught the noon sun.

Mara walked slowly, to match Kiva's knee. She had the roster consent form in her apron pocket, where it had gone on the Works bench. Under eighteen, household under review, provisional placement: guardian's countersignature required.

They walked for a long time before Mara spoke.

"I'm going to sign it," she said.

Kiva turned to her.

"I want to tell you why. All of it." Mara kept walking, with her eyes on the path ahead. "If you're on a Compact roster with an accountable sponsor, the Compact carries your ash liability in sanctioned work. Not the household. That's in the rules. When my license goes to review, the first thing the Works will ask is whether the household condition still stands." She paused. "If you're on a roster, I can tell them it doesn't. Not the way it did. It might save my license. It might save the shop."

"That's good."

"It is good. It's also why I don't trust myself." Mara stopped at the rail above the brown water. "I've said no to you for four years because I was afraid for you. And now I'm saying yes, and part of the reason is that it's good for me. I don't like that. So there it is, before I sign anything, and you can decide what my signature's worth."

Kiva stood beside her at the rail.

"What's the rest of the reason?" she said.

Mara was quiet.

"On the step last night," she said at last. "I asked you if there'd been time to say two words. When you went over the rail." She turned her scarred hand over on the rail. "You said yes. You could have said no, and I'd have believed you. You didn't make it smaller than it was." She let out a breath. "And you told me what you saw. All of it. For the first time."

"I should have told you years ago."

"And I should have told you about Rusk. We're both bad at it." Mara almost smiled. "We'll get better."

She took the form out of her pocket and unfolded it on the rail, against the wind, and took the pencil from behind her ear.

"Three things," she said. "My conditions. Not the Compact's. Mine."

"All right."

"You never touch a cell in the flat. Not the panel. Not the cold-cupboard. Not a lamp-cell. Not ever, until I say."

"All right."

"When the dream comes, you tell me. That night. Even if it's three in the morning. Even if there's nothing to say but that it came."

"All right."

"And every night, you give me that hand." Mara nodded at the sling. "The left. I press those two fingers, and you tell me what you feel. The truth, not the brave one." She faced her. "Not to check up on you. To know. The way I'd put my hand on a bank."

"All right," said Kiva. "Yes."

Mara signed the form, *M. ROWAN*, in her square capitals, with the date. Above her name, on the line for the sponsor, Ysra Crest's clerk had written *O. Dane* and stamped it in red. Mara looked at that for a moment before she folded the form and handed it to Kiva.

"It isn't safe," she said. "I won't have either of us pretend it is. But I can see the meter now. And the shutdown path. And there's a hand on it." She put the pencil back behind her ear. "Orla Dane's, heaven help her. The rest we find out."

---

Rhea Sorn went over the run in her head, again and again, before she let herself look at the thing she had seen the first time.

She sat alone in the briefing room at Graywater's company hall on the Brickfields road, on a hard chair at the long table, with the lamps turned down. It was Saturday evening, and most of the company was out on a rotation at the Sag Line or home with their families, so that the hall was quiet except for the hum of the core under the floor and, somewhere above her, a lift craft settling onto the roof pad.

She had watched the Cellhouse run from the good chairs at the end of the gallery, with her arms folded and her pocket glass in her hand, the way she watched every run she bothered to watch. She went over it now with her eyes shut, from the moment the girl on the mezzanine leaned out over the rail.

She saw the girl go over it. The landing was bad, and Rhea had winced at it in the gallery and did not wince now, because she had seen a hundred bad landings, and this one was a girl with a strained knee coming off struts onto one leg and still, somehow, getting where she was going, two paces in front of the gap, between the husk and the boy.

And then the stance.

Rhea knew stance. She was not still-door. She was Ember, Edge only, Torch rank, which was as far as her door had taken her and as far as it would ever take her, and she had stood on rifts for twenty-three years with nothing but a blade and her feet. But she had stood beside still-door fighters on every one of them, and she knew what it looked like when one planted.

The girl had planted with the struts still half on her calves. Rhea had put the glass on her and kept it there to be sure of what she was seeing. Nothing was handed cleanly from the one door to the other. The Ember was simply stuck open, and the still door had been forced open on top of it, and the girl was standing in the jam of the two when the husk hit her square. It was ugly, and it was the kind of thing a body did once and paid for, and she did not move.

And her left hand came up. Rhea had seen that in the gallery and not understood it. She understood it now. It was the hand a hand-door fighter puts up to throw a ward, and the girl had put it up, and no ward came. She had kept that door shut, on purpose, while a husk dragged its limb down her bare arm and the breaker cracked overhead and the shutter came down on the boy behind her.

Then she turned her head and called something to the Ember-door boy on the stair, and Rhea had not needed to hear it through the glass to know what, because she saw the boy hear it, and decide, and go up the rail.

Rhea had been doing this for twenty-three years. She had trained four probationary classes and sat on Graywater's draft board for six seasons. She knew what kills looked like from a gallery and what standings looked like on a board, and she knew how little either of them told you about whether a person would be any use on a real rift at three in the morning with the water rising.

That girl was not a fighter. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

She was a responder.

Doran had it from the booth that her clock calls had been right all run. Rhea had seen for herself that she saw the husk's line before anyone else on the floor, and put herself where the danger was going and not where the points were. And when she could not reach the thing that would fix it, she had told someone who could, and been right.

None of that would show on any standing. Graywater's own board would not know how to rank it.

Rhea opened her eyes and sat for a moment in the dim room.

And there it was, the thing she had seen the first time and made herself not look at.

As the husk hit the girl, something had swung out of her collar on a cord. It had hung against the front of her shirt for the rest of the run, grey with ash. Small. Round. Dark. Rhea had leaned forward, and put her hand flat on the gallery glass, and brought the pocket glass up to it.

Nine grooves. Fine rings, each a little narrower than the last.

She had seen it before, once. She had been nineteen, a probationary with Graywater, seconded to a Home Line crew on the Ferren culverts in the spring floods. She had gone into a culvert after a child, and the water had come up behind her, and she had been sure, for a long and very cold minute, that she was going to drown in the dark with the child in her arms. Then a hand had come down through the grating above her and closed on her collar and hauled, and the hand had belonged to a tall woman with cropped grey hair and a long scar on her jaw. Ilse Corran.

Afterward, on the bank, Ilse had sat beside her wrapped in a blanket while the medics worked, and something had hung out of her collar on a cord, grey with mud. Small. Round. Dark. The same.

Rhea had asked her what it was. Ilse had tucked it back inside her shirt and said, *Something I carry.*

Ilse had been dead for seven years.

Rhea sat in the dim briefing room for a long time with that in front of her, a girl with ash on her face and a disk on her chest, holding a husk off a boy.

She did not know what the girl was. A threat, maybe, a cell that kept the wrong things. A tool that some industry house would try to buy before the season was out. A recruit, in five years, if she lived and learned to close her doors. Or something else, something that Ilse Corran had seen in a six-year-old and never explained to anyone.

She got up and went down the corridor to the draft room, where Doran Mace was still at his desk with the lamp on.

"The Rowan girl," she said from the doorway. "Not for draft this season. Watch her first sanctioned meet. I want to know who stands next to her when it goes wrong."

Doran looked up. "That's all?"

"That's all for the board."

He nodded and went back to his work, and Rhea stood in the doorway a moment longer, with her hands in the pockets of her coat.

*Corran's,* she thought. And then, before she could stop it, and with no right to it at all: *Mine.*

---

The rosters posted on Monday at noon.

They went up on the long board in the basement of the Placement Hall, the way they always did, and by a quarter past there were five hundred people on the basement stairs. Kiva did not push to the front. She stood at the back with her arm in its sling and let Perrin push for her, because Perrin was very large and very happy and nobody argued with him.

He came back with his bandaged hands in the air.

"Stonehand bursary," he said. "Second Division. Full tuition and a stipend. My father cried. On the tram. In front of people." He grinned so widely it looked painful. "Second Division, Kiva. I've never been in the second anything."

"Perrin, that's—"

"I know. I know." He bumped her good shoulder with his, gently for once. "You're in the Third. Crooked Line. Provisional. Your name's at the very bottom. In small letters. With a little star and *Sponsor: O. Dane* next to it, like a footnote." He beamed at her. "It's the best thing I've ever seen on that board."

Anwen was beside them with her list. She had read the boards already, every one.

"Graywater posts field placements on the twentieth," she said. "I'm on Doran Mace's list. Not at the top. Higher than I was." She folded the list. "I've got five days to get higher. I'm going to the Wren House lanes this afternoon. I won't be at Orla's tonight."

"All right."

"I'm not angry with you. Don't spend the week guessing."

"I won't."

"It's because it's mine," said Anwen. And then, after a moment, more quietly, "Come on Thursday, though. I want to see whether that drill of yours has got any faster."

Kellan Renn came out of the crowd at the front of the board. He had a fresh scab on his right knee, Kiva saw, and the red mark on his forearm from the Terrace Ring had gone yellow-green. He stopped in front of her.

"First Division," he said. "Crooked Line, first team. Kellan Renn, Fire. No asterisk."

"Congratulations."

"Thank you." His eyes went to her sling. "Third Division."

"Provisional."

"The lowest defensible place." He said it as if he were reading it off a meter. Then he said, "Good."

"Good?"

"Good. It's a number. It's on the board. I know where you are." He put his hands in his pockets. "I'll be comparing."

"I know."

He nodded once and started to turn away. Then he stopped.

"Your fingers," he said. "The two on the left. The medic said they'd come back."

"They're coming back."

"All the way?"

Kiva pressed the tips of the first two fingers with her thumb, one at a time. She could feel it, better than on Saturday and still not right, duller than the third and a little further away, as if the thin gloves were still on.

"Not yet," she said. "More each day."

He weighed that for a moment longer, the way he weighed any number that was still moving. He did not say anything. Then he went away up the basement stairs into the crowd, and she watched the back of his head until she lost it.

---

That evening, Kiva went to the Old Yard.

Orla was already there, on her crate by the first shed, with the hand lamp hung on its hook and her bad leg stretched out in front of her. She did not get up.

"No drills tonight," she said. "Not with that arm. You can sit."

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

Kiva did not answer at once. She walked down the lane between the black posts, over ground that was rutted and not quite level, where she knew every rut. At the far end, the last post leaned out of the line.

She stopped beside it.

She thought about the Cellhouse, and the cell under her hand, and the joy, and the fear, and the three doors going in, and the ringing that would not stop. She thought about Perrin's purple hands, and Kellan's foot on the rail, and Anwen not coming out. She thought about Mara on her knees in this yard four years ago, looking up through the open gate at the whole of the Eastbank going dark. She thought about the bright people on every road, and the sky that was not there.

She did not understand any of it. She understood a little more than she had a week ago. That was all.

She put her good hand on the post.

The wood was rough and cold, and so were the iron bands. Under her shirt, the heat-lines ached from her breastbone to her elbow, a long, low, healing ache, and the disk lay cold in the middle of them.

"Why is it crooked?" she said.

She had asked Orla before. She had asked everyone. She asked the way you press on a bruise.

"I don't know," said Orla, from the far end.

Kiva stood with her hand on it a while longer. Then she took her hand away, and walked back up the lane to where Orla was sitting, and stood in front of her with her arm in its sling and her two half-woken fingers and her three burning lines.

"When the arm's better," she said. "Again."
