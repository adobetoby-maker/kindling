# Chapter 8 — Kiva: The Lowest Defensible Place

Ysra Crest's office was at the top of the Measure Hall, at the end of a long corridor lined with framed rosters going back two hundred years. Kiva walked past them at five to nine with her book in her good hand and her arm in its sling, and did not look at any of them.

Her fingers were coming back. On the tram she had pressed the tips of the first two, one at a time, with her thumb, the way Mara had all night. She could feel it now. Not the way she felt the third finger, sharp and close. Duller. As if the first two were wearing thin gloves. But she could feel it.

She had written that in the book too.

The door was open. Ysra was sitting behind her desk with a cup of tea and a stack of paper. She was not alone. Orla Dane sat in a chair by the window with her bad leg stretched out and her stick across her lap. She looked as if she had not slept either.

"Sit, Miss Rowan," said Ysra. "Give me the book."

Kiva gave it to her. She sat.

Ysra read it.

She did not skim. She started on the first page, the Friday night in the Old Yard, and read every row, every column. She read the Terrace Ring. She read *What went wrong: Perrin. Why: Perrin.* and did not smile. She read the trial. She read the card copy Kiva had written out underneath it. She read the last page, the one from three o'clock that morning, and stopped there for a long time.

Then she closed the book and set it on the desk and folded her hands on top of it.

"Tell me about this morning," she said.

Kiva told her. The fever. The dream, though she did not say what was in it, only that it came. Waking in the hallway with her hand on the panel. The core tripping. Mara's quench. The letter with the red stripe.

"The household filing will be on a desk at the Works by now," she said. "My aunt's gone to file it herself. Her license goes to review."

Ysra was quiet.

"That isn't mine," she said at last. "That's the Works. I can't touch it." She looked at Kiva. "But I'm glad you told me. I'd have heard by Monday, and I'd have wondered why you didn't."

"I didn't want you to wonder."

"No." Ysra picked up her tea and drank some. "Now. The run. Why did you tune?"

"Because the cell was going to trip with Perrin and Anwen under the shutter. I could feel the fault. I knew how to fix it. I thought I could hold one door on it."

"You'd said you wouldn't."

"I'd said I wouldn't. Then I changed it. Out loud."

"And when the husk came up the stair?"

"I was frightened," said Kiva. "And the Ember came in behind the hand door before I could stop it. And then the still door. And all three went into the cell."

"Did you know that would happen?"

Kiva thought about it.

"I knew it could," she said. "I told Kellan it could. In the corridor. I thought I could keep them out." She looked at the book under Ysra's hands. "I could, until I was frightened. I don't know how to keep them out when I'm frightened. I haven't learned that yet."

Ysra nodded slowly.

"And the rail. Why didn't you declare?"

"I didn't think of it," said Kiva. "There was time. I've thought about it all night. There was time to say two words. I didn't say them."

"Would you say them now?"

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"I don't," said Kiva. "I know I'll try. I know I wrote it down. That's all I know."

---

Ysra put her tea down. She leaned back in her chair and looked out of the window for a moment, at the roofs of the Compact Quarter and the flight lanes over them, and then back at Kiva.

"I had my hand on the master stop," she said. "From the moment the needle crossed eight. Did you see?"

"After. I saw your hand on it after."

"I've run the Cellhouse for nineteen years. I've pulled that lever eleven times. Every time, it was because I thought a team had lost control of something they couldn't get back." She tapped the book. "I didn't pull it on Friday. Do you want to know why?"

Kiva nodded.

"Because I wanted to see whether your team could stop what you'd started." Ysra's voice was dry and level. "If I'd pulled it, the cell would have quenched and the run would have ended, and I'd have learned exactly what I already knew. That you can't hold three doors. That they fight. That a cell keeps what you leave in it. I've known all that for four years. I've had it on a board in front of me every spring." She paused. "What I didn't know was what happened next. Whether you'd see it. Whether you'd call it. Whether anyone would listen."

"Kellan listened."

"Renn listened. Because, he told the booth afterward, your numbers had been right all run." Ysra almost smiled. "That's the highest praise I've ever heard that young man give anyone."

Kiva did not know what to say.

"The run failed," said Ysra. "I want that clear. It failed on the board and it failed in fact. You broke your own declared role. You put the cell into a state no one in the Compact has seen before. You took an undeclared violation. You hurt yourself, and you could have hurt Cade and Pryce badly. If you'd been on a real substation, people would have lost power at the worst possible moment. Those things are true."

"I know."

"And this is also true." Ysra laid her palm flat on the book. "For four years, I've had a board that can't read you. Columns that won't hold still. A Rank that reads Ember, then Flame, then nothing. Every spring I've looked at it and deferred, because I had no way of knowing what you could and couldn't do. Not safely. Not in a way I could defend to three thousand people if something went wrong."

She lifted her hand.

"Now I have this. A book that can read you. Written in your own hand. Every limit. Every failure. Every time you said it out loud and every time you didn't." Ysra looked at her steadily. "That's not a classification. The board still can't name you. I still have no column for this. But it's something I can rule on."

Kiva sat very still.

"I'm prepared to post you," said Ysra. "Provisional. Third Division, Crooked Line School, the third team. It's the lowest place on the Circuit. It's the lowest place I can defend in public. It carries tuition. No stipend. No live-cell events until your sponsor certifies you in writing. Before every sanctioned event, you declare to the referee which single door you'll use. Any change, you declare before you make it. Your book goes to your sponsor every week. You're reviewed after your first sanctioned meet. If there's one incident in a sanctioned event, you're off the roster for the season."

"My sponsor," said Kiva.

"Your sponsor," said Ysra. She turned her head and looked at Orla. "A provisional placement for an unclassified candidate whose household license is under review requires an accountable sponsor. Someone whose name goes on the roster beside the candidate's. Someone who carries it if it goes wrong." She paused. "That's the condition. I can't waive it. I wouldn't if I could."

---

Orla had not said anything. She was sitting by the window with her hands on her stick, and she was looking at Kiva, not at Ysra.

"Four years," said Ysra quietly.

"Twenty-one," said Orla.

Ysra did not answer that. She only waited.

Orla turned to Kiva.

"I told your aunt I'd put my name next to a student's once," she said. "You were on the stairs. You heard."

"I heard."

"His name was Jessamy Hart. Twin-kindled. Ember and hand. I put him in an ashcraft trial because I thought I understood him and I wanted the board to see what I saw." Orla said it plainly, without stopping, the way you pull a splinter. "His doors fought on a live cell. He lost a hand. Two other students were burned pulling him out. The board sealed his file. He left Meridian at nineteen and I never wrote to him." She looked down at her stick. "That's why. Not a good reason. The true one."

Kiva did not know what to say. So she said the thing that was in front of her.

"I'm not him."

"No," said Orla. "You're not. He never wrote anything down. He never said *one* when the line warmed. He never stood in a yard at ten o'clock at night doing the same drill eleven times because it was eleven seconds and not ten. And he never told the board, to its face, exactly what he couldn't do." She looked up. "I didn't know how to teach him that. I didn't know it needed teaching. I learned it from losing him."

She reached into the canvas bag at her feet and took out a sheet of paper folded in half. It was soft at the folds, as if it had been carried a long time. She unfolded it on her knee. The first page was filled in, in a hand Kiva knew. The second was blank.

"I filled this in four years ago," said Orla. "The week after the Old Yard. I've carried it every day since. I never did the second page."

She held out her hand to Ysra for a pen. Ysra gave her one without a word.

"You don't have to," said Kiva.

"I know," said Orla. "That's why it counts."

She filled in the second page. It took her a long time. Her hand was not entirely steady, and she stopped twice to read back what she had written. When she came to the line at the bottom, she signed it, *O. Dane*, and under it, *Senior Lecturer, Crooked Line School. Former Field Officer, Home Line.* Then she handed it across the desk.

Ysra read it. She signed it below Orla's name. She took a stamp from a drawer, pressed it into a pad of red ink, and stamped the page, very firmly, once.

"If she has an incident in a sanctioned event," said Ysra, "your sponsoring standing goes. And the board will look at your teaching contract. You know that."

"I know that."

"Then it's done." Ysra slid the page into a folder. Then she looked at Kiva, and for the first time, her long plain face did something that might have been warm. "Provisional, Third Division. It'll post on Monday with the others. Congratulations, Miss Rowan. You've earned the lowest defensible place in the Collegiate Circuit. I hope you hate it."

"Why?"

"Because people who hate the bottom work hardest to leave it," said Ysra. She picked up her tea. "One more thing. Graywater asked the booth for your run record last night. The floor, not the scoring. I gave it to them. It's public." She drank. "Don't let it go to your head. They ask for a great many records."

---

Mara was at the district records office on Hanley Street, at a long table in a back room, with three enormous bound books open in front of her.

Kiva found her there at half past ten. The clerk at the front desk, an elderly man with ink on his cuffs, had taken one look at the sling and the salve and pointed down the corridor without asking her name.

The back room was cool and dim and smelled of old paper. It was lined floor to ceiling with shelves of identical books, each bound in grey cloth with a year stamped on the spine. *Transient Register.* Every flicker, every trip, every unexplained surge on every municipal meter in Meridian, written down by hand. Six hundred years of it, nearly. Some of the oldest books were falling apart and tied with tape.

Mara did not look up when Kiva came in. She had her reading glasses on and a pencil behind her ear and one finger on a line in the nearest book.

"I filed it," she said. "Ours. Last night. It's in today's book." She turned it so Kiva could see. "Coper Street core. Trip, two forty-four. Upstream fault, flat three panel. Household filing, M. Rowan, by her own hand." She took off her glasses. "The clerk says the Works will send the review notice by the end of the week."

"I'm sorry," said Kiva.

"I know you are. Sit down. I want to show you something."

Kiva sat. Mara pulled the second book across the table. Its spine said *2676*.

"After I filed," said Mara, "I sat here for a while. I was thinking about the lamps in the Old Yard. About what you said in the hall. *Every lamp.*" She opened the book to a page marked with a slip of paper. "So I looked up the day you were Kindled."

She put her finger on a line.

It was written in a cramped, careful hand, in faded ink.

*T-0. 14:52. Eastbank grid, Lantern grid, Weighbridge grid. All meters, same second. Duration under one second. No trip. No load change. Cause not found. Not grid.*

Kiva read it twice.

"T-zero," said Mara. "That's the register's code for a transient with no cause. A flicker on a meter that nobody can explain. You get a few a year. A bad cell somewhere, a rift on the Sag Line, a fault on a wire. But always one meter. One street. One substation." She tapped the line. "This is three grids. Every meter. The same second. Fourteen fifty-two." She looked at Kiva. "That's when you went down in the Old Yard. I know, because I looked at the clock over the assessors' table when they put you in the frame. It said a quarter to three."

Kiva stared at the line.

"So it was me," she said. "My Kindling. Three doors opening. That's what it was."

"Maybe," said Mara. "That's one story." She pulled the third book across. It was older, much older, its grey cloth faded almost to white. The spine said *2604*. "The clerk here has been in this room for forty years. I asked him if he'd ever seen a T-0 across more than one grid. He went very quiet. Then he went into the back stacks and came out with this."

She opened it to another marked page.

*T-0. 03:10. Compact Quarter, all meters, same second. No fault. Not grid.*

And in the margin, in a different hand, small and slanting:

*Again. Same as the '46 entry in the old book. Every lamp at once.*

"The forty-six entry," said Mara, "is in a book from 2446. The clerk says it's too fragile to bring out. He's going to copy it for me by hand. He says it says the same thing. Every lamp, every district, one instant, at night. No fault found."

Kiva sat with her hand on the old book.

"Three times," said Mara quietly. "In two hundred and thirty years. Every lamp in this city has flickered at once, and nobody could say why. One of those times was the day you were Kindled." She took off her glasses and rubbed her eyes. "I don't know what that means. I don't think anyone does. It could be your Kindling. It could be the dream. It could be something else that happened at the same time, that has nothing to do with you at all. A rift. The filter. The six-hundredth year. Three stories." She put her glasses on the table. "I've got three lines in three books. I know which I trust more."

"What do you trust?"

"That it happened," said Mara. "That somebody wrote it down. That it isn't a fever."

Kiva looked at the slanting note in the margin. *Every lamp at once.* She thought about the bright people coming from every road. She thought about the sky with nothing in it, and the whole city ringing like a bell.

She did not know if a clerk in 2604 had dreamed it too. She did not know who had been dreaming in 2446. She did not know if anyone had been dreaming at all, or if the lamps had simply flickered three times in two hundred years for a reason nobody had ever found.

She took out her book and the pencil on its string, and copied all three lines onto a clean page, exactly as they were written, with the dates and the times and the note in the margin.

In the last column, she wrote: *Why: unknown.*

---

They walked home along the river because Kiva could not face the tram.

It was Saturday and the embankment was busy. Families walking. Kids on lift-boards, the small, cheap kind that only rose a hand's height off the paving and ran for ten minutes on a thumb-sized cell. A long Halden barge going downstream with its induction rings humming. On the far bank, the white arenas of the Compact Quarter caught the noon sun.

Mara walked slowly, to match Kiva's knee. She had the roster consent form in her apron pocket. Kiva had given it to her on the steps of the records office. Under eighteen, household under review, provisional placement. Guardian's countersignature required.

They walked for a long time before Mara spoke.

"I'm going to sign it," she said.

Kiva looked at her.

"I want to tell you why. All of it." Mara kept walking, looking ahead. "If you're on a Compact roster with an accountable sponsor, the Compact carries your ash liability in sanctioned work. Not the household. That's in the rules. When my license goes to review, the first thing the Works will ask is whether the household condition still stands." She paused. "If you're on a roster, I can tell them it doesn't. Not the way it did. It might save my license. It might save the shop."

"That's good."

"It is good. It's also why I don't trust myself." Mara stopped at the rail and looked down at the brown water. "I've said no to you for four years because I was afraid for you. And now I'm saying yes, and part of the reason is that it's good for me. I don't like that. I want it said out loud, so you know. So you can decide what my signature's worth."

Kiva stood beside her at the rail.

"What's the rest of the reason?" she said.

Mara was quiet.

"The book," she said at last. "I read it again this morning. Before I went to file. Every page." She turned her scarred hand over on the rail and looked at it. "You wrote *Should have said it.* About the rail. You wrote it on the team card for Ysra Crest to read. You didn't make it smaller than it was." She let out a breath. "And you told me what you saw. Last night. All of it. For the first time."

"I should have told you years ago."

"And I should have told you about the license. We're both bad at it." Mara almost smiled. "We'll get better."

She took the form out of her pocket and unfolded it on the rail, against the wind. She took the pencil from behind her ear.

"Three things," she said. "My conditions. Not the Compact's. Mine."

"All right."

"You never touch a cell in the flat. Not the panel. Not the cold-cupboard. Not a lamp-cell. Not ever, until I say."

"All right."

"When the dream comes, you tell me. That night. Even if it's three in the morning. Even if there's nothing to say but that it came."

"All right."

"And the book stays on the kitchen table. Every night. And I read it." Mara looked at her. "Not to check up on you. To know. The way I'd read a cell book."

"All right," said Kiva. "Yes."

Mara signed the form. *M. ROWAN*, in her square capitals, and the date. She folded it and handed it to Kiva.

"I'm not saying it's safe," she said. "I'm saying I can see the meter now. And the shutdown path. And the book. That's all I ever asked for." She put the pencil back behind her ear. "The rest we find out."

Rhea Sorn watched the run four times before she let herself look at the thing she had seen the first time.

She sat alone in the plate room at Graywater's company hall on the Brickfields road, on a hard chair in front of the projection table, with the lamps turned down. It was Saturday evening. Most of the company was out on a rotation at the Sag Line or home with their families. The hall was quiet except for the hum of the core under the floor and, somewhere above her, a lift craft settling onto the roof pad.

On the table, the Cellhouse run played out in pale light. The booth's run plates were good ones. Every move on the floor, from above, slightly grey, the way the Cellhouse always looked from the gallery.

She ran it from three thirty.

She watched the girl on the mezzanine with her hand on the drum. She watched the three husks come out of the chute and two go for the stair and one cut across the floor in a straight line for the pump room door. She watched the girl see it. Rhea could not see her face from above, but she could see her see it. Her whole body changed.

She watched her go over the rail.

The landing was bad. Rhea winced at it the first time and did not wince the second. She had seen a hundred bad landings. This one was a girl with a strained knee coming off struts onto one leg and still, somehow, getting where she was going. Two paces in front of the gap. Between the husk and the boy.

And then the stance.

Rhea knew stance. She was not still-door. She was Ember, Edge only, Torch rank, which was as far as her door went and as far as it would ever go. She had stood on rifts for twenty-three years with nothing but a blade and her feet. But she had stood beside still-door fighters on every one of them. She knew what it looked like when one planted.

The girl planted. With the struts still half on her calves. With the Ember still in the doorway. Two doors, open at once, and the husk hit her square, and she did not move.

And her left hand came up. Rhea had seen that on the first run-through and not understood it. She understood it now. It was the hand a hand-door fighter puts up to throw a ward. The girl had put it up. And no ward came. She had kept that door shut. On purpose. While a husk dragged its limb across her bare arm and the breaker cracked overhead and the shutter came down on the boy behind her.

Then she turned her head and called the quench to the Ember-door boy on the stair. Rhea could not hear it on the plate. She did not need to. She saw the boy hear it. She saw him decide. She saw him go up the rail.

Rhea had been doing this for twenty-three years. She had trained four probationary classes. She had sat on Graywater's draft board for six seasons. She knew what kills looked like on a plate and what standings looked like on a board, and she knew how little either of them told you about whether a person would be any use on a real rift at three in the morning with the water rising.

That girl was not a fighter. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

She was a responder.

She had read the cell and called the clock to the tenth. She had seen the husk's line before anyone else on the floor. She had put herself where the danger was going and not where the score was. She had kept a door shut that wanted to open, under impact, with her own lines burning. And when she could not reach the thing that would fix it, she had told someone who could, and been right.

None of that would show on any standing. Graywater's own board would not know how to count it.

Rhea ran it again, slowly, from three thirty-nine.

And there it was. The thing she had seen the first time and made herself not look at.

As the husk hit the girl, something swung out of her collar on a cord. It hung against the front of her shirt for the rest of the run, grey with ash. Small. Round. Dark.

Rhea stopped the plate. She leaned in until her face was a hand's width from the table.

Nine grooves. Fine rings, each a little narrower than the last.

She sat back.

She had seen it before. Once. She had been nineteen, a probationary with Graywater, seconded to a Home Line crew on the Ferren culverts in the spring floods. She had gone into a culvert after a child, and the water had come up behind her, and she had been sure, for a long and very cold minute, that she was going to drown in the dark with the child in her arms. Then a hand had come down through the grating above her and closed on her collar and hauled. A tall woman with cropped grey hair and a long scar on her jaw. Ilse Corran.

Afterward, on the bank, Ilse had sat beside her wrapped in a blanket while the medics worked, and something had hung out of her collar on a cord, grey with mud. Small. Round. Dark. Nine grooves.

Rhea had asked her what it was. Ilse had tucked it back inside her shirt and said, *Something I carry.*

Ilse had been dead for seven years.

Rhea sat in the dark plate room for a long time with the frozen image on the table in front of her. A girl with ash on her face and a disk on her chest, holding a husk off a boy.

She did not know what the girl was. A threat, maybe. A cell that kept the wrong things. A tool that some industry house would try to buy before the season was out. A recruit, in five years, if she lived and learned to close her doors. Or something else. Something that Ilse Corran had seen in a six-year-old and never explained to anyone.

She took a draft card from the stack on the table and wrote on it in her blunt hand.

*Rowan, K. Unclassified. Crooked Line, provisional. NOT for draft this season. Watch first sanctioned meet. Called a quench from under a husk with two doors open and was right. Kept the third shut under impact. Responder. — R.S.*

She put the card in the tray for the draft board.

Then she took a second, smaller card, and wrote on it only two words. She looked at them for a while. Then she put that card in her own coat pocket, not in the tray, and turned off the projection table, and sat for a while longer in the dark.

*Corran's disk.*

---

The rosters posted on Monday at noon.

They went up on the Measure Hall gate under glass, the way they always did, and by a quarter past there were five hundred people in front of them. Kiva did not push to the front. She stood at the back with her arm in its sling and let Perrin push for her, because Perrin was very large and very happy and nobody argued with him.

He came back with his bandaged hands in the air.

"Stonehand bursary," he said. "Second Division. Full tuition and a stipend. My father cried. On the tram. In front of people." He grinned so widely it looked painful. "Second Division, Kiva. I've never been in the second anything."

"Perrin, that's—"

"I know. I know." He bumped her good shoulder with his, gently for once. "You're in the Third. Crooked Line. Provisional. Your name's at the very bottom. In small letters. With a little star and *Sponsor: O. Dane* next to it, like a footnote." He looked at her. "It's the best thing I've ever seen on that board."

Anwen was beside them with her list. She had read the boards already. She had read every one.

"Graywater posts field placements on the twentieth," she said. "I'm on Doran Mace's list. Not at the top. Higher than I was." She folded the list. "I've got eight days to get higher. I'm going to the Wren House lanes this afternoon. I won't be at Orla's tonight."

"All right."

"I'm not saying that because I'm angry with you."

"I know."

"I'm saying it because it's mine," said Anwen. And then, after a moment, more quietly, "Come on Thursday, though. I want to see you do the drill in ten."

Kellan Renn came out of the crowd at the front of the gate. He had a fresh scab on his right knee, Kiva saw, and the red mark on his forearm from the Terrace Ring had gone yellow-green. He stopped in front of her.

"First Division," he said. "Crooked Line, first team. Kellan Renn, Fire. No asterisk."

"Congratulations."

"Thank you." He looked at her sling. "Third Division."

"Provisional."

"The lowest defensible place." He said it as if he were reading it off a meter. Then he said, "Good."

"Good?"

"Good. It's a number. It's on the board. I know where you are." He put his hands in his pockets. "I'll be comparing."

"I know."

He nodded once. He started to turn away. Then he stopped and looked back.

"Your fingers," he said. "The two on the left. The medic said they'd come back."

"They're coming back."

"All the way?"

Kiva pressed the tips of the first two fingers with her thumb, one at a time. She could feel it. Duller than the third. A little further away. As if she had felt them once through thin gloves and the gloves were still on.

"Mostly," she said.

He looked at her for a moment longer. He did not say anything. Then he went away down the Measure Hall steps into the crowd, and she watched the back of his head until she lost it.

---

That evening, Kiva went to the Old Yard.

Orla was already there, on her crate by the first shed, with the hand lamp hung on its hook and her bad leg stretched out in front of her. She did not get up.

"No drills tonight," she said. "Not with that arm. You can sit."

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

Kiva did not answer at once. She walked down the lane between the black posts. The ground was rutted and not quite level. She knew every rut. At the far end, the last post leaned out of the line.

She stopped beside it.

She thought about the Cellhouse. The cell under her hand, and the joy, and the fear, and the three doors going in. The ringing that would not stop. She thought about Perrin's purple hands, and Kellan's foot on the rail, and Anwen not coming out. She thought about the transient register, and three lines in three old books. *Every lamp at once.* She thought about the bright people on every road, and the sky that was not there.

She did not understand any of it. She understood a little more than she had a week ago. That was all.

She put her good hand on the post.

The wood was rough and cold. The iron bands were cold. Under her shirt, the heat-lines ached from her breastbone to her elbow, a long, low, healing ache. The disk lay cold in the middle of them.

"It's still crooked," she said.

"It is," said Orla, from the far end.

"Somebody had a reason once."

"Somebody did."

Kiva took her hand off the post. She took out her book and opened it against her knee, and wrote the date at the top of a clean page, by the yellow light of Orla's lamp.

*Exercise:* Third Division, provisional. Sponsor O. Dane.

She looked at the other columns. *What went wrong. Who was hurt. What it cost.* She did not know yet. Nothing had gone wrong yet. Everything was still in front of her.

She left them blank.

Then she closed the book and walked back up the lane to where Orla was sitting, and stood in front of her with her arm in its sling and her two dull fingers and her three burning lines, and said the only thing she could think of that was true.

"When the arm's better," she said. "Again."
