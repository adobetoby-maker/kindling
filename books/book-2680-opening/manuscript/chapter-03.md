# Chapter 3 — Kiva: Sequence, Not Stack

The Old Yard was not supposed to be used after dark.

There was a sign on the gate that said so, in brass letters gone green. *CROOKED LINE SCHOOL. OLD YARD. CEREMONIAL USE ONLY. CLOSED AT SUNSET.* Orla Dane had a key to the gate, and a hand lamp, and a very loose idea of what *ceremonial* meant.

"Every time I teach something," she said, unlocking the padlock, "it's a ceremony. Ask anyone."

She swung the gate open with her stick and went in, and Kiva followed with Anwen and Perrin behind her.

It was Friday, the day after the north round, with seven days to go to the Cellhouse. The yard was dark and cold and smelled of old earth. Orla hung her lamp on a hook on the first shed, and its yellow light spread down the long strip of packed ground and over the line of black practice posts until the dark took them.

Kiva had been here on Thursday nights for four years, and on Mondays and Wednesdays too, when it was just her and Orla. She knew every rut in the ground. She knew which post had a nail sticking out of it at knee height and which shed roof dripped when it rained.

Tonight it felt different, because tonight there was a form folded in Mara's apron pocket with three words on the back of it.

"Right," said Orla. She lowered herself onto an upturned crate by the shed and stretched her bad leg out in front of her. "Your aunt is a better teacher than I am. Did you know that?"

"She doesn't teach," said Kiva.

"She just did. She told you what you need in three words, on the back of a form." Orla held up one finger. "Meter. You need a way to see what your doors are doing before they do it." A second finger. "Shutdown path. You need to be able to close a door on purpose, cleanly, when it opens without being asked."

She did not hold up a third. She looked at the lamp instead.

"Hand," she said. "Every serious cell in this city has somebody's hand on it, who answers for it when it goes wrong. Your aunt wants to know whose is on you." She lowered her fingers. "That one isn't yours to learn. It's between me and your aunt. We'll do the first two."

"It's in seven days," said Perrin.

"It is in seven days," agreed Orla. "So we'd better start."

---

She started with the thing Kiva already knew, as she always did.

"Rank," said Orla. "Tell me what it is."

"How much of you is lit."

"And Hold?"

"Control. How well you can manage what's lit."

"Those are the board's words. Give me yours."

Kiva thought about it. She thought about the shop, and the cells on their racks, and the flat above it with its six rooms.

"Rank is how many rooms in the house have the lamps on," she said. "Hold is whether you can carry a lamp from one room to another without dropping it."

Orla smiled, which made her crooked nose more crooked. "Good. And you?"

"I've got three rooms lit," said Kiva slowly. "And every time I try to carry one lamp, I try to carry all three. And I drop them."

"That's it. That's your whole problem, in one sentence." Orla leaned forward on her stick. "Your rank isn't small, Kiva. The board can't read it properly, but it isn't small. Three doors open at thirteen is three doors, and that's more light than most people in this city will ever have. But the board doesn't post you on light. It posts you on what you can carry, and you can't carry anything yet without the other two lamps jumping into your hands."

"I know."

"Then here's what you don't know. Or what you know and won't do." Orla put her stick across her knees. "You keep trying to use them together. Ward and Edge at once. Stride and stance at once. You think if you could just stack them, you'd be strong. You'd be faster than Kellan Renn and steadier than Perrin and you'd ward like Anwen while you did it." She shook her head. "That's not a skill you have. It might be one day. It isn't now. Right now, every time you stack, they fight."

"So what do I do?"

"Sequence," said Orla. "Not stack. One door. Close it. Next door. Close it. Next." She tapped the ground with her stick three times, slowly. "It'll be slower, and it'll look stupid, and people will laugh. It's still better than a cell screaming in your hands."

Kiva stared at the three marks the stick had left in the dirt.

She had heard this before, because Orla had been saying some version of it for two years. *One at a time. Slower. Clean before fast.* And Kiva had tried, and it had been slow, and it had looked stupid, and in every sparring bout she had ever fought, somebody faster with one good door had walked straight through the gap while she was still closing the first.

So she had gone back to trying to stack, because stacking, when it worked for half a second, felt like flying.

"You're thinking about the gap," said Orla.

Kiva looked up.

"You're thinking that the gap between one door and the next is where everybody hits you." Orla nodded. "It is. It's going to be. That's the price. But you can't make the gap smaller by pretending it isn't there. You make it smaller by closing cleaner, and you can't close clean until you can see what's opening." She leaned back. "Which brings us to your aunt's first word."

---

"You've had a meter all along," said Orla. "You just don't read it."

She pointed her stick at Kiva's chest.

Kiva put her hand there without thinking. Under her shirt, under the disk, the three heat-lines lay quiet.

"They light up when the doors compete," said Orla. "Yes? Every time. In the frame. At the draw bench. That night in the lane when your slab discharged and took the mast out."

"Yes."

"Do they all light at once?"

Kiva opened her mouth to say yes. Then she stopped.

She thought about the draw bench on Wednesday, with the lamp lit orange and the hand door waking up to look, and then the heat. But it had not been all three at once. There had been a moment, very short, when it was only the bottom line, the one nearest her breastbone, which had gone warm first. Then the second had come, and then the third, at her shoulder, and only then had the lamp flared.

"No," she said slowly. "The bottom one goes first."

"How long before the next?"

"I don't know. Not long. Half a breath?"

"Half a breath," said Orla, "is a long time. You can do a lot in half a breath. You can close a door in half a breath, if you see it coming." She pointed at Anwen. "Anwen. Stand there, please. About a pace off her left side. Have a ward ready, but don't raise it unless I tell you."

Anwen went and stood where she was told, holding her hands loosely in front of her, palms in, not quite touching.

"Perrin. Behind her. Hand flat between her shoulders. If her stance goes, I want you to feel it before she does."

Perrin moved behind Kiva and put his big warm palm on her back. "I'm feeling it," he said. "I'm very present."

"Kiva," said Orla. "Edge. Just Edge. Hold it. Don't light it. And the moment you feel that bottom line go warm, before anything else happens, you call the door that's coming. Name it. That's all. You don't have to stop it. You just have to know which one it is."

Kiva raised her right fist and asked for Edge.

The metal came off the copper slab on her left forearm and flowed across to her right hand the way it always did, as if it were going downhill, and it ran over her knuckles and closed into the bar, short and flat and square-ended and ugly. She held it out in front of her, dark.

Nothing happened. She held it while the yard stayed quiet and, somewhere out in the city, a tram bell rang.

Anwen shifted her weight beside her.

And the hand door woke up.

Kiva felt it lift, cool and curious, toward Anwen and the faint shimmer of the ward Anwen was holding ready between her palms. The hand door liked wards the way it liked cells. It wanted to see.

And the bottom line went warm. Then the second.

"Hand," said Kiva. Too late. The second line was already hot, and the third was starting, and the bar on her fist jerked as if someone had bumped it.

"That was late," said Orla. "Again."

They did it again. Kiva held the dark bar while Anwen stood with her ward ready and Perrin breathed slowly behind her. This time Kiva did not watch the bar, or anything else. She listened to her chest the way Mara listened to a cell.

The hand door lifted.

The bottom line warmed.

"Hand," said Kiva.

The second line warmed a moment later. But she had called it first. She had felt the first wire go hot and named the door before the second.

"That's early," said Orla. "Good. Again."

They did it again, and again after that. She was early, and then late twice, and then early, and once Orla made Perrin shift his weight instead of Anwen, and the door that woke was the still door, and Kiva called *Hand* out of habit and was wrong. After that she listened harder. By the end her chest ached the way it did after a long day at the shop, a dull, used ache, and she could feel the bottom line move before the door behind it had quite finished waking up. It was like hearing the click in a tram's brakes before the car begins to slow.

"That's your meter," said Orla. "It's always been there. You've just been reading it after the needle's already swung."

Kiva let the bar fall back into the slab. Her right hand was shaking a little.

"Is it always going to hurt?"

"I don't know," said Orla honestly. "Probably, while they're fighting. Maybe less when they stop." She shifted her leg. "Rest five minutes. Then we'll learn how to close."

---

Closing was harder.

Each door shut differently, and Orla could only teach one of them from the inside. She had been Ember-door for forty years and had reached Torch rank, which was as far as her own door had ever taken her, and she had used it on a hundred rifts with the Home Line before her knee gave out. She could feel Edge. She could not feel a ward or a stance any more than she could feel someone else's toothache.

So she made the others teach.

"Ember first," she said. "I know that one. Form the bar."

Kiva formed it.

"Now let it go."

Kiva let it go, and the metal ran back over her fist and into the slab.

"No," said Orla. "You dropped it. Do it again, and this time put it down."

"What's the difference?"

"When you drop it, it goes back to the slab, but you're still holding the door open behind it. The door's still awake. It's just got nothing in its hands, so it looks around for something else to hold." Orla held up her own right fist. Her Ember was a dark iron-grey slab on a worn steel cuff, and the metal ran from it over her knuckles and closed into a short, broad, leaf-shaped blade, old-fashioned and very plain. She held it, and then the metal ran back. It did not hurry. It went back into the cuff the way water goes back into a bucket when you tip it gently, and then, after it was gone, Orla let out a slow breath through her nose.

"There," she said. "Did you see that?"

"You breathed out."

"After. After it was home. That's when I shut the door behind it. Not while it's going. After." She pointed the stick at Kiva. "You've been closing the door on the metal while it's still in the doorway. That's why it catches."

Kiva formed the bar and let the metal run back. She waited until it was all the way home in the slab, the copper warm and heavy on her forearm, and then she breathed out and imagined a door closing.

It closed. She felt it go quiet, like a cell powering down.

"Better," said Orla. "Anwen. Your turn."

Anwen came and stood in front of Kiva. She did not like teaching, and she had told Kiva so many times, because she said it was like being asked to explain how you walk. But she did it the way she did everything, plainly and thoroughly, as if she were explaining a lock.

"You make a ward like a tray," she said.

"I make a ward like a ward."

"You make it like a tray. Flat. Out in front of you. With your arms stiff." Anwen raised her own hands, and a ward came up between them, faintly silver, the size of a dinner plate, and not flat at all but curved a little, like the side of a bowl. "A ward's not a thing you hold. It's a door you hold shut. You lean on it. And when you want to put it down, you don't drop it. You fold it." She brought her palms slowly together, and the silver curve folded in on itself like a closing hand and was gone. "If you drop it, it splashes."

"Splashes?"

"Everything you were holding back comes through at once. Like taking your thumb off a hose." Anwen gave her the level look. "You splash every time. That's why the hand door doesn't close after. It's still got water all over it."

Kiva raised a ward, which came up between her palms tray-sized and flat.

"Curve it," said Anwen.

She tried. It wobbled, and then it curved, a little.

"Now fold."

She brought her palms together. The ward folded halfway, and then the hand door, startled, let go of the rest of it all at once, with a soft pop and a puff of cool air in her face.

"Splash," said Anwen. "Again."

The fourth time, it folded. It did not fold neatly, but it folded, and the hand door closed after it without reaching for anything else, and Kiva felt it go quiet.

"Perrin," said Orla.

Perrin rubbed his hands together. "Right. Stance." He came around in front of Kiva. "You're good at stance. You're better at stance than you should be. But you never come out of it. I've watched you. You sink down into it like a bath, and then you just sort of stay there, and the still door's still open under you, holding the ground."

"So how do I get out?"

"You give the ground its weight back." He planted his feet, and Kiva felt him do it. He did not move, and nothing about him looked different, but something about the way he stood changed, the way a bench changes when someone heavy sits on it. "You took it, when you went into stance. You borrowed it. So when you come out, you give it back. Lift your heels a tiny bit. Breathe in, not out. And sort of—" He made a face. "Say thank you."

"Say thank you?"

"To the ground. Not out loud. In your knees." He shrugged. "That's what my first teacher at the Hall told me. I thought it was stupid. Works, though."

She went into stance and felt the heaviness come up through her feet and settle in her knees. Then she lifted her heels very slightly, and breathed in, and thought *thank you* at the packed earth of the Old Yard.

The still door eased. It did not close all the way, but it eased.

"Good," said Perrin. "Weird, isn't it?"

"Very."

"Now," said Orla, from her crate. "All together. Sequence. Edge. Close it properly. Then ward. Fold it. Then stance. Give it back." She tapped the ground three times with her stick. "Slowly. I don't care how long it takes."

---

The first time, it went wrong in the first gap.

Kiva formed the bar and held it. She let the metal run back toward the slab, and she waited for it to get home, and she waited, and it was taking so long, and the ward was the next thing, and she could feel Anwen standing there, and she reached for the ward.

The metal was not home yet.

The bottom heat-line flashed, and then all three, as the hand door opened straight into the Ember door's doorway. The last of the metal, still running back over her wrist, jerked and spat a hot orange spark from its edge, though she had never lit it. Her slab went hot on her arm, hot enough to hurt, and somewhere down the yard, on the shed at the far end, the lane's little sensing mast gave a sharp electric ping.

Anwen's ward came up between them before Kiva could blink. The spark hit it and went out.

Kiva stood very still, breathing hard, with her chest burning.

"What happened?" said Orla.

"I didn't wait."

"What else?"

"I was thinking about the next one. While I was closing the first one." She thought of the tag on the Tallis Street regulator. *Runs fast. Hot on load.* "I was in a hurry."

Orla said nothing. She did not need to.

"Again," said Kiva.

The second time, she waited. She let the metal run all the way home and felt it settle in the copper, and she breathed out and closed the door behind it, and felt it go quiet. And then she let a whole breath go by, in and out, before she reached for anything else.

Then she raised the ward.

It came up flat and tray-shaped, but it came up into an empty doorway, and nothing pulled against it. The bottom heat-line stayed cool. She curved the ward a little, and held it, and then she folded it, and although it splashed a little at the end, it folded.

She stood for two breaths with her palms together. Then she went into stance, and the weight came up through her feet, and she held it, and she gave it back.

Then she stood in the dark yard with her hands at her sides, and her chest did not hurt at all.

"Fourteen seconds," said Orla. "From Edge to the end of stance."

Perrin whistled. "That's a long time."

"It's a very long time," said Orla. "Kellan Renn would cut you in half three times in that. Any Riftspawn bigger than a Handful would walk through you. You'd fail every timed lane in the Circuit." Her face, across the lamp-lit yard, was very still. "And it's the first time in four years I've seen you go through all three doors without a single line lighting."

Kiva had not realized it until Orla said it. She put her hand to her chest, where the lines lay under her shirt, quiet and cool.

"So that's a sequence," said Perrin.

"No," said Orla. "That's a drill. It's three doors one after another, with nothing passing between them. Each one starts from standing still." She tapped the three marks in the dirt again. "A real sequence is when the first door hands its work to the next one without dropping it. The speed of a Stride going straight into a stance, so you stop where you mean to and nobody moves you. The stop of a ward going straight into a strike. The work carries across. Nobody I know can do that across doors. I've heard it talked about. I've never seen it done." She nodded at Kiva. "This is the drill that gets you there. If anything does."

Kiva understood something then that Orla had been telling her for two years, and that she had not been able to hear. The gap was not the enemy. The gap was where the closing happened, and if she rushed the gap, the doors fought, and if she let the gap be as long as it needed to be, they did not. It was slow because it was clean, and it would get faster only by getting cleaner first.

She had always thought it was the other way around.

"Again," she said.

---

At ten o'clock, Orla called a stop. She did not get up from the crate. She put her stick across her knees and looked at Kiva across the lamp-lit dirt.

"Right," she said. "What went wrong?"

Kiva had heard her ask it of other people, at the end of Thursday lanes, while she was packing up. She had never been the one standing in front of the crate.

"The first try," she said. "I rushed the gap. I reached for the ward before the metal was home. All three lines lit. It sparked, and the mast pinged."

"Who was hurt?"

"Nobody. Anwen caught it."

"What did it cost?"

Kiva turned her left arm to the lamp. There was a pink patch on the skin where the slab had gone hot. "That. And the lines ached for a while."

"Second go?"

"I waited. A whole breath in every gap. Nothing lit." She made a face. "Too slow."

"Why?"

Kiva opened her mouth, and found she had nothing to put in it.

She did not know why. She did not know why waiting worked, or why the doors fought in the first place, or why there were three of them, or why a stance she had never trained was better than an Edge she had trained for four years. She stood in the dirt with the lamp on her face and the question in front of her, and the longer she stood, the less she had.

"I don't know," she said at last.

"Then that's the answer." Orla did not seem disappointed. "Don't make one up to please me. I've had twenty years of people making them up to please me, and every one of them cost somebody later."

Perrin had been leaning on the shed with his arms folded through all of it. He straightened up.

"Do you ask everybody that?" he said.

It came out more sharply than he probably meant, and Orla turned to him.

"Everybody who's still here at the end."

"I'm never here at the end. I get the early tram."

"You could have stayed. Last year. Any Thursday you liked." Orla tipped her head. "You didn't ask."

"I didn't know it was for asking."

"Everything's for asking." She waited. "Well? What went wrong?"

Perrin opened his mouth, and shut it, and for a moment he did not say anything at all, which was rare enough that Anwen looked up from folding her ward-gloves.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing went wrong. I stood behind her with my hand on her back, and she did everything, and I was very present." He rubbed his face. Then he turned to Kiva, and his face was honest and a little ashamed. "That's the thing that went wrong. You get the private nights and the old Home Line teacher and the mysterious necklace and Ysra Crest in a little room, and I get to stand behind you with my hand on your back. I've been trying for a year to make that funny." He shrugged. "It's not your fault. It's just that the joke keeps coming out jealous."

"I know," said Kiva. "I'd rather have the jealous than the joke."

"Who was hurt?" said Orla, as if nobody had said anything.

Perrin considered. "My pride. Slightly."

"What did it cost?"

"A tram." He sighed. "I've missed the early one."

Orla almost smiled. "Second go. Thursday. You stay to the end, and you bring something that went wrong, and we'll see." She reached for the lamp. "Go home, all of you. Monday we'll do it again, and you'll be faster, Kiva, and you'll hate every second of it."

Perrin bumped Kiva's shoulder on the way to the gate, harder than usual. "I'm staying to the end on Thursday," he said, and then he was off down the hill at a run, after a tram that had almost certainly gone.

---

When they had gone, Orla Dane sat on the crate in the empty yard and let her knee hurt.

It hurt every evening now. Twenty years on the Home Line had done it, twenty years of Stride, of landing hard on broken ground with the struts locked and the whole weight of a fall going through one joint. The healers at the Wren House had done what they could, and she had another five years of teaching in it, maybe, before she needed the stick for everything.

She did not mind. Mostly.

She sat and looked down the dark lane, where the lamplight gave out among the posts.

She had told Mara Rowan the truth, as far as it went. She had put her name beside a student's once. Twenty-one years ago, when she was newly off the Home Line and full of herself, she had sponsored a twin-kindled boy from the Brickfields named Jessamy Hart, who had opened Ember and hand together at thirteen. It was two doors and not three, and it had seemed like enough of a miracle at the time.

She had thought she understood him. She had thought she could teach him the way she taught everyone, with patience and the old lane and her own field sense, and in his second year she had put him into a Circuit trial, because he had asked, and because he was ready, and because she wanted the board to see what she saw.

She did not let herself go through the rest. She had not gone through it in order for a long time, and she could not have told it to anyone. It was enough to know that the boy had left Meridian at nineteen, and that she had never gone to find him, because she had not known what to say.

They had suspended her sponsoring standing for ten years. She had had it back for longer than that now, and she had never used it.

She thought about that, and about the girl who had stood in this yard tonight and gone through all three doors without a single line lighting.

That time was nothing. It was a beginner's time, the kind to be ashamed of in any sanctioned lane. And Orla had sat on this crate and watched it and felt something rise in her chest that she had not felt in twenty-one years.

She knew what it was. It was the same thing she had felt about Jessamy.

That was what frightened her.

She thought, too, about the disk. She had seen it before, though not on Kiva. She had seen it on Ilse Corran, twenty-five years ago, at a field camp on the Ferren levee, the night after a bad rift, when Ilse had taken it out from under her shirt and turned it over in her fingers by the fire and said nothing about it at all. Orla had known Ilse for six years by then, and had never asked. You did not ask a bearer. The disk went by appointment from one bearer to the next. It was not owned. It was carried.

Ilse had carried it for thirty years. And then she had stood before the Line's watch and named a six-year-old girl whose mother had just died. Orla had not been in the room. She had heard about it the way everyone on the Line heard things, late and in pieces, and she had not believed it until four years ago, when the disk came to Coper Street on the day of the girl's Kindling.

She knew one other thing, and she had told no one, not Kiva and not Mara and not Ysra Crest.

Sixteen years ago, five years after Jessamy, she had gone up to the house of the Order of the Even Hand in the hills, because the Order trained a single door for a lifetime and she wanted to know whether anyone there had ever seen two open in one child. She had not gone for the boy. The boy was gone. She had gone because she could not stop asking.

The Warden had sat her in a chair by a cold window and given her tea, and had not answered the question. Instead she had told Orla what the Order believed, and why it trained the way it did. Their master, Hesper Annick, had dreamed in her fevers that the sky came off the world like the roof off a house, and that whatever was above came down, and every lamp in Meridian answered. The Order meant to be standing when it came.

Orla had drunk the tea and thanked her and gone down the hill and not thought about it again, until four years ago, when every lamp in the Old Yard went out and a thirteen-year-old girl lay on the ground with her eyes open, staring at something very far away.

Kiva had never told her what she saw, and Orla had never asked. She did not know if there was any connection at all. A fever was a fever, and a Glory master's nightmares were not a map. She had no proof of anything, and she had been on the Home Line too long to mistake a feeling for a fact.

But she could still see the Warden's face across the cold window, saying *every lamp*, as if it were the plainest thing in the world.

Orla got up, stiffly, and took the lamp off its hook, and locked the gate behind her.

She walked home the long way, up through the Compact Quarter, and the long way went past the Placement Hall. At the foot of its white steps she stopped, the way she had stopped on the night after the Old Yard four years ago, with her stick planted and her bad leg aching, and looked up at the dark doors where Ysra Crest kept her stamp.

She did not go up.
