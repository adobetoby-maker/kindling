# Chapter 10 — Kiva: The Tin

On Wednesday the warden sent the catchers up to the glass.

There was one from each team in that afternoon's section, and they sat in a row along the front bench of the cast room with their kits at their feet. Below them, on the settling floor, the other three members of Team Nineteen were unrolling a cloth.

"You'll each tell me two things," said Druce. He stood behind the bench with his grey fingers folded. "Before the kill, where you think it will fall. After the fall, which way it runs. Quietly, and to me. Don't tell me what you'd like. Tell me what the floor is going to do."

Kiva sat with her hands on her knees and studied the floor.

She had learned, in one afternoon on her knees, that it was not flat. Nothing in that hall was flat. The brick between the tanks had been laid with a fall toward each grate, so that two hundred years ago the refinery men could sluice it down, and a heap of dust that landed a pace from a tank's lip would creep toward the lip like water finding a drain. The plank bridges bounced. And along the north wall ran a shallow gutter, with four open grates in it, that she had not noticed at all until she had swept a pinch of dead ash into one.

Team Nineteen had laid their cloth in the middle of the widest stretch of brick, which was sensible. Their hand-door boy stood behind it with a ward half raised. Their still-door girl had the sandbag in a corner and was pretending it had a broken leg.

Their Ember-door was new. She was a wiry girl with cropped red hair and a Saltmere-green band on her sleeve, and she was bouncing on her toes at the far end of the floor, nowhere near the cloth.

"That's Selka Dray," said Perrin's voice, low, from the bench behind. The rest of Team Seven had been allowed up to watch, and he had put himself directly at Kiva's shoulder. "Second Division. She's standing in until Kellan makes up his mind. I fought her in the open lane. Three to two, to me." He paused. "She hits like a door. I've still got the mark."

Kiva was not watching Selka Dray's blade. She was watching her feet, and the long straight line between those feet and the steel door.

A husk went at the brightest thing. It went straight. If the brightest thing in that hall was going to be a lit blade at the south end, a dozen paces from the cloth, then the cloth might as well have been left in its roll.

Druce came down the row, bending to each catcher in turn. When he reached her she told him, low. "South tank, east lip. It runs into the grate."

He made no answer. He went back to the lever and pulled it.

---

It took Selka Dray four seconds.

Kiva had to admit that they were a lovely four seconds. The husk came out of the door and found her at once, and she did not wait for it. She went to meet it in two Strides with the struts flashing on her calves, and her blade was on her fist and lit before the second landed, a quick hot yellow, and she cut the thing through the knot at a dead run and was three paces past it before it had finished falling.

It fell on the east lip of the south tank.

About half of it fell there. The other half went over the lip and down the sloping tank floor in a long soft slide, and then the heap on the lip began to creep after it, lifting and settling, and Team Nineteen's catcher came sprinting over the plank bridge with his cloth flapping behind him like a flag.

By the time the cloth was down there was nothing left on the lip to put it under. The catcher went into the tank on his knees with a pan in each hand. Selka Dray stood on the bridge above him, breathing easily, with her blade still lit and held low, ready for the next one.

Kiva saw it. She saw Druce see it. His mouth went thin, and he said nothing at all.

"Settled," he said at last.

They tinned less than half. Nearly all of the rest had gone through the grate.

Druce walked back along the row of catchers and stopped beside her.

"Why the grate?"

"The tank floors slope to them. Everything in there runs downhill." She hesitated. "And she was always going to kill it where she met it. She didn't look at the cloth once. So I looked at where she was standing, and where it would come from, and drew the line."

"Hm," said Druce. He moved on.

"*Hm,*" said Perrin in her ear. "You've had a *hm* from the warden. I've been here two days and he hasn't noticed I'm alive."

"You haven't got any hands."

"I've got a lot of other qualities."

Below, Team Nineteen had squared themselves for a second release. They had moved the cloth to the south tank's lip this time, which was at least where the last one had died, and the still-door girl had dragged her sandbag out of the way. Druce went to the tube. "Dray. Dark blade until it's on the canvas. Kill it where your catcher is." He put his hand on the lever.

The second husk came through the door, and Kiva frowned.

She could not have said what was wrong with it. It was the right size. It had the low front and the four thick limbs. But the light in that hall was poor and yellowish, and the front of the thing seemed to catch it. From its blunt nose back over what would have been its shoulders, there was a faint sheen on the grey, like the skin on a bowl of warm milk.

Selka Dray had not noticed, and she lit her blade. The husk swung to her and came, and she let it get onto the canvas this time, and stepped in, and cut.

Her blade skated.

It struck the husk's front quarter and slid off sideways with a high clear ringing noise. Kiva had heard that noise before. It was the sound Anwen's ward made when a clerk tapped it with a rod.

Dray staggered. She recovered faster than Kiva could have, and cut again, harder, and the blade rang again and turned. The husk drove into her hip. She went back two paces, swearing, with the hand-door boy shouting something, and cut a third time straight down with both fists on the hilt of nothing, and whatever was on the front of the thing broke.

It broke like a dropped plate. A thin bright curve of something stood in the air for half a breath, silver, the size and shape of a pot lid. Then it fell in pieces and the pieces were ash before they hit the brick, and her blade went on through into the knot, and the rest of the husk poured down after.

Nobody on the bench moved.

"Dark," said Druce. His voice was exactly as soft as it had been. "Catcher. That's yours."

He did not speak again until it had settled. He watched the tins go into the carrier. Then he stood at the glass with his grey fingers spread on the sill and looked at the steel door for a long moment, the way a man looks at a dog that has just bitten somebody.

"Third this month," he said, to nobody.

"Was that a bad batch?" said one of the other catchers, a Wren House boy with a scared face. "Warden? Is the Kennel making bad ones?"

"The Kennel doesn't make batches." Druce turned from the glass. "It's one tear, held in one lattice, and it has given this school one husk at a time for sixty years. I've stood at that lever for a good many of them. Handfuls don't wear a skin. Flasks do, sometimes, out on a real line. I've seen it at Flask twice in my life." He looked down at the page. "I'd not seen it at a Handful until the fourth of this month."

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know," said Druce. "When I do, you'll hear it from me. Up you get. Next section's waiting."

---

Kellan was at the Old Yard gate on Thursday night before Orla had the padlock off.

He stood under the brass sign with his hands in his pockets and read it, including the part about sunset. Then he looked at the sky, which had been dark for an hour.

"It says ceremonial use only."

"Every time I teach something, it's a ceremony," said Orla. She pushed the gate open with her stick. "Ask anyone. Did somebody invite you, Mr. Renn?"

"No."

"Good. I don't hold with invitations. Mind the rut."

He minded it. He came down the lane behind Kiva and Perrin with the wary step of someone walking through another family's kitchen, and when Orla hung the lamp on its hook he stood at the edge of the light and did not sit. Anwen was already there, on the upturned crate beside Orla's, with her coat buttoned to the chin. She had come straight from the Wren House lanes, and there was a ward-burn on the heel of her hand that she had not mentioned.

"I said Thursday," she told Kiva. "I said the whole drill, bar and ward and stance, with everything in your hands, and I'd stand here and watch."

"I've got one arm until tomorrow."

"I know. I came to hear the excuse in person."

"There's no excuse and there's no bar," said Orla, lowering herself onto the crate. "There's a medic's order that runs until morning, and I don't cross medics. But the order says she's not to use the arm. It says nothing at all about doors." She pointed the stick at Kiva. "Sling off. Hands at your sides. You're going to do the drill with nothing in them."

"Nothing?"

"No bar. No ward. No stance. Open the door, let it stand open, shut it. Next door. I want to know how long your doors take when you aren't hauling anything through them."

Kiva worked her arm out of the sling. It felt thin and strange, and the skin along the new length of the heat-lines pulled when she straightened her elbow. She stood in the middle of the lane with her hands hanging and found that she did not know how to begin.

She had never opened the Ember door without asking it for something. She tried it now. She thought about the slab on her forearm, warm and heavy, and about the place behind it where the asking came from, and she simply leaned on that place without asking.

It opened. She felt it stand there, awake, with nothing in its hands, looking about.

She breathed out, and it shut.

Then the hand door, cool and close and curious. Open. It reached at once for Anwen and found that Anwen had no ward up, and while it was still puzzled, Kiva closed it. Then the still door, heavy under her feet. Open. *Thank you.* Shut.

She stood in the dark with her chest cool and quiet and knew before anybody spoke that it had been quicker.

"Seven seconds," said Orla.

"Seven?"

"I had my watch on it, and I didn't believe it." Orla's face in the lamplight was thoughtful. "Do you understand what that is? Your best with shapes is ten, near enough. Three seconds of that is not doors. It's furniture. You've been carrying an iron bar and a tea tray through three doorways and wondering why you kept catching the frame."

"That's not what I asked for," said Anwen.

"No. It's quicker."

"It's quicker with nothing in her hands. I asked for the whole thing with all of it." Anwen folded her arms. "I'm not being difficult. I want to see the thing I asked to see, and I don't want anyone telling her that was it."

"Nobody's telling her that was it," said Orla mildly. "Next week you'll get the whole thing. Tonight she's learned where the other three seconds live."

Kellan had come a step into the light. He was looking at Kiva's right hand.

"You watch it," he said.

Kiva turned. "What?"

"The metal. When it runs home. I saw you do it in the Ring and again on the mezzanine. You watch it all the way back to the cuff. You hold its hand." He raised his own right fist, and his long dark blade came off the steel and stood there, and then it was gone again, a smooth quick flow like a fish turning, and only after it was gone did the small breath leave his nose. "It knows the way. It's been going home to that slab for four years. If you escort it, it goes at the speed you can watch. If you let it go, it goes at its own." He lowered his hand. "That's half a second, at least. Probably more."

It was the longest thing he had ever said to her.

"Why are you telling me?" said Kiva.

He took the question seriously, which she was beginning to learn he did with every question.

"If I stay on Seven, I need you faster," he said. "If I go, it's cost me nothing. Either way I'd be stupid to keep it." He put his hands back in his pockets. "And it annoyed me. Watching you do it wrong."

Perrin made a sound like a kettle. "He's *helping*," he said. "Kiva, he's helping you, and he's found a way to make it sound like a complaint. I've never admired anyone more."

---

"Now," said Orla. "The other thing."

She reached into the canvas bag and took out one of Druce's squat tins, with its lid off, and after it a small flat case of worn leather. Inside the case was a brass balance no longer than her hand, with two pans on fine chains and a row of tiny weights in a slotted block.

"Home Line field scale," she said. "You weigh a yield on the bank before you trust anyone's word for it, including your own. Miss Pryce, would you fill that tin to six drams from the bag at your feet? It's dead ash. The warden lent it to me, and he'll want it back, so don't spill."

Anwen filled it. She did it the way she did everything, in careful pinches, and the balance settled level.

Orla held the open tin out to Kiva.

"Both hands. Your left may hold a tin tonight and do nothing else. Hold it in front of your breastbone, level, as if it were a Handful somebody had bled for." She waited until Kiva had it. The dust lay a finger's width below the rim, grey and fine. "You told Ysra Crest that you don't know how to keep your doors out when you're frightened. I've thought about that sentence every day since. It's the truest thing you've said to anyone, and I can't sign anything until it changes. So we're going to frighten you."

"With what?"

"With Mr. Renn," said Orla. "Since he's here, and uninvited, and by a long way the best blade in this yard. Dark edge, flat of the blade, body and legs. He's going to hit you. You may not block. You may not step off your ground. You may not open a door, any door, for any reason. You may turn your body to keep the tin level, and that's all you may do." She settled the balance on her knee. "Afterward we weigh what's left. That's your answer, and it doesn't flatter anybody."

Kellan had not moved.

"She won't be defending," he said.

"That's the drill."

"I've never hit anyone who wasn't defending."

"Then you'll both learn something," said Orla. "Hit her properly or go home. I won't have her practicing against kindness. It was kindness I tried last time."

Kiva did not know what last time meant, and then, a moment later, looking at Orla's face, she did.

Kellan came and stood in front of her. The blade was on his fist, dark. Close to, it was a very long piece of metal.

"Ribs," he said quietly, "the first time. So you know."

"Don't tell her," said Orla.

He hit her in the ribs.

It was not hard. It was the flat of a blunt blade, swung at half his strength, and it would leave a mark and nothing worse. But she saw it come, and every part of her that had fought him in the Terrace Ring saw it come too, and the Ember door did not wait to be asked. It came open like a dog through a gate. Her right hand left the tin. The bar was half formed on her knuckles before the blow landed, and the bottom heat-line was hot and the second was warming, and the tin, left to one weak hand, tipped.

Dead ash went down her front in a soft grey spill.

"Ember," she said, much too late, and shut the door on a bar that had nowhere to go. Her face burned.

Orla held out her hand for the tin. She set it on the balance and moved the little weights with one fingernail, unhurried.

"Not quite three on the ground," she said. "Two lines lit. Again. Miss Pryce, fill it."

The second time Kellan did not tell her. He looked at her ribs and hit her on the thigh.

That was worse, because she had braced for the ribs, and something in her had been so busy not flinching one way that it flinched the other. More went on the ground. The third time was worse again, and it was not Kellan at all, because Perrin said, "Can I have a go? I can't make a fist, but I've got shoulders," and before anybody had answered, he had walked into her like a tram.

The tin left her hands entirely. It landed in a rut four paces down the lane.

"That one wasn't fair," said Kiva, from the ground.

"Nothing on a floor is fair," said Orla. "Six drams lost. No lines lit, which is interesting. You were too surprised to be frightened. Mr. Cade, sit down."

"I helped," said Perrin. "I think I really helped."

Kiva got up. Her ribs hurt and her thigh hurt, and her left hand was shaking, and she was angry, with a mean hot useless anger that had nowhere to go, because the only person there to be angry at was a boy who was doing exactly what he had been asked. She took the filled tin from Anwen and held it at her breastbone and glared at the long dark blade.

It moved. She saw his shoulder turn before his arm did.

"Left ribs," she said.

She had not meant to say anything. It came out of her flat and automatic, the way a word comes out when you stub your toe. And the blow landed on her left ribs, exactly where she had said, and it hurt.

The Ember door lifted. She felt it come up to the threshold, eager, with its hackles raised.

It stayed there.

The bottom line was warm. The second was cool. Her hands were on the tin, both of them, and the grey surface of the ash had rocked to the rim and rocked back.

Orla weighed it for a long time.

"A dram lost, and a little," she said. "One line." She looked up. "What did you do?"

"I don't know."

"You said something."

"I said where it was going." Kiva was still staring at the tin. "I don't know why that helped. I didn't plan it."

"Then *don't know* is your answer for tonight. Try it again tomorrow and see whether it helps twice." Orla closed the balance case with a click. "That's enough for a first night. You'll be black and blue, and Mr. Renn looks as though he'd like to go and wash his hands."

Kellan did look like that. He had let the blade go the moment she called a halt, and he was standing with his empty fist held slightly away from his body.

"Same time tomorrow?" he said, not to Kiva but to Orla.

"I thought you hadn't decided if you were staying."

"I haven't." He frowned, as though the two facts did not fit together and he had only just noticed. "I'd still like to see her get it to nothing."

---

When they had gone, Orla Dane sat on her crate in the dark and went over the night.

Four tins. Nearly three drams on the ground the first time, and more the second, and the whole tin in a rut when Cade walked into her. Then a dram and a little, with one line lit, after the girl opened her mouth and said where the blow was going.

It was a thing she had seen. That was what Orla kept coming back to. She had taught for twenty-one years on patience and field sense and the feeling in her chest when a student was coming right, and she had never once in that time seen, with her own eyes, what a student did in the half second after they were surprised.

She had never surprised Jessamy Hart. She made herself look at that squarely, because the dark was a good place for it. She had been proud of how gently she brought him on. She had built him up a step at a time, with every step explained beforehand and every drill agreed, and he had been splendid, and she had put her name beside his on the strength of two years in which nothing had ever come at him sideways. The first thing that did was a live cell in front of four thousand people.

She had not needed to be crueler to him. She had needed to know.

Her knee was aching in the deep, sour way that meant rain before morning. She shifted it and made herself say, in her head, plainly, what she would need before she signed for recovery and nothing else.

The tin, five nights running, with next to nothing on the ground and never a second line. Not five good nights in seven. Five running. Then six seals of six, sound, with the bad hand, called and checked.

The third was the one she had been walking around since Saturday. A dark blade swung by a friend in a yard was not a husk. The girl knew Kellan Renn was not going to kill her. Some part of the body always knew. Orla could weigh tins until midsummer, and all she would have proved was that Kiva Rowan could keep her doors shut when she was hurt and embarrassed and angry, which was worth knowing, and was not the same as afraid.

There was a rule for it. It was older than the Compact, and it sat near the back of the license code, where nobody under fifty had any reason to look. A sponsor might walk an uncertified candidate onto a contained floor, once, under the sponsor's own Edge, for the purpose of deciding whether to sign. The sponsor was the declared Kill. If anything went wrong on it, the incident was the sponsor's.

She had not stood on a floor with a live thing in six years. She had not Stridden in seven. She had a stick.

One floor, under her own hand. She turned it over, and it did not get any less foolish.

And what frightened her was not the contract, or the standing they would take away again, or even Ysra's long plain face across a desk. She had gone looking for that fear tonight and found the place where it used to live quite empty. What frightened her was a picture, very clear, of a knee folding at the wrong moment with a seventeen-year-old kneeling two paces away, trusting her to be between.

That was a fair thing to be frightened of. She would do the exercises the Wren House had given her, the ones she had been skipping since the autumn, and she would see what the knee said in a week.

And she would not hurry. She had taught young Brannock for two years and never cured him of hurrying. She did not intend to leave any of her own in this.

Then she took down the lamp and went to walk the lane, alone, because it had been a long while since her own feet had been down it in the dark.

The notice came on Friday by the noon post, in a long envelope with a red stripe.

Kiva was in the shop when the postman pushed it through the door. She had come home at midday because the sling was to come off, and Mara had said she would rather be the one to do it. She saw the stripe from across the room. So did Mara, who put down a pressure gauge, wiped her hands on her apron, and opened the envelope with a bench knife in one clean stroke.

She read it standing. Her face did not change, which told Kiva nothing, because Mara's face would not have changed if the letter had been on fire.

"Well," said Mara. She folded it once. "You may as well hear it from me, now. I'm not having any more drawers."

Kiva waited.

"It's my tuning license. After the night of the thirteenth, the Works has put it under review." Mara spoke the way she gave a fault, with nothing added. "Until the hearing I'm not to tune, refill, or seal any licensed cell. I may go on mending the city's meters, if the Works pleases, and it seems it does. On the ninth of next month a man called Rusk sits across my bench for an afternoon and goes through my work, and I find out whether I get the other half back. And they want to know two things about this household." She looked at Kiva over the letter. "Whether what happened here in '77 is still going on. And, since there's a minor in the house with a Compact roster place, whether her sponsor has signed for her yet."

"It's not suspended," said Kiva. "Not all of it."

"No. They've cut it in half and left me the half that's mending." Mara looked at the letter in her hand, and her left thumb moved across her right palm. "Tuning was a third of the money. A bit more in a bad winter. That regulator from Tallis Street, I can't touch that sort of thing now. They'll send it to somebody on the east bank who'll charge them double and miss the hurry." She put the letter on the bench, under the brass weight, where it could not be mistaken for a drawer. "But the van still comes. I can mend a meter and tell the city when its banks are drifting. It's half a loaf. I've eaten worse."

"They want to know if Orla's signed."

"They want to know if anyone who knows what they're about has said you're safe to stand near things. It's a fair question. It's the same one I asked." Mara came around the bench. "Turn round. Let me have that knot."

Kiva turned, and felt her aunt's fingers at the back of her neck, working the medic's knot loose. The sling came away. Her arm hung at her side, lighter than she had expected and oddly cold, and when she bent the elbow, the long ache of the heat-lines woke and stretched like something that had been asleep in a cramped place.

Mara undid her collar button and looked. Kiva looked too, in the little spotted mirror over the shop sink.

The blisters had gone, and the skin had peeled where they had been and come in pink and tight. The lines themselves had faded from welts to three raised threads, darker than before. They still ran over her collarbone and past the point of her shoulder and halfway down the outside of her arm. She had half believed they would draw back as they healed, to where they had lain for four years.

They had not drawn back at all. The new length had stayed, like the mark a flood leaves on a wall.

"Tender?" said Mara.

"Only when I stretch."

"Then stretch. Twice a day. Scars that aren't stretched pull tight." Mara did the button up again, and her hand rested a moment on Kiva's shoulder. "You'll tell Orla about that fourth condition. Tonight, or whenever you see her. She ought to know it's there."

"I wasn't going to ask her to—"

"I know you weren't. I'm saying tell her. She's carrying my half loaf whether she likes it or not, and she's a right to know the weight." Mara's voice was level and dry. "And when you've told her, you tell her this as well, from me. If she signs one single day early on my account, I'll know. And so will the cell. She taught Brannock. She'll understand."

Kiva flexed her left hand. The first two fingers closed with the others, a little behind them, like the slow pair in a team of horses.

"I'll tell her," she said.

Anwen Pryce was at the Graywater hall by half past eight on Saturday, and she was not the first.

There were a dozen of them on the pavement of the Brickfields road, under the long slate-blue front of the company hall, and nobody spoke. They were all field-track candidates. She knew every face from the Wren House lanes. She knew what each of them held on a ward, because she had stood beside every one of them at the posts.

On the hour a clerk came out with a single sheet and pinned it in the glass case beside the door.

Anwen did not push. She waited until the two tall boys in front of her had read it and turned away, one of them grinning like an idiot and the other with a face like a shut door, and then she stepped up and read it from the top, although the whole of her wanted to read it from the bottom.

*GRAYWATER COMPANY. FIELD PLACEMENTS, WARD LINE, SEASON OF 2680.*

There were four names.

Hers was the fourth.

She read it again to be sure of the spelling. *Pryce, A. Wren House.* And after it, where the other three names had nothing, a small black dagger.

She stood very still on the pavement and let it go through her. It was like setting down something heavy that she had carried so long she had forgotten its weight. For about the space of four breaths she was simply and entirely happy, and she let herself be, because she had earned four breaths.

Then she went in to find Doran Mace and ask him what the dagger meant.

He was in a small office behind the muster room, with the season's postings on his desk and a cup of something that had gone cold. He stood to shake her hand, which nobody had done at the Wren House in two years.

"It means conditional," he said, when she had sat down and asked. "You hold a first-step field license within thirty days, or the place goes to the next name. The other three tinned their Handful at the Ferren culverts on Monday. They were on teams near the top of the call. You're on the team at the bottom of it. I know why, and I was in the gallery, and I don't hold it against you. But the ward line doesn't put anyone on a culvert at night who hasn't caught ash with a live thing in the room. That isn't my rule. It's older than I am, and I've seen what it's for."

"I understand."

"Your team has a member who can't be certified yet." He said it without weight. "I asked. So here's what I can do. Team Twenty-Two's hand-door has gone home to Saltmere with her mother ill. They stand eighth on the call, and they're licensed except for her seat, and the board lets a field officer move a placed candidate for cause. The warden will put Twenty-Two back on the end of a license day to seat a new hand-door. The second group's day is full, so it would be the third group's, twenty-seven to forty, whenever the season gives us that tear. One word from you, and you walk in with Twenty-Two that day and walk out licensed."

Anwen looked at her list. She did not need to. It gave her hands something to do.

It was a good offer. It was exactly the offer she would have made herself if she had been sitting on his side of the desk. It asked nothing of her except that she go where she was going anyway, a little faster and by a shorter road.

And she thought of Kiva on her knees on the settling floor, grey to the elbow, saying *again* to an empty tin.

"How long does it stand?" she said.

"Until that bell rings. The third group's. After that Twenty-Two goes in with a Wren House reserve."

"That's the same bell that calls Seven." She worked it through once, to be certain. "Then hold the seat until it rings. Not after. If Team Seven's whole when it rings, with all four certified, I go in with Seven. If it isn't, I go in with Twenty-Two, that morning, and I don't look back at it."

Doran Mace leaned back in his chair. "That's having it both ways, Miss Pryce."

"Yes," said Anwen. "I know it is." She met his eyes. "I'm not giving up a placement to be loyal. I've wanted this since I was twelve and I've got thirty days. But I've been through one run with those three and a week on the Kennel floor, and I know what they'll tell me when something goes wrong, which is the truth, straight away, out loud. I don't know that about Twenty-Two. I'd be walking onto a live floor with three people I've never heard say *off*." She folded the list. "So I'd rather have Seven, if I can have it. And if I can't, I'll take the other. That's the order. I've checked it."

He studied her for a moment longer, and then he laughed, and put out his hand again.

"Held until the bell," he said. "You'll tell her?"

"This morning."

"She won't thank you."

"She will, actually," said Anwen, and stood up. "That's why I'd rather have Seven."

---

Anwen told Kiva on the Cellhouse steps at noon, with the sun on the stone and Perrin eating a meat bun very carefully out of the tips of his fingers. The bandages had come off his hands that morning. Underneath, the palms had gone from purple to a blotched and startling yellow-green.

Kiva listened to all of it. Fourth of four. The dagger. Thirty days. Team Twenty-Two, and the bell.

"So if the bell goes before Orla's signed," said Kiva, "you go."

"I go. That morning."

"And if you go, Seven's three, and one of the three can't set foot on the floor." Kiva said it to see how it sounded. It sounded like a fault traced all the way to its end. "So Seven doesn't run. And Perrin and Kellan wait for the tear after that."

"Yes."

"All right," said Kiva.

Anwen waited. "That's all?"

"You've got a placement." Kiva found, somewhat to her own surprise, that she was smiling, and that the smile was real, and that it had got in ahead of everything else. "Anwen. You've got a *Graywater placement.* You've had a poster of Rhea Sorn on your door since you were twelve. I'm not going to sit here and be sorry for myself about the footnote."

Anwen's level face did something it very seldom did, which was to come apart at one corner.

"I didn't want you hearing it from anyone else," she managed. "The bell part."

"I'd have been angry if you'd hidden it." Kiva bumped her with her shoulder, the way Perrin did, though more gently. "Fourth of four. They took four this year."

"They took four," said Anwen. "Last year they said four and took three."

Kellan came up the steps while Perrin was still trying to shake Anwen's hand without using his palms. He stopped two steps below them, where he had stood on Tuesday.

"I went to see the master," he said. "I told him I'm filing nothing. I stay on Seven."

Nobody had expected it put quite so flatly. Perrin lowered his bun.

"Why?" said Kiva.

"Two reasons. I'll give you both, and you can check them." Kellan held up a finger. "I stood at the glass for Nineteen's floors this week. Three kills in under six seconds, and they never once tinned half. Nobody on that team looks at the cloth. They'd fail a field Handful with me exactly as they'd fail it without me, only quicker." A second finger. "And it would be a ruling. The master would ask, and the board would agree, and I'd go up the call twenty-odd places because someone in an office decided I was worth it. I've said for a year what I think of places that are rulings." He did not look at Kiva when he said it, and then, deliberately, he did. "I can't say it about yours and then take one."

Kiva did not know what to say to that. Nobody had ever chosen a worse place in a queue on principle and then shown her how he had got there.

"And the quench," Kellan added, more quietly. "The board has my name on a failed run. I mean to have a second go at the same thing with the same people. That's what a second go is for."

"So Anwen's got one foot out of the door," said Perrin slowly, "and Kellan's got both feet in, on principle, and I've got hands like a pair of old cabbages." He looked from one to the other. "I notice that nobody has offered *me* a transfer. I'd have turned it down, mind. I'd have been very noble about it."

"Nobody wants you, Perrin," said Anwen.

"Stonehand wants me. Stonehand gave me a bursary. I'm only saying a person likes to be asked."

Kiva laughed with the rest of them. But underneath the laughing she was working it through again, and it kept coming out the same. One of them was staying because of what she had told him on these steps. One was staying unless a bell rang. One had never been given the choice. And the whole of it rested on a signature that belonged to someone else, which she could not earn any faster by wanting it, and would not ask for.

She would tell Orla tonight where everybody stood. Orla had a right to know the weight.

She would not ask her to hurry. Mara had been quite clear about what a cell did with that.
