# Chapter 1 — Renn, Then Rowan

By the fourth hour of the afternoon, Kiva had counted everything in the holding pen that could be counted.

Forty-one candidates had started the day in her group. Thirty-eight were left. There were nine posts holding up the rope, eleven Compact stewards in grey coats, and two water buckets, one of which had a dead moth in it that nobody had removed. The Table on the south wall had filled with three hundred and six rows since the morning bells, every one of them a name, a door, and a placement, projected in pale gold onto the whitewashed stone.

She had stopped reading them around the two hundredth. They had started to blur together.

"You're doing it again," said the girl beside her.

"Doing what?"

"Moving your lips." The girl had a long brown plait and a canal-district coat with the cuffs turned back twice. She had introduced herself at noon as Anwen Pryce, and then not said much else for an hour, which Kiva had liked. "You were counting the stewards."

"Eleven," said Kiva.

"Twelve. One's behind the frame."

Kiva looked. There was a twelfth steward behind the frame, half hidden by its canvas, eating an apple.

"He doesn't count," she said. "He's not doing anything."

Anwen considered this and seemed to accept it. "That's fair."

The pen was a long rectangle of rope along the west wall of the Founders' Yard, and the candidates stood in it by family name, in the order the Table would call them. Kiva's group was the last of the day: P through Z, minus the Quills, who had been sick. Across the yard, behind a second rope, was the other pen, the one for those already Kindled. It had been filling since morning. Its people looked different, though Kiva could not have said exactly how. They stood less still. A few of them kept looking at their own hands.

One of them was waving at her.

He was a stocky boy with a round face and hair that stood up at the back, and he had been Kindled at mid-morning, in the second group. Since then he had been leaning on the rope and offering commentary to anyone in the waiting pen who would listen. Most of the candidates had stopped listening around noon. Kiva had not, because he was funny, and because the alternative was counting the moth.

"Rowan!" he called. "Still alive?"

"Still waiting," she called back.

"It's worse than it looks. It's also better. I can't explain. I've been trying for six hours." He held up his left hand and turned it, as if he were checking it for splinters. "My name's on the wall, though. Row ninety-four. Go and look."

Kiva did not need to go and look. She had read row ninety-four that morning, when it was new.

*Cade, Perrin. Still door. Reach 1. Hold short, 4. Draw fast. Stonehand Hall. Listed.*

"Congratulations," she called.

"Don't. It's a terrible reading. Four breaths. My grandmother could hold four breaths, and she's dead." He grinned, but his eyes slid to the Table and away again. "Stonehand says it'll come. They say the still door's always slow. They say a lot of things, Stonehand."

A steward told him to step back from the rope. He stepped back from the rope, waited for the steward to turn around, and stepped forward again.

Anwen watched this with the air of someone taking notes. "Is he your friend?"

"I met him this morning," said Kiva. "In the latrine line."

"That's how it starts," said Anwen darkly.

---

The bells rang the fourth hour, and the yard went quiet for the anniversary walk.

Kiva had seen it every year since she was six, standing on Mara's shoulders and later on a crate, but she had never been this close. The candidates were allowed to come forward to the rope. The crowd along the east wall pressed against the lane rails. On the Compact platform above the frame, the four heads of the Meridian Compact sat in their chairs in their four colours: the grey of the Crooked Line School, the brown of Stonehand Hall, the green of Wren House, and the plain undyed wool of the Home Line, whose chair was always a little apart from the others.

Rhea Sorn came out of the Crooked Line gate and walked to the head of the old lane.

She was not tall. Kiva had expected her to be tall. She was a compact woman of about forty with grey in her short hair, wearing a working coat like any lane warden, and she carried her Ember on her right fist as if she had forgotten it was there. It was a plain dark band of metal, like a heavy bracelet worn across the knuckles.

The crowd went silent.

"Six times at the Regional Measure," said a boy at the rope beside Kiva, under his breath. He was tall and narrow, with straight black hair cut close and a stillness about him that was different from the stillness of the other candidates, who were mostly just nervous. "Four wins. Torch at twenty-six."

"Torch is the top," said someone else.

"Torch is the top of one door," said the boy, without looking around. "That's not the same thing."

Rhea Sorn raised her right hand.

The metal of her Ember moved. It ran forward off her knuckles like water poured from a jug, and it was a blade before Kiva could follow how. A short straight blade, the length of a forearm, dull grey. Then a line of light woke along its edge, from the root to the point, white and thin and absolutely steady, and the dull metal was suddenly a thing you would not want to touch.

She walked the lane.

At the fourth pace the blade became a spear-point, long and narrow, its light drawn out to match it. At the eighth it split into two knives, one in each hand, and Kiva saw for the first time how the metal divided: it did not break, it parted, the way a stream parts around a stone. At the twelfth, both knives flowed back into the band on her fist and went dark, and the struts came.

They ran down her legs from the hip in two lines of jointed metal, light as wire, and she went from walking to flying in a single step.

It was not really flying. It was a leap, then a long low glide a foot above the packed earth, then another leap, so fast that Kiva lost her in the middle of it and found her again only at the far end of the lane, where the post was.

Rhea Sorn stopped there. The struts folded back into the band.

Then she did something Kiva had never noticed in seven years of watching. She put out her left hand and laid it flat against the crooked post, the way you might lay a hand on a horse's neck, and she stood like that for a moment with her head bowed. Then she stepped around the post's lean, not past it, and walked back down the side of the lane to the gate.

The crowd broke into noise. People stamped on the boards and called her name. The tall boy beside Kiva let out a breath he had been holding.

"Did you see the switch?" said Kiva.

He glanced at her, surprised. "What?"

"When she changed shapes. Her hand closed first. Every time, before the metal moved." She showed him, closing her own right fist slowly. "Half a breath. Maybe less."

He looked at her for a moment longer than was polite. "That's the cost," he said. "Every switch costs something. Even at Torch." He turned back to the lane. "Most people don't see it."

"Most people don't count," said Anwen, on Kiva's other side.

The boy did not answer that. When the stewards called the next name and the pen began to move again, Kiva saw that he stood one place ahead of her in the line, with Anwen just in front of him, and that the steward's list said *Renn, Kellan.*

---

Anwen went before him, at the tired end of the afternoon, when the light had begun to slant across the yard.

The station stood in the middle of the Assay frame, and the frame stood at the south end of the yard where the founders' weighbridge had been. Kiva had stood beside Mara's bench a hundred times while Mara cleaned its coils. She knew every part of it by its name and by the sound it made when it was out of true. It was a ring of brass and dark iron as tall as a cart, raised on three iron legs, with a gauge fixed to each leg facing outward so the platform could see it. Inside the ring was the station: a slab of grey stone set into the ground, worn into two shallow hollows by six centuries of feet. On the right of the slab, at the height of a hand, was a rest with an issued Ember on it, a ring of dull metal like the hub of a small wheel. On the left, on a post, was a lead case the size of a bread box with its lid open, and in the case, on a bed of felt, was a sliver of tear-glass.

Copper wires ran from the Ember's rest, from the lead case, and from a plate under the slab, up into the ring and out to the three gauges. From the gauges, a cord ran to the drum where three pens drew the trace on a roll of paper. From the drum, another cord ran to the projector lamp in its iron hood, which threw everything onto the Table.

It was the most complicated instrument in Meridian. Mara said it was also the stupidest. It could not do anything but listen.

Anwen stepped up onto the slab and put her feet in the hollows.

"Right hand to the Ember," said the reader, a Compact steward with a deep, bored voice who had been saying the same words all day. "Left palm over the glass. Don't touch it. Eyes open. Breathe when you like. Count with me."

Anwen put her right fist through the ring of the Ember. She held her left palm over the lead case, two hands' width above the glass. Her face was very calm, and Kiva thought that she was probably terrified.

"One," said the reader. "Two."

The crowd had gone quiet again, but not as quiet as for Rhea Sorn. People were tired. A child somewhere along the east wall was asking to go home.

"Nine. Ten."

At eleven, the tear-glass rang.

It was a thin high note, like a wet finger run around the rim of a cup, and Kiva felt it in her teeth. Anwen's left hand trembled. Beneath it, the air did something Kiva could see only because of the dust: a fine haze of yard grit that had been lying on the lip of the lead case lifted and slid sideways, as if it had been pushed by a hand that wasn't there.

The left gauge on the frame woke. Its needle climbed, steady, and stopped a little way up the dial.

"Rift door," said the reader. "Covering." A steward stepped forward and slid the Ember's ring off Anwen's fist, carefully, and lifted it away. Anwen let him. Her eyes were on her left hand.

"Holding load," said the reader, and on the frame a lever moved, and the left gauge's needle shivered as the frame began to press back against whatever Anwen's door was doing. "Hold it, candidate. Count."

Anwen held it. The dust over the lead case stayed pressed flat against nothing, a small grey shelf in the air. Kiva could see the effort in her shoulders and the line of her jaw.

"Seven. Eight. Nine."

On the tenth count the shelf of dust collapsed and drifted down. The needle dropped.

The projector lamp clicked, and a new row of pale gold wrote itself onto the Table. A woman in a dark green-trimmed coat stood up from a desk on the platform, read it aloud, and signed a sheet in front of her with a short, decided stroke.

"Pryce, Anwen," she said. "Rift door. Reach two. Hold steady, nine. Draw even. Wren House. Listed."

Kiva knew who she was. Everyone knew who she was. Ysra Crest had been the senior assessor of the Meridian Compact for eleven years, and no placement in Meridian was real until she had signed it.

Anwen stepped down from the slab. A Wren House steward in green came to meet her at the frame's edge with a ribbon, and she let him pin it to her coat. On her way to the far pen she passed close to the rope, and Kiva saw her face, which was not calm anymore.

"Steady nine," said Kiva. "That's good. That's really good."

"Wren House." Anwen said it the way you might say *rain.* "Wren House is medicine and walls."

"You wanted something else?"

"Field." Anwen looked back at the Table, at her own row in pale gold. "The north rotation takes one first-year a season. One. From any school. It goes out with the Home Line." She touched the green ribbon, not quite pulling at it. "Wren House never sends anybody. They say healers are too expensive to lose."

The steward was waving her on. She went. Kiva watched her go to the far pen, where Perrin Cade immediately said something that made her shake her head and almost laugh.

"Renn," said the reader.

---

Kellan Renn walked up to the station as if he had walked up to it before.

Perhaps he had, Kiva thought. There were families in Meridian who paid for that: private stillness tutors, practice slabs in back rooms, a decade of breathing exercises before a child was old enough to spell *Kindling.* Mara called it buying a head start on a road that went to the same place. Watching Kellan put his feet into the hollows of the stone, Kiva wasn't sure that was fair. He did not look like somebody who had bought anything. He looked like somebody who had practised.

He put his right fist through the Ember. He held his left palm over the glass. He breathed out, slowly, once.

"One," said the reader.

The Ember moved on the first count.

The crowd made a sound. The dull ring flowed over his knuckles and forward, and it was a blade almost at once: short, straight and narrow, clean as a ruler, with none of the lumps and false starts Kiva had seen on two hundred other fists that day. Then, before the reader had said *two,* a line of light woke along its edge.

It was pale blue-white and very thin. It shook once, and then it was still.

"Ember door," said the reader, sounding for the first time all day as if he were awake. "Covering." A steward closed the lead case over the glass. Another slid a felt mat under Kellan's feet, between his boots and the stone. Kellan did not look at either of them.

"Holding load," said the reader. "Hold it, candidate."

The frame's lever moved. The right gauge's needle, which had climbed and settled two marks up the dial, began to shiver. Kellan held the blade out in front of him with its edge lit and did not move.

"Ten," said the reader. "Eleven. Twelve."

Kiva found that she was holding her breath, and made herself stop. Beside her, a boy whose name started with S was whispering the count along with the reader. The whole pen was. The whole yard, almost.

"Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one."

On twenty-two the edge-light flickered and went out, and the blade sank back into the ring.

The Table clicked. Ysra Crest stood up.

"Renn, Kellan," she said. "Ember door. Reach two. Hold long, twenty-one. Draw even." She paused, and for the first time that day Kiva saw her look up from the sheet at the person she was ruling on. "Crooked Line School. Entered."

The crowd did not cheer exactly. It made a low surprised noise, and then people began to clap.

*Entered.* Kiva had read every row on the Table that day, and none of them had said *entered.* A first-year was listed, to train, and entered for the Meridian Circuit only after a season of work had proved them. A long hold at Kindling could skip the season. The last one had been eleven years ago.

Kellan stepped down. A Crooked Line steward in grey pinned a ribbon to his collar, and then a second steward, a woman with a burn scar over one cheek, came forward and shook his hand. He did not smile. He nodded, as if a piece of work had come out as he had measured it, and he walked toward the far pen.

As he passed the rope he looked straight at Kiva.

"You were right," he said. "About the hand." Then he was gone.

"Rowan," said the reader.

---

The stone was cold through her boot soles. The hollows were smoother than they looked, worn into the shape of every foot that had stood there, and her feet did not fit them. They were too short. She stood with her heels on the lip.

Mara was at the base of the frame's south leg, crouched with her toolbag open, where the repairer on call always waited. She looked up at Kiva once and nodded, the way she nodded across the bench when a reading was good. Then she looked back at her gauges.

The disk lay cold against Kiva's breastbone.

"Right hand to the Ember," said the reader. "Left palm over the glass. Don't touch it. Eyes open. Breathe when you like. Count with me."

She put her right fist through the ring. It was heavier than she had expected and slightly warm from Kellan's hand. She held her left palm over the open lead case, two hands above the sliver of glass, and she looked at the glass, which was cloudy and grey and very ordinary.

"One," said the reader.

Kiva breathed out. She tried to do what Kellan had done: one slow breath, nothing else. She thought about the card in Mara's box with the remarks column and the three words on it. *Left coil rebuilt. Watch.*

"Two. Three."

Nothing happened. The yard was very quiet. Far away the child on the east wall was still asking to go home.

"Six. Seven."

She thought that perhaps nothing would happen at all. It did, sometimes. There were one or two rows on the Table every year that said *Unopened*, and those children went home and came back the next season to try again, and some of them never opened at all. Kiva had always felt sorry for them. Now, standing on the cold stone with her heels on the lip of somebody else's footprints, she understood for the first time that it might be her.

"Ten."

It started in her chest.

It was not pain. It was heat, low and sudden, as if someone had set a coal against the inside of her breastbone. She heard herself make a small sound. The heat was not in one place. It was in three, side by side, very close, like three fingers laid against her from inside.

Then the glass rang.

It was not Anwen's thin note. It was louder and lower, and it did not stop. Kiva felt it in her teeth and her jaw and the bones behind her ears. Under her left palm, the air went hard. It happened all at once, the way stepping on a stair that isn't there happens all at once, and she felt the hardness push up against her hand and keep pushing. The dust on the lip of the case did not slide. It flew. It went out sideways in a grey ring, and the steward nearest the lead case stepped back with his hand over his eyes.

On her right fist, the Ember moved.

It did not flow like Kellan's. It lurched. The dull metal heaved forward off her knuckles and stopped, heaved again, and became something short and thick and heavy, a blunt bar as long as her hand with a hook at the end of it, like a tool for lifting drain covers. It was ugly. It was dark. It did not light.

And her feet were gone.

That was what it felt like. She could not feel the stone. She could not feel her boots. Below her knees there was only a deep, dull, enormous weight, as if her legs went down through the slab and into the ground and kept going, and nothing in the world could move them.

"Ember door," said the reader. Then: "Rift door." Then, in a different voice: "Still—"

He did not finish.

All three gauges on the frame were moving. Kiva could see the nearest one, the left one, on the leg beside the lead case. Its needle had gone up the dial in a jerk, fallen back, gone up again. The others were doing the same. On the south wall, the projector lamp clicked and clicked, and the Table, which had shown one door for every row all day, began to write three bars side by side in pale gold, and then to rub them out, and to write them again at different heights.

"Covering," said the reader loudly. "Covering. Cover the glass."

A steward lunged and slammed the lead lid down over the tear-glass.

The note did not stop. It went on ringing from inside the closed case, muffled now, a deep hum like a bell heard through a wall. The hardness under Kiva's palm did not stop either. She could feel it pressing against the lid of the case, and the lid was rattling on its hinge.

"Ember off her," said the reader. "Get the Ember off."

A second steward took hold of the ring on her fist and pulled. The ring did not move. It was not stuck; it was simply part of her hand now, the way her knuckles were part of her hand. He pulled harder, and the blunt hooked thing on her fist swung with the pull, and he let go before it hit him.

"Off the slab. Take her off the slab—"

Two of them took her by the arms.

Kiva felt their hands. She felt them pull. She felt their boots slide on the stone and their breath go out of them in a grunt, and she felt herself not move at all, not even a little, not even the way a tree moves in a wind. They might as well have been pulling at the frame.

The heat in her chest had become three lines.

She could feel exactly where they ran. They started together at the notch at the top of her breastbone, where the disk's cord lay, and they went up and outward toward her left shoulder, not quite side by side, three thin burning strokes. They were getting hotter.

On the frame the right gauge's needle swung up the dial and hit the pin at the top with a sharp metal click, and stayed there.

Mara's voice came from the foot of the south leg, very clear.

"It's reading all three through the left coil. It's cross-feeding. Cut the load."

"The load stays on until the assessor—"

"Cut the load or it'll burn." Mara was on her feet. Kiva saw her hand go into the frame's base, under the gauge, to the place where the fuse plug was. She had shown Kiva that plug on the bench a dozen times. *You never pull it while it's running. Unless you have to.* "I'm pulling it."

"Leave it," said a voice from the platform.

It was a woman's voice, low and level. It did not seem loud. It carried anyway.

"Pull it, Rowan. Do it now." Then, in exactly the same tone: "Stewards, let go of her. Don't touch the candidate. Stand back from the ring."

They let go. The pressure on Kiva's arms vanished.

Mara pulled the fuse.

There was a crack from inside the frame, and a smell like singed hair. The projector lamp died. The Table on the south wall went white and empty and then simply pale stone again, every row of gold since the morning bells winking out at once. The gauges' needles fell, all three, and lay still against their stops. The frame stopped pressing.

Nothing in Kiva stopped at all.

The bar on her fist stayed. The hardness under her left palm stayed, rattling the lid of the closed case. Her legs stayed rooted in whatever was under the stone. And the three lines in her chest stayed hot, and she could not breathe properly around them, and she understood that she did not know how to make any of it stop.

She had wanted a door. She had not known it would be like this, a thing that happened to her instead of a thing she did.

Someone was standing at the edge of the ring.

It was a woman in a grey Crooked Line coat, perhaps fifty, tall and lean, with iron-coloured hair cut very short and a long face that looked as if it were used to being patient with things that were not going well. She did not come into the ring. She stood with her hands at her sides and her weight in her heels, and she looked at Kiva as if there were nobody else in the yard.

"Kiva," she said. She knew her name. Of course she did; it was on the list. "My name's Orla Dane. Look at me. Not at the gauges. At me."

Kiva looked at her.

"Good. You're not hurt. You're very hot, and it's frightening, but you're not hurt." Her voice was the voice from the platform: unhurried, level. "Now. Don't try to stop anything."

"I can't—" Kiva's voice came out thin. "I can't stop it."

"I know. So don't try. Trying is pushing, and pushing is feeding it." Orla did not move her hands. "Breathe out. All the way out, until there's nothing left. Then wait."

Kiva breathed out.

It was hard. The lines in her chest pulled when she did it, and her ribs did not want to empty. She did it anyway, all the way, until her lungs ached.

"Wait," said Orla. "Don't breathe in yet. Wait."

She waited. The yard was silent. She could hear the hum from inside the lead case, deep and steady, and under it, very faintly, her own heart.

"Now in. Slowly. Don't hold it. Let it go out again."

Kiva breathed in. She let it go.

The hum in the lead case wavered.

"Again," said Orla.

On the fourth breath, the hardness under her palm loosened, the way a fist loosens when you forget you were making one. The lid of the case stopped rattling and settled with a small click.

On the seventh, she felt her feet again. They were cold. The stone was cold. She was standing on it with her heels over the lip of somebody else's footprints, and her knees were shaking.

On the eleventh, the bar on her fist lurched backward and became a dull ring again, and slid off her knuckles, and fell. It hit the stone with a flat iron sound and rolled once against her boot.

Kiva sat down. She did not decide to. Her legs simply stopped holding her, and she sat on the edge of the slab with her hands open in her lap, staring at them.

They looked like her hands.

"There," said Orla Dane quietly. "That's all. That's all it is."

---

Afterward, Kiva remembered it in pieces.

She remembered Mara's hands on her face, very cold and very steady, turning her head to look into her eyes. She remembered the smell of the burnt fuse. She remembered a Wren House healer in green kneeling beside her and unbuttoning the top of her shirt without asking, and the healer's breath catching, and the healer saying "Oh," in a small professional voice that frightened Kiva more than anything else had.

She looked down.

Three thin lines ran from the notch at the top of her breastbone toward her left shoulder. They were dark red, like a scald, and not quite parallel; the lowest was the longest and curved slightly toward her arm. They were each about as wide as a thread. Where the disk's braided cord crossed them, the skin was pale. The disk itself lay on her breastbone, a little to the right of the lines, near-black and grooved and exactly as cold as it had been that morning.

She put her hand over it without thinking.

Cold. It had done nothing. Whatever had happened, it had not come from the disk, and she felt a strange small relief at that, as if it would have been worse to be told that she had been borrowed.

On the platform above the frame, Ysra Crest stood with her pen in her hand and the ruling sheet in front of her. The Table behind her was blank stone. She looked down at Kiva for a long moment. Then she looked at the four Compact heads in their four colours, who were all looking at her.

She put her pen down.

"Rowan, Kiva," she said, loudly enough for the yard. "Unread. I will not rule on an instrument that cannot read. Held for review." She paused. "Wren House will take her tonight."

There was no ribbon. Nobody came forward to pin one.

The stewards were already resetting the frame's fuse with a spare from Mara's bag, because there were eleven candidates still in the pen behind her, and the day had to finish. Kiva let the healer help her up. As she stepped down from the slab, she looked across the yard at the far pen without meaning to, and saw three faces she knew turned toward her among the hundreds.

Anwen, with her green ribbon, very still.

Perrin Cade, with his mouth open, for once not saying anything.

And Kellan Renn, his grey ribbon at his collar, looking not at her but at the blank south wall, where the best reading in eleven years had been written in pale gold an hour ago and was not written anymore.
