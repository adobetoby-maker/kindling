# Chapter 28 — Kiva: The Cheap One

On Thursday she sat with her back to the drum for an hour, and by the end of it she could not have told the pen from the lamp.

It was the dullest hour she had ever spent in the Old Yard. Noa ran the tape empty, and Kiva sat on the spare crate six paces off with her hands in her lap, and Orla read a newspaper. At first every scratch of the nib went up her spine. Then it was only a sound, like the hiss of the wick, and somewhere near the end of the hour she found that she was thinking of how a stiff new sheet might be got to lie flat, and had been for some time.

"That'll do," Orla had said. "You've stopped sitting up for it."

On Friday night she stood on the scraped line with the thread cold and the step behind her, and called it to four people and a lane of posts.

"Rowan. Ember, to still."

The struts closed. She left the line.

There was the nib, running up its paper. It ran, and it was a clock.

She shut the Ember at the top, and this time she did not wait to learn whether anybody had heard. The struts let go together. The metal went home along her ribs like water into a drain, and it arrived, and she had nothing.

She had forgotten how much nothing it was.

On the shelf there had been a wall coming, and copper under her, and no room to notice. Here she noticed. For as long as it takes to say a short word she was a girl in the air with no doors, and all the speed of the bound in her body, and she felt the ash go. It went out of her all at once, not in a trickle, a quarter of a dram, like the breath out of somebody who has been thumped between the shoulders, and it went nowhere that she could follow.

*That's the price,* she thought. *That's what he drew.*

She set the pan.

She set it under herself and ahead, wide and waiting, on a patch of dirt with two scratches in it.

Her right foot came down on the cross.

There was no jar. The yard took her as a great hand takes a small weight, and the bound went in at her heel and down, through the packed earth and whatever five hundred years of feet had made of it. The left knee never knew.

She did not put the lid on.

Orla's ferrule came against her left shoulder. Orla leaned on it. She was a heavy woman and she leaned as she would have leaned on a door, and it went through Kiva like a rumor and into the ground.

"Hold it."

She held it. There was a small bright thing rising in her chest. She knew what that was as well. She named it under her breath, as she would have named a door, and it sat down.

"Give it back," said Orla.

Kiva gave the ground its weight, slowly, as someone sets down a full pan, and shut the still door behind it with nothing left in the doorway.

Then she stood on two scratches in the dirt, in her ordinary boots, and shook.

"Hand."

She held it out. The fingers were pink. The first two were cold, and no colder than they had been at breakfast.

"Thread?"

"Cold. It never warmed."

"No. I was watching your collar." Orla took her stick back under her own weight. She did not smile. She looked at the cross, and at the boots on it, and at the line four paces off. "I've sat on that crate since the seventh of last month waiting to see a foot come down there. I'd begun to think the mark was badly placed."

"Is it a crossing?"

"It's a crossing." The crooked face was perfectly grave. "I saw a shelf from the far side of a pit, over a rail, and I've wondered every day since whether the place was being kind to you. It seems not. Now show me it wasn't the yard." She turned. "Mr. Bexley."

Noa read it with his thumbs on the tape. Two hundredths for the bound. Twenty-five in less than a fifth of a second, straight up, with the same small blot where the nib had jarred. Three for the stance.

"Thirty," he said. His voice cracked on it. "A dram and sixty-seven left."

"I knew what it would cost," said Kellan. He had gone rather pale. "I proposed it. I hadn't watched it go."

"I mind," said Perrin, from the other crate.

"We know."

"I'm saying it anyway. That's near half a Kennel floor, gone in the time it takes me to sneeze." He was grinning from ear to ear. "And she came down like a cat on a cushion, and I held a capstan with my ribs for less. Tell Anwen I'm furious."

Kiva's hand had gone to her collar. She had not sent it. The disk was there under the cloth, round and plain, and it was as cold as the inside of a well.

It had been warm once. It had been warm for the shelf, for the length of a road stair, and she had not asked it why. It did not seem that tonight was to be told apart from a wet Tuesday.

She found that she had expected that. She let go of the cord, and it was not until she was on the tram home that she noticed she had not been disappointed, and wondered a little at how much quieter a thing was when nobody was waiting on its opinion.

---

The third team went to Stonehand on Saturday and lost by two.

She was let play. Aske had said lanes, and Orla had said one door and no cleverness, and so for three carries Kiva stood on the Long Terrace with her still door declared and her heel on a riser and did nothing whatever that would have interested a gallery. Idony Keld feinted at her knee all afternoon. Pru leaned a plank from the porch to the sill and talked to it. The litter went the wrong way twice.

It finished six to four against them. Nobody fell over in an interesting manner, and afterward, in the passage under the terrace, Lowen Brask was waiting with his coat on and a canvas roll at his feet.

"I go out on Monday," he said. "A Home Line hold crew, till the autumn. Vane asked, and I've said yes. So that's the last you'll see of me in a lane this season, and I thought I'd say so, since I've had the better of you twice now and you've had me once."

"Once was enough."

"For you. I've been turning it over ever since." He was a square, quiet young man, and he looked at her as he had looked at her from a gallery, with his arms folded. "Cade tells me you're doing it on a mast. He tells everybody. He minds it very loudly."

"He does."

"Is it still the right that opens late?"

"It opens before, now. That's all it ever wanted. Somebody told me to arrive before I was there."

"Then somebody taught you my trade behind my back." He nearly smiled. "I'll give you one more for nothing, since I won't be here to take it off you in a lane. I've had the junior lanes three springs. Every spring there's one who finds a landing easy, a week before the others. I used to think that was talent." He picked up the roll. "It's nearly always a child standing on two things at once and not knowing it. A step and a rail. A foot and a friend. So when it comes easy, look at your feet."

"It doesn't come easy."

"Not yet," said Lowen Brask, and went up the passage toward the light.

On the tram home she decided that it was the kind of thing said by somebody who liked to win, and put it away.

---

Monday's go was the best that she had ever made.

She knew it in the air. The new week's ration was in her, and four-tenths over, and the step had been clean, and the thread was cold when she said so. She had slept. She had not listened for the pen since Thursday.

"Rowan. Ember, to still."

She left the line as she might have left a curb.

It was the third time in a week that she had gone up over that strip of dirt, and something in her had begun to know the road. On Wednesday she had fought the top of it. On Friday she had gone through it like a diver through cold water, with her teeth shut. Tonight she came to the top, and the Ember was closing, and she found that there was no need to wait in the dark for it to finish.

She could feel exactly where the ground would be. She could feel the pan wanting to be set. And the metal was nearly home, all but the last of it, a thread along the ribs, and it seemed the most natural thing in the world to begin the one as the other ended. It was how one hand gives a cup to the other. She did not set anything down.

There was no nothing.

There was no girl in the air without a door. The breath was not knocked out of her. One thing ran into the next as smoothly as a seam, and the cross came up, and her foot came down on it.

It was soft. It was softer than Friday. She stood on the cross and the whole yard stood under her, and when Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder it went through her so easily that she could have laughed.

"Hold."

She held. She could have held all night.

"Give it back."

She gave it back, and shut the door, and this time she was not shaking. She stood with her hands at her sides, breathing as if she had walked up a flight of stairs.

"Well," said Orla slowly. The stick came back to the ground. "I felt nothing wrong in that. I'd my whole weight on you and there was nothing there to find." She was frowning a little, as a person frowns at a sum that has come out too neatly. "Hand."

Pink. Both fingers. Not even cold.

"Thread?"

Kiva laid two fingers on her breastbone.

She had known before they touched. She had known in the air, and had put it by, because the cross was coming and the cross was what she was there for.

It was warm. It was only a little warm, like a cup an hour after the tea, and it was going as she felt it.

"A little," she said. "It's nearly gone."

Orla did not move.

"When?"

"In the air. At the top." Her mouth had gone dry. "It wasn't a jam. I'd know a jam; I've had enough of them. Nothing fought. I thought it was only that I was pleased."

"You were pleased before you'd landed?"

"I was—it was easy. It was the first one that's ever been easy."

"And your thread told you, and you didn't tell me."

There was nothing to say to that. On the spare crate, Perrin had stopped smiling. He was looking at his own hands.

"I never called it," he said. "I had nothing to call. It looked better than Friday's."

"It did," said Orla. "It looked better to me, and I've a stick. Mr. Bexley. She's finished. I'd be obliged if you'd read it out before anybody in this lane says another word, myself included."

Noa had the tape already. He had had it for some moments, and he was holding it very close to the lamp on the mast's leg, and his face was not that of someone with good news.

"The bound," he said. "Two hundredths. The stance, three." He stopped.

"And between?" said Kellan.

"There isn't a between." Noa turned the tape so that they could see. "There's no cliff. Look. On Friday it goes straight up; you could cut yourself on it. Tonight there's a hump. It's about a third of a second across, and it's got a round top." He swallowed. "The whole go cost a tenth. A tenth and a hair."

For the space of a breath, nobody understood him.

Then Kellan made a sound.

"A *tenth,*" he said. "That's a third of Friday. That's a Draw of four, on the same work. Kiva, at that price the two drams would—" He was working it in his head; she could see it moving behind his eyes, and they were alight. "It's the gap that costs. You've found a way to close the gap."

"I didn't find it. It was just there."

"Then find it again. That's the best figure anybody's—"

"No," said Noa.

It came out too loud. He went red to the hair, and he did not take it back.

"No. I'm sorry. It doesn't stand, and I'd rather say it now than let him get to the end of that sum." His hands were shaking, and he laid the tape down on the drum case and smoothed it flat. "I know this shape. I've drawn it before. It's on the Low Lane's tape for the thirteenth of last month, the try where she called *off* inside the breath and stood with her hands on her knees. Most of a tenth, in less than a second. I said the pen went up like a mouse up a curtain. It had a round top."

"That was a jam," said Kiva.

"That was two doors drawing at once, and fighting. This is two doors drawing at once, and not." He looked at her, and she saw what it was costing him. "A crossing's a cliff because there's nothing open when it happens. That's what's so strange about it. That's the whole of my paper, if I ever write one. This isn't strange at all. It's a hump because for a third of a second there were two of them open together, and each of them was paying for itself in the ordinary way." His voice dropped. "I've wanted a Draw of four out of you since the spring. I'd have given a year for it. It isn't one. It's the other thing with good manners, and it's on a sanctioned tape, and it goes to the Chair in the morning."

The mast ticked.

Kiva stood where she was. She could feel her own face, hot and stiff.

"I landed it," she said. "On the cross. She leaned on me with her whole weight and there was nothing to find. She said so."

"She did," said Orla.

"If it holds, and it's soft, and it costs a third—"

"Then it's what I put on a floor at the Regional, once, with a boy." Orla had not raised her voice. "Sit down, Rowan. Not because you're in disgrace. Because I'm about to tell you what you did, and I'd as soon you weren't standing on it."

Kiva sat on the cross. It was the nearest place.

"There are two ways to get from one door to another," said Orla. "I told you in the spring that I'd never seen the right one. I'd seen the wrong one a hundred times, in a dozen coats. You open the second before the first is shut. Do it badly and they fight, and you get a line on your chest and a post in your face, and that's a jam, and nobody mistakes it for anything. Do it well—" She stopped, and began again. "I never saw it done well. I didn't know it could be. I'd have told you an hour ago that a jam always throws."

"It didn't throw."

"No. It set you down like an egg. That's why it's worse." The stick came round and pointed, not at Kiva, but at the brass head on its three legs. "For a third of a second you were two things at once. In a dirt yard, with nothing near you but a lane of posts, that's a soft landing at a third of the price. Now put a charged cell three paces off. Put a sheet of open ash. You know what a door does to open ash; you've knelt beside enough of it. What do two do, together, in one body, with a seam between them that you can't feel?"

Kiva did not answer. She was thinking of a drum under her left palm, and a needle that had gone on climbing after she took her hand away, and what had come up a chute to look for it.

"The Cellhouse," she said.

"That was three, and you were frightened, and the line lit on your chest where nobody could miss it. This was two, and you were happy, and your own sponsor would have put her name to it." Orla breathed out through her nose. "The gap's the price because the gap's the point. For the length of a word there's nothing of yours open for anything to get into. That's what you're buying with a quarter of a dram. It isn't waste. It's the wall between the rooms."

"A crossing," said Kellan quietly. He had stopped working the sum. He was looking at the tape under Noa's hands as if it had been a stamp that said *First* on a tin of soot. "And that was a stack."

"That's as good a word as any."

"I'd have sworn to it. I'd have taken it to the board." He let out a long breath. "My mother put her cups down once, at her bench, over a figure that was false, and kept them down till the Works came to hear her. I believed this one for about as long as it takes to sharpen a pencil. I was wrong faster than anybody here."

"You were wrong second," said Orla. "I was first, and I'd the stick." She turned to Noa, and for a moment she only looked at him: a narrow boy of seventeen with a strip of paper, who had just told a Torch of thirty years' standing that she could not tell a crossing from a stack. "Mr. Bexley. I'd have put my name to that landing. I'd have called it a crossing and gone home pleased with the pair of us. You and your pen have kept me from the second great mistake of my life, and you did it against a figure you wanted. I'll thank you to do it again, whenever I need it."

Noa could not find anything to say. He nodded, and went on smoothing the tape.

"Crest hears this tomorrow, from me, as it was," said Orla. "A stack, a third of a second, landed, nobody hurt. Called a miss by her reader before her sponsor had seen it. Thread warmed in the air; the candidate didn't call. I'll not soften the last, and you'll not ask me to."

"No."

"Then tell me why."

Kiva looked at her hands. She thought of Lowen Brask in a passage under a terrace, with a canvas roll at his feet, and of what he had said there, which she had put away on a tram as the kind of thing a boy says who likes to win.

"It was easy," she said. "And I was pleased. And I didn't look at my feet."

---

A boy in blue came down from the Brickfields road on Tuesday with a message, and he stood in the drying yard and said it as though he had been made to learn it on the way and would lose it if anybody interrupted.

"Sorn says. Finch: a corner and a lamp. Not a wall. Saturday the twenty-third, six o'clock, the hall. Night van. Bring your sheet wet." He took a breath. "That's all she said, miss."

He went before anybody could ask him anything.

"What's a lamp?" said Pru.

"I think it's what a ward is when it isn't asked to hold anything," said Kellan.

"I can do that. I do that most of the time." Pru looked at the gate he had gone out by. "*Not a wall.* She sent a boy two miles to tell me what I'm not. I've never had that from a Torch before. And why's the sheet got to be wet?"

Nobody knew. It was Mrs. Pryce who told them, from the scullery door, with her arms floured to the elbow.

"Because it's new, and it's waxed, and it'll stand up in ridges like a tent the first time you throw it down in a hurry. Anybody who's ever hung a tarpaulin could tell you. You want it trodden." She nodded at the long stone trough under the pump. "In there, with cold water, for an hour. Then it'll lie where it's put. Boots off."

So they trod it.

It took the three of them who had whole ribs, in the trough, in the last of the light, with their trousers rolled, marching up and down on six paces of waxed canvas like people pressing grapes. Perrin sat on the mounting block and called left and right at them like a sergeant. The water was so cold that it hurt, and Kiva's left knee ached with a dull, honest ache that had nothing to do with landings.

"This is the sort of thing they don't put on the poster," said Pru. "You see a woman with a white streak in her hair and a blade as long as a street. Nobody shows you her feet."

"Tread," said Perrin. "Left. Left. Renn, you're out."

"I've been asked to a practice on Thursday," said Kellan.

He said it to the canvas. He went on treading.

"The Halden team. They train twice a week at the refinery yard, with a chartered foreman, on a contained string. Every week. They'd like to see me cut behind their ward line." His jaw was set. "I've said I'll go and watch. I haven't said anything else. I'm telling you because I said I'd tell you when the sum moved, and it's moved."

Nobody broke step.

"A contained string," said Perrin, after a moment. "Every week."

"Yes."

"We get Rhea Sorn for one night in a fortnight, and a trough." Perrin considered it. "I'll mind that for you, if you'd like. I've room."

"I wouldn't, particularly. Thank you."

"Then tread. Left."

Kiva said nothing. She trod. The canvas was beginning to give under her heels, a little, like stiff dough.

"Can I ask something?" said Pru, some while later. "Since we're all in a trough. I know there's a dream, and a woman in bronze, and a house on a hill that wants you for a year. I've decided to know it badly. I think that's safest. But I'd like to know why you said yes to me."

"Anwen said you."

"I know what Pryce said. She said it at me like a sentence from the bench; my ears rang. I'm asking you."

Kiva trod six paces to the end, and turned.

"I had somebody else," she said. "I had reasons. I asked Anwen, and she said you, and she was right, and I wouldn't have thought of you by myself." It was not flattering, and she let it stand. "Why did you say yes?"

"Because I got my first step by calling *off* twice." Pru's round, anxious face was pink with cold. "Once at a husk and once at a spaniel. The officer passed me. He said he'd sooner have a ward who ran from a dog than one who wouldn't run from anything. I've thought about that every day since." She trod. "I'd like to find out whether I can stay. Only once. Then I'll know which sort I am, and I can stop narrating it."

"You won't stop," said Perrin.

"No. But I'd know."

They wrung the sheet between four of them and hung it over two lines, where it dripped. It did not stand up in ridges. It hung like a sheet.

---

On Wednesday she chose the dear one.

That was the whole of the work, and it was harder than landing. She stood on the line with the thread cold and the step clean behind her, and she knew now that there were two roads over that strip of dirt. One of them was soft and cost a third and felt like a hand passing a cup. She had been down it once. Her body had liked it very much.

"Rowan. Ember, to still."

Up.

It was there at the top, waiting for her, like a known step on a dark stair. The metal was closing. The pan wanted setting. It would have been so little to let the one begin.

She shut the Ember, and made herself wait for it.

She waited until the last thread of metal had run home along her ribs and had arrived, and she knew that it had arrived, and there was nothing of hers open anywhere. It took no longer than a word. It was the longest thing she had ever done on purpose.

The ash went. A quarter of a dram, all at once, out of her like a breath, and she was nobody at all, in the air, over a yard, and she let herself be it.

Then the pan.

The cross. No jar. The whole of the Old Yard under one heel.

Orla's ferrule, and Orla's weight. "Hold."

She held.

"Give it back."

She gave it back, and shut the door.

"Thread?"

"Cold. All the way."

"Hand."

She held it out, and she knew before Orla took it. She could see it in the lamplight. The last two fingers were her own. The first two were the color of candle wax, to the second joint.

Orla did not say anything. She held the hand in both of hers, as it had not been held since Aske's cart at the Pit's west gate, and she watched the fingertips. Nobody else in the lane made a sound.

Kiva could not feel them. She watched them lying in the old hard palm as if they had been two things that somebody had put there.

The lamp hissed on its hook. Behind her the mast ticked on with nothing left to draw. Perrin had half risen off the spare crate and did not seem to know it. Then a faint flush came along the nail of the first finger. It came up slowly from the root, like tea into a lump of sugar, and after it, a long way off, she felt something that belonged to her.

"There," said Orla. She let out a breath that she had been holding rather longer than was good for anybody. "That's inside your minute, and I don't care for how near the edge of it you were." She let go. "That was a crossing, and it was the dear one, and you took it with the cheap one lying there in plain sight. I saw you choose."

"And we're done for the night."

"We're done for the week. I said one go in a night, and I begin to think I was generous." She took up her stick. "You'll show that hand to Aske tomorrow and tell him how long. If he says there's to be no go until he's seen it after a rest, then there's none, and I'll not be argued with by a girl who's just had two fingers come back from wherever they went."

"Thirty-one," said Noa, very quietly, from the mast. "Two, and twenty-six, and three."

"A dram and a quarter left," said Kellan. He had worked it before the tape was read. "Four more at that price, and a hundredth over. There isn't a miss left in it. Not a drop. Not a stack." He looked up. "I said it came out about right. I'd no idea how right."

"I mind it more now it's working," said Perrin. "I thought I ought to say."

Kiva was flexing her left hand. The fingers closed. They did it late, and they told her nothing whatever about the palm they closed on.

Mara was at the kitchen table when she came in, with the lamp trimmed and a Works can at her elbow that she had not touched. She did not ask how it had gone. She held out her own left hand across the oilcloth, as she had every night since the spring, and Kiva put hers into it.

Mara pressed the first finger with her reading thumb, and then the second.

"Well?"

"Late," said Kiva. "Later than last night. A long way off."

Mara kept her thumb on the first finger a moment longer than she needed to. Then she let go, and got up, and put the kettle on, and stood at the stove with her back to the room until it boiled.
