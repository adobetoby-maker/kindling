# Chapter 5 — Kiva: The Cellhouse

The board posted the teams on Thursday afternoon, and Perrin did not speak to her for an hour.

Kiva found out why when she read it. The list was pinned up on the Cellhouse gate under a sheet of glass, forty teams of four, and most of them were the same as the draw from Classification Day. Hers was not.

*Team Seven. Afternoon, second run.*
*K. Renn — Ember.*
*A. Pryce — Hand.*
*P. Cade — Still.*
*K. Rowan — Unclassified.*

Under it, in small print, was a note in Ysra Crest's hand. *Composition by board ruling. Each door represented beside the unclassified candidate.*

"They moved me," said Perrin, when he finally spoke. They were sitting on the Cellhouse steps with the late sun on them. "I was morning. I was with three Stonehand people I've trained with for two years, and I knew exactly what we'd do. Now I'm afternoon, with the best Ember-door in the Eastbank, a girl who wants a Graywater placement more than she wants to breathe, and you." He kept his eyes on the street. "They moved me so there'd be a still-door standing next to you. In case you need one."

"I didn't ask them to."

"I know you didn't. That's not the point." He picked at a splinter on the step. "The point is my bursary's riding on a team the board built around somebody else. I don't get to be on my own team. I get to be on yours."

Kiva did not know what to say to that, because it was true.

"I'll do my job," said Perrin. "Whatever it is. I'll do it properly. I'm telling you I'm angry about it now, on a step, so it doesn't come out sideways in the middle of the run." He stood up and brushed off his trousers. "I had a joke ready instead. It wasn't good enough."

"It wasn't," said Anwen, from the step above. "I heard it this morning."

---

On Friday morning, Mara walked her to the tram.

She did not usually, because she had the shop to open and the municipal van coming at nine. But she put on her coat and walked down Coper Street beside Kiva, slowly, because of the knee, and stood with her at the stop under the anniversary banner.

The knee was better, though it was not good. Kiva had iced it for two nights and the swelling had gone down, and she could walk on it without limping if she thought about it. Running would hurt, and landing hard would hurt a great deal. She had written that in the book too.

*What it cost:* Knee. Don't land on it.

"You've got your book," said Mara.

"I've got it."

"Your dram?"

"They issue it at the gate."

"Right." Mara watched the tram coming over the bridge with its rings glinting. She put her hands in her coat pockets and took them out again. "I'm not coming to watch."

"I know."

"I'd want to stop it. Every time something went wrong. I'd be on my feet in the gallery shouting about quench handles." She almost smiled. "Orla will be there. She'll tell me."

The tram pulled in, and the doors folded open.

Mara put her hand flat on Kiva's chest for a second, over the coat, over the disk, the way she had in the shop four years ago. Then she took it away.

"Say it out loud," she said.

"I will."

Kiva got on the tram and stood without holding the rail. When she looked back, Mara was still standing at the stop in her work coat with her scarred hand at her side, watching the tram go, and she did not wave, and she did not leave.

---

The Cellhouse had been an ash refinery once, two hundred years ago, before the Halden yards on the east bank made every other refinery in the region unnecessary. Now it belonged to the Compact. From outside it was a long, tall brick building with a sawtooth roof and a row of high windows, and inside it was the most watched room in the Collegiate Circuit.

The candidates' waiting room was a long whitewashed corridor along the north side, and through a thick glass window at the end of it you could see straight down into the main hall. Kiva stood at the window with Anwen and Perrin and studied it while the first afternoon team ran.

It was a big room, about forty paces long and fourteen wide and two stories high, with steel trusses across the roof and the old refinery's crane rails still running along the walls. The floor was concrete, painted with faded yellow lanes, and there were props scattered across it to make it look like a real street after something had gone wrong: a toppled cable reel, some stacked crates, a section of broken railing.

At the west end, nearest the waiting room, was the Gate, a big roll-up steel door where the teams came in. Just inside it, a stripe of green paint crossed the floor from wall to wall. That was the green line, and once the casualty was over it, the extraction was done.

At the east end, forty paces away, was the substation.

It was a raised steel platform, a mezzanine, two and a half meters off the floor, and a narrow steel stair of twelve steps ran up to it along the south wall, with a rail on the open side. On top of the mezzanine sat the cell. Kiva could see it from here, a regulator drum like the one from Tallis Street, only bigger, with a big round meter on the front and a red quench handle on its side. Thick cables ran from it across the platform to a board on the east wall covered in rows of small lamps. That was the load board, where each lamp stood for a house, or a shop, or a streetlight, and the whole board was the district. When the cell was running, the district was lit.

Underneath the mezzanine was the pump room, a low space enclosed in steel walls, with one wide doorway facing west into the hall. Across the doorway, a heavy steel fire shutter had come down two-thirds of the way and jammed there, leaving a gap about knee-high, and an electric winch was bolted above it with its cable running up into the shutter's housing. Inside, in the dim light of a single lamp, Kiva could just make out a shape lying on the floor.

That was the casualty, a weighted training dummy dressed in a pump-crew coverall, with its legs pinned under a fallen pump housing just inside the threshold.

And halfway along the south wall, set into the brick, was the chute.

It was a heavy, square steel hatch, about chest-high, and behind it was a ramp that went down into the dark. Below the Cellhouse, in a sealed vault in the old refinery basement, was the Kennel.

The Kennel was a bottle-rift, a real tear, very small, held open in a lattice that the Wren House maintained and a Glory master had impressed sixty years ago. It made husks. They were small ones, the smallest kind of Riftspawn there was, the kind the yield books called a Handful, because that was about how much ash one left behind. The Wren House kept them in the vault and let them up the chute on a timer, four releases to a run, one or two or three husks at a time. Every collegiate team in Meridian trained against them, and every professional company had, once.

"They're coming up," said Perrin quietly.

Down in the hall, the first afternoon team was running, four Wren House and Crooked Line candidates Kiva half knew. The chute hatch swung open with a clang, and something grey poured out of it onto the concrete.

It was about the size of a large dog. It pulled itself up into a shape with four thick, stubby limbs and a low front end and no face, and it turned toward the nearest of the candidates, a hand-door boy holding a ward across the pump room door, and lurched at him.

Kiva had seen husks before, as everyone had. They came through on the Sag Line once or twice a year, and there were drawings of them in every schoolbook. But she had never been this close to the Kennel while it was running. She watched the husk go at the boy's ward and stop against it, the way a bird stops against glass, and she watched an Ember-door girl come in from the side and put her lit blade through the middle of it.

It came apart. There was no blood. It just let go of its shape and fell into a heap of fine grey ash on the concrete.

"Why does it go for the ward?" said Perrin. "Not the girl?"

"Worked power," said Anwen, without looking away. "They go at whatever's lit. The more lit, the more they want it."

"Like moths."

"Like moths that weigh as much as you do."

---

Ysra Crest briefed them herself.

She came into the corridor at two o'clock with a clipboard and stood in front of Team Seven while the others waited at the far end, and she took a moment over each of their faces in turn.

"You've watched it," she said. "So I'll be brief. Four objectives. One. The casualty is pinned in the pump room. Get it free and over the green line. The standard is four minutes from the Gate. Two. The cell is running at evening peak and it has a fault in it. I won't tell you what. Bring it to a safe state, either by stable tune or by controlled shutdown. Controlled means you call it, you pull the quench yourself, and you write it down. If the cell goes into red and trips its own breaker, that is uncontrolled. Uncontrolled is a fail on objective two." She turned a page. "Three. Contain the spawn. The chute opens four times. No husk reaches the casualty. No husk crosses the green line. Four. Call. Declared roles, said aloud, before you start. Any change of role is said aloud before you make it. After the run you fill in your team ledger card. I read every one."

She looked up.

"Violations. A lit Edge on any person. An undeclared role change. Overspending your dram. The board on the gallery watches all four of you, and so do I. If I think the run has become unsafe, I hit the master stop, the cell quenches, the chute locks, and the run is over." Her eyes went to Kiva and stayed there a moment. "Questions?"

"The cell," said Kellan. "What's the charge?"

"Grade Second. Standard municipal tune, with one fault."

"And the winch on the shutter?"

Ysra raised an eyebrow, very slightly. "It runs off the cell. Same circuit as the load board."

Kellan nodded, and Kiva saw Anwen write something on her list.

"Settle your drams," said Ysra. "You have ten minutes to declare roles. Then the Gate."

---

They argued about the roles for nine of the ten minutes.

It started the moment Ysra walked away. Kellan turned to face the other three, and Kiva saw at once that he had done this before, many times, because he did not ask. He laid it out.

"Spawn is me," he said. "I hold the chute side and clear anything that comes up. Pryce, you're the casualty. You've got the Sustain read and a ward. You get under that shutter, read the dummy, ward it if anything comes near. Cade, you're the shutter. It's jammed. The winch is weak. You hold it so it doesn't come down on Pryce while she's under it, and you lift the pump housing off the legs." He turned to Kiva. "Rowan. You're the cell."

Sustain was the healing side of the hand door, the read that told you where a body was hurt and how badly, and Anwen was better at it than anyone else in their year.

"I can't tune it," said Kiva.

"I know you can't tune it. You read it. You tell us what it's doing. If it goes wrong, you quench."

"That's what I was going to say."

"Good." He nodded once. "Then we're done."

"No," said Perrin. "Wait."

Kellan turned to him.

"If she quenches," said Perrin slowly, "the cell goes dead."

"Yes."

"And the winch runs off the cell. Crest said so."

"Yes."

"So if she quenches while I'm under the shutter," said Perrin, "the winch dies and the whole weight of that thing comes down on me. And on Anwen. And the dummy."

There was a short silence.

"You're still-door," said Kellan. "You can hold it."

"Can I? How heavy is it?"

"Heavy," said Anwen, who was studying the shutter through the window. "Steel. Four hundred kilos, maybe. More, with the housing."

Perrin let out a breath. "I've held three hundred in the Hall, on a rack, for thirty seconds. Ready. Planted. Not lying on my back halfway under a door."

"Then don't be under it when she quenches," said Kellan.

"Then she has to know where I am."

"She'll be on the mezzanine. She can see the door from there."

"Then the cell has to last," said Anwen, "until Perrin and I are out. Which means the cell is the clock." She turned from the window. "If the cell has a fault in it and it's drifting, then we need to know how fast. If it's going to trip in two minutes, we've got two minutes to get the casualty out. If it's going to trip in one, we have to do it differently."

Kellan was quiet for a moment. Kiva saw him take the information in, the way he had taken in the step. He did not like it, and he did not argue with it.

"Rowan," he said. "Can you tell how fast?"

"I can read it," said Kiva. "I can't tell you until I've got my hand on it."

"Your hand." He frowned. "With a door?"

"No. Just my hand. I can feel cells without opening anything. I always could." She hesitated. Then she said the rest, because she had promised. "I can feel it. I can't fix it. If I open a door near it, they'll fight, and the cell will take the fight. That's what cells do. So I'm not opening anything. I'm reading it, and calling it, and if it goes, I'm pulling the handle with my hands. Same as anybody."

Kellan weighed her for a long moment. "You're sure?"

"I told my aunt. I'm telling you."

"Fine." He turned to the others. "Then that's it. Renn, spawn. Pryce, casualty. Cade, shutter. Rowan, cell. Read, call, quench. No doors near the cell." He came back to Kiva. "That's what goes on the card."

"That's what goes on the card," said Kiva.

Anwen wrote it on the team ledger card in her small neat hand. Then she read it over, glanced at Kiva, and added one line underneath.

*Rowan: calls clock to Pryce/Cade.*

"So we know," she said.

They settled their drams. Kiva tipped the grey powder into her left palm and closed her hand around it, and it went in warm and settled, and the ash in her hand went dead. Beside her, Kellan brushed his off onto the floor. Anwen held hers in both hands and closed her eyes. Perrin closed his fist around his dram and wore the expression of someone swallowing medicine.

A bell rang at the end of the corridor.

"Team Seven," called a clerk. "To the Gate."

---

From the floor of the hall, the gallery looked very high.

It ran along the whole north wall above them, a long balcony behind thick glass. At the near end sat the assessors' booth, where Ysra Crest stood with two others in front of a bank of instruments and a big brass lever with a red grip, which was the master stop. Beyond the booth, the public seats rose in tiers behind the glass, full to the back row, four hundred people with their faces pressed close. Somebody had a sign. Further along, there was a section with better chairs, and the slate blue of Graywater coats, and the green of Saltmere, and the Halden black and gold.

She found Orla in the third row of the public seats, sitting very straight, with her stick across her knees.

Kiva dropped her eyes and did not raise them again.

The Gate rolled down behind them with a long rattling crash. They were inside.

The air smelled of old brick and hot metal and, very faintly, of something else, something like the air before a storm. That was the Kennel, below the floor. Kiva could feel it through her boots as a low pressure, the same pressure the masts on the Sag Line drew on their slow paper drums.

A bell rang overhead.

"Go," said Kellan.

They went.

Kiva did not run. She had decided that on the tram, because the knee would not take it, and the cell would still be there in ten seconds. She walked fast down the long hall, along the north wall under the gallery, past the crates and the cable reel. To her right, Kellan was already halfway to the chute side, moving in a long, light stride with his blade forming on his fist as he went, and Anwen and Perrin passed her at a run, heading for the pump room.

She crossed in front of the pump room to the south wall, put her good leg on the first step of the steel stair, and climbed.

There were twelve narrow steps, and the rail on the open side was cold under her hand. At the top she stepped out onto the mezzanine, and the cell was in front of her.

It was a big drum, bigger than Tallis Street, waist-high and bolted to the platform, with a meter on its face as wide as a dinner plate. The needle sat at six, just inside the yellow. Beyond the drum, the load board on the east wall glowed with its rows of little lamps, a whole district at evening peak with every light lit, and from the top of the board a thick cable ran down the wall and along the underside of the mezzanine to the winch over the shutter.

On the side of the drum was the quench, a red handle with a wire seal through it, and below it the steel quench box with its hinged lid.

She put her left hand flat on the drum. Her hand. No door.

She felt it at once.

It was a good tune, standard municipal, even and clean. But it leaned. Under the clean shape there was a tightness, a forward tilt, like someone standing on the edge of a step, and the ash was releasing a little faster than the pattern meant it to. And faster. And faster.

She knew it. She knew it exactly.

"It's a hurry," she said aloud. She did not mean to say it. It came out.

Somebody had put Brannock's hurry in it on purpose. The assessors had tuned the cell the way a tired man tunes it at six in the morning with two more substations to do before seven, and now it was running at evening peak, with the whole board lit, and the hurry was feeding on itself.

She watched the needle and counted. It went from six and one to six and two in ten seconds. It went from six and two to six and three in nine.

So it was not only climbing. The gaps were closing.

The breaker would trip at the red line, at eight and a half.

She did the sum, allowing for the way each tenth came a little sooner than the last, and then she did it again. Then she turned and leaned over the mezzanine rail.

Directly below her, at the pump room door, Perrin was on his back. He had slid in under the jammed shutter and lay along the threshold, right beneath its bottom edge, with both hands planted flat against the steel above his chest. From the rail she could see the length of him and nothing past him. She could feel his stance from up here, though, the still door open under him like a held breath. Anwen had crawled in through the gap beside him, and Kiva could not see her at all.

"Clock!" Kiva called. "It's drifting fast. The needle's at six and three. It gained a tenth in ten seconds and the next in nine, and it's getting faster. It'll trip in under two minutes."

"Under two minutes," said Anwen's voice from inside, muffled. "Copy. Perrin, the housing."

"Working on it," said Perrin through his teeth.

At the chute, the hatch swung open with a clang.

---

The first husk came out at a minute and ten.

Kiva heard it hit the concrete. She turned and saw it pour out of the hatch and pull itself into shape, grey and heavy and faceless, about the size of a large dog. It swung its low front end toward the pump room, toward Perrin's open still door and Anwen's Sustain.

Kellan was already there.

He came in from the side in two long Strides, with the struts running down his calves and back up as he landed and the blade on his fist before his second foot touched. He lit it, and the thin blue-white line ran along the edge, perfectly steady. He put it through the middle of the husk at the height where a heart would be on a dog, and the husk came apart into a heap of grey ash on the concrete.

He did not spare the ash a glance. He stepped back to the chute side and waited, blade low, already watching the hatch.

The needle on the drum read six and four.

Kiva's eyes went to the ash on the floor. It lay in a fine pale heap, clean as flour. In a real rift that was a Handful of clean ash, worth catching and grading and writing into a company's book, and the scoring sheet gave points for clean ash recovered once a run was safe.

Kellan had not even checked where it fell.

By the time she looked back at the needle, it had reached six and seven, and the tenths were coming every seven seconds.

"Six and seven," she called. "Seventy seconds."

"Copy," said Anwen.

Under the shutter, she could see Perrin's arms trembling.

"I've got my shoulder under the housing," he said, to Anwen or to nobody. "It's heavy. It's caught on the frame. I need another thirty."

He was lifting the housing with his body and holding the shutter with his hands, with his still door holding the ground under his back while the rest of him did the work. It was not stacking, because it was all one door. But it was a lot to ask of one door.

"You'll have it," said Kiva.

The needle read six and eight.

The chute clanged, and this time there were two husks, one after the other. Kellan met the first as it came out of the hatch, before it had finished pulling itself together, and it came apart in a spray of ash. The second got past him and went sideways along the south wall, low and quick, toward the bottom of the mezzanine stair.

Toward her.

Kiva felt it before she understood it. She was the only thing on the mezzanine, and she was standing next to a cell running hot. The cell was worked power, a pattern held on ash, burning faster and faster, and it was the brightest thing in the room.

The husk was not coming for her. It was coming for the cell.

Kellan saw it too. He Strode after it in two long bounds and caught it at the foot of the stair and put his lit blade through its back, and it came apart on the first step, so that ash ran down between the steel treads.

"It's the cell," Kellan called up. He did not sound worried. He sounded as if he had just read a number off a meter. "They'll keep going for the cell as it runs hotter. I'll hold the stair."

"Seven," said Kiva. "Fifty seconds."

"Perrin," said Anwen.

"I know."

The needle crossed seven, and the next tenth took five seconds.

---

Kiva stood with her hand on the drum and felt the hurry in it and knew how to fix it.

That was the worst of it. She knew exactly. She could feel the tilt in the pattern under her palm, the lean of it, as clearly as she had felt Brannock's in Mara's shop. If Mara had been standing here, she would have laid her hand on the drum and smoothed it out in twenty seconds, or ten. She would have found the edge of the hurry and pressed it flat, the way you press a wrinkle out of a sheet, and the needle would have stopped climbing.

And then the cell would last, and the winch would run, and Perrin would have all the time he needed.

The hand door was right there. It had been there since she put her palm on the drum, lifted and curious, at the threshold. It wanted the cell. It always wanted cells.

*No doors near the cell.* That was on the card. She had said it to Kellan's face.

Seven and one.

She leaned out over the rail. Perrin's whole body was shaking now, and his face was red, and his teeth were bared.

"Housing's half off," came Anwen's voice. "I can't slide it out yet."

Seven and two.

"Thirty more," said Perrin. "I need thirty more."

He did not have it. At this rate, the needle would be in the red in forty seconds, and thirty of those would only get the housing off the legs. Anwen would still be on her knees inside with the dummy when the breaker tripped, and the winch would die, and the shutter would come down with its whole weight on a boy who was already holding all he could.

*One door,* thought Kiva. *Only one.*

She had done it in the Terrace Ring. She had kept the hand door out for two minutes while Kellan Renn tried to hit her. She could keep the Ember out now, and the still door, and open only the hand door, and lay it on the cell, and press the hurry flat, and close it again. One door. Sequence. Not stack.

It was not on the card.

"Changing role," she called, loudly, so the gallery could hear it, so Kellan could hear it. "Rowan. Cell. Tuning. Hand door only."

"*What?*" said Kellan from the bottom of the stair.

"I can feel the fault. It's a hurry. I can press it out. One door."

"You said no doors!"

"I'm saying it out loud!"

She did not wait for him. She closed her eyes, and opened the hand door, and laid it on the cell.

---

It was like putting her hand into warm water.

The cell was right there, all of it, the ash packed in its steel can and the pattern holding it, and the hurry running through the pattern like a crease in cloth. She found the edge of it. She pressed.

The hurry gave.

It did not go all at once. It went like a knot coming loose, slowly, a little at a time. She felt the tilt in the pattern ease, and she felt the ash, which had been rushing, begin to slow.

She opened her eyes. The needle read seven and three.

It read seven and three. It read seven and three.

It had stopped.

"It's holding!" she called down. "Perrin, it's holding, you've got time—"

The bottom heat-line was cool, and so was the second. She had the hand door open and only the hand door, with the Ember shut behind her like a door with her foot against it, and the still door quiet under her. She was doing it. One door, on a live cell, in the Cellhouse, with the gallery watching.

Something leapt in her chest that was not a heat-line. It was joy.

She held it there while Perrin grunted below her and Anwen said something sharp and pleased, and the needle did not move. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Twenty.

Below, the chute clanged.

Three husks.

They came out of the hatch in a grey heap, all at once, faster than the others had. Kellan met the first and put his blade through it and it came apart. The other two were already past him.

They did not go for the pump room, and they did not go for Kellan. They went straight along the south wall toward the stair.

Toward the mezzanine. Toward the cell.

Toward her.

She understood it in the same instant she saw them move. The cell had been bright before. Now it was brighter, because now there was a person standing over it with a door open, laying worked power into it with her hand. She had made it the most lit thing in the whole Cellhouse.

She had made it a lamp. And the moths were coming.

Kellan Strode after them and caught one at the foot of the stair. The other was already on the steps, climbing. It went up the narrow steel stair the way a dog climbs, all four stubby limbs scrabbling, heavy and sure, and the treads rang under it.

Kiva saw it coming up the stair at her.

And she was afraid.

She did not decide to be. It went through her like cold water. And the Ember door, which had been shut behind her with her foot against it, felt her fear and came open on its own, the way a dog comes through a door when it hears you shout.

The bar came up on her right fist.

The bottom heat-line went hot. Then the second. Then the still door, under her, startled, opened too.

And all three doors went into the cell.

She felt it happen. She still had the hand door on the drum, open, and the other two came through behind it before she could stop them. Edge and stance and ward, three doors pulling from three sides, and the cell under her hand took all of it. It did not care what she meant. It held what she put in it.

The heat-lines blazed.

The needle on the drum swung from seven and three to seven and six in a single breath.

The husk reached the top of the stair.

Kiva tore her hand off the cell. She swung the ugly bar around and hit the husk flat in its front end as it came onto the platform, hard, with all her weight behind it. It was not a cut, because the bar was dark. But the husk was small and she hit it hard, and the shape went out of it, and it came apart into ash and slid back down the stair in a grey spill.

She stood on the mezzanine with the bar on her fist and her chest on fire, breathing in short, hard gasps.

She had let go of the cell.

She turned back to the drum.

The needle read seven and seven.

Seven and eight.

It was still climbing, a tenth with every few breaths, as fast as the hurry had ever driven it, and her hand was off it. The doors were off it. She shut the hand door, hard, and felt it close. The Ember was still open, with the bar still on her fist, and the still door was half open under her. But none of them were touching the cell.

The cell did not care.

She put her left palm back on the drum, just her hand, no door, and felt it.

It was not the hurry anymore. The hurry was gone, because she had pressed it out. In its place was something else, something she had never felt in a cell in her life. It was three shapes at once, an edge and a wall and a weight, pulling against each other and twisting round and round inside the pattern like three people pulling on one blanket.

Her own doors. Her own fight. She had put it in there.

And it was still going, after she had let go.

A cell keeps what you leave in it.

Below her, Kellan was shouting something up the stair. She did not hear what. She was watching the needle.

Eight. Eight and one.

And she could feel it now, through her palm. The cell was not only running hot. It was calling. The three-door noise she had left in it was ringing out of the drum like a struck bell, louder and louder, and every husk still waiting in the dark under the floor could hear it.

"I've stopped," she whispered. "It hasn't."

Eight and two.
