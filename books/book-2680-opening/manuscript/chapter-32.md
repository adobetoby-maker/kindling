# Chapter 32 — Kiva: Held of the Line

Healer Aske turned her hand over on the towel on Tuesday and did not reach for his pin.

"I'm told that it went away," he said. "For most of a minute in a yard on the twentieth, two fingers. For the better part of an hour on a wharf on Sunday morning, the whole of it, to the wrist. Your sponsor told me the first. A Graywater catcher came up my stair on Sunday afternoon to tell me the second, which in thirty years no catcher has ever done. He stood in that door with his cap in both hands and said it as if it had been his fault."

Kiva looked at the hand on the towel. An hour was a better answer than the true one. It had come from somebody who had seen the wharf and nothing after it, and she had only to sit still.

"It was longer," she said. "He only saw the wharf. It went at the sixth rung, a little after three, and it was supper before it came back. Fifteen hours, about."

Aske did not move for a moment.

"Fifteen," he said. He looked at her over the hand. "And you were in two minds whether to say so. Don't trouble to answer; I've a face in front of me." He turned the wrist toward the window. "Well. Now I've the truth of it, which is the only thing I can work with."

"Is it burned?"

"No. I've looked for that twice now and I'd tell you." The cold thumbs went along the two fingers and stopped at the knuckle. "It's a nerve that's being asked a question it can't answer quickly. Every time you do whatever it is that you do on that mast, something goes through here at a rate it was never built to carry. It doesn't scorch. It starves, and then it sulks, and each time it sulks a little longer. The wharf wasn't the mast. That was bag necks hauled across the crook of an elbow, over a nerve that had been emptied on the Wednesday and hadn't filled. The mast leaves it hungry. The cord found it hungry." He let go. "I can ease it. I can't tell you how many more times it will come back to where it was. I'd be guessing, and I don't."

"There's a house in the hills that says a year's rest would help it."

"Is there." Aske's eyebrows went up a little. "Then I'll ask them what they mean by rest, and what they've seen it do, and I'll tell you what they say. I won't take it on trust from a house, and neither will you." He sat back. "No go until Monday. After that, one in a week, and I see the hand the next morning, every time, before you're let have another. That's not a ban. It's a price, and I'm telling you it before you've run up the bill."

One in a week. That was most of a month for the four crossings she still owed Orla, if nothing went wrong, and there was not a hundredth of the team's ash left over for anything to go wrong with.

The grading had gone up on the board in the Placement Hall basement by the time she got there. Seven's two Handfuls off the Weighbridge, graded First, ward full, induction a trace. Seven stood sixth on the standings now, with the brackets still after its name.

The brackets were still there. She found that she agreed with Kellan about them.

Mr. Adlam was standing at the foot of the board with his hat in his hand, as if he had been waiting for a tram, and when he saw her he came across.

"Miss Rowan. I'd hoped I might find you here; it saves me a stair." He was perfectly pleasant. "I'd not like you to hear it from a clerk. The Works has put in a claim on the bag you tagged on Sunday, at the Weighbridge wharf. A hundred and thirteen drams, in the Wren House cold room. Under the foundation patent." He said it as he had said it to Noa, without looking anything up. "*All ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.*"

Kiva said nothing.

"The Works asks on what grounds a candidate, provisional and unclassified, kept that bag from civic use. On her word, I understand, and a field comb." He turned his hat round once in his hands. "The wardens of Weighbridge Street have asked to be heard as well. They don't want the bag. They want it not carried off their street's account by anybody who wasn't there, which I thought rather fine of them. And the grader wants it held for reference. The Chair will hear all parties on Tuesday next, the third, at nine."

"On a comb," said Kiva, "and my hand on the hem, and a shed foreman's on the neck. I said I couldn't weigh it. I'll say so on Tuesday."

"I'd expect nothing else." He put his hat on. "One likes to be thorough, Miss Rowan. It isn't the same as unkind. Good afternoon."

He went up the stair. Kiva stood where she was, with the standings in front of her and her own team sixth on them.

"That's you," said somebody at her elbow.

It was the thin boy in Wren House green. He had no catch roll this time. He had a yellow card, folded small, and he was turning it over in his fingers.

"*Provisional and unclassified.* I heard him say it from the stair." He did not sound unkind. "We've got our held tear tomorrow. The river flats, at first light, if it obliges. Sixth company, at last."

"I'm glad."

"I expect you are. I've stopped minding whether you are or not; it was wearing me out." He nodded at the card. "We were a blade short. Our Kill went home to Saltmere when the day was lost. So the Circuit's seated a reserve, off the list." He almost smiled. "Dray."

"*Selka* Dray?"

"She asked for it. She came and found me on Sunday. She said she had an objection waiting on the Chair, and she'd sooner stand a floor in the meantime than sit about waiting." He put the card away. "She told me straight out that she still thinks you oughtn't to be seated. Then she asked what my father's shop sold. He's opening at noon tomorrow whatever happens. He says he won't shut it twice."

He went away up the stair.

Noa found her there a little later.

He had the weigh-house tape in a roll under his arm, since the Chair had sent him for it, and he looked as if he had not slept since Saturday either.

"Nothing," he said, before she could ask. "I've been over the Weighbridge mast from the bell to the closing. There's a Flask on it, and five husks, and one blade getting longer. There isn't a notch. Whatever jumped sixteen pens at the Pit, it didn't happen on that wharf." He lowered his voice. "And I went back over your two on the travelling mast, Friday's and the twentieth, past the cliff and on for a good minute. Nothing there either."

"So it isn't every crossing."

"Or it's only the first. Or it wants more than one pen to show, and a ring of lamps. I can think of five things it might be and I can't choose between any two." He rubbed his eyes. "I'm not going to say which. Adlam reads the same cliff now it's open. I'd rather hand him a blank than a guess with my name on it."

"That's a hard thing to bring to a hearing."

"It's what there is," said Noa.

---

Kellan said it on Wednesday at the Rowan kitchen table, with the team's book open between them and three chairs filled where there had been four.

He had a speech ready. She could see it on him, the way she could see a sum on him, and he did not give it.

"I went to the refinery yard last Thursday," he said. "I stood behind a rail for two hours. They've got a contained string, a six-knot, in a lattice of their own. They've got a chartered foreman every Tuesday and Thursday, and a ward line that's paid. Their Kill's a second-year called Pettit." He was speaking to the oilcloth. "He isn't better than I am. I watched him. He's four-tenths at the top, the same as me, and his feet are worse."

"But," said Perrin.

"But he's cut a rung a week since the thaw, and I've cut one in my life." Kellan looked up. "On Sunday morning I stood on a wharf with the corner of a sheet in my hand and watched one blade make seven cuts, each longer than the last, until she had to sit down. The second blade was in a van with his leg in a board. The third was me, and I wasn't allowed, and I shouldn't have been. I've been over it every hour since. It isn't that I mind holding a corner. I found out I don't."

"The slow one," said Kiva.

"The slow one. I'd hold that corner again tomorrow." His jaw was tight. "I want to be allowed. I want to be the second blade a foreman can turn to. I can't get that off one night in a fortnight and a trough, and I can't get it by being excellent at a thing I do alone at the top of a bound."

Nobody at the table spoke.

"So I've said yes. To the Halden license team, for the Regional. Not for the summer, and not for their yard after; I've told them so." He drew a breath. "I stay on Seven. I asked; a house entry for the Regional isn't a transfer. If Sorn will take a blade on her van who's wearing somebody else's colors in the autumn, I stand the Flask we owe. If she won't, I understand."

"They've claimed the bag," said Kiva. She had meant to wait, and found that she had not been able to. "Your team's house. Adlam told me in the basement yesterday. *However produced.*"

"I heard. It'll have my mother's seal at the foot; every paper of theirs does." He did not look away. "I think it's lawful. I think it's wrong. I held one of the corners of that bag and I heard a shed foreman say he wouldn't put a thimble of it in his own kitchen. I've told Mr. Adlam I'll say what I saw on that wharf to anybody who asks me, whoever's paying my stipend."

"What did he say?"

"That they'd expect nothing else." Kellan's mouth twisted. "I know that's exactly what a pleasant man would say. I've told my mother as well. She put her cups down once. She knows what I mean."

Perrin had been turning a spoon end over end. He put it down.

"I mind," he said. "And I'd have done the same, and I'm going to the Hall's second team in the autumn myself, so I've a nerve." He blew out his cheeks. "A month ago I said there were four of us and I couldn't find two going the same way. Now there's one of us going two ways at once. I didn't know that was allowed. Is it in Rule Fourteen?"

"It's in Nineteen," said Kellan gravely. "I looked."

"Of course you did."

Kiva had not said anything yet.

"What do you want from us?" she said.

He was silent for a moment. It was plainly not the question he had come ready for.

"Nothing," he said. "That's what's the matter with it. I'd a speech for if you asked me to stay, and you haven't asked." He looked at the table. "I mean to be on Seven when the Weighbridge share comes in. And Anwen should hear it before a runner does."

"It can't be spent without her say."

"I know. I've thought of that." He looked at Kiva. "It shouldn't be me that tells her."

"No."

"I'll go," said Perrin. "I'm the only one of us she's not cross with."

"She's cross with you about the bean poles."

"That's an old crossness. It's nearly affection." He leaned back, carefully, on account of the ribs. "I'll go out on Saturday with the runner. I'll tell her about Halden, and about Finch walking a Flask down a hill, and I'll bring back what she says about the lot of us."

There was a small sound from the end of the table.

Pru Finch had been let in at the beginning and had sat so quietly ever since that Kiva had forgotten her. She had a yellow card in both hands.

"I've got to give this back to the Circuit on Friday," she said. "Or ask to keep it. Nobody's told me which." Her round face was very pink. "I stayed. I found that out. I'd like to do it again, with a bit more warning, and I'd like it to be with you, if there's a you. I don't know who's meant to decide."

The three of them looked at one another across the table.

"Four of us," said Kiva slowly. "And one's on the Sag. We'll ask Anwen. She said you before any of us did." She turned to Pru. "Keep the card. That much I can say by myself. It's a corner and a lamp, and you've done both."

"Not a wall."

"Not yet," said Kellan. "If you want a blade for the Regional, and you will, I'd ask Talley. He's a second step and no team, and he's the only Ember in this school who's ever told me I was wrong about something and been right. It isn't mine to fill. I'm only saying who I'd ask."

---

Orla Dane had sat on a great many benches in her life, and she thought that this was the hardest.

It stood in the passage of the Home Line hall, against a whitewashed wall, opposite a door. It was a plain door. There was no plate on it. She had walked past it perhaps four hundred times in twenty years as a field officer, with mud to the knee and her stick not yet needed, and had not once been asked through.

*Your sponsor as far as the door.* Hollis had kept to it. He had come out for the girl himself at four, with his hat off, and had held the door for her, and had looked at Orla over her head with an expression that said a number of things and apologized for none of them. Then it had shut.

That was a quarter of an hour ago.

She did not mind. She had looked at that carefully, as she would have looked at a candidate's stance, pushing at it from several sides. She had expected to mind a good deal. She had saved a favor for twenty-five years to get this near that room, and had spent it, and here she sat with her stick between her boots like a parent outside a school.

It was only that the wrong person had been asking, all that time.

She had wanted to be in that room because Ilse had stood in it once. That was the whole of her reason, and it had never been enough to open a door, and she saw now that it ought not to have been. The girl had a better one. It was about her.

A boy went by with a bucket. A bell rang somewhere for the change of the levee watch.

There had been a visitor to the Old Yard, a week and a half ago, on a night when there was no go.

Mara Rowan had come down the lane at half past eight in her work apron, with her sleeves still rolled. She had not sat. She had stood beside the spare crate and said two sentences, in the voice she used for a fault that she had found and did not intend to discuss.

*You kept a thing from my table. Don't do it twice.*

*I shan't,* Orla had said.

*Good. Then that's all. I've a re-sit in the ninth month and no room in me for a second grievance.* And she had looked at the cross, and at the brass head on its three legs, for a long moment. *She came down on that mark on Friday. She told me at the table. She was a good deal more pleased about the one that she called off.*

Then she had gone home. Orla had sat on for an hour afterward and found that she had been let off nothing and forgiven nothing, and that she could work with it.

Ysra Crest had stopped her on the Placement Hall stair that morning, too, on her way up to the Chair's office with a sheaf of papers under her arm. *Tuesday,* Ysra had said, *with the rest. I'll want the tape, and the reader, and the sponsor, and the stack.* It was not a reprimand. Ysra Crest did not give reprimands. She gave appointments.

Orla knew what would be asked in that room. A boy with a strip of paper would be made to tell a Placement Chair whether a Torch could tell a crossing from a stack with a stick, and he would have to say no.

She would have to say it too. She found that she did not mind that either.

*And the hand goes away a little longer each time,* she thought.

It was not the landings that Orla would put her name to, when the day came. She had watched this one miss, and miss again, and call *off* in the air with a door ready under her and every reason to take it. It was that.

The door across the passage stayed shut.

Orla Dane settled her stick, and her bad leg, and waited, as she had learned to wait on a tear watch, without leaning on the time.

---

The room was smaller than Kiva had imagined, and there was nothing in it but a table and a window and seven people.

The window looked out over the Ferren levee. The table was oak, and old, and scarred along its edges where hands had been laid flat on it for a very long time. The seven sat round it with the window at their backs. None of them was introduced. Vane had told her on the stair that nobody in that room had a name for as long as the door was shut; a person was the Ferren, or the Weir, or the Lantern, or the River Flats, and whatever was said was said by a stretch of water and a wall. He was the Sag. He went and sat in the one empty chair, and left her standing at the foot of the table by herself.

The oldest of them was a spare woman of seventy with a river pilot's squint and her hands folded on the oak like somebody waiting for the usual thing. Kiva knew without being told that she was the Ferren.

"Sit, if you like," said the Ferren. "Most stand."

"I'll stand."

"Then stand." The squint narrowed a little further. "The Sag asked that the bearer be let come before the watch, and ask what she came to ask. That's allowed. It's not often asked for. We witness, Miss Rowan. We don't choose. And as a rule we don't answer questions, because we've mostly no answers to give." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat. "Ask."

Kiva had carried the question about with her for a month. She had first asked it of Orla, in the dark, on a crate. She found that it had not changed.

"Why me?" she said. "Why did she give it to me?"

"I can't tell you why," said the Ferren. "Nobody can, now. I can tell you what she said. I was in this chair."

She turned her head a little toward the window, toward the levee and the low grey water.

"It was the spring of '69. Ilse Corran stood where you're standing, in a wet coat; she'd come straight off the culverts. And she said that she named the next bearer, which is her right and nobody else's. A child. Six years old. Ione Rowan's girl, on Coper Street." The Ferren did not hurry. "She said she'd been at the Tallow fire. Afterward she'd gone to Coper Street and sat an hour on a stair with that child, whose mother had died that night pulling a quench by hand. She said the child hardly spoke. She asked one thing, the whole hour."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below the window a barge went by with its engine thudding.

"*Will they say why?*" said the Ferren.

Kiva stood at the foot of the oak with her hands at her sides.

She could not hear herself saying it. What she had was the stair: a tall woman's coat that smelled of rain, and nobody making her talk. She had been six, and her mother had gone out to the Tallow substation and not come back, and somebody had pulled a red handle that ought to have pulled itself. Years afterward Mara had told her why, at a bench, with her thumb on the scar in her palm: cut ash, stamped First, sold to a substation by somebody who knew. Kiva had thought that was the first time anybody had said it.

She had asked. She had sat on a stair at six and asked whether anybody would.

"Corran said she'd looked for a month for a better reason than that," said the Ferren, "and hadn't found one. And she said she didn't know that it wanted one." She let that stand a moment. "Then she asked that it go to the child on the day of her Kindling and not before, sealed, by the Line's hand. That was all. She was never a woman for a long reason. We heard it, and we let it stand."

"That's not a reason a watch can weigh," said the River Flats. He was a broad, careful man, and he did not say it unkindly.

"We don't weigh it," said the Ferren. "We hear it. That's the whole of what this table's for."

Kiva found that her eyes stung, and that she did not mind that the seven of them could see it.

"There's one thing more," said the Ferren. "Corran said in this room that she'd asked the guardian first. Your aunt. She'd gone to Coper Street and asked, and your aunt had said no."

"I know," said Kiva. Her voice came out thin. "She told me. The morning it came."

"Did she tell you that we went round her?"

Kiva looked at her.

"She said Ilse chose me anyway."

"Ilse named you. That's a bearer's right, and the Line's custom, and it doesn't wait on a guardian's leave. The watch heard her say your aunt had said no, and heard her name you all the same, and let it stand." The Ferren's voice did not change at all. "And not one of us went to Coper Street to tell your aunt that there'd been a room, and a rule, and seven people in it. Not in all the years since. She's thought all that while, I expect, that a friend didn't listen to her."

Nobody at the table moved.

"I'll go," said Vane.

"Go tonight," said the Ferren. "With your hat off." She turned back to Kiva. "May we see it? You needn't."

Kiva put up her right hand, without hurry, and drew the cord out of her collar, and laid the disk on the oak in front of her.

It was the first time that she had ever taken it out in front of strangers on purpose. It lay on the table, dark and smooth, the width of her palm. There was still a thread of grey Kennel ash in some of the grooves.

Nobody touched it. Nobody asked to.

"Has it done anything?" said the Ferren.

"Once," said Kiva. "At the Pit, on the twenty-ninth of last month. It was warm after the crossing. I first felt it when I'd shut the still door, with the lever down; I don't know when it began. It was warm from the shelf to the top of the road stair, and cold by the keeper's gate. It's been cold since. Through two more crossings on a mast."

The Ferren looked at the disk for a long while.

"I've sat in this chair four-and-thirty years," she said. "That's the first time anybody's stood there and told me it did anything at all."

"A plate that's warmed," said the River Flats slowly, "on a girl who's on a Circuit tape that opened to chartered parties this week, with a house claiming a bag she tagged and an Order at her aunt's kitchen door." He looked round the table, and then at Kiva, and he did not look away. "I don't say she's unfit. I say I'll move at Sunday's sitting that it be called in until the season's out. I'll say my cause then, before the watch."

"Then say it then," said the Ferren. "And before her, if she'll come." She looked at Kiva. "That's our custom as well. A bearer ought to hear the cause said, if there's to be a vote on it."

"I'll come," said Kiva.

"Good." The Ferren laid her hands flat on the oak again. "Pick it up, Miss Rowan. It's held of the Line, and it's yours to carry. Until Sunday it's no different from what it was this morning."

Kiva picked the disk up off the table. It was cold. She put the cord over her head, and tucked it in, and it lay against her breastbone where it had lain for four years.

It weighed what it had always weighed. She had rather expected that it would feel different, now that she knew why it was there.

---

Orla got up from the bench when the door opened. It took her a moment, and she did not hide that it did.

Kiva did not say anything at first. They walked down the whitewashed passage together toward the daylight at the end of it, at the pace of the stick, and it was not until they were nearly at the door that Kiva told her: the stair, and the wet coat, and the one question.

"*Will they say why,*" said Orla.

Her crooked face did something that Kiva had never seen it do. It did not last. Orla shut her mouth hard, and stopped, and looked for a while at the wall.

"That's Ilse," she said. Her voice was rough. "That's her to the life. She never could find a grand reason for anything. She'd sooner have a true small one and be laughed at." She began to walk again. "I'd wondered for four years what she'd said in there. I'd made up a dozen things. Not one of them was as plain as that, or as hard to argue with."

"They went round Mara," said Kiva. "Nobody ever told her."

"I'd heard she was asked. I never heard that anybody went to tell her she'd been gone round." Orla's mouth went thin. "That's the Line all over. It'll carry a child out of a culvert on its back and forget to knock on the mother's door."

"Vane's going tonight."

"Is he. Good." Orla took her stick in her other hand. "And on Sunday a man from the river flats will stand up and give his cause, and you'll stand at the foot of that table and hear it. On Tuesday there's the Chair, with a house and a street and a shelf all wanting one bag. And there's a house on a hill that means to ask again." She glanced sideways. "You've your answer ready as to why you laid that thing on their table, I suppose."

Kiva opened her mouth.

She had. It was about open hands, and a watch that did not choose, and it was rather good. She shut her mouth, and walked four paces, and found something behind it that was smaller.

"They asked," she said. "Nobody's ever asked to see it. They asked, and said I needn't."

"Hm," said Orla. "That'll do."

They came out onto the levee. It was a grey, mild evening, with the water low in the Ferren and the first lamps showing in the streets beyond. Somewhere over there was a pump house that had never dropped a stroke, and a street with a cracked lintel in it, and a cupboard with a little lamp inside its door.

"What was she like?" said Kiva.

It was a plain question. She had never put it to anybody. She had a coat that smelled of rain, and a laugh in a kitchen, and a question she could not hear herself asking.

Orla did not answer at once. She stood with both hands on her stick, looking down the levee to the place where it bent.

"Loud," she said. "She was the loudest officer on the Line, and she never raised her voice at a tear in her life. She laughed in other people's kitchens. She fell asleep on watch once, standing up, and woke before anybody noticed but me." A pause. "She took me down my first culvert. I've told people for twenty-five years that I took Hollis down his. I never said who took me."

"You never told me that."

"You never asked. You've only lately begun asking things you've not already answered." It was said without any edge at all. "Come along. There's a tram at the half hour, and I'd as soon you were at your aunt's table when that man knocks."

They went down the levee steps together, the one on a stick and the other on a strapped knee, at about the same pace.

---

He came at seven, and he did take his hat off.

Kiva saw him from the shop window, coming down Coper Street past the bakery, and she had time to say "He's here" before the bell went. Mara did not get up at once. She finished what she was doing, which was feeling for a fault in a cell can with her left palm. Then she wiped her hands, and went down to let a Home Line watch officer into her kitchen.

She gave him tea. She gave it to him in the good cup, which was worse, and sat down opposite him with her hands on the table.

"Kiva says you've something to tell me," said Mara. "I'd as soon you told it me, and not her."

"Ilse Corran named your niece before the Line's watch in the spring of '69," said Vane. "That's our custom. A bearer names the next, and says why, and the watch hears it and doesn't choose. She said in that room that she'd come to this house first and asked you, and that you'd said no." He had not touched the tea. "The watch heard her say it. It let the naming stand regardless, because by the Line's custom it can, and a guardian's leave isn't wanted. And nobody from the Line came to this door afterward to tell you there'd been a room at all, or that you'd been gone round by a rule and not by a friend. I've come to say so. Not to defend it."

Mara's face did not change.

"She told them I'd said no?"

"In those words. The Ferren was in the chair. She told your niece today."

Mara sat very still. Her left hand lay on the oilcloth beside the good cup, and it did not move.

"I told her no on that step out there, with my sister three weeks buried," she said. "I said the child had lost enough already, and I'd not have her given a thing nobody could explain. And she nodded, and said she understood me, and went away and did it." Her voice was perfectly even. "I've been angry with a dead woman since this child was six, for not listening. It turns out that she listened. She told them what I'd said, and then she did it anyway." She looked at the cup. "I find I'm no less angry, and I can't any longer think her careless, and I don't thank you for that."

"I'd not expect it."

"Good." Mara was quiet a moment. "And where were you, in '69?"

"On the river flats," said Vane. "With a crew of my own. I could tell you I'd have come, if it had been mine to do. I don't know that I should have."

"No. I don't suppose you would." She turned the cup's handle a little on its saucer, but not toward him yet. "Then I'll ask you what I'd have asked her, if I'd known there was any room to ask in. Every device in this house has a meter, and a shutdown path, and a hand that answers for it. What's that plate's?"

Vane was silent for a moment.

"There's no meter," he said. "Nobody's ever found one. As to shutting it down, the watch can call it in, on a vote, for cause said before it. One of the seven means to say his cause on Sunday."

Kiva's hand went to her collar before she could stop it.

"And the hand?" said Mara.

Vane looked across the kitchen at Kiva, standing by the stove.

"Hers," he said. "It always was."

Mara looked at him for some time. Then she turned the cup's handle the rest of the way round toward him, which meant that he might drink.
