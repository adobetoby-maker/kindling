# Chapter 23 — Kiva: Ember, to Still

"Rowan," said Hollis Vane. He did not waste a breath on being surprised. "Can you put a still-door on that shelf?"

Below the lip of the race, the grey slid under another grating.

"There's one way over, and it's a Stride." She had to shout it down forty paces of cinder. "I've both. One after the other. It isn't on my paper. I've tried it in the yard and missed every time. The last few didn't hurt. One held for a breath."

It was the truest thing she had ever said, and it was a list of failures, and there was no time to be ashamed of it.

"Dane!"

Orla was not in the first row. She had come down. Kiva saw her at the floor rail on the east side, both hands on the iron, with the stick hooked over her wrist. She must have started down the steps when the board began to pulse.

"I've not seen her land one," said Orla. Her voice was hard and clear, the voice of the Home Line twenty years ago. "I'll not put my name to it. I won't certify a thing I haven't watched."

"I didn't ask for a certificate. I asked what you've seen."

"Every miss called inside the breath, by her, before I'd opened my mouth." Orla did not look at Kiva. She looked at the officer. "If her line lights over that sump, she'll say *off* and go in it on her back before she'll stand a jam on your bus. That, I've seen. That, I'll answer for."

"That isn't a certificate."

"No. It's a reason." The knuckles on the rail were white. "And if it goes wrong, it's mine. I'll not have it laid on her."

Vane did not take his eyes off the race. He spoke flat and quick, in the field voice that went to every corner of the floor.

"Field direction. Rowan, K. One attempt at the south link, door to door, declared. Because nobody else on this ground can stand on it, and it'll be at the sump before the keeper's at his door." He did not stop. "And this, which is mine. The lid's not down. I said the lid first, and I'm opening that link with the doorway two-thirds clear, and the lattice goes when the blade lifts. Pumps before doorway. I've eight still-doors on the bar to stand in it. That's the foreman's trade, and I'll answer for it." His eyes came up. "You'll call your own off. I'm told you're good at it. If your first door isn't shut before your foot's down, you go in the ditch. Dray!"

There was a movement at the reserves' rail.

"Here."

"You're a Stride. Over after her. There's a pin on a chain. When that lever's level, you drop it through the lower eye."

Selka Dray stood with her yellow card in her hand. For a breath she did not move, and Kiva saw her face. It was the face of somebody who has been handed a job by the wrong person, for the wrong reason, on the one morning she had come to be proved right.

"Pin," said Dray. "Lower eye."

She put a hand on the rail and went over it.

Kiva had heard the part about the lid as one hears a thing that is being said to somebody else. She had been told three times what that lever did to a lattice, twice by this foreman and once by a keeper, and since she had begun watching the race she had not thought of it once. He had. He had thought of it, and weighed it, and taken it on himself, in the time it had taken the grey to slip under one more grating.

"Go," said Vane.

---

It was thirteen paces to the lip of the sump.

Kiva took them at a hobble that was nearly a run. The knee howled. She let it. She was not going to need it for anything but leaving the ground.

She went to a place on the brick lip a pace east of the race's end, and stopped, and looked across.

Three paces of air. At the bottom of it, a man and a half down, two bars of bare copper ran down their ramp into the dark, and the grey had not reached them yet. Beyond, the wall went up sheer and yellow. The shelf was an iron grating a pace deep. The lever hung along the brick with its crossbar at the height of her eyes, and above it stood the two copper jaws with the blade bedded in them, wide as a pair of open hands.

There was room on that shelf for her two boots and nothing else.

*It isn't a blow,* she thought. *It's only weight, arriving.*

She would not be putting it into the lever. She had learned that on her face in a brick trough. The speed was going into the ground, through her, as it had always been going to. The lever came after, and that was only standing.

The grey was two gratings from the ramp.

"Rowan!" It went up the terraces like a thrown stone. "Ember, to still!"

Forty paces north, Hollis Vane's right hand went up over his head, with the chalk in it. He had no flag. He had had the whole of her from the Chair, and he knew the rule.

She did not wait for it. That was the rule. She waited for herself.

The metal came off the cuff. It ran down her sides, cool and quick, and closed on her calves, and she bent the good knee and the bad knee together and left the ground.

The lip fell away. The sump opened under her, with its copper.

She shut it at the top.

The struts let go. She felt the metal run for home, up her legs and her ribs, and arrive. She had nothing. She was a girl in the air over a brick ditch with no doors at all, and the whole speed of a Stride in her like a stone in a sling, and a yellow wall coming.

It lasted about as long as it takes to say her name. She had fallen through that gap in the yard more times than she liked.

Listening for the iron would have made her late.

She set the pan.

She set it under herself, before, as she had set it under ash that had not yet fallen, in the dark, with two bad fingers. She opened the still door downward into a shelf that her boot had not reached, and held it there, wide and waiting, for the weight that was coming.

Her right foot came down into it.

The shelf rang like a struck rail. That was all. There was no jar. There was no landing. The bound went in at her heel and through her, the whole of it, and out by a road that had been ready, down through four bolts and into two hundred years of Brickfields wall.

Her left knee did not know that anything had happened.

She was standing on an iron grating with her shoulder a hand's width from the brick. She had not swayed. Nothing on earth could have moved her.

*Caught,* said her hands.

She did not put the lid on.

She took the crossbar. She put her right fist round it and hooked her left arm over it at the elbow, because she knew to the hair what those two fingers would tell her. Then she let herself go down.

She did not pull. A still-door did not pull. She only became heavy, heavier than the shelf, as heavy as the wall, and the lever was in the way of it.

It came.

The spring groaned like a ship. The iron bar swung down along the brick through a slow quarter of a circle. Above her head the copper blade lifted out of its jaws with a crack and a spit of blue.

Every lamp in the Pit went out.

She felt them go more than saw it. The hum that had lain under that floor all morning stopped, as a held note stops. The great board was a black slab. And far off at the north end, something that had been pressing on the back of her neck through a lattice was suddenly pressing through nothing.

The lever was down. It was not level. She could see the lower eye in its bracket, and the hole in the bar, and a thumb's width of daylight between them. She had no more to give it. She was a first-year with a Hold of six and two, and one good arm.

She looked down.

The grey had come to the end of the race. It lay on the bare copper at the head of the ramp, three paces below her and a pace north, two ropes of it as thick as her arm, and it had been reaching for the dark mouth in the wall.

It stopped.

It lay on bars that had nothing in them, and it stopped as a hand stops on a door that it expected to open. Then, along the whole length of it, it let go. It slumped. It ran down off the copper into the bottom of the sump in a soft dry spill, and was only ash.

Twenty paces up the floor, the shine went off the string.

It went in a wave from the legs upward, as frost goes off a pane when the sun reaches it. The bloom drew in. It gathered to one face of the topmost knot, and sat there.

The knot swung. It was reading again.

It read for a worked thing. It found the nearest one there was. That was a stance, wide open, on a shelf across a ditch.

It turned to her.

It had no eyes. She had told a warden that once. But the whole blunt frosted top of a seven-knot Flask came round across the bright air until it was square on her, and stayed. One leg lifted. It came down a pace nearer the sump.

Her hands began to go.

It came up through the fear, as it always had. *Stride. Three bounds and you're off this shelf.* The Ember stirred against the cuff. Behind it, the hand door rose cold and wild, a ward, a tea tray's worth, anything to put between her and that.

If either of them opened, she would be standing over a live trunk on a jam.

"Ember, cold," said Kiva. Her voice was a croak. "*Hand. Cold.*"

She gave them the call. She gave it to the Ember and to the hand door, one each, like crusts to dogs. She kept her eyes on the hole in the bar. The left arm had started to shake at the elbow.

Something landed beside her.

It was as light as a cat on a wall. The shelf hardly spoke. Dull yellow struts flexed and held, on the last half pace of grating that there was, and a hand shot past her shoulder for the pin on its chain.

"It's high," said Selka Dray. She was not out of breath. "It won't go. It's a thumb off. Get it down."

"I can't."

"Then move your elbow."

Two hands came down on the crossbar, outside her own. Dray picked her feet up off the shelf. She hung there with all sixty kilos of herself, like a child on a gate.

The thumb's width closed.

"Hold that. Hold it—"

One of the hands let go. There was a scrape of steel.

*Clack.*

"It's in." Dray's boots were on the iron again. "It's pinned. Let go."

"Yours?"

"It's the *pin's,* you—yes. Mine. Let go of it, Rowan!"

"Ember, cold," said Kiva. "Hand, cold."

Up the floor, a wall of silver had come up like a sunrise. Three wards, bright and seamless, walked in to ten paces. The frosted face wavered. It hung between a girl on a shelf and three women in blue.

She gave the wall its weight back.

*Thank you.*

She said it to two hundred years of yellow brick. She shut the still door behind it, cleanly, with nothing left in the doorway, and stood on an iron shelf with no doors open at all.

The lever stayed where it was.

The Flask turned its face to the Graywater line, and walked north.

---

For a moment she only stood.

There was a very great deal the matter with her. She took it as it came. Her left arm hung from the shoulder like a wet towel, and the first two fingers were bone white. Her legs were shaking. There was something running over her lip.

And the dram was gone.

It was not all gone. But she could feel the place where it had been whole, half an hour ago, at a gate. A third of it was simply missing. A bound cost nothing, and a stance next to nothing; she had stood a whole carry on one for a tenth.

It had gone at the top. It had gone in the gap, in the time it took to say her name, when she had shut one door and set the other and been nothing in between. Something had carried a Stride across that gap. Whatever it was had drunk a third of a dram to do it.

The lines on her chest were not burning. She put her right hand up to be sure. They had not fought. They ached, all three, the whole length to her arm, with the deep, stupid ache of a muscle that has been used for the first time in its life.

Her fingers were on her breastbone. They were on the lines.

There was something else there.

It was small. It was exactly round. It lay between the bottom line and the next, where it had lain for four years, and it was warm.

She knew what her own skin felt like through a shirt. This was warm as a hand is, when somebody lays it flat on your chest to feel whether you are breathing.

Kiva closed her fist round the disk through the cloth.

She did not ask it anything. She noticed that. She had nothing to ask. She only stood on a shelf with blood on her lip and held it, as you would hold a cup that somebody had put into your hands.

And the shelf went away.

---

She was high up. Very high.

They were coming. From every road. She could not see their faces, only their light, and no two lights were alike.

This time there was sound.

Each of them had a note. She had not known that before, or had not kept it. They were not in tune. They came on toward one another over a dark country, each humming its one note, old and bright and unlike.

She tried to find the nearest.

There was no nearest. Every time she had hold of one light, another came in past it from the edge, and the edge went on. She, who could tell a lit cell through a wall, could not hold one of them still for the length of a breath.

The lights touched.

The notes did not make a chord. They made one note.

She looked up. There was no sky.

And the one note went on, with nothing over it, and what was above came down into it, full and enormous and paying attention.

Below her, very small and very exact, something answered.

*Tick.*

---

"—cold. *Cold,* you said. Rowan. *Rowan!*"

Somebody had her by the belt.

She was against the wall. The brick was rough on her cheek. A fist was knotted in the back of her belt and another in her collar, and both were hauling her inward, hard, away from the edge of an iron grating that she was a good deal nearer to than she had been.

"You stopped," said Selka Dray, in her ear. Her voice was furious. "You were saying *cold* at your hands, and you stopped, so I said it. I don't know what it's for. Don't you dare go in that ditch. I've only just pinned this."

Kiva got her feet under her. The shelf was there. The Pit was there, dark and roaring.

"How long?"

"I don't know. A few breaths. You went white and started to lean." The grip on her collar did not ease. "Sit down. Against the wall. *Down.*"

She sat. There was nowhere else. She sat on the grating with her back to the brick and her bad leg sticking out over the sump, and found that her right fist was still closed at her chest.

It was still warm.

Dray was looking at the fist. Then she was looking at Kiva's face.

"You're bleeding," she said. "Your nose."

"I know."

"What have you got?"

"Nothing. It's—" She could not think of a lie. She had never been any good at them. "It's a thing I wear. It's warm. It isn't, generally."

Dray plainly did not think that was an answer. She let go of the collar, slowly, and stood with her back to the wall, a pace away, with her yellow struts gone home and her arms folded tight.

"The lamps jumped," she said.

"What?"

"Just now. When you went white. All of them, round the top. Once." Dray jerked her chin at the rim, where forty dead lamps stood on their posts against the morning. "There's no current in them. I pinned it myself. They jumped anyway."

Kiva looked up at the lamps. They were only lamps.

She had no answer to that either. She put it with the plate in the meadow and the tick in the bag, and held on to the disk.

---

Perrin Cade had asked, once, to be told before he was rescued.

He thought about that on the capstan. It was a poor time to think of anything, but his mind had always wandered when his body was busy, and his body just then was a boy leaning on an oak bar with two cracked ribs.

"Two to a bar," said Lowen Brask. "Cade, with me. Walk it. Nobody plants till I say."

There were eight of them. Four were the Hall's duty crew, third-years with necks, and two were strangers from the east wall, and one was Perrin, and one was the man who had taught him to fall. They walked the capstan round against its pawl, and the chains paid out over the wheels, and the iron lid came down in its frame a link at a time. *Clack. Clack.*

He knew this capstan like his mother's mangle. It took two minutes with four. With eight it ought to take one.

It was a third of the way down when he heard her.

It came the whole length of the Pit, thin and cracked and unmistakable. *Rowan! Ember, to still!*

He could not turn round. The bar was across his chest.

*There she goes,* thought Perrin. *And I'm stuck on a mangle.*

Whatever came after was lost under the lamps going.

They went all together. One moment there was a ring of pale lights round the rim and a note in his back teeth that he had stopped noticing on Monday. The next there was neither. The note stopped as if it had been cut with scissors.

It was a great deal worse without it.

"Lattice is off!" That was the warden, somewhere in her shed. "It's off, it's off, I've nothing!"

The gap under the lid was as high as a man's waist. There was blue in it.

Then there was grey.

It came out under the iron low and blunt, a leg as thick as a quay bollard, feeling for the cinder. Another came beside it. Behind them, the deep tired blue bulged outward into the morning.

"That's a second one," said one of the duty crew. His voice had gone up like a girl's. "Brask, that's a second—"

"I see it. Walk."

They walked. The lid came down two more links, and met the top of a grey leg, and stopped.

The bar kicked back into Perrin's chest. Something in his side said a thing that he did not let out of his mouth.

"Plant," said Lowen Brask. "All of you. Now."

Eight still doors opened at once.

Perrin felt the platform take them. He had never been part of that before. It was like standing in a wall. The bar stopped kicking. The lid sat on the grey leg with the weight of two hundred years of cast iron and eight people who could not be moved, and the leg shoved upward, and nothing went either way.

"It's pushing," said Brask. He sounded interested. He always did. "Good. It's got its whole self on that. Listen to me, the lot of you. This is a caller's rule, and it isn't ours, and I learned what it's worth in their lane. When I call *give*, we give it the bar. One step back, all together. Then we take it again and we don't stop. Cade, what did I tell you every morning for a month?"

"Arrive before you're there," said Perrin, through his teeth.

"That's the one. Ready, all of you. *Give.*"

They gave.

The capstan spun back a quarter turn. The lid jumped up a hand's width in its frame. Beneath it, the leg that had been straining against a ton of iron found suddenly that there was no iron, and shot upward and out, and lost its footing on the cinder.

"*Take!*"

Eight still-doors drove the bars.

Brask had asked for a walk with a wall's weight in it, not a stance, and Perrin gave him that, and his ribs gave him the rest.

The lid came down. It came down the hand's width and a yard more, in one long rattle of chain, onto a thing that was off balance and half out of a doorway. It did not stop this time.

It seated in its stone sill with a boom that he felt in the soles of his feet.

On the cinder outside, a piece of grey as long as his arm lay by itself. It twitched once. Then it let go and poured, and was a heap of warm ash on the open floor with nobody to catch it.

"That's open," said somebody, stupidly. "Somebody ought to catch it."

"That's sweepings," said Brask. "Let it lie. Don't any of you go lit near it." He had not taken his hands off the bar. "Stay planted. All of you. Give me a good long while standing on this before I believe it."

Perrin stood on it.

He had said *ow*. He was fairly sure that he had said it loudly. There was a hot wire in his side where two ribs had nearly finished knitting, and he suspected that they would now have to begin again.

A hand came down on his shoulder, broad and careful.

"I'm telling you first," said Lowen Brask. "I'm going to ask you to stand here a while longer, on those ribs, because you're the only one on this bar who didn't have to be told twice. After that I'll carry you to the healer myself."

"I mind, you know."

"I know you do. Stand."

Far off at the dark south end, a girl's voice was saying one word over and over. It was not Kiva's. It sounded cross.

Perrin listened to it, and stood.

---

The lamps went out, and the thing in front of Rhea Sorn went back to being a husk.

She saw it happen. The shine drew in off the whole column and sat on one face of the top knot, a thick dull bloom. It was thicker than it had any right to be. It had not broken when the boy cut the first rung. It had slid, and she had seen it slide, and thought, *that's new,* and then the floor had gone dark and there had been no leisure for the thought.

It swung south. It had found something on the wall.

"Mace."

"I see it. Line, walk in. Ten paces. *Brighter.*"

He had three. Stroud was on a stretcher at the gate with her hands in wet cloth.

That was Rhea's, and she did not dress it. Hollis had asked whether she could cut what had gone down through the grating, and she had said *a ward might,* and Greta Stroud had gone onto one knee beside a leg as thick as a tree because the senior Edge of her company thought it a fair notion. It had been a fair notion. Rhea had had a great many of those in twenty-three years, and until this morning not one of them had been paid for with another woman's hands. She would go to the gate when the last rung was bagged. It would give nothing back.

Three Torch wards made a wall that was bright enough and not wide enough, and Rhea heard Mace say a name that was not on his roster.

"Pryce. Behind Imrie's shoulder. Hands below your belt. You're not in the line. If she says *take,* you take, and you hold it till she's breathing, and you give it back."

A girl in a plain dark coat came across the cinder at a walk and stood where she had been put.

The face came round to the wall. Rhea let out a breath.

She did not look south again. She had seen what there was to see, and she had a Flask. But it sat at the back of her eyes while she worked: a girl crossing three paces of air and stopping on iron as if she had been set there with a hand. There had been nothing on her shins.

Rhea had run the Cellhouse plate four times, six weeks ago, to be sure what a jam looked like. That had not been one.

*Not for draft this season,* she had told Doran then. She had been right about that much. This was a different thing, and it had happened once.

"Whose sheet's nearest?" she said.

"Old Four's." Hollis was at her elbow. "Their catcher's a third-year. He's waited two springs for this."

"Then he can have it."

The quiet boy was kneeling at his hem with a face like paper. She walked round the back of the string, a long pace off, and stopped.

"Catcher?"

He started as if she had struck him. She saw him swallow. He looked at his canvas, and at the cinder, and not once at her.

"Not yet," he said. It shook. "It's two paces north of me. I need it two paces north."

"Mace."

"Step. Step."

"Now."

Her left foot came down. Her right.

She had never had a Stride. She had stood under knots for twenty-three years. She raised her fist and let the blade be as long as it needed to be, a line of white from her knuckles to the place behind the frost, and took it away again.

Sixty drams came down. The skin slid.

It did it again, as it had for the boy. It tipped and went down one rung and sat on the next knot, and it was thicker.

"Dark."

The line drew off. Ten paces down the floor, three wards and a student's shoulder stood burning in the dead grey morning, and the blind thing swayed, and walked to them, and leaned.

*It likes a gate.*

Somebody had told sixteen children to put out every light on this floor. It was inside the rules. Hollis had let them, and she knew him well enough to know that he would have his own hide for it before the board did. She would have left a wall. So would he.

Neither of them would have guessed what it would find instead. She was honest enough to grant that, and she was in no position this morning to be superior about other people's notions.

She cut the third. She cut the fourth, with a student team from the east wall bagging under Bettan's eye, and on the fourth she heard Imrie's breath go ragged.

"Take," said Imrie.

Rhea was looking at the knot. She listened for the join, as she always did, and for the space of a few slow breaths there was a place in the Graywater wall that was held by a seventeen-year-old in a laundry coat.

She did not hear it.

"Give," said the girl. Her voice did not shake. "Yours."

"Mine."

*Well,* thought Rhea, and cut the fifth.

By the sixth she could not find the edge of the skin by eye.

It had come down five rungs without breaking, and every rung it had kept what it had. It lay on the reader now like a second knot, a shell as thick as her wrist. A blade went in behind the edge of a skin. This one had hardly any behind left.

She found it by ear. She stood under it with her fist dark and listened to it ring on Mace's wall, and where the note was truest, she put the line.

It went. But it had been lit for a full second. She had not been lit for a full second since the Ferren floods.

"Two left," said Hollis. "The sit-down. I've no second blade, Rhea. Mine's at Brickfields with a broken wrist, and I won't put a student under that."

"No."

She looked at what remained. It was low and wide, six legs and two great knots in its belly, side by side. The skin had come down with nowhere further to go. It lay over both of them like a lid on a pan.

Two knots. The rule said two blades, together, or it would fall wherever it liked.

She had cut two with one line twice in her life. Once it had not worked, and a catcher had lost a foot.

"I want it turned," she said. "Broadside to me. Both knots in a row, edge on, so that one line goes through the pair. Mace, walk your wall round to the east. Slowly. Bettan, a double sheet under it, and your word and nobody else's."

"You'll have it when I've got it," said Bettan.

It took the best part of a minute. The wall went round in an arc, a step at a time, and the low grey thing shuffled after it, turning. Rhea stood with her fist at her side and did nothing whatever. She had long since stopped minding how that looked.

She had time to look south.

There were two of them on the shelf. The red-haired one was standing against the brick with her arms folded. The other sat with her back to the wall and one leg stuck out over the ditch, and there was blood on her chin.

Her fist was closed at her collar.

Rhea had been nineteen. She had been in brown water to her chest, in a culvert, in the dark, and a tall woman with a scarred jaw had got an arm round her and held on. With the other hand that woman had been gripping something at her own throat, hard, as a person grips a rail.

She had never asked what Ilse Corran felt in her fist that night, or what the girl felt in hers.

She knew what it looked like from across a floor.

"Now," said Bettan.

Her left foot. Her right.

The line went out level, at the height of her hip, as long as a tram pole. It went in at the edge of the shell and through one knot and through the other, and she held it lit until she felt both open. A second. A second and a half.

It sat down.

It went onto the double sheet with a sigh, all of it, and four catchers were on it. One of them was a student, with brass on his collar and tears running openly down his face, hauling a cord.

Rhea let the metal run home. Her arm ached to the shoulder.

Doran Mace came and stood by her while the ash was still open, and she did not look round at him.

"That girl on the shelf," she said. "Not for draft. Not this year. But get her team attached to us on the second step, if the board gives one, and put them on the van for a night." She flexed the hand that had held the blade. "I'll take the shift myself."

Mace looked at her for a long moment, and then he nodded.

Rhea walked to the west gate, where the stretcher was, and sat down on the step of the cart beside it.

---

The keeper came through his iron door at last.

He stood in the doorway with his keys in his fist, heaving like a bellows, and looked at two students on his shelf and a lever pinned level in its lower eye.

"Who brought that down?"

"She did," said Selka Dray. "I hung on it."

Dunmore looked at Kiva's strapped knee. He looked at the three paces of sump. He opened his mouth, and shut it, and took a brass padlock off his ring and put it through the pin.

"Well," he said. "I don't know what I'll tell the Works."

He took them out by the road stair. It was a long, steep one. Dray went up them behind Kiva with one hand an inch from her belt, not touching it, and said nothing the whole way.

At the top, in the daylight, with the Brickfields road in front of them and the Pit roaring behind the wall, she spoke.

"I've not changed my mind."

"I didn't think you had."

"You were on a floor and a branch went dark. Again. I'll be taking that to the Chair." The red head was up. Her face was pale and set. "You called that floor dark. All of it. I was at the rail and I heard you call it, and there wasn't a reserve along that rail who couldn't tell me whose notion it was."

"It was mine."

"Yes." Dray seemed thrown by having it handed to her. She recovered. "I'd have pinned that lever for anybody. Don't make anything of it. It's nothing to do with you."

"I know. Thank you for calling it."

"Somebody had to," said Selka Dray, and walked away down the road toward the keeper's gate.

Kiva put her hand inside her collar.

The disk was cooling. She could feel it go. By the time she had limped the length of the wall to the gate, it was the cold of a coin on a windowsill, and she could not have proved to anyone that it had ever been otherwise.

---

They had bagged the last of it before she reached the table.

All she saw was the floor afterward: a dark arena under a bright sky, with seven canvas bags in a row along the east wall and the Kiln shut behind its iron. Graywater's people were carrying their coats. A healer's cart stood at the west gate.

Her own bag lay on the trestle where Perrin had set it down.

"It's to be panned inside the hour," said the clerk in the apron. He had not left his post. He was a brave boy, she thought, or a very frightened one. "It's the rule. I've been watching the clock. You've a quarter of an hour."

Kiva held up her left hand. It was shaking like a leaf on a twig, and the first two fingers were the color of candle wax.

"I can't."

"Then tell us how," said Anwen.

She had come down the floor with her coat open and her hair stuck to her forehead. Kellan was behind her. Behind him, walking very carefully, with Lowen Brask's hand under his elbow, came Perrin.

"I said don't pan it without me," said Perrin. "I've been thinking about it for a quarter of an hour. It's all that's kept me upright."

Kiva sat on an upturned crate and called it.

It was the strangest catch of her life. She did not touch a grain. Anwen had the right pan and Kellan the left, and he held it as he had held a corner of canvas, with deep suspicion. Perrin cut cords with his teeth. She told them when to fill and when to level, and she listened for the lids.

"Seal check."

"Sound," said Anwen.

"That one isn't. I heard it. Kellan, turn it over."

He turned it over. A thread of grey ran out along the rim.

"How did you hear that?" he said.

"It didn't click. Do it again."

It made eight tins, and part of another. Bettan came with her brass balance and set them on the pan one at a time, moving the little weights with a fingernail.

"Fifty-one," she said. "And two-tenths."

The mark was fifty.

Nobody cheered. Perrin sat down on the cinder. Kellan looked at the eight and a bit tins as though they had been a board he could not make add up.

"We called it dark," he said. "The whole floor. It walked off and found the bus, and a woman's hands are burned, and a branch is out. And we've passed."

"So you have," said Gower.

He had come down the east wall with one end of Old Four's pole on his shoulder and the quiet boy at the other, and their bag swinging between them. It was the rung that Rhea Sorn had cut. He set his end down on the cinder to say it.

"I voted against, on Thursday. I'd give a good deal to be able to tell you I saw this coming. I didn't. I thought it would go and lean on the lattice house and frighten you at twenty-two paces, and I'd be right in a small way." His sardonic mouth had not much in it. "I've waited two springs to cut a rung of a Flask. I lit a blade on top of that thing to be looked at, and it wouldn't look. And then the best Edge in the district cut my rung for me, on my own catcher's word, and did it so well that I'll be a long time getting over it."

"I'm sorry," said Kiva. Her voice was a thread.

"Yes. I thought you would be. It doesn't seem to be a thing I can use." He took up the pole again. "The next time somebody asks me to put a light out, Rowan, I'll want the reason from a person who's watched a string do it. No offense."

He did not wait to hear whether there was any.

"Your rung's passed," said Hollis Vane.

He had come up behind Bettan. He looked ten years older than he had at ten o'clock.

"Subject to the grade," he said. "Fifty-one and a fifth, bagged on your own sheet, sealed in good time, nothing printed. The rest is for the board. I'll say this once." He looked at the four of them in turn. "I told you that I'd leave a gate, and I let you not. It was inside the rules. I didn't know a string could take a bus. Nobody did. That's mine as much as yours. The next part is mine alone. I said the lid first, and I sent a candidate to that lever with the lid a third down, knowing what the lattice would do. I'd weigh the pumps against that doorway the same way tomorrow. But Mr. Cade's ribs are in the sum, and they're mine to answer for."

"Brask told me first," said Perrin, from the cinder. "Before he asked. I'd recommend it."

Vane looked down at him for a moment, and nodded, as he would have nodded to an officer.

His pale eyes went to Kiva. "You told me it was keeping time. I looked at that board twenty seconds sooner than I would have. I've been trying to work out what I'd have done with twenty seconds less."

She had no voice to answer him with.

"Kiva." Anwen was kneeling in front of the crate. Her palms were an inch above Kiva's chest, and her face had gone still and inward. "The lines aren't burned. They're—I don't know what they are. Tired. But your hand. When did those fingers go white?"

"On the lever."

"That's forty minutes." Anwen opened her eyes. "Aske said the same hour. Not the next day. I was in the room." She turned her head. "Kellan. The reserve."

"It's sealed."

"It's sealed for this. Her hand, now. It gets opened, all four of us agreed." She was already getting up. "Perrin?"

"Agreed," said Perrin, from the ground. "Somebody help me up."

"Agreed," said Kellan.

Kiva had her right hand up before anybody thought to ask her.

She looked across the dark floor.

A woman was coming over it from the keeper's gate with a tool roll under her arm, walking fast. Beside her, on a stick, slower, came another.

She did not know which of them she wanted to tell first. She found that she was going to have to tell them both.
