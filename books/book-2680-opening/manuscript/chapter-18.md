# Chapter 18 — Kiva: A Gate to Lean On

The purse sheet weighed more than Kiva did.

That was Perrin's opinion, and he gave it twice on the Kennel stair, carrying one end of the roll on his shoulder like a man bringing home a carpet. Anwen had the other end. It was six paces of waxed canvas with a hem full of lead, wound on a pole, and the warden had lent it on the understanding that it came back dry.

"Set it," said Druce.

It was four o'clock on Friday, and the settling floor was the same long brick room it had always been, with its three drained tanks and its plank bridges and the yellow line at the west end. Kiva had laid a cloth here three times. She walked it again anyway. She dropped a pinch of dead ash at the middle tank and watched it fall straight, and another at the north gutter, where it slid a finger's width toward the grates.

"Here. Between the first tank and the second. Clear of the gutter."

They unrolled it. It covered the floor like a tarpaulin over a cart. Her own two-pace cloth would have gone into one corner of it. Kiva knelt at the east hem and found the cord where it came out of its brass eye, a waxed line as thick as her little finger, and gave it a pull. The whole hem drew in an inch, all the way round, with a whisper.

"It's a bag," said Perrin. "I've carried the washing in a smaller one."

"Roles," said Druce, from the lever.

"Renn. Kill."

"Pryce. Hold."

"Cade. Carry. And a corner. I'd like the corner in the book."

"Rowan. Catch. Door declared: none."

The warden wrote that down without looking up. Then he threw the lever, and the double steel door at the west end groaned, and the thing that stood up out of the dark behind it had frost on its face.

"Skin," said Kiva.

"Seen." Kellan was already walking. He went along the north side, behind the second plank bridge, dark, with his fist down.

Anwen's ward came up low and leaning. The husk read it and came on down the floor, and its skinned front rang on the silver with the thin note of a wet finger on a glass. She walked it backward. She did not hurry. Her heels found the hem of the sheet and crossed it, and she kept going until the grey thing's four lent feet were on the canvas, and then she stopped and stood under her wall.

"On," said Kiva. "All four."

Kellan's left foot came down. His right came down. His shoulder dropped.

He lit.

It was the same short hard line of blue-white, and it went in behind the edge of the frost as a knife goes in behind the lid of a tin. The skin stood a heartbeat alone. Then it fell and broke, and the husk poured after it.

"*Count!* Dark! One. Two."

"One. Two." Kellan was backing off. Six paces. His hands were open.

"Corners!"

Anwen's ward folded and she dropped at the south hem. Perrin had the west. Kellan came in at the north, and knelt, and for a moment the best blade in the first year of the Crooked Line School held a fistful of lead-weighted canvas and plainly had no idea what to do with it.

"Up," said Kiva. "All together. Bring it over the top of the heap. Eleven. Twelve. Kellan, higher, you're tipping it at Perrin."

"I'm aware."

"I'm wearing it," said Perrin. "Thirteen."

The four hems came up. The heap slid to the middle and lay in the hollow of the canvas, warm and grey, lifting and settling. It was listening. She felt it through her knees, and she felt the few grains in it that had been skin, already faintly bent, like a spoon seen through water.

"It's curved," she said aloud. "Some of it. Same as before. Twenty."

She hauled the cord.

It was nothing like sealing a tin. The neck drew in hand over hand, stiff and heavy, and when it was shut to the width of her two fists, she had to take three turns round it and pull the end through and lay the wax tab over the knot. She had practiced on the stair. On the stair, there had been nothing in the bag.

Her left hand did the first turn. It did the second. On the third, she lost the cord.

She had not dropped it. It was lying across her first two fingers exactly where she had put it. But those fingers were reporting late, as Healer Aske had said, like a clerk who takes his time, and for the length of a breath she did not know whether the turn was on the neck or on her own thumb.

"Thirty-eight," said Anwen. "Kiva?"

"Cord check. I can't feel the third turn. Somebody look."

Anwen looked. "It's crossed. Under, not over." Her hand came in, quick and neat. "There. Now."

Kiva pulled the end through and pressed the wax down with the heel of her right hand.

"Sealed. Fifty-eight."

"Fifty-nine," said Kellan. "Sixty."

The listening stopped. Four people knelt round a canvas bag on a brick floor and looked at each other.

"We did it," said Perrin. "We bagged a dog."

"Hm," said Warden Druce.

He came down from the lever. He walked round the four of them and the bag, slowly, with his grey fingers hooked in his apron, and then he stopped behind Kellan's shoulder and stayed there.

"Fifty-eight's inside the count. The cut was as clean as I've had on this floor. I'll weigh it, and I expect to be pleased." He waited. "Now, where was I standing, just then, while you were at it?"

Nobody spoke.

"By the lever," said Anwen.

"I was by the second tank. I walked down the floor at the count of thirty and stood four paces behind Mr. Renn for the best part of half a minute. I coughed, once." He did not sound triumphant. He sounded tired. "None of you turned a head. You'd no head to spare. That's what I wanted you to have in your knees before the Pit. On a Handful it doesn't signify, for the Handful's in the bag. On a Flask there are seven more of it, and they're standing where I stood."

Kiva looked at the place. It was a bare patch of brick. She had not known there was anybody on it.

"That's what the other three teams are for," said Kellan.

"It is. So you'd best hope they're watching the string and not watching you. And when it's their rung, you'd best watch it for them, and not your own bag going off to the table." The warden bent and took hold of the neck of the canvas. "Three-and-twenty and a bit, I'd say, by the heft. It goes back on my shelf, mind. You bought the afternoon. You didn't buy the ash."

It was twenty-three and two-tenths on the scale. It was the best catch that Team Seven had ever made, and not a grain of it was theirs.

Druce wrote *Second this month* in the Kennel book, under the date. Then, a line lower, in the same large soft hand, *Catcher reports curved. Again.*

---

On Saturday the third team went to the Wren House and lost by one.

Kiva did not write much of it down afterward, because there was so little of it that had happened to her. The Wren House fought its Third Division lanes in a court they called the Shallows, a long tiled floor under a hand's depth of water, and their third team was four hand-doors who moved through it like herons. She declared the still door. She took Donny's cousin by the head poles in the first carry and let her weight go down through the cold water into the tile.

And nobody came.

They did not lean on her. They did not pull. One of them looked at her, once, as a person looks at a bollard on a quay to be sure of not walking into it, and then all four went round her on both sides and took Jude and Noa and Pru apart with wards like closing doors. She stood there for the best part of twelve minutes, holding a sandbag that nobody wanted, with wet knees.

It was six to seven at the end. Jude said very little on the tram. Pru said a great deal.

There had been one other thing. When Kiva climbed out of the Shallows after the third carry and looked up to find Mara, she had found a grey coat first, two rows from the front, alone. Lowen Brask had a notebook open on his knee. He had not been watching the Wren House team.

He saw her see him. He gave her the small, civil nod of somebody caught copying another person's homework, and went on writing.

---

The tear over the Ferren weir meadow was as long as a tram.

It hung a little above the grass in the first grey light on Sunday morning, the same deep tired blue as the one on the Sag Line, and Kiva felt it on the back of her neck from the moment she came over the embankment. It was not a thumb this time. It was the flat of a hand.

Sixty-odd candidates sat along the top of the bank in their coats, with the river loud and brown on their left and the weir roaring. Nobody had told them to be quiet. Nobody was talking.

Below them, the Home Line had staked a square forty paces to a side. There were eight ward-holders on it instead of four, and their silver was so faint in the dawn that Kiva kept losing it and finding it again. Down the middle of the square, in a row, somebody had laid out eight purse sheets end to end, pale against the grass, like bedsheets on a drying green.

"That's the ladder," said Kellan. He had a watch in his hand, his mother's, with a sweep for the seconds. "Laid flat. One sheet a rung. They mean to walk it down the row."

Inside the square stood ten people in slate blue.

Four of them made a short line across the head of the first sheet, shoulder to shoulder, with their hands low. A bald brown man stood behind them with his arms folded, and Kiva knew Doran Mace by the way he did not fidget. Two more waited at the sheet's hems with a knot of line-hands. And a little apart from all of them, with her back to the bank and her hands in her coat pockets, stood a tall woman with a white streak in her dark hair.

"That's her," said Perrin, very low. "That's the poster."

"I know."

"She's just standing there."

"She's Edge," said Kellan. "Only Edge. She hasn't a Stride." He frowned. "I don't see how she gets to the top knot. It'll be over her head by the height of a door."

Behind the four in the line, half a step back from the third, stood a fifth figure in a plain dark coat with no blue on it. She had a shuttered lamp at her feet and a coil of waxed cord over one shoulder. She was holding very still, as a person holds still who has been told exactly where to put her feet.

Kiva had known Anwen Pryce since she was nine. She had never before seen her from so far away.

The blue bulged.

---

It did not heap out as the Handfuls had. It unfolded.

Kiva watched grey come down out of that long slit and stand, and go on standing. First there were legs, six of them, as thick as the bollards on the tram quay, set under a body as long as a cart. Then from the middle of the body a column went up, and up, taller than any of the people in front of it, and all the way up the column there were swellings, one above the other. She counted them without meaning to. There were eight, with the lowest two down among the legs.

The topmost was the size of a barrel. It had a bloom on it, like frost on a turnip.

"Skin," said sixty people, almost together, under their breath.

"Third in my life," said Warden Druce, somewhere along the bank. "I'd begun to think I'd imagined the other two."

The four wards came up as one wall.

She had seen Anwen hold a husk's face. She had seen four Home Line holders stand all day on the Sag Line. This was neither of those. This was four people making a single thing between them, with no seam in it that she could find, and they brought it up *under* that barrel-sized knot as a hand goes under a tray. The top of the column swung. It found the wall. The six legs began to walk.

It rang when it met the silver. The note was the wet-glass note, and it was a great deal lower, and she felt it in her back teeth.

"Walking it," said Mace. He did not shout. His voice carried the whole length of the meadow. "On my count. Step. Step."

The line went backward down the first sheet, and the Flask came after it, and the frosted face stayed square on the wall all the way.

The woman with the white streak took her hands out of her pockets.

She walked round the back of it. She did not hurry. She might have been going round a parked van to see whether the tailboard was up. Her right fist hung at her side with dark metal on it, and when she was behind the column, a long pace off, she stopped and looked across the sheet at the square man kneeling by its hem.

"Catcher?"

"Not yet," said the square man. He was looking at the grass. "It's a foot short. Another step, Mace."

"Step."

"Now."

Rhea Sorn's left foot came down. Her right came down.

She did not jump. Kiva understood that afterward. In the moment, she only saw a woman standing flat on the ground with her fist raised, and a line of clean white light going *up* from it, no wider than a finger, twice the length of her arm and then three times, to the place behind the frost where the grey was only grey.

It was there, and then it was gone.

The skin stood by itself against the sky. It fell. Sixty drams came down the front of the column like sand out of a split sack, onto the middle of the sheet.

"Dark," said the square man. "One."

"Seven-tenths," said Kellan. His thumb was white on the watch. "She was lit for seven-tenths of a second."

The line-hands had the corners up before Kiva had reached five. The square man did not look at the Flask. That was the part she could not stop watching. Seven knots of it stood four paces from his back, blind, on six legs, and he looked at the canvas and the wind in the grass and his own two hands, and at nothing else, and the bag closed.

"Sealed. Thirty-eight."

Thirty-eight. With a neck as thick as his thigh.

And all the while the four wards had not gone dark. They had drawn off ten paces down the row, beyond the next sheet, and stood there, lit and level and steady. The headless thing swayed. Then it turned, slowly, with nothing to read by, and walked to the wall and leaned its blunt top against it, and was still.

"Why don't they drop it?" somebody said, along the bank. "There's a count open. They're a lamp."

"They're ten paces off," said a dry voice. "The rule's three."

Kiva turned her head. A grey-haired woman in an undyed canvas coat was standing behind the candidates with her arms folded, watching the Graywater catcher as one cook watches another. It was Bettan, who had weighed four tins on a bank.

"It likes a gate," said Bettan. "Always leave it a gate. It'll go and sulk at whatever's there, so you'd best decide what's there." She did not take her eyes from the meadow. "Look at his cord hand. He never changes his grip. I've been telling my lot that for six years."

Kiva wrote it down. She wrote both things, one under the other, the grip and the gate, because they had been said in the same breath and she had no way yet to tell which mattered.

---

Anwen Pryce had been told three things.

Stand behind Stroud's left shoulder. Keep your hands below your belt. Do nothing until somebody says your name.

She had been doing all three for two hours and forty minutes, most of them in the dark before anything had come out of the blue at all, and it was the hardest work she had ever done in her life. Her fingers wanted to come up. Since the Flask had stood, it had been worse: every time the wall took the weight of that frosted head, she felt it through the turf and her own ward stirred in her wrists like a dog at a door.

Greta Stroud was third in the line. She was a rangy woman of thirty with a broken nose, and she had said perhaps twenty words to Anwen in two nights on the culverts, most of them *hold this*. Her shoulders were steaming faintly in the cold.

"Relief on two," said Mace. "After the cut."

The second rung had gone like the first. *Catcher? Now.* The white line, the fall, the count. Now the wall stood ten paces down the row with the blind thing leaning on it, and a fifth holder in blue had come up from the tape and stood behind the woman at the end of the line.

Anwen watched it happen from a yard away.

The new wall did not rise in front of the old one, or beside it. It rose inside it, as a hand goes into a glove. For a breath there was a place in the line that was a little brighter than the rest. The first woman leaned. Anwen saw her do it, saw the weight go out of her forearms and into the silver that was not hers, and saw her open her hands only when there was nothing left in them.

She stepped back. The line had not flickered.

*If you can hear the join, it's a bad one.* She had told Kiva that on Thursday, as a thing she understood. She had not understood it. She had only seen it once in the dark.

"You've stopped breathing, Pryce," said Stroud, without turning round.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Breathe. You're no use to me blue." A pause. The next cut was coming; Anwen could feel the catcher measuring. "You want to do that."

"Yes."

"Course you do. Took me a year to get let." Stroud rolled her steaming shoulders. "He's a summer line going. Mace. Six weeks, the culverts and the Sag. Reliefs and lamps. He'll not say till he's sure, he never does. I'm only telling you so you don't go and promise your summer to a school."

Anwen looked up the bank.

She could not help it. They were all up there in a row against the sky, small as birds on a wire. She found Perrin by his width, and Kellan by the way he sat, and between them a dark head bent over a book.

Kiva was writing. Of course she was. She would have the wind in it, and the counts, and where every fall had landed. In a little over a fortnight she would stand in a pit with a canvas bag, and she would need somebody at the south hem who knew which way a cord crossed.

*I want it,* thought Anwen. She was nearly used to the thought by now. *I want it anyway. And I want to be at that hem.*

She did not see, yet, how she was to have both.

"Catcher?" said the tall woman, behind the grey column.

"Now."

---

On the fourth rung, the wind came off the river.

Kiva felt it lift her hair. Below, the grass along the row of sheets leaned over, all one way, and the square man at the hem put up his hand flat without raising his head.

"Catcher?"

"Not yet."

Rhea Sorn stood behind the column with her fist at her side.

It had five knots left, and the fourth from the top was reading now, pressed to the Graywater wall with that low glass hum. It was on the sheet. All six of its feet were on the canvas. The grey behind the frost was an arm's length above her head, and she had put a line of light through that same place three times in four minutes.

She did not light.

Kiva counted, because she counted everything. Ten. The wall leaned. Twenty. One of the four in the line shifted her feet, and Mace said something short. Thirty. The best Edge in the district stood in a wet meadow with a Flask in front of her and sixty students watching, and did nothing whatever.

"She's got it," said Kellan, between his teeth. "She's had it since five. What's she waiting for?"

"Him."

"He's not doing anything."

"He's watching the grass."

The grass stood up. The square man's hand turned over.

"Now."

The white line went up and was gone.

Kellan let out his breath as if he had been the one holding it. He looked at the watch. He looked at it for some time.

"Forty-one seconds," he said. "She had the knot the whole of it. She didn't have the fall." He turned the watch face down on his knee. "I'd have cut at five. I'd have been right about the knot, and it would have gone into the river."

"You'd have asked," said Kiva.

"Would I?"

She thought about a cinder path, and a hand that had started to come up.

"You would now."

He did not answer. But she saw him take out the folded paper, and write on it, and what he wrote was not a number.

They cut the fifth. It was while that rung was being bagged that she saw the other thing, and she nearly did not write it, because it was so small.

The wall had drawn off, as before, ten paces down the row. The blind thing swayed and turned and began to walk to it. Halfway there its legs crossed a square iron plate set flush in the turf, one of a line of them that ran away over the meadow toward the weir.

It stopped.

It stood on the plate for two counts, perhaps three. The lowest of its knots, down among the legs, hung a little slack, she thought, and brushed the iron like the hem of a coat that wants taking up.

Then it went on to the wall and leaned.

*Rung 5. Stopped on an iron plate in the grass, 2–3 counts, on its way to the wall. Bottom knot dragging?* She put a question mark after it. Bettan, behind her, had seen it too. Kiva knew because the dry voice had stopped in the middle of a remark about cord.

Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say. A cow will stop at a puddle.

The seventh and eighth they cut together.

A second blade had come in through the tape, a Home Line man with a red edge, and he and Rhea Sorn stood on either side of what was left. It was not tall now. It was a low, wide, six-legged thing with two great knots in its belly, and it shifted its feet like a tired horse.

"Catcher?"

"Both. On the double sheet. Now."

One white line and one red. It sat down. That was the only word for it. The legs went all at once, and the whole grey mass settled onto the last two sheets with a sound like a sigh, and four catchers were on it before it had finished falling.

Kellan pressed the stem of the watch.

"Eleven minutes and twenty seconds. Eight rungs, and seven of the cuts were hers." He was speaking carefully, as a person speaks who is reading out a figure he does not expect to be believed. "I timed her light. All seven. It comes to under six seconds. She was lit for six seconds in eleven minutes."

"And nobody shouted," said Perrin.

It was true. Kiva went back through it in her head and could not find one raised voice. There had been a Flask on a meadow with a river beside it, and the loudest thing she had heard was a man saying *step*.

"I'd like to go home and be a baker," said Perrin. "I've thought it over. I'd be good. I'd carry the flour."

Down on the grass, a clerk was walking along the row of bags with a slate. He called the tally up to the Home Line officer on the tape, and Kiva heard it.

"Four hundred and forty-six. Of four-eighty."

She tried to do the sum in tins and gave it up.

---

They came up the bank afterward, the ones in slate blue, in no order, carrying their coats.

The candidates had stood to let them by. Kiva stood with the rest, on the side of her good knee, with the book shut in her hand. The square catcher went past rubbing his wrists. Doran Mace went past, and nodded to Druce. Greta Stroud went past with her broken nose and her steaming shoulders, and behind her, with the shuttered lamp, came Anwen.

She did not stop. She could not; she was at somebody's heels. But her eyes found Kiva's, and for an instant her whole tired face lit up with a thing that was too large to be said on a bank, and Kiva grinned at her like a fool.

*Write it down,* Anwen's mouth said. *Then I can read it.*

Rhea Sorn came last.

She walked as she had walked round the Flask, with her hands in her pockets. Close to, she was older than the poster, and there were lines at her eyes that the poster had left out. The pale grey gaze went along the row of students, taking each one in and dropping it, as a grader's finger goes along a shelf.

It reached Kiva. It stopped.

It was not long. It was hardly longer than the look she had given anybody else. But it went, quite plainly, to the open collar of Kiva's coat, where a length of cord crossed her collarbone, and then it came up to her face.

Kiva did not know what to do with that. She stood very straight.

The woman walked on, and said nothing.

"She looked at you," said Perrin.

"She looked at everyone."

"She looked at you *twice*."

"She was at the Stonehand meet," said Kellan. "In the chairs with arms. I thought it was for Pryce." He was frowning after the tall figure. "I don't think it was for Pryce."

Kiva put her hand up and closed her collar.

---

Mara had the trunk book open on the kitchen table that night, and a column of baselines half copied out beside it.

"Read me the next," she said. "Slowly. I've lost my place twice."

Kiva read. It was a thing she had done since she was ten, on evenings when the figures ran long. Mara would not look from the old page to the new and trust her eye to carry a number across the gap. Somebody read, and somebody wrote, and then they changed over and did it again.

"Bank, cell nineteen. Twenty-six seventy-four, nought point one four. Seventy-six, point one five. Seventy-eight, point one seven. This spring, point one eight."

"Up four in six years. That's the same as mast six." Mara wrote. "Go on."

"Feeder, weir sluice motors. Laid twenty-five eighty-eight. Runs under the meadow from the bank to the weir house. Inspection plates every twenty paces." Kiva stopped. "Iron ones?"

"I'd suppose so. What else would they be?"

"Square. Set flush in the grass."

Mara looked up.

"The Flask stood on one," said Kiva. "This morning. After the fifth cut, when it was blind. Only for a couple of counts. Then it went and leaned on the ward, same as the other times." She heard how it sounded. "It's probably nothing. It's flat there. Things stand on plates."

"They do." Mara considered it for no longer than it deserved. "Was the feeder live?"

"I don't know."

"Then you've half a reading. Put it in your book with the half you haven't got written beside it. Next line."

Kiva read the next line.

Later, in her own room, she opened the field book at Sunday and filled in the columns. It was not an exercise, exactly. She had done nothing but sit on a bank. She wrote it up as one all the same, because Orla would ask.

*Exercise:* Watching. Ferren weir. Graywater took a Flask, eight rungs, 11 min 20.

*What went wrong:* Nothing I could see. 446 of 480 bagged.

*Who was hurt:* Nobody.

*What it cost:* Six seconds of light, Kellan says. I don't know what in ash.

*How long to come back:* They walked up the bank carrying their coats.

*Second go:* There wasn't one. On rung 4 she waited 41 seconds with the knot in reach, because the catcher said not yet.

She read that over. Then she filled in the last column, and found that she had more for it than would fit.

*Why:* Their catcher never looked at it once. He trusted four people to hold it and one to cut it and he looked at the sheet. The blade asked him. Every time. And the wards stayed lit ten paces off the whole way through, and it went and leaned on them. Bettan says it likes a gate.

*I don't know yet which of those is the thing to learn.*

She thought of Lowen Brask, two rows from the front, writing.

Under the last line, smaller, she added: *Ask Jude what rule nine is.*
