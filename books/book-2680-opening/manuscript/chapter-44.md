# Chapter 44 — Kiva: Two to the Bar

The man with the saw turned out to be a perfectly good shovel, and by breakfast on Tuesday he had the cross dug up.

Kiva watched it go. She stood in the Old Yard with her hands in her coat, and a stranger in a leather apron put a spade into the mark that she had come down on since the summer and turned it over like a garden bed.

"Two hands down," he said. "There's iron."

It was a lid. It was a yard square, of the old thick sort, with a ring let into it, and it lay at the foot of the leaning post on the side toward the lane. It had been under the packed dirt for so long that the dirt had taken the print of the ring.

Three fitters lifted it with bars. A smell came up, cold and green.

"Lamp," said Mara.

She lay down at the edge of it, in her work apron, and let the lamp go on its chain. Kiva knelt beside her with the second lamp.

It was a square brick shaft, eight feet deep. At the bottom the Ferren main went by, black, a yard wide. The water in it rose a finger on the brick, and fell, and the fall was short. And across the wall side of the shaft, back under the post, hung a plate of iron as broad as a door, held in two grooves in the brick like a window in its sash. Its bottom edge was down in the water a hand's depth, and no further.

"That's a penstock," said Mara. Her voice came up hollow. "That's as pretty a sluice gate as I ever saw. It's hanging on a screw." The lamp swung. "That's what Cade did last night. He brought it down a hand into the water, and the water's had to go round it ever since. And the screw goes up. It goes up through the brick, on a slant. Kiva. Where does that slant come out?"

Kiva looked along it. She did not need a rule to tell her.

"The post," she said.

They all looked at the post.

It was black, and split, and bound with two iron bands. It stood a pace out from its row, and it leaned, and the short iron bar through its top stood a quarter round from where it had stood on Monday afternoon.

"It isn't a post," said the man in the apron. He had gone round to the wall side, and had his thumbnail in the split. "There's iron in the middle of it. It's a wooden coat on a spindle. And that's no split, mistress. That's cut. That's where the coat was let on." He looked up at the bottom wall, thirty feet of brick a yard and a half behind him. "That's why it's out of the row. Put a bar through a post in that row and it'd strike the wall on the first quarter turn."

"And the lean?"

"It's on the rake of the screw. You'd not get a straight spindle down to a gate that's eight feet under and a pace back toward the wall." He patted the black wood, as a man pats a horse. "It was never crooked. It's aimed."

Somebody let out a breath behind them. It was Orla. She had been on her crate by the first shed since before the spade went in, with her stick between her boots.

She did not say anything. She got up, which took her some time, and came down the lane, and stood in front of the post as she might have stood in front of a person.

"One-and-twenty years," she said. "I've called you no help. In so many words. I said it as lately as the eighth month." The crooked face was doing something that Kiva had not seen it do. "I sat under a shut-off valve with my back to it, wondering what it was for, and told six hundred students that I didn't know."

"You didn't," said Kiva.

"No. That's the worst of it. It was true." Orla laid the flat of her hand on the iron band. "I told Vane that I'd hold the lamp for his man. I'll hold it for a fitter. It comes to the same."

Mara had got up off her stomach. She stood at the edge of the shaft, wiping her left hand on her apron.

"I told a watch officer in the seventh month that every device in my house had a meter and a way to shut it down," she said. "I told my niece on Monday week that I'd found the meter for this, and not the other." She looked at the post, and at the hole where a cross had been. "A girl's been landing on it. Since the summer. I've been to that yard to watch her."

She began, rather helplessly, to laugh. Kiva had not heard her laugh like that since before the hall panel went dark.

"Well," said Mara, when she could. "*Two to the bar.* Run it home."

It did not go home.

The old iron bar was too short to get four men on it, so the fitters drove it out of its socket with a mallet and laid it by, and by ten they had a longer one through, an ash pole as thick as a wrist, with two men a side. They walked it on round, and the screw groaned in the brick like a ship. Then it stopped as though it had struck a wall, at much the place where Perrin and Jude had stopped in the dark.

"Rust," said the man in the apron. "And four hundred years of whatever the hill's washed into that slot. The gate's to go down into a groove in the floor of the drain, and the groove's full of silt to the brim. It wants oil, and it wants patience, and it wants somebody down that hole with a rake." He spat on his hands. "I can give you the oil."

Hollis Vane came at noon, as he had said that he would. He looked at the open shaft, and at the bar standing through the post, and at four men in shirtsleeves leaning on it.

"I'm told that I owe a sponsor a lamp," he said.

"You owe me nothing," said Orla. "I'd no reason. I'd only a feeling about one. You said so."

"I did. And I gave it three days on the strength of a girl and a carry team." He looked at Kiva. "Rowan. What's it shut?"

Kiva had been waiting to be asked that. She had worked it out with Mara on their knees by the shaft, in the last hour.

"The hill from the street. Everything above this gate is the School's side. The drain that goes on along the lane, and the hill's rain, and one thing more." She pointed down the shaft. "There's a second mouth. A small one, in the north wall, above the gate. Mara had the lamp on it. It comes in from the core house."

"So that's how it would have reached your core," said Mara, to the keeper.

Ansty had come to look. She stood at the low wall with her keys.

"Round by the post," she said. "And I had a cork in my gully."

"If the gate's down, the breath stops here," said Kiva. "The core house never falls in. It can't; there's a door shut in the road. But it does nothing for the pumps. That's all below."

"And if it's shut, where does the hill's rain go?"

"Nowhere," said Mara. "It fills this yard to the top step in a wet night. So I shut it when I must, and not an hour sooner, and I open it when the bank's been seen to." She looked at the bar. "Supposing that it shuts at all."

"And if there's a thing in the street with an arm to put down?"

Mara did not answer at once.

"Every grating on Stopgate will be planked and bagged by tomorrow noon. Rusk's had carts out since six. The School's gully's done. That's Sorn's way, and it's a good one, and I'm taking it." She nodded at the shaft. "This is the one hole on the hill that I can't plank. I've a man going down it with a rake. It stays open until it's shut itself."

Vane looked at the hole for some time.

"Then I know where I'd go," he said, "if I were a thing that wanted water."

They filled twenty-six and twenty-seven on Tuesday at one, since there was no longer any reason for six hundred families to sit in the dusk.

Both drums went in almost as soon as the cans were home. Mara had her palm on the first. She took it away as one takes a hand off a stove.

"So that's my bank," she said. "Four-and-twenty fresh cans, and every one of them open for the better part of a minute, in a house with the main going by under the floor. If the water's still, I've a chance. If it so much as rocks, I've filled them for nothing." She looked at her hand. "And every can in the Compact's store has had the hair in it since the thaw. They're halfway to listening before I've drawn the stopper. I want ash that keeps no time at all. A pinch, to salt each one. Something that's never heard a beat in its life."

"There's a shelf of it," said Kiva.

It was in the Wren House, at the end of the passage where the tins spent their night. Kiva had not been in that room since the spring. It ran from floor to ceiling, hundreds of small stoppered jars, and Grader Halloran stood in front of it with her arms folded like a woman defending a larder.

"Plain," she said. "You want plain. So does everybody, this week. I've had the Halden technical office here, very civil, and I sent it away." She looked at Mara, and the work apron, and the license in its case. "You're the Works' reader. What's it for?"

Mara told her. She did it in four sentences.

Halloran heard her out with her head a little on one side.

"Four-and-twenty," she said. "A pinch each. And you'll be back for thirty more, for your drums."

"The drums can wait. They're on open links; each of them's alone. If the bank's taken out and the water's quiet, I can do a drum a day through the winter with whatever old stock the wardens have in their cupboards. I'm told there's a woman on Weighbridge Street who can smell a cut."

"Cobb. She can. She's better than my comb." Halloran turned to the shelf. She ran her eye along a row low down, where the jars were dusty. "Handful, plain. It's what we grade against. Every pinch I give you is a pinch I can't hold a doubtful tin up to." She took down a jar, and looked at it against the light, and set it on the table. "Four-and-twenty. I'll choose them. And you'll not have the thirty. Not if the Chair asks. A shelf that gives whenever it's asked nicely isn't a shelf."

"That's fair," said Mara.

"It's not fair. It's what's left when I've done being frightened." Halloran took down another.

Three of Seven had come to carry. Jude was at the door, and Anwen, and Perrin with a crate.

"There's plain nearer than that," said Jude.

He had not said it loudly. He was looking at the cold-room door across the passage, with its three seals.

"I'm only saying it since somebody will. We've two drams of our own that came off a husk on the Sag in the fourth month, before any of this got into the ash. Ward-trace. No hair. It's sat under wax for five months." He scratched his jaw. "That's two hundred pinches, by my reckoning. It'd do the bank and the drums and leave some over. And there's six hundred drams in that room of the loudest ash in Meridian, if anybody wanted a thing for a string to walk to that wasn't a drain."

"No," said Anwen.

She said it without heat.

"The first isn't salt. I said in the seventh month that it isn't training ash, in case anybody was tempted. It's the same answer. Ask me what it's for, Talley."

"What's it for?"

"It's medical. It's for somebody's hand, on a night that we haven't had yet. And ask me what it costs, if we salt a bank with it."

"What's it cost?"

"That night." Her level eyes stayed on Jude. "You're on the line by name. You've a right to say it. I'm glad you did. I'd sooner it was said by one of us and refused than thought by all of us and left lying."

"And the second?"

"That's hers," said Anwen.

Kiva had known that it would come round to her. There was a bag in that room under her red tag.

"I tagged two of them not for the ground," she said. "I'd be the one saying that they were for the ground after all, since we'd found a use." She shook her head. "It's under three seals. One's the Works', and one's a street's, and Mrs. Cobb would walk up here in her overcoat and stand on the step. And I don't know what it would do. I'd be putting six hundred drams of a thing I don't understand on the cinders as bait for another thing I don't understand."

"It's evidence," said Halloran, without turning round. "Nobody burns the only one of anything while I've the key."

"Then that's two things not spent," said Jude. He did not look put out. "I thought I'd ask. A man with a yield line gets to wondering what ash is for."

"It's for the next attempt," said Perrin. "That's what my dad says. He says it's the only thing it's ever bought anybody." He picked up the crate. "If I trip on that stair, I'm going to keep walking and change my name."

Rhea Sorn sat in the Graywater watch room on Tuesday night with the map wall at her back, and did nothing for a long time.

She had a way of working that she had kept for twenty-three years, and she knew it as she knew the weight of her own blade. A call began when a bell rang. She went to it and cut, and then she looked to see what it had cost, and who was hurt, and what was left. It was the whole of her trade. She had never found anything wrong with it.

*I cut first, and I ask the price after.*

She had said that to a girl with her hand in a cloth. She had been rather pleased with it at the time, as one is pleased with a thing that is true and costs nothing.

"You've been sitting there a quarter of an hour," said Pike.

He was mending a hem by the stove. He did it every Tuesday, whether a hem wanted it or not.

"I've been thinking about Sunday."

"I thought that you might be." He bit off the thread. "You said it was too dear."

"It is too dear. I've seen what two of those gaps did to her. I've seen Stroud eat off a spoon." Rhea looked at her own hands. "She's seventeen. She's attached to me. If she goes up over that hole and comes down with a hand that only grips, that's mine. I'm not sorry that I said no."

"I'd not ask you to be."

"Then what is it, Pike?"

He folded the canvas over his knee. He took his time.

"You told her you'd never named a price first. I was by. I thought, that's handsome, that's a thing she'll carry." He looked up. "On Sunday you named one. Good. Then you paid it for her, before she'd opened her mouth. That's not naming a price. That's the same thing you've always done, the other way up. You cut, and she pays after."

Rhea did not answer.

It was a hard thing to hear from a man who had knelt at her hems for the best part of her time on the van. It was harder that he had said it while threading a needle.

*What's there?* she asked herself. It was her oldest question.

A catcher, with eight fingers that reported. A healer who had said he would name the price himself, before, and not by a runner. A sponsor who had stood at a rail and said, *I don't know that it will work.* A girl who had said, in a yard, with the Chair at the gate, *Sorn's right that it isn't hers to have offered,* and had then asked a sensible question about a cage.

*And what's behind me?*

She turned round, in her chair, and looked.

The map wall. A lamp lit for every mast in the district that had a student sitting under it with a drum and a boy to run. And a long way behind that, a culvert on the Ferren in the spring of '57, and a young blade who had gone in too far being hauled out of the wet by a Home Line woman who kept one hand shut on something at her throat.

Ilse Corran had not asked her whether she wanted pulling out. She had been glad of that for twenty-three years.

She had been a casualty. The girl was not a casualty. She kept putting her among them, and it was the wrong place.

There was a stick on the stair. It came up slowly, a step at a time.

"They said I'd find you," said Orla Dane.

She was out of breath. She stood in the door of the watch room in her old coat, and looked at the map wall, and did not ask leave to sit.

"We've never much cared for one another," she said. "I'll save you the trouble of being civil. I've come about my candidate."

"She's my attached catcher."

"She's both. That's what I've climbed your stair to say." Orla lowered herself onto the bench. "I'm her sponsor. If a hand's the price of a thing that she does, I answer for the hand. You're her officer. You answer for the floor, and for who's seated on it, and you've every right to say that it isn't a floor for her." The stick turned in the old hands. "Neither of us answers for her. I thought once that I could. I'd a boy of seventeen and a plan worked out to the last step, and I never asked him anything, since I knew the answers."

"I know about your boy."

"Everybody does. It's why I'm believed." She said it quite flatly. "I'm not asking you to say yes. I'd not, in your place; I don't know that I shall in my own. I'm asking you not to say it's settled. If that thing finds the one hole on the hill, somebody will turn round and look at her whether you forbid it or not. I'd like her to have been left something to say."

"And her aunt?"

"Her aunt has given Rusk a plan with a hole in the middle of it, and told him so, sooner than draw the child into it." Orla almost smiled. "She's licensed. It's a wonderful thing in a guardian."

Rhea sat for a while with her elbows on her knees.

"Then here's how I'd hear it," she said. "If it moors, and if there's no other way, and if she offers it herself without being looked at. I hear the price from her healer, before. I hear from her what she does if it's refused. And I want to know who has every piece of it that isn't hers. Then I say yes, or I don't." She looked at Orla. "It isn't a yes."

"I didn't climb two flights for a yes," said Orla. "I'd have distrusted one."

"That's new," said Pike, to his hem.

"It's an order I can ask in," said Rhea. "I've not had one before."

Kiva went to the Wren House infirmary that evening, at the hour when the lamps were turned down, and found that she had no speech at all.

It was a long room with twelve beds, and Selka Dray had the one by the window. Her left leg lay outside the blanket in a board, from the hip to the heel, and she was reading the Circuit's rules by a shaded lamp, as other people read novels.

She looked up.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"If it's about my rule, I've put it to the Chair already. I'll not take it back."

"It isn't. I want to ask you for something." Kiva stood at the foot of the bed. There was a chair, and she had not been invited to it. "I'll say all of it, and then what I do if you say no."

"You've got that off Pryce. Half the Circuit's doing it." The grey eyes were sunk, and perfectly sharp. "Go on."

"There's a thing that may come up in the street below the School. A string, or something like one. If it does, it'll put an arm down into the drain, and the arm will haul and ease, as that cord did on your leg. If somebody's to take the arm off the water, they'll want its time called. Exactly." She kept her hands still. "I can't call it. I tried on Sunday and I nipped a first-year's fingers. I've one hand that hears and one that doesn't, and I took the middle."

"I heard. Marten told me. He said she folded like a conjuror."

"Brask called it after that. He's good. But if it comes to the real thing, he's wanted on a bar, with Cade; there's a gate to be shut, and it's two still-doors' work." She drew a breath. "You had it across your leg for the best part of a quarter of an hour. You told me how it bites, lying on your back. I don't know anybody in Meridian who's been as near it for as long."

Dray said nothing.

"I'm not seated for it. Sorn's said no, and it isn't to be asked of me, and nobody's asking. So this may be for nothing whatever. You'd be carried up a hill in a chair in the cold, to sit and watch planks." Kiva made herself finish. "And if you say no, it's Brask if he can be spared, and Wace if he can't. Wace has a good ear. He's never had it on him."

There was a long silence. Somewhere down the ward somebody coughed.

"You've not said that I owe you," said Selka Dray.

"You don't."

"You took a thing off my leg."

"That was mine to spend. You told me not to make a speech."

"So I did." Dray put the rules down, open, on the blanket. "I'll tell you what I've been doing for seventeen days. I've been lying here with a blade arm and no leg, being of no use to anybody, and I find that I mind that a good deal more than I mind you. I'd not have thought it possible."

"Nor would I."

"Don't be pleased. It's not a compliment." Her jaw moved. "I'll do it. I've terms."

"Go on."

"There's a needle on you. A mast, in that yard, and Bexley at it with his flags. I'll not call anything of yours that isn't being read. If the red goes up, I stop, in the middle of a word." She held up a second finger, exactly as Jude had once. "Marten carries the chair. With a porter. He'll not let anybody else, and I'll not have him argued with."

"That's two."

"The third is that nobody takes it that I've come round. I objected to you twice, and I think what I thought. I'm calling it because I'm the one who can, and for no other reason."

"Nobody will think it."

"They will. Let them be wrong." Dray picked up the rules again. "It bites hardest at the start of the pull. Then it only holds. I'd call *in* a hair before it lets go, since you'll want the time to move your hands. You'd have learned that if you'd lain under it."

"I'd not have liked to."

"No," said Selka Dray. "I didn't much."

Kiva went down the infirmary stair with her right hand on the rail.

She went through it on the tram home, with her forehead against the cold glass. Sorn had said no, and she had been relieved, and then she had not been. She wanted it to be her. That was not a reason. It was only what was so.

If it came, she would have to say what it cost, and what she would do if it was refused, and who had the parts that were not hers. There were three of those now. The cord was Pru's. Its time was Dray's. The corner, after she had planted it, was Brask's; he had said yes to that one at the Hall on Monday as though she had asked him to hold a ladder. And if Brask was kept at the bar, it was Wace's. She had asked him. He had said, *I've been a post before.*

It did not look like being ready. It looked like a sheet with a great many corners, and somebody else's name on most of them.

Seven settled the three-door dram on Wednesday at five, standing up, on the lid of the Low Lane's quench box.

"Three-door, K. Rowan," said Anwen. "The three-door line, from held. One go. Only on a foreman's direction, under the officer's hand, with the price said first by her healer. Cade keeps the word. If it isn't directed, it goes back to held, whole." She looked at Kiva. "And I've a condition. If it's ever spent, I ask you first what it's for and what it costs. Before each one. And you tell me."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"That's a dram of mine that I didn't expect to see again," said Perrin. "I said *no* to this in the seventh month. I said *yes, with a brake* in the eighth. Tonight I've not the least idea what I think, and I'm saying yes so that there's something to say *off* to."

"Held, thirteen and fifty-three hundredths," said Kellan. "I've had the others' call drams out of the press. They're combed."

The fitters had the gate most of the way down by then.

Kiva had been to look. The ash pole stood through the black post, and four men in shirtsleeves sat on the ground beside it with their backs against the wall. Down the shaft, on a ladder, a boy with a rake was bringing up grey silt in a bucket.

"It's the slot," said the man in the apron. "The last of it, the gate's to go down into its groove in the floor of the drain, and the groove's full to the brim. He's been at it since noon. I'd say midnight. I'd not say it loudly."

The top drum on the hill fell in at twenty to eight.

It went as the others had. There was a crowd at its rail, and a needle that stood, and trembled, and went up without any hurry at all. Nobody cheered and nobody cried out. They had seen it before. A warden rang her handbell once, and people on the top stretch of Stopgate came to their doors and stood there with their coats on.

"Friday for the core house, if it goes on as it has," said Mara. "I shall have my gate down by then if I've to go down that ladder with a spoon." She had the plan under her arm. "And the bank on Thursday night at one, when the sump's lowest. Every operator that Rusk has, and every student who stood a Bench. I've told them to sleep tomorrow afternoon. I don't suppose that one of them will."

The planks were down. Kiva had walked the hill at dusk to see them. Every grating between the tram rails had three thick boards across it and a row of sandbags on the boards, from the Brickfields road to the black gate. It looked like a street that was expecting a flood.

She was in the bottom yard late that night, on no duty at all; she had gone to see whether the boy with the rake had come up.

He had not. There was a lamp down the shaft still, and the scrape of iron on brick.

She was standing at the gap in the low wall, with her hand on the cold stone, when she felt it change.

It did not grow. She would have said, afterward, that it turned over. The slow even push that she had had under her palm all week had been a thing going by. It was not going by any longer. It had stopped, somewhere below her on the hill, and was standing still, and getting deeper, as water deepens behind a hand.

"Noa," she said. And then, as loudly as she had ever said anything, up at the black bulk of the core house roof: "*Noa! What's the drum doing?*"

There was a pause of a breath.

The School's bell rang.

It rang two and two and two, over her head, and it did not stop. And through it came a thin cracked voice that she would have known anywhere.

"*It's the drum! It's the drum, it isn't the mark! It's climbing! It's the drum!*"

She ran to the wicket.

She had no business there. She was recovery, and dark, and seventeen. But the wicket stood open, and there was nobody in it, and she looked down Stopgate.

It was a long straight hill, wet, with a lamp at every twentieth pace. Near the top of it, not forty paces below her, the lamps had a different color to light.

It lay in the crown of the road. It was between the tram rails, where the planks were, and it ran from the rail of the twenty-eighth drum house up to the rail of the twenty-ninth, sixty paces and more. It was thin. It was blue.

It was not a stroke standing in the air, like the gap at the edge of a door. It was lying down. It was a seam, a hand wide, in the surface of the street, as though somebody had drawn a knife along a tablecloth and the color of the table were showing through.

The planks over the nearest grating had gone white. She saw frost run along them, and round the sandbags, like spilled milk.

Then, the whole length of that seam, things began to come up.

They did not come one above another. She had seen a string climb out of a hatch a knot at a time. These rose together. Eight grey shoulders came up through the blue, side by side, eight paces apart, each on a pair of legs as thick as lampposts, and between each and the next there ran a white cord at the height of a man's chest.

It was a string. It was a string laid on its side.

There was not a top to it. Every one of the eight had a blunt head of its own, and every head was turning, slowly, a quarter of a circle, like a man looking for his hat.

Below her, on the hill, the wardens' handbells went off all at once.
