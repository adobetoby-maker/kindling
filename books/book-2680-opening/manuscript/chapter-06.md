# Chapter 6 — The Account

They put her in a side room this time, not the long room with the fish on the ceiling. It had one bed, one chair, one window over the Wren House herb garden, and a door that the healer left open a hand's width.

"So I can hear you," said the healer, "if you decide to open anything."

"I won't."

"You said that last time." The healer set down her tray: the salve, a roll of soft linen, a dish of cool water, and a small pair of scissors. "Lie back. This will hurt more than before."

It did.

The blister along the longest line had broken on the walk from the yard, and the healer cleaned it with the cool water and laid a strip of linen soaked in salve along it, from the notch at Kiva's breastbone, over the collarbone, out across the top of her shoulder. Kiva held the edge of the mattress with her right hand and counted the herbs in the garden through the window. Fourteen beds. Something in the third one was flowering yellow.

"Why did they move?" she said, when she could talk. "The lines. They were all on my chest before."

"They followed the load." The healer smoothed the linen down. "You took that weight on your left shoulder, with three doors drawing through you at once. The pulling went where the weight went." She sat back. "The nerves for your arm run right there. Under the collarbone, over the top of the shoulder, down. That's why your fingers are thick."

Kiva lifted her left hand off the blanket and looked at it. She pressed her right thumb against the tip of her first finger. It was still there, the glove feeling, a little less than in the lane. Or perhaps she was only hoping it was less.

"Will it come back?"

"Sometimes it comes back in a day. Sometimes in a season." The healer put the scissors back on her tray. "Sometimes it doesn't. I'll know more in three days. Until then, you don't use that hand for anything harder than a spoon." She looked at Kiva. "And you don't open a door. Any door. For any reason."

"What if one opens by itself?"

"Then you call me." The healer stood. "Loudly. I'm told you have trouble with that."

---

Mara came after the seventh bell, when the light in the garden had gone grey.

She did not say anything at first. She took the chair from the corner, set it beside the bed, and sat in it with her coat still on and her toolbag at her feet, and looked at the linen on Kiva's shoulder for a long time. Then she looked at Kiva's left hand on the blanket.

"The first two fingers," said Kiva. "They're thick. She says three days."

Mara nodded. She knew, Kiva realized. The healer had told her on the stairs. She had wanted to hear Kiva say it anyway.

"You said warm," said Mara.

"I know."

"She asked you, and you said warm, and they were hot. I could see them from the gate. I could see your face." Mara's voice did not rise. It went lower instead, and steadier, the way it did when a reading came out wrong for the third time and she was about to take the instrument apart. "I wrote it on the bond. *If she says stop.* I wrote it so they'd have to listen to you. And you didn't say it."

"I didn't want to stop."

"I know you didn't."

"It was one more pass. If I'd held one door on the third pass, she'd have had a number. She could have signed it. And then we'd have—" Kiva stopped. Her throat had gone tight. "The account. The house."

Mara was quiet for a while.

"I'm not angry that you went to him," she said. "The boy. Cade. I want you to understand that. If I'd been in that lane, I'd have gone. Anybody worth anything would have gone." She leaned forward and put her hand flat on the blanket beside Kiva's left hand, not touching it. "I'm angry that you said *warm*. Because you said it for me. For the account, and the house. And I'd rather have no house than a niece who lies to a healer to keep a roof over me."

Kiva looked at the ceiling. There was no fish on this one.

"I won't," she said.

"You will," said Mara. "You're proud. You're like your mother. She once walked four miles on a broken ankle because she'd told someone she'd be at their wedding." She sat back. "But you'll know you're doing it. And next time, maybe, you'll stop."

They sat for a long time without speaking. Out in the corridor, a healer went past with a lamp, and the light swung across the gap in the door and away again.

"Did I break the frame?" said Kiva at last.

"You bent a stop that was already worn, and knocked an arm off a bracket that was already loose, and tore a seam." Mara almost smiled. "And you put a ram through the inner upright hard enough that the Crooked Line will have to look at its timber for the first time in thirty years. Which they should have done anyway."

"Is it on the account?"

"It'll be on the account," said Mara. "It's all going to be on the account. Go to sleep."

---

The account came on the ninth day, in the Compact's red seal, with Ysra Crest's signature at the bottom.

Mara read it at the bench. Then she put it down flat, and read it again, and Kiva, who had been home since the second hour with her arm in a sling and orders to do nothing, came and read it upside down.

*Supervised-test bond, candidate Rowan. Damage to Compact property arising from the test:*

*Old lane frame. Counterweight channel, lower stop, bent; channel foot splayed. Replace stop, re-set channel. Swing arm, rounded bracket, displaced; re-seat. Inner upright, timber inspection and repair as required. Ram, seam, resew. Haul rope, cut; replace.*

*Ash drawn beyond the school's emergency allotment: one spoon and a half, clean grade, Wren House.*

At the foot of the page was the total.

Kiva had known it would be bad. She had done the arithmetic of the first chit in the long room on Kindling night, and she had lain awake doing the arithmetic of the bond. She had not done this arithmetic, because she had not known what a counterweight stop cost, or a channel re-set, or a timber inspection on a frame older than the house.

It was more than Mara made in a season.

Under it, clipped to the back, was a second, smaller notice, on the municipal housing office's grey stock.

*In view of an open liability on the Compact's books against this household, the tenancy review for No. 14 Gauge Row is brought forward to the half-season.*

Mara read that one only once. Then she turned it face down, and put the account on top of it, and sat with her hands flat on both.

"It's not fair," said Kiva.

"It's the bond," said Mara. "I signed it."

"I know. But it's not—" Kiva stopped. She was looking at the account again, at the first line. *Counterweight channel, lower stop, bent.* Something about it was tugging at her the way a reading tugged when the two columns of a card were nearly the same, but not quite.

"Mara," she said. "Where's the old lane frame's card?"

Mara looked at her.

"You said in the lane. You said the stop was on your card." Kiva was already at the brass box. She walked her fingers along the tabs with her right hand, past the lamp regulators and the canal gauges and the Assay frame, to the section for the school frames. "You wrote it last spring."

"Kiva—"

The card was there. *Old lane frame, Crooked Line School.* The instrument column was short, because the old lane was only used once a year, and most years nobody asked Mara to look at it. But last spring someone had, and in the remarks, in Mara's small flat hand, there were two lines.

*Counterweight stop worn, foot of channel. Pawl worn. Recommend both replaced before next season.*

*School advised in writing. No order received.*

Kiva laid it on the bench beside the account.

Mara looked at it for a long time.

"The bond says damage *arising from the test*," said Kiva. "The stop was worn before the test. You wrote it down. You told them. They didn't fix it." She put her finger on the line. "If it had been replaced, it wouldn't have bent. The stack would have stayed in the channel. It wouldn't have tipped at all."

"Perhaps," said Mara.

"It's on the card."

"It's on the card," Mara agreed slowly.

She took a clean sheet from the drawer and uncapped her pen. She wrote, in the same flat hand she used for everything, a short letter to the office of the senior assessor. She copied out the two lines from the old lane card exactly as they stood, with the date. She asked that the charge for the counterweight stop and the channel foot be reviewed as pre-existing wear, recorded and reported before the test. She did not argue. She did not explain. She simply set the two lines down where somebody would have to read them.

At the bottom, she signed it.

Then she gave the pen to Kiva.

"You found it," she said. "You sign under me. *Reconciled by.*"

Kiva wrote her name, slowly, with her right hand. *Reconciled by K. Rowan.* It was the first time her name had gone on anything that left the house.

It would not touch the ash. It would not touch the bracket or the seam or the rope or the timber. The total would still be more than a season. But it was something, and it was true, and it had been sitting in the box all along, waiting for someone to put the two columns side by side.

---

Kellan Renn came to the old lane at dawn on the tenth day, because it was the only time it was empty.

The frame stood propped on two rough timbers where the Crooked Line carpenters had braced it. The swing arm had been taken down and laid along the rail. The ram was gone, and the chain hung empty. At the foot of the outer upright, inside a square of rope with a sign that said *Do not touch*, the counterweight still lay on its side where it had fallen, its discs shifted on the rod, the splayed foot of the channel bent out above it like a broken finger.

He stood at the rope and looked at it.

He had a folded sheet of paper in his coat. He had written it the night before at his desk at home, three times, until it said exactly what he meant and nothing else.

*To the Head of the Crooked Line School. I request that the school's lane hours not be reassigned for the supervision of the unplaced candidate Rowan until the senior assessor has ruled. Lane time is finite. A supervised student with no defensible door takes a lane-mistress from twelve students for every hour of supervision. The school has a duty to the twelve. — K. Renn, Entered.*

It was true. Every word of it was true. He had counted the lane hours in the school's posted schedule, and he had counted the students, and the numbers were not in dispute.

He looked at the counterweight.

He had been at the gate when the ram went into the frame. He had seen the arm jump off its bracket. He had heard the pawl go, that small clean tick, and he had moved before he knew why, because the tall girl from Stonehand had jumped clear and then frozen with her back against the yard wall, directly under where the top of the stack would come down if it came. He had got her by the jacket and pulled. That was all. It had not been difficult.

What he could not stop thinking about was the other thing.

The Rowan girl had been on the painted line in the middle of the lane, with her right hand behind her back and her left out, holding a Ward she had just opened on purpose. Four paces from the frame, maybe five. A rail and a swing arm in between. She had not declared anything. She had not set her feet. She had simply gone, and the struts had come down her legs in one movement, and she had been over the rail and under the arm and beside Cade in a single stride.

Kellan had been training his Stride for three years in a back room with a hired tutor. He knew exactly how far a first Stride went. He knew how many first-years at the Kindling could even form struts, and how many of those could do more than hop.

It had been the fastest first Stride he had ever seen. It had been completely undeclared, completely uncontrolled, and aimed at nothing but a boy on his knees. And it would never be written anywhere, because the belt gauges had been reading three doors to the top of the scale at the time, and there was no column on any table for *went anyway.*

He hated that he could not stop thinking about it.

He walked down to the end of the lane, past the propped frame, and stood in front of the crooked post. He had stood in front of it on every anniversary for eight years, waiting for the day his own name would go on the Table. He had never looked at it closely. It was ceremonial. It was custom. It did not affect the drills.

He looked at it now.

*Retained. Ceremonial. Custom.*

Twenty-one was a true number. He had earned it, breath by breath, over eight years of stillness and three of Stride and a hundred nights in the back room when his tutor had gone home and he had kept going. It meant something because everyone in the yard had stood on the same stone and been read by the same frame and set against the same scale. That was the whole point of a scale. That was why his parents had paid for the tutor, and why he had never once complained. If you worked, it showed. If it showed, it counted.

And now there was a girl who could not be set against the scale at all, and everyone was reading her instead.

He took the folded sheet out of his coat. He did not tear it up. He had meant every word. He put it back in his pocket to deliver at the first bell, as he had planned.

But he took out his pencil, and on the back of it, under his name, he added one more line.

*When the candidate is placed, I request to walk the lane against her. So the number means something.*

Then he went to the gate to wait for the first bell, and did not look back at the post.

Perrin came to Gauge Row on the tenth afternoon with a loaf of bread under each arm and a limp.

"My mother's," he said, holding them out. "She bakes when she's upset. She's been upset since Saturday. We've got eleven loaves in the house."

Kiva took them with her right hand and put them on the bench. Mara, who was at the far end with a lamp regulator in pieces, looked up, said "Thank you, tell your mother thank you," and looked down again with the particular blankness of somebody deciding not to listen.

They sat on the front step. Perrin stretched his bandaged ankle out in front of him into the street. His right hand was bandaged too, across the palm.

"Rope," he said, when he saw her looking. "Tried to pull it off my boot. Took the skin off instead. The Stonehand healer says I've got the hands of a lock-keeper now." He flexed them. "Anwen says that's an insult to lock-keepers."

"How's the ankle?"

"Purple." He looked at her sling. "How's the—"

"Thick," said Kiva. "Two fingers. She says three days."

Perrin nodded. He didn't say anything for a while, which for Perrin was so unusual that Kiva turned to look at him.

"They're calling me Counterweight," he said at last. "At Stonehand. The second-years. *Here comes Counterweight. Mind he doesn't fall on you.*" He tried to grin. It didn't work. "It's funny. I'd think it was funny if it was anyone else."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't." He said it quickly. "Don't be sorry. That's the thing. That's what I came to say." He looked down at his bandaged hand. "You saved me with the doors I don't have. You did a Stride over a rail and a Ward and a root, all at once, and I was on my knees in a loop of rope looking up at two hundred pounds of iron, and I couldn't move." His voice was very quiet. "I didn't freeze because I was scared. I mean, I was scared. But I froze because I didn't have anything. I'm the still door. I'm supposed to be the one who doesn't move. And I didn't move, and it was the wrong kind of not moving."

Kiva said nothing.

"Four breaths," said Perrin. "My hold's four breaths. I didn't even get four. I didn't get one." He laughed, short and unhappy. "And you went over there in one step and held it for four. With my hold. On top of everything else."

"I didn't count," said Kiva.

"I did." He looked at her. "So I'm jealous. I'm going to keep being jealous. I wanted you to know that. It's there, it's not going anywhere, and I'd rather tell you it's there than have it sitting under the floor where neither of us can see it." He shrugged with one shoulder. "That's better. I think. Anwen thinks it's better."

"You talked to Anwen about it?"

"I talk to Anwen about everything. She's a terrible listener. She just looks at you until you say the true thing." He sighed. "It's very annoying."

Kiva almost laughed. She didn't, because his face had changed.

"And there's the other thing," he said. "Don't do it like that again."

"Like what?"

"Without saying." He turned on the step to face her. "You went. You didn't say anything. Not *mine*, not *taking*, nothing. Anwen didn't know you were going until you were already over the rail. She had to guess. Orla had to guess. *I* had to guess. If you'd said it, Anwen could've been there a breath sooner, and you might not have had to hold it on your shoulder till your shirt burned."

"There wasn't time."

"There was time for Anwen to say *mine*," said Perrin.

Kiva opened her mouth and closed it again.

She saw it. She had been seeing it for two days without quite looking at it, the way you can look at a card for an hour without seeing the bent spring. In the lane she had gone without a word, and everyone around her had been left to work out what she was doing from what they could see. And Anwen had come anyway, running, and shouted *mine*, and the weight had gone onto her Ward, and Kiva had been able to let go only because there was suddenly somewhere for it to go.

It had not been Kiva's strength that ended it. It had been the word, and the person who said it.

"I didn't think," she said.

"I know. That's what I'm saying." Perrin turned back to the street. "I'm not telling you to stop saving people. I'm not stupid. I'm telling you to say so first. So the rest of us can help instead of standing around being Counterweight."

They sat for a while. A cart went down Gauge Row, and the driver looked at Kiva's sling and then quickly away.

"What are you going to do?" said Kiva.

"About what?"

"Being Counterweight."

Perrin thought about it. "There's a thing at Stonehand," he said. "Dawn load. You go in at the first bell, before the yard opens, and a second-year pushes you for an hour. Just you. Over and over. It's for people who want it." He picked at the edge of his bandage. "I've been too embarrassed to ask. Because everyone would see I was the one who needed it."

"Are you going to ask?"

"I'm going to ask tomorrow," said Perrin. "If my ankle lets me stand. And if it doesn't, I'm going to ask the day after." He stood up, carefully, on his good leg, and looked down at her. "Eat the bread. It's very good bread. She's very upset."

The rotation mistress of Wren House kept her office at the top of the north stair, in a room so full of maps that there was barely space for a desk. Anwen stood in front of the desk with her hands behind her back and looked at a map of the country past the Sag while the mistress read her a page from her own file.

"*Left the safety bench during a supervised test without the healer's word,*" read the mistress. She was a stout, cheerful-looking woman with tired eyes, and she read it without any expression at all. "*Entered the test area under a failing load. Placed a Ward beneath the load without order.*" She looked up. "Do you know what that would be called in the field?"

"Brave?" said Anwen.

"Fatal," said the rotation mistress. "In the field, a healer who leaves her post without a word is how healers die. Not the brave ones. All of them. You leave the bench, you're not at the bench. Somebody else goes down behind you, and nobody's there." She set the page down. "That's why we don't send first-years. It's not that you're precious. It's that you haven't learned yet how not to run."

Anwen kept her eyes on the map.

"The healer hadn't said anything," she said. "There wasn't time. The stack was tipping. I could see it from the bench."

"Then you should have said something."

"I did," said Anwen. "I said *mine.*"

The rotation mistress paused.

"I said it before I got there," said Anwen. "Loud. So the healer would know I'd gone and why, and so Kiva would know I was coming." She hesitated. "I don't know if it was right. But I said it."

The mistress looked at her for a long time. Then she looked back down at the file, and turned a page, and read something on the next one.

"The assessor's note," she said. "*Pryce: hold eight breaths under two hundred pounds, with a partner under the load.*" She tapped the line. "That's the kind of thing a partner record is meant to show. A Ward held under load with somebody depending on it. Witnessed and signed by the senior assessor herself." She sighed. "I can't accept it. It wasn't a logged drill. It was an accident with a frame."

"I know," said Anwen.

"But," said the rotation mistress. She sat back. "If the candidate Rowan is retested, the Compact will want a Ward on the bench who has already stood under her load once and knows what it's like. Somebody who'll say the word when it needs saying. I could assign you to that." She looked at Anwen steadily. "It would be a logged bench, under the senior assessor, with a partner who might need you. It would count. It would also mean your name sitting beside hers on another sheet, and the whole Compact knows how the last sheet ended."

Anwen thought about it.

She thought about the north rotation: one first-year a season, from any school, out past the Sag with the Home Line. She thought about her mother on the towpath every spring, watching them go by. She thought about the way the iron had felt when she slid the Ward in under it, the weight coming onto her and staying, and how she had held it for eight breaths when her best in the quiet room was nine.

She thought about Kiva's face, under the stack, the moment Anwen had said *mine.*

"Assign me," she said.

"You're sure?"

"I'm not doing it for her," said Anwen. "I want you to know that. I need a partner record, and she's the only one testing." She paused. "And I'd rather it was me on the bench than somebody who doesn't know what she's like."

"That's two reasons," said the mistress.

"Yes," said Anwen. "They're both true."

The rotation mistress almost smiled. She picked up her pen and wrote something short on the page, and blotted it.

"Go on," she said. "And Pryce. Next time you run, say it first. You did. Keep doing it."

---

On the twelfth day, the thickness in Kiva's fingers began to go.

It went slowly, from the outside in. On the eleventh evening her middle finger came back almost all the way, so that when she pressed it with her thumb she felt the press close and clear, the way she was supposed to. By the twelfth morning, only the very tip of her first finger still felt as if she were touching the world through a glove. It was a small patch, no bigger than a lentil. She kept pressing it, all morning, at the bench, until Mara told her to stop.

The healer came to the house at noon to look.

"Mostly back," she said, pressing each fingertip in turn. She pressed the first finger longest. "That bit may come, or it may not. I'd say it will." She let go of Kiva's hand. "Don't do that again."

"I'll try."

"Don't try. Don't do it." The healer re-dressed the long line on her shoulder, which had begun to close under the linen and itched furiously. "I've told your aunt what I've told you. That arm is where your doors go when they fight. Every time you let them fight under load, they'll go a little further down it. One day they'll go further than I can help."

She left a fresh jar of salve on the bench and went away. Mara looked at the jar for a while, and then wrote it on a card.

Orla came at the third hour.

She had sent a note that morning asking whether she could, and Mara had written back *Yes*, and nothing else. It was the first time Orla had been inside the house on Gauge Row. She stood in the doorway for a moment and looked at the bench, the long brass box, the lamp regulators in pieces, the abstracts on the shelf, the swept place on the floor near the hearth where the glass had been. She did not say anything about any of it. She took off her coat.

"Sit," said Mara. "I'll make tea."

"Thank you," said Orla. "No milk."

They sat at the bench, the three of them, the way Kiva and Mara sat when a card would not reconcile. Orla put her hands flat on the wood and looked at Kiva.

"You found three things on the eighth day," she said. "Tell me what they were."

Kiva had known she would ask. She had been turning them over for four days.

"The doors answer where the load lands," she said. "Whichever part of me gets hit, the door in that part wakes up and tries to help. Even if I've declared a different one. Even if I'm not pressing."

"One."

"When I opened a door on purpose, I opened it with all of me at once. Because I didn't know any other way. It was far too strong. It didn't hold the ram. It threw it."

"Two."

"And when Anwen said *mine*," said Kiva, "the weight went."

Orla waited.

"I couldn't let go of it until she said that," said Kiva slowly. "I didn't know how. I was holding three doors open against the iron and I didn't know how to stop holding any of them. And then she said *mine*, and slid her Ward in under mine, and there was somewhere else for the weight to go. And then I could let go. All of them." She looked at her hands. "I didn't even decide to. It just happened, once there was a space."

"Which of the three matters most?" said Orla.

Kiva thought about it for a long time. Mara brought the tea and set it down, and sat, and did not interrupt.

"The third," said Kiva.

"Why?"

"Because the first two are about what goes wrong," said Kiva. "The third one is the only time anything went right."

Orla picked up her tea and drank some of it. When she put the cup down, her face had not changed, but something in the way she sat had.

"Here's what I know," she said. "It isn't much. I'll tell you all of it." She held up one finger. "You can't shut a door by sitting on it. You tried in the house, and it came out sideways. You can't shut it by not using it either; you tried in the lane, and it came anyway, out of whatever part of you was struck. The only way I know to close a door is to let go of it. All the way. On purpose. And then wait, without asking for anything else, until it's actually closed."

"How do I know when it's closed?"

"I don't know how it feels for you. For me, it's a kind of quiet. You'll have to find yours." Orla held up a second finger. "Three doors at once is something nobody can teach you. I can't. Nobody in the Compact can. Nobody's ever had to. But three doors *one after another*, each one opened, used, let go, and closed before you touch the next: that might be something I can teach. It's slower. It's much less impressive. It's also the only thing I can think of that you could do without three of them fighting over you."

"A sequence," said Mara.

"A sequence," said Orla.

"And when one gets loose?" said Kiva. "Because it will."

"Then somebody says *mine*," said Orla. She held up the third finger. "That's the only reason the word exists. Not to be said at the start of a drill like a password. So that when you're holding something you can't let go of, somebody can step in and take it, and you can stop."

Kiva looked at the three fingers. Limits. A sequence. Somebody beside you.

"Will Crest let me try it?"

"I've asked her to," said Orla. "I wrote her a protocol yesterday. A static reading, on the slab, with the frame and the belt gauges, no ram, no load. One door at a time, in an order you choose, with a full release and a rest between each, and a Ward on the bench who knows what you're like." She glanced at Mara. "She's agreed to the fifteenth day, if the guardian signs."

Mara set her cup down very carefully.

"I won't sign anything," she said, "without an abort line. Written. Not *if the healer calls it.* Not *if she says stop*, because she won't. A line she can see before she's there."

Orla nodded slowly, as if she had expected it.

Mara got up and went to the drawer under the bench. She took out a blank instrument card, the ordinary kind with the two columns and the narrow strip at the edge, and came back and laid it on the wood in front of Orla. Then she uncapped her pen and wrote at the top, in the space for the name of the instrument:

*Rowan, K. — doors.*

She turned the card around and pushed it across the bench with the pen.

Orla looked at it for a long moment. Then she picked up the pen and, in the narrow remarks column at the right-hand edge, in her narrow upright hand, she wrote:

*When the lines are hot, she says hot. Not warm. Whoever hears it stops the reading. Whoever's nearest says mine.*

She blotted it with her sleeve and turned it back to Mara.

Mara read it twice. Then she nodded once, and filed it in the brass box, under R, between the canal gauges and the municipal clocks.

Orla stood up and put on her coat. At the door she stopped and looked back at Kiva.

"Do you know why the post's crooked?" she said.

"The plate says *custom*."

"That's what the plate says," Orla agreed. She seemed about to say something else. Then she didn't. She nodded to Mara, and went out into Gauge Row, and shut the door behind her.

Kiva sat at the bench and looked at the brass box, where the card with her name on it had gone in among the instruments. She thought about *hot*, and *mine*, and *let go*. She pressed the tip of her first finger with her thumb, and felt the small dull patch there, no bigger than a lentil, and for the first time since the lane, she found she was not afraid of it. She was only paying attention to it, the way you pay attention to a reading you mean to understand.
