# Chapter 6 — Kiva: What the Cell Kept

Eight and two.

Kiva stood on the mezzanine with her palm flat on the drum and felt her own three doors fighting inside it, round and round, and knew what she had to do.

The quench handle was right there, a hand's width from her hip, red, with its little wire seal. All she had to do was break the seal and pull, and the ash would drop through the gate into the wet salt below, and the pattern would die, and the fight she had left in the cell would die with it. The calling would stop. The needle would fall. It would be controlled, because she would call it, and pull it, and write it down.

And the winch would die.

She leaned over the rail above the pump room door.

Perrin was still on his back along the threshold, under the edge of the shutter, with his hands flat against the steel and his face dark red.

"Anwen!" she shouted. "How far?"

"Housing's a hand's width off the legs." Anwen's voice came up strained and flat from inside. "Not enough."

If Kiva quenched now, the winch would let go of the shutter, and four hundred kilos of steel would come down onto Perrin's hands, and then onto Perrin, with Anwen and the dummy shut in behind him.

"Out!" she shouted. "Perrin, Anwen, I need to quench. Get out!"

"Not out," said Anwen. "Legs still pinned. Ten seconds."

"I haven't got ten seconds!"

"Then you've got whatever you've got," said Anwen. "I'm not leaving it."

Eight and three.

The chute clanged for the fourth time, and Kiva turned her head.

Three husks poured out of the hatch onto the concrete. They came out faster than any of the others, and they did not stop to find their shape. They found it as they moved, grey and heavy, pulling themselves into legs as they ran. Two went along the south wall toward the stair, toward Kellan, who was standing on the fourth step with his blade lit and his back to her.

The third did not go to the stair.

It went straight across the floor, in a line, toward the mezzanine and the cell. And the shortest way to the cell, for a thing with no face and no sense and only one want, was not up the stair. It was underneath.

Under the mezzanine. Under the cell. Through the pump room.

Through the only gap in the pump room wall, which was a knee-high space under a jammed steel shutter, with a boy lying in it.

Kiva saw it the way you see a cup start to tip off a table, all of it at once: the line the husk was taking, and the gap, and Perrin's red face, and Perrin's hands on the shutter, and Perrin's still door, wide open under him, holding the floor. It was worked power, lit and bright, lying right there in the way.

He could not move. If he let go of the shutter, it would come down on him, and if he let go of the housing, it would come down on the dummy. He was holding everything, and the husk was coming for the thing behind him, and he was in the way.

She did not decide.

She put her left hand on the rail and went over it.

---

The bar was still on her fist, and the Ember door was still open. She let the bar go, and the metal ran back up her arm and down, the same door in a different shape, into struts that came down the backs of her calves and along her soles as she fell.

It was two and a half meters to the floor. She landed on the struts.

They took most of it, because they were made for this. The metal flexed and caught her and drove her forward in a long, low bound, and for a moment she felt what Kellan must feel every time he did it, the whole fall turned into speed. Then her left foot came down on the second bound and the knee went.

It did not give way. It screamed, with a white flash straight up her thigh, the same as the step in the Terrace Ring and worse. She took the third bound on her right leg only and came down two paces in front of the pump room door, between the gap and the husk.

It was five paces away. Coming.

She needed the still door.

She had one breath to get it, and she knew how it was supposed to go, because Orla had said it in the yard. *The speed of a Stride going straight into a stance, so you stop where you mean to and nobody moves you.* That was not a drill. That was a sequence, the Ember's work handed to the still door without dropping it.

Nobody Orla knew could do that.

Kiva reached for it anyway.

She let the struts go and felt the metal start to run home toward the slab. She felt the still door under her, open, ready. And the husk was four paces off, then three, and the metal was not home yet, and she could not wait for it.

She went into stance with the Ember still in the doorway.

It was not a hand-off, and she knew it as she did it. Nothing passed from one door to the other. The speed of the Stride was simply gone, spent in her knee and the concrete, and the struts hung half-melted on her calves, caught in a door she had not managed to shut, while the still door came open under them. It was two doors jammed against each other, with her in the middle.

The bottom heat-line lit. The second.

The hand door lifted to help. It wanted to ward, as it always did, and she felt it come up cool and quick inside her, reaching, and for one instant the third line began to warm.

"*One,*" she said, through her teeth, and shut the hand door on it.

She held it. Two doors were open, still and Ember, with the struts stuck on her calves and the stance coming up through her feet, and two lines were burning. The third line was warm and not lit. The hand door stayed shut, with her foot against it, while everything else fought.

The husk hit her.

---

It was like being hit by a sack of wet cement thrown from a cart.

It hit her square in the chest and left side, low and heavy, and the force of it went into her and down, through her ribs and her hips and her knees, the good one and the bad one, and through her feet into the concrete. And the concrete took it.

She did not move.

The husk did not understand that. It had no way to understand anything. It shoved at her, all four stubby limbs scrabbling on the floor, and she stood in its way like a post set in the ground. Behind her, she could hear Perrin breathing in great ragged gasps, and she could feel his still door and hers, side by side, both holding.

And the cell rang.

She felt it through the stance and the ground and her own body. Above her, on the mezzanine, her own three-door fight was still twisting inside the drum, and it was calling out louder than ever, and something inside her answered it.

It came back down the heat-lines.

She had felt backwash before, as every Kindled person had, once or twice, when a cell kicked. It was like a burn from the inside. This was worse, because this was her own fight coming home. The cell held three doors pulling against each other, and it was pulling on the three doors in her, and the lines on her chest went from hot to white.

They burned past her shoulder and down the top of her arm. She had never felt them go past her shoulder before. She felt them now, three wires drawn tight from her breastbone over her collarbone and down toward her elbow, and the skin along them felt as if somebody had laid a poker on it.

The husk shoved. She had put her left hand up without thinking, the way you put up a hand to stop a door from slamming. It was the hand that wanted to ward, and she had not let it, so it was just a hand. One of the husk's front limbs came up and dragged down her left forearm from the elbow, hard, scraping the skin off it, until it struck the leather cuff and skidded over the slab.

Then the limb ground across the back of her left hand and over the first two fingers. They went numb. It did not hurt. They were simply gone, as if they had been in snow.

Something knocked against her breastbone from the outside. The disk had swung out of her collar on its cord.

"*Kiva,*" said Perrin behind her. It was half a word and half a groan.

Above them, the needle on the drum crossed the red line.

There was a sharp, flat crack from the mezzanine. The breaker.

The load board on the east wall went dark, every little lamp in the district at once. The lamp in the pump room went out. And the winch above the shutter, which had been humming quietly all through the run, stopped.

The shutter dropped.

It did not fall far. It could not, because Perrin's hands were under it. Kiva heard it come down onto his palms with a sound like a dropped anvil, and she heard Perrin make a noise she had never heard a person make, deep and short and not quite a scream.

Then she heard nothing at all from him.

For one terrible moment she thought it had crushed him.

Then, behind her, through the stance, she felt his still door. It was open wider than she had ever felt it. It had gone down into the floor under him like roots, and it was holding four hundred kilos of steel on two hands, and it was not moving.

"Holding," said Perrin, in a whisper. "Holding. Holding."

---

The cell was still calling.

Kiva could feel it. The breaker had cut the load, so the board was dark and the winch was dead, but the cell itself was still running, with the pattern still in it. Her pattern. Her fight. It was not driving anything now. It was just ringing, louder and louder, like a bell with nobody to stop it, and the husk on her chest was still pushing toward it, and the one alive on the stair was climbing at Kellan.

The breaker did not stop a pattern. Only the quench did that.

The quench was on the mezzanine, two and a half meters over her head, on the far side of a steel rail. She could not get to it. She was holding a husk off a boy holding a shutter off a girl holding a dummy, and if she moved one step, all of it came down.

She turned her head and shouted.

"*Kellan!*"

He was on the stair. She could see him past the edge of the husk, on the fifth step, with his lit blade low in front of him. One husk was coming up the stair at him. The other was in pieces on the bottom tread.

"Quench!" she shouted. "The red handle. It's still calling. The breaker doesn't stop it. You have to pull it. It's mechanical. Just pull it!"

He did not turn. The husk on the stair was almost on him.

"*Kellan, the quench!*"

---

Kellan Renn heard her the first time.

He did not look round, because a husk was two steps below him and coming up. He had been guarding the foot of this stair for a minute and a half, and he had learned it in the first five seconds. It was narrow, one pace wide or a little less, with the brick wall on his left and a steel rail on his right, and beyond the rail a drop to the floor. The treads were open steel grating, and the husk ash from the last one had run down between them and made every step slick.

You could not Stride on a stair. He had known that before he stepped on it. Struts were for open ground, and on a stair they caught on the treads and threw you, so it was the blade, and only the blade, and his feet.

He liked it, a little, though he would not have said so. A stair took everything away except the thing he was best at.

The husk came up. He thrust down at it, straight, because there was no room to cut sideways without hitting brick or rail, and his blade went into its front end, but not deep enough. It shoved up against the blade, and he felt the weight of it come into his arm, and his front foot slid on the ash.

"*Kellan, the quench!*"

He glanced over his shoulder at the mezzanine.

It was above and behind him. The stair went up seven more steps to the platform, and at the top was the drum, with the red handle on its side. He could see it from here. He could see the meter, too, with its needle pinned past the red line.

And he could feel it.

He had felt it for most of the last minute and not had time to think about it. It was a ringing, a wrongness. The cell up there was not running like any cell he had ever stood beside. It was loud, not to his ears but to the part of him that his Ember door opened onto, like standing beside a bell that was being struck over and over.

The husk on his blade was pushing toward it. So was the one on Rowan's chest. So, he realized, had every husk that had come out of that chute since she put a door on the drum.

She had not said it would call them. Nobody had ever said a cell could do that. But she had said the rest of it in the corridor, to his face. *If I open a door near it, they'll fight, and the cell will take the fight.*

She had been right.

She had been right about the clock, too. *A tenth in ten seconds, the next in nine. Under two minutes. Seventy seconds. Fifty.* He had heard every number she called, and he had checked them, without meaning to, against the meter whenever he glanced up. She had been right every time, to the tenth.

His mother would have liked her numbers.

The husk shoved, and he held it on the blade. He had two choices and he could see both of them clearly, the way he saw everything clearly when it mattered.

He could finish this husk. He could turn it, free the blade, put it through the knot of the thing, and then finish whatever came up next. He would score. That would be two kills on a stair, clean, with the gallery watching, and Graywater would write it down. That was his role. Spawn. It was on the card.

Or he could leave it.

He could leave a live husk on the stair behind him, break off, and get to the quench. He could do the thing that was not on the card, because the girl who had broken the card twice already was shouting at him from the floor that it was the only thing that would work.

He did not believe in people. He believed in measurements.

Her measurements had been right all run.

He made the decision. He did not like it. He made it anyway.

"Renn!" he shouted, loud enough for the booth. "Changing role. Taking quench!"

He yanked the blade free. The husk lurched forward on the slick step, off balance, and he let it, and went up two steps backward. Five more steps of slick grating with a husk at his heels was three seconds, and he did not think the cell had three seconds to give him. The rail was one.

He turned, and put his left hand on the rail, and asked for struts.

Not to Stride. To jump.

The metal ran off his fist and down his legs in one smooth flow, and the blade was gone. He felt the empty instant, the gap he never let anybody see, and he breathed out. Then he put his right foot on the rail and pushed off it with everything the struts had.

The rail was not wide, and it rang under his boot. For a moment he was in the air beside the stair, over the drop, with nothing under him.

Then his hands caught the edge of the mezzanine.

He hauled himself up onto the platform, and his knee hit the steel plate, and he was up. He was at the drum. The ringing was so loud here that it made his teeth ache.

He put his hand on the red handle and broke the wire seal with his thumb.

He pulled.

---

Kiva heard the quench go.

It was a heavy clank, and then a long, rushing hiss, like water thrown on a stove, as the gate in the bottom of the drum opened and the ash poured through it into the box of wet salt.

And the ringing stopped.

It did not fade. It stopped, the way a bell stops when you put your hand on it. One moment her own three doors were twisting round and round inside the drum and calling to everything in the room, and the next moment there was nothing in the drum at all but steel and dead salt.

The husk on her chest went slack.

It did not fall apart. It just stopped pushing. It stood against her with its limbs braced, heavy and grey, and seemed to forget what it had been doing, like a dog when the whistle stops.

The heat-lines did not stop. They kept burning, but the white went out of them, and they went back to merely hot.

"That's it," she heard someone say. It was Anwen, inside the pump room. "That's it, Perrin, it's moving, it's off—"

There was a grinding scrape. Kiva could not turn to look, but she could hear Anwen hauling, and the dummy's coverall dragging over concrete, and Anwen's breath hissing through her teeth.

Something came down off the mezzanine beside her. Fast.

Kellan landed on his struts with a clang, and by the time he straightened, the blade was back on his fist. It was dark. He had not lit it. He came at the husk on Kiva's chest from the side, and he did not cut. He drew the dark blade back and brought it down flat on the middle of the husk's back, as hard as he could, like a man splitting a log with the back of an axe.

The husk came apart.

It came apart all over her. Grey ash, fine as flour, poured down her front and over her arms and into her collar and her hair, faintly warm. She stood in the middle of it, still rooted, with her arms out and ash running off her fingers, and could not quite believe it was gone.

"Don't light near people," said Kellan. He was breathing hard. "It's a violation."

"I know," said Kiva.

"I know you know." He was already turning. "Stair husk's still up there. I'll get it."

He went.

---

Anwen brought the dummy out under the gap head-first.

It was a seventy-kilo bag of sand in a coverall, and she was not big. She had it by the collar with both fists and was walking backward on her knees, hauling it a hand's length at a time through the gap beside Perrin's boots, until Kiva felt the dummy's heels scrape past her own.

"Kiva," said Anwen. "Move. I need the room."

Kiva gave the ground its weight back.

She did it badly. She did it with two doors open and both of them hurting, and the bad knee nearly folded when the stance let go. But she lifted her heels and breathed in and thought *thank you*, and the still door eased. Then, at last, the Ember let go of the doorway, and the half-formed struts on her calves ran home into the slab.

She stepped to the side, on a left leg that would barely hold her.

Anwen hauled the dummy clear of the doorway and stood up and got her arms under its shoulders and began to drag it toward the green line, forty paces down the long dark hall.

"Perrin," said Kiva.

He was still under the shutter. She could see his hands on its bottom edge, and they were shaking, and so were the whole of his arms. The shutter was resting on his palms and the pump housing was resting on his shoulder, and his face had gone from red to grey.

"Can't—" he said. "Can't let go. If I let go it drops on me."

"Then let it down slowly."

"Can't do slowly. I'm too tired to do slowly."

Kiva crouched beside him. Her knee screamed, and she did not care. She put both hands flat on the shutter beside his, her right hand and her numb left hand, and she did not open any door. She could not have lifted it anyway. But she could feel him through it.

"Give it back," she said. "Like you told me. Heels. Breathe in. Give it back a little at a time."

He rolled his eyes toward her, and they were wet.

"That's stupid," he said.

"I know. It works."

He breathed in. She felt his still door ease, a fraction, and the shutter came down a finger's width onto his palms. He held it. He breathed in again. Another finger.

"Roll," said Kiva. "When I say. Roll out to me."

The shutter came down, a finger and then another, until his elbows were on the concrete and the steel was a hand above his chest. He let the pump housing slide off his shoulder onto the floor inside with a heavy clang. Then he took his hands away, all at once, and rolled toward her, and the shutter came down the last of the way behind him with a crash that shook the floor.

He lay on his back on the concrete beside her with his eyes shut and his hands lying palm-up on his chest. They were purple across the palms, and they were shaking so badly that his fingers twitched.

"I held it," he said, without opening his eyes.

"You held it."

"Four hundred kilos. On my hands. On my back." He laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "Nobody's going to believe me."

"Everybody saw."

"Nobody's going to believe me anyway."

Down the hall, Anwen dragged the dummy over the green line.

A bell rang overhead. Then a long horn.

The run was over.

---

It was very quiet after the horn.

The lamps in the hall came back on, the ordinary overhead lamps that ran off the Cellhouse's own core and not the trial cell. The chute hatch was shut and locked. The load board on the east wall stayed dark.

Kiva sat on the concrete beside Perrin with her back against the pump room wall and took stock of herself.

She was grey from her chin to her knees. The ash had got everywhere, into her collar, down her sleeves, into the creases of her hands. Her left forearm was scraped raw from the elbow down to the edge of the cuff, with ash in the raw part, and the leather of the cuff itself was scored pale where the limb had gone over it. Her left hand was numb across the back and the first two fingers. She tried to bend them, and they bent, but she could not feel them bending. It was like moving someone else's fingers.

And there was something hanging against the front of her shirt.

The disk had come out of her collar when the husk hit her. It lay on its cord against her chest, dark and grey with ash, with the nine grooves in it full of powder. She closed her good hand around it.

It was cold.

Of course it was cold. It was always cold. It had been cold through everything, through the hurry and the joy of fixing it and the three doors going in, through the jump and the landing and the stance. She had reached for the thing Orla said nobody could do, and she had not done it. She had jammed one door open with another and stood in the wreck of it, and it had held for as long as it had to, and she did not think she could do even that again on purpose. The disk had not cared one way or another.

She tucked it back inside her shirt, and ash came with it.

A Wren House medic came across the floor with a bag and knelt beside her. He was young and brisk and had the calm, slightly bored face of someone who had seen a great many Cellhouse runs. He examined the forearm first, then the knee. Then he noticed the way she was holding her left shoulder.

"Open your collar for me."

She did, and he bent over the heat-lines.

She had not seen them yet herself. They were not pink anymore. They were an angry red, raised along their length like welts, and in two places the skin had gone shiny and tight over a blister. They ran from the middle of her breastbone, over her collarbone, past the point of her shoulder, and down the outside of her upper arm, halfway to her elbow.

They had never gone past her shoulder. In four years. Not once.

The medic's face did not change, but he was quiet for a moment.

"Backwash," he said. "From the cell?"

"From the cell. Through me."

"Mm." He laid two fingers lightly beside the lines, not on them. "These are new? This far down?"

"New."

He did not say anything. He took a jar of burn salve out of his bag and began to spread it along the lines, very gently, with a flat wooden blade. It was cold and it stung. Then he took her left hand and turned it over and pressed his thumb into the tips of her first two fingers, one at a time.

"Feel that?"

"No."

He pressed the third finger. "That?"

"Yes."

He pressed the first two again, harder. She felt a faint, dull pressure, very far away.

"Nerve irritation," he said. "It follows the same line as the backwash. Down the arm into the hand. Those two fingers are on the same nerve." He let go of her hand. "It should start coming back in a few hours, and most of the rest over a week or two. Cold cloths on the lines tonight, not ice. If the fingers aren't better by tomorrow night, you come to the Wren House and you ask for Healer Aske, and nobody else." He waited until she met his eyes. "It'll come back this time. Next time it might not. Do you understand me?"

"I understand."

"Good." He stood up. "Ice the knee. Don't use that arm for a week. And stay away from live cells until somebody with more sense than me says you can."

---

The numbers went up on the gallery board ten minutes later.

The board was a long sheet of pale glass along the bottom edge of the gallery, facing down into the hall, so that the teams on the floor could read it. When it lit, the whole hall went quiet, and Kiva could hear the gallery behind the thick glass go quiet too.

*TEAM SEVEN.*

*Objective 1 — Extraction: 4:41. (Standard 4:00.) PARTIAL.*

*Objective 2 — Cell: Breaker trip 3:38, uncontrolled. Quench 3:44, Renn. FAIL.*

*Objective 3 — Containment: No spawn reached casualty or line. PASS.*

*Objective 4 — Call: Role changes declared — Rowan (tune), Renn (quench). Undeclared role change — Rowan (cell to floor, 3:31). One violation.*

*Dram spent: Renn 0.41. Pryce 0.55. Cade 0.93. Rowan 0.96. No overspend.*

*Run result: FAILED.*

Kiva read it twice.

She had known. She had known since the needle crossed the red. But it was different, seeing it on the board in letters a hand high with four hundred people reading it at the same time. *FAILED.* And above it, her own name, twice, once for the role she had declared and broken, and once for the one she had not declared at all.

She had not said anything when she went over the rail. She had not said *leaving cell*. She had not said *protecting Cade*. She had just gone.

Mara had told her. *You'll want to. When you do, say it out loud.*

She had not said it out loud.

Kellan came and stood in front of them. He had ash down one side of his face and a long scrape on his right knee where it had hit the edge of the mezzanine. He read the board, and then he turned to Kiva.

"You said no doors," he said. His voice was quiet. It was not angry. It was something more careful than angry. "In the corridor. To my face. And it went on the card."

"I know."

"You declared the change. I heard it. It bought about twenty seconds, and then it cost the run." He did not raise his voice. "If I'd known it was coming, I'd have been up top, next to you, instead of at the bottom of the stair with my back to it. That's what a card is for. It tells me where to stand."

Kiva did not say anything. There was nothing to say. He was right.

"And every call you made was right," said Kellan. "Including the last one, from the floor." He stopped, and his eyes went back to the board, to his own name next to the word *Quench*. "I've got one column that says you broke the run and one that says I'd have been wrong without you. They don't add. I don't know what to do with that."

"Neither do I," said Kiva.

He held her eyes for a long moment. Then he nodded, very slightly, as if they had agreed on a number.

"Fill in the card," he said. "Properly. All the columns."

He walked away toward the Gate.

---

They filled in the card sitting on the concrete, all four of them, while the clerks came out with pans and soft brushes and began to gather the ash off the floor around them, heap by heap, into sealed tins for the Wren House graders.

Anwen held the card on her knee and wrote. She did not ask anyone what to put. She wrote what had happened, in order, in her small neat hand, and then she held out the pencil to each of them in turn for the last column.

Perrin took it first, with hands that were still shaking. He wrote *Why: shutter weight on hands when winch died. Held with still door. Gave it back slowly. Rowan told me how.* Then he gave the pencil back and lay down flat on the floor again and shut his eyes.

Kellan was gone, so Anwen wrote his for him, from what he had said. *Why: cell still calling after breaker. Quench is mechanical. Rowan's numbers right all run.*

Then she held the pencil out to Kiva.

Kiva took it. Her right hand was steady enough. She stared at the column.

She thought about the cell under her hand, and the hurry in it, and the joy of pressing it out. She thought about the fear, when the husk came up the stair, and the three doors going in, and the ringing that would not stop after she let go.

She wrote: *Why: a cell keeps what you leave in it. I left all three doors in it. Then I took my hand off and it kept them.*

She read it over. It was true. It was not all of it.

Under it, smaller, she wrote: *Went over the rail without declaring. Because Perrin was in the way. Should have said it.*

She gave the pencil back to Anwen.

Anwen read it. Then she folded the card and stood up to take it to the booth. She stopped beside Kiva and took in the ash and the salve and the raw forearm and the arm held close against her side.

"You protected him," said Anwen.

"I failed the run."

"You did both." Anwen held the card against her chest. "I didn't come out. When you called for us to get out. You know that? I heard you, and I didn't come out. I kept working on the legs."

"You were right not to."

"I know I was right. That's not the part." Anwen's face did something complicated. "I chose the casualty. Over getting out. Over you. Because it was my objective, and it's what the field panels score, and I wanted it." She paused. "And you were holding a husk off us while I chose it. I want that said."

"It's said," said Kiva.

Anwen nodded. Then she went up the stairs to the booth with the card.

Kiva sat with her back to the pump room wall and Perrin lying beside her and raised her eyes to the gallery.

Ysra Crest was standing at the glass in the assessors' booth, looking down. Kiva could not see her face clearly from here. She could see her right hand. It was resting on the brass lever with the red grip. The master stop. It had been resting there, Kiva realized, for a long time, maybe since the needle crossed eight.

She had not pulled it.

Ysra lifted the hand from the lever and leaned down to the speaking tube on the booth wall. Her voice came out of the horn over the hall, dry and level.

"Miss Rowan. My office. Tomorrow at nine. Bring your book."

Further along the gallery, in the section with the better chairs, a woman in a slate-blue Graywater coat had leaned forward and put her hand flat against the glass. She had a white streak through her dark hair. She was not looking at the board.

She was looking at Kiva.
