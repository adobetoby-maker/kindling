# Chapter 27 — Kiva: One Crossing

The first tram on Sunday left Coper Street at twenty minutes past five, and there was nobody on it but the driver and two girls and a kit bag.

Kiva carried the bag. It had been settled on the laundry step without discussion, by her picking it up; it was slate blue, with a white wave stitched on the flap, and it was a good deal heavier than Orla's crate. Anwen had let her take it. She sat by the window with her hands in her lap and her list in her coat, and for the first three stops she watched the street lamps going out one after another as the light came up behind them.

"Finch crosses that cord right four times in five," she said, at the fourth. "I had her do it twenty times after you'd gone up. Watch the fifth. She gets it wrong when she's talking, and she talks most when she's frightened."

"I'll watch."

"Don't correct her in the count. Call the check and let her find it. She'll be quicker next time if she's found it herself."

"You never told me that was what you were doing."

"No. You'd have minded." Anwen turned from the window. "The left pan's still slower than the right, when Kellan has it. He knows. He'll tell you that he's allowing for it, and he is, and it's still slower."

It went on like that along the whole of the Brickfields road. She gave the team to Kiva a piece at a time, the corners and the pans and which of Perrin's jokes meant that his ribs were hurting, and Kiva put each piece away without writing it, because she was holding a kit bag in both arms and because none of it wanted a column. Neither of them mentioned a gate. It rode between them on the slatted seat, and they both knew where it was.

The company hall was a long slate-blue front with its lamps still lit. There were six people on the pavement already, standing about with bags at their feet in the manner of people who have been told a quarter to six and have come at half past five, and at the top of the steps a broad grey man with a board under his arm was counting them without appearing to.

"That's Mace," said Anwen. "He'll not speak to you. He doesn't speak to anybody before seven."

They stopped at the foot of the steps. Kiva set the bag down on the flags.

"The culverts first," said Anwen. "A fortnight of nights. Then the Sag. We hold a lamp for the inspectors and stand reliefs on anything that opens. I'll be allowed a wall of my own by the fourth week if I don't flicker." She looked up at the blue front, and for a moment her face was not level in the least. It was seventeen, and it wanted this more than it could conveniently hide. "I've wanted to stand on those steps since I was eleven."

"I know. I've seen the poster on your door."

"Everybody's seen the poster on my door." She bent and took the strap. "Write me the counts."

"What?"

"Whatever you stand under while I'm gone. The shift. Where the mark was, and why, and what it sealed at, and who called. Send it up by the hall; Mace's runner comes out to the line twice a week." The strap went over her shoulder. "I'd like to read what I wasn't at. I'm not asking for a letter. I'm asking for the counts."

"With the *why* column?"

"Particularly that." Anwen settled the bag. "And if you find that you've had an answer ready, put that in too. I'll know anyway. I'd as soon see it in your hand."

She went up the steps. She did not look back from the top, and Kiva had not thought that she would. The broad man glanced at her, and made a mark on his board, and she took her place at the end of the row with her back to the road.

Kiva stood a little longer on the pavement than there was any need to.

She had no answer ready. She had looked for one all the way from Coper Street and had found the place quite bare, and it had not felt like a virtue. It had felt like standing in a doorway with nothing to carry. Then she turned, and went to wait for the tram going the other way.

---

Hollis Vane had carried the tracing in his slate case for eleven days, and on Monday morning he took it down three flights of iron stairs.

He had shown it first to the two oldest hands on the Line. One had said that it was a laundry mark and the other had held it upside down. Then Orla Dane had sent him a note on Saturday that was four words long. *Arkwright. Stacks. Ask nicely.* He had not known what the last two meant, and had put on a clean collar to be safe.

The woman at the table read it without touching it. She had two pairs of spectacles and she looked through both.

"It's the same hand," she said, mostly to herself.

"The same as what?"

"As a great deal that I've read. It's the Works' late record hand; your people borrowed it for a generation because it was quick." She did not explain further, and he had the impression of a door being shut with great courtesy. "Where does this stand in its book?"

"The back. There's a part headed in the old print. *Directions to the Unplaced.* Any officer who gives a field direction to somebody the schools haven't classified writes it there, with his reason. I wrote one on the thirtieth." He kept his voice even. "I'd thought I should be the first. There was this above mine, and nothing else on the leaf."

"One entry."

"One before me. In a book that's been copied forward since before the Compact had a charter."

"Then somebody thought it worth copying as it stood, and couldn't read it either, or he'd have put it into a fair hand." She drew the lamp close. "You're a patient man, Officer. You've not once told me to hurry. I'll give it to you whole."

She read.

"*Ferren levee, the spring flood, 2471. Direction to Tansy Wold, unplaced. Two doors, hand and still. On no school's roll and no company's. One crossing, hand to still, called aloud before. Reason: the sluice wheel wanted a ward to clear its race and a stance to turn it, and there was no reaching it twice. Result: turned. Eleven streets dry.*"

Vane had stopped breathing through his nose.

"*Cost: her left hand, without feeling to the wrist, that night and after. Not returned by midsummer. She says the crossing took more than both together. I believe her. I would not direct it twice in one flood, nor to anyone I had not watched miss. E.K., watch.*" The finger moved down a line. "And beneath, the same hand, a different ink. *Entered on the Line's roll as catcher, autumn. Hand as before.*"

It was very quiet under the lamp.

He had stood on a cinder floor with a slate, and a girl forty paces off had told him in a carrying voice that she had missed fourteen of fourteen. He had weighed that for about as long as it took to draw a breath. He had thought, at the time, that he was weighing it rather well.

*Nor to anyone I had not watched miss.*

A man on a levee two hundred and nine years ago had set that down as a rule, having learned it in one night. Vane had been handed the same rule by luck, by a candidate who happened to be honest, and he had not known that it was one.

"A crossing," he said. "They'd a word for it."

"They'd a word, and a heading for the page, and a column for what it cost." The woman took off the upper spectacles. "Your present ledger asks for the reason and the result, I should think. Somebody tidied the third away. It's nearly always the third."

It did. He had written *Hurt: Rowan, collapse after. Cade, ribs, again.* He had written nothing about a hand, because he had not known that there was a hand to write about, and nobody at the Wren House cart had thought it was a foreman's business.

"What became of her? Wold."

"That's every word in your ledger. If the Line kept a roll for the 2470s, I've never been shown it."

"It did. It was recopied in the 2550s. Names and years." He heard how that sounded. "No remarks."

"No. There wouldn't be." She folded the tracing along its crease and gave it back to him, and with it a half sheet on which she had written the whole thing out in a clear round hand as she read. "That's for whoever it concerns. I don't ask who. I'll say only that it's the second time in a week I've been asked to read that hand by somebody who went white at the end of it, and I've a notion that the two of you ought to be introduced."

He met them on the stair.

He was going up and they were coming down, the aunt first, whom he knew from a berm on the Sag Line with a mast drum under her hand, and the girl behind her, one step at a time on a strapped knee. There was not room to pass. He backed down to the landing and took off his hat.

"Miss Rowan. And Miss Rowan."

"Officer," said the aunt. She did not ask what a Home Line watch officer was doing in a library basement, and he saw her decide not to.

He looked at the girl. He looked, since he could not now help it, at her left hand on the rail, and at how the last two fingers held and the first two only rested.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "Your sponsor's yard, if she'll have me. There's a thing I've had read. It's about you only as far as it's about anybody who's ever done what you did, and I'd sooner you heard it from the page than from me."

"Is it bad?"

"It's two hundred years old. I don't know yet what it is." He put his hat on. "I know that I wrote four lines about you on the thirtieth and thought them complete. I'll be mending that."

---

The Reader wrote them in with a steel pen.

She did it standing, at the tall desk beside the stair, and she did it in what was plainly her best hand, a round black script that made the endowment's yearly clerk look like a man scratching with a nail. *K. Rowan, student, the Crooked Line. Annick, Personal. With the hand of M. Rowan, guardian.* She turned the book for each of them, and they signed below, and the ink of the two names stood wet and dark under a line that was sixteen years old.

"Monday the eleventh," said the Reader. "Fourteen days. That's Monday the twenty-fifth, at two, in the small room off the reading room; I'll have the fire lit, since the Order feels the cold and won't say so. I shall send both notices by this evening's post." She blotted the page. "You may still withdraw, any day up to the day. They'd be told that as well."

"We'll not withdraw," said Mara.

She had read the settlement herself, all four lines, in the Library's own copy, with her left forefinger moving under the words. She had asked two questions about it, and both had been about what happened to the black book when it was full.

"There's one thing more," she said. "I've a page at home in my own hand. It was written on a night this spring, the same night as what it describes, and it's dated. I don't know what's in your box, and I've been told by somebody who does that I'm not to ask." Her eyes went briefly to Kiva. "But if it should turn out that my page and your box have anything to say to one another, I shall want it proved which was shut first."

The Reader put down her pen.

She looked at Mara for some time, with her head a little on one side, and Kiva saw two people of the same trade discover each other across a desk.

"What do you do?" said the Reader.

"I reconcile the Works' books against its instruments. I've done it since before she was born."

"I thought it might be that. Nobody else asks about the wax." She came round the desk. "I can't witness what a page says, Miss Rowan. I'll not read it and I'll not have it read to me. What I can witness is that it was closed before my box was opened. Bring it on the day, sealed, with your own mark over the fold. I'll put my wax across yours, with the hour written through the join, and lock it in the press behind that door with the settlement. It comes out to your hand or to a magistrate's and to nobody else's." She smiled a little. "There are perhaps three people in this city who'd take my hour over a court clerk's. They happen to be the three whose opinion will count."

"It's the only copy," said Mara.

"Then it's the only one that will be believed. A copy made afterward is a recollection."

Mara stood with her hand flat on the desk. Kiva knew what was being asked of her. It was the page of the dream, the whole of it, in pencil, written at the kitchen table at ten o'clock on the night of the Pit by somebody who had spent that morning across the road with her hand on a cell. Mara had never let it out of the flat.

"The household rule's that it's told the same night and that I write it," she said, to Kiva and not the Reader. "I made that rule so that it should be kept somewhere I could put my hand on it."

"You'd know where it was."

"I should know that it was in a locked press in a basement with a stranger's wax on it." A breath. "Which is a good deal better kept than a kitchen drawer, and I'd say so about anybody's page but mine. Very well. Sealed, on the day."

They went up the iron stairs together, slowly, Mara a step ahead.

"She liked you," said Kiva.

"She liked that I asked about the wax. It isn't the same; don't write it down as though it were." Mara's hand found the rail. "That man on the landing. He looked at your fingers."

"I saw."

"I've stood beside him on a berm while he sent a team home with its kit unopened, sooner than put them in front of a thing he couldn't time. He doesn't look at what doesn't bear on the work." She stopped at the turn, and got her breath. "Whatever he's bringing to that yard tomorrow, I want it in the book the same night. Every word of it. I'll not have a second thing kept from this table because it was in a hand nobody could read."

The Circuit's travelling mast came into the Old Yard on Tuesday evening, in three pieces, and Perrin supervised.

He could do nothing else. He stood in the lane with his arms held a little away from his strapped sides, like a man in a barrel, and told Kellan and Noa where to put their feet. There was a brass head as big as a bucket, and a tripod of jointed legs taller than he was, and a drum in a felt-lined case that Noa would let nobody else touch. On the lid of the case hung a lead seal with the Placement Chair's stamp in it.

"A foot to your left," said Perrin. "No. The other left. You've a post behind you, Renn; it's been there five hundred years and it has the right of way."

"It leans."

"It's leaning away from you. Take the hint."

They stood it at last on the east side of the lane, six paces from the scraped line and well clear of the cross. Noa broke the seal in front of all of them and read out the number on it, and wrote the number down, and threaded a tape with his tongue between his teeth.

"One pen," he said. "Set to her and nobody else. I copy every tape for the Chair the next morning. That's her letter; it's what makes the instrument sanctioned." He hesitated. "It's also what makes the tape hers to show. Not to chartered parties in thirty days; that's for events. But she's a hearing coming with Dray's objection, and she's told Dray that she'll hear it on evidence."

"So anything I do in front of that drum, Selka Dray may read," said Kiva.

"If the Chair thinks it bears. Yes."

"It was Anwen's condition," said Kellan. "A sanctioned mast, every go. I thought at the time that it was for your safety."

"It was. It's for everybody's." Kiva looked at the brass head, which had begun to tick. She had asked a library for a private box the day before and had been told, very fairly, that there was no such thing. "A meter's a meter. It doesn't choose who looks."

Orla had watched all of this from her crate without a word. Hollis Vane stood behind her with his hat in his hand. He had come down the lane at eight exactly, and had taken off the hat to Orla and nodded to the rest, and since then he had waited, as a man waits who has learned on tear watches how much of the work is standing still.

"Now, Hollis," said Orla.

He did not make a speech of it. He told them what the ledger was and where the page stood in it, in about four sentences. Then he took a half sheet from his slate case and read out what was written there, in a flat, careful, outdoor voice, from the levee to the initials.

Nobody spoke while he read. The mast ticked. At *her left hand, without feeling to the wrist,* Kiva felt Kellan's eyes come round to her, and then Perrin's, and she kept her own on the half sheet.

*She says the crossing took more than both together.*

Kiva had written that. She had written it nine nights ago on that crate, with a yellow lamp over her, in words that were hardly different. *The work goes across. It costs more than both doors.* She had thought that she was the first person alive to find it out, and that had been a lonely thing to know, and rather a grand one. Now a watch officer was reading it to her out of the year 2471.

The woman had had a name. It had been written down by a man who believed her.

"*Entered on the Line's roll as catcher, autumn,*" said Vane. "*Hand as before.*" He lowered the sheet. "That's the end. There's no roll for those years that keeps a remark. I don't know how long she lived or whether it ever came back."

"A catcher," said Kiva.

"So it says."

"With one hand that couldn't feel a cord."

"I noticed that as well." His weathered face did not change. "I wrote on my own slate, at the Pit, *Hurt: Rowan, collapse after,* and I copied it into the ledger next morning as it stood. I never asked what your hand was doing. E.K. would have known to look. I've amended the entry. It reads now, *Cost: two fingers of the left hand, eased within the hour, returned to their earlier state and no better.* If that's wrong in any word, tell me, and I'll have it right before I go."

"It's right."

"Then it'll stand under my name." He put the sheet away. "Dane. That's what I had. The rest's yours."

Orla got up.

She did it slowly, with the stick, and she stood in the lane between the mast and the crooked post and looked at each of them in turn, the two who had carried and the one who could not and the one who was going to jump.

"I've run this yard on a stick and two eyes," she said. "I'd no rule for this, because nobody had left me one. Somebody's left me one now. It's two hundred years late and I'm going to use every word of it." She held up her left hand, and folded down a finger. "A step first, every night, off her own ration. A curb's height and a pace. If it isn't clean, there's no go, and nobody argues."

A second finger.

"One go in a night. Never two. *Not twice in one flood.* I don't know why he wrote that, and I'd sooner keep his rule than learn his reason."

A third.

"Before she leaves the line she tells me whether the bottom thread is cold. Aloud. If she says *one,* she sits down." A fourth. "In the air: if that Ember isn't shut clean at the top, she calls *off* and comes down as herself, on the right leg, rolling. That isn't a miss to be ashamed of. That's the only part of this I can teach, and I mean to see it done before I see the other."

"And after?" said Kiva.

"After, you show me that hand before you show me your face. Any white in those two fingers and we're done for the night. White past a slow sixty, and you see Aske before you see this yard again." The thumb came down on the rest. "Bexley says nothing till she's finished. That's Pryce's condition and it's a sound one. And there's to be one voice in this lane besides mine that may stop it."

"Anwen said I'm to call *off* for Seven," said Perrin. He looked rather surprised to hear himself. "While she's gone. Because I mind things."

"Then you call it for this as well. If you see what I don't, you say the word three times and I'll thank you afterward." Orla lowered her hand. "I'm told that there are two drams in a glass at the ash office with four names on them. I'd like the four to know what they're buying. It isn't six landings. It's six that I'd ask for twice. The misses will cost you next to nothing, and they're most of what there is to learn."

Healer Aske passed the knee on Wednesday morning with his thumbs and a grunt, and on Wednesday night Kiva stood on the scraped line with a week's ration in her and four-tenths of the team's on top of it.

It was the thirteenth. She had not lit so much as a strut in a fortnight.

The step came first. She pushed off the line, a curb's height and a pace, and shut the Ember at the top, and set the still door under her foot before the ground. It was like stepping onto a mattress the size of the yard. Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder and went through her and into the earth, and she counted three and gave the ground its weight back and shut the door.

"Clean," said Orla. "Is the thread cold?"

Kiva laid two fingers of her right hand on her breastbone.

"Cold."

"Then you may have your go. To the cross. One low bound, as it was in the spring, and not an inch higher because there are people looking." The stick pointed. "Say it."

"Rowan, changing on three. Ember, to still."

It sounded very small. She had last said it across an arena to eleven hundred people. There were four here, and a brass head on three legs, and a post that leaned.

The mast ticked behind her. She had her back to it, on Orla's terms.

"*One.*"

The metal came off the cuff and ran down her sides and closed on her calves. She bent both knees, the good and the newly passed, and left the line.

It was four paces. It was nothing. She had crossed a brick sump on the same push with a Flask at her back.

And she could hear the pen.

She had not thought of that. On the shelf there had been a roar in the terraces and her own blood. Here there was a quiet yard, and behind her left shoulder a steel nib that had been scratching evenly and was now, as the struts took her weight, running up its paper with a small, thin, eager sound. She knew what it was drawing. She knew to the hundredth.

"*Two.*"

She shut the Ember at the top.

She shut it, and listened to hear whether the pen had heard.

It was no more than that. It was a quarter of a breath. But it was the same fault that she had spent a month unlearning with a tin and a stick, when she used to watch the metal run home along her arm to make certain it had arrived. The struts let go late. One of them clung for an instant, and she felt it cling. The metal was still running up her ribs when the top of the bound was already behind her and the cross was coming up out of the dirt.

She had half a breath in which to choose.

The still door was there. It was ready; she could feel it under her as she felt a pan under ash. If she opened it now it would open on a doorway that was not yet empty, and she knew precisely what that was called, and she had done it four times in this yard and once in a cart shed.

*Is the Ember shut clean at the top?*

*No.*

"*Off!*" said Kiva. "Off, off—"

She let go of the pan. She let the whole of it go, and came down as nothing but a girl of seventeen travelling a good deal too fast, and she took the ground on her right foot and did not try to keep it. She folded at the hip as she had been shown and went over her own shoulder, and the Old Yard came round once and hit her in the back.

She lay a pace short of the cross and half a pace to the side of it, looking up at the lamp.

Nothing hurt. That was the first thing. The left knee had not been asked to do anything. There was dirt in her collar and her right elbow was skinned, and the bottom thread on her breastbone was as cold as it had been on the line.

"Hand," said Orla's voice, above her.

Kiva held up her left hand. The old fingers turned it over in the lamplight, and pressed the two tips, and let go.

"Pink. Good." The crooked face came into view against the sky. "Now your face, since you're so anxious I should have it. You're angry."

"I was listening to the pen."

"I know you were. I watched your head go." Orla straightened, with a grunt. "And then you did the only thing in sixteen goes that I've set you and you've done. You asked yourself the question at the top, you got the wrong answer, and you came down on it. I've seen that ordered a great many times. I've seen it obeyed about twice."

"It's another miss."

"It's the first thing you've done in this yard that I'd have paid for, and it turns out that somebody has." She looked over her shoulder. "Mr. Bexley. She's finished."

Noa came out from behind the mast with the tape across both palms. He was trying very hard to keep his face from saying anything, and not succeeding.

"The step. Two hundredths; it always is. Then the bound, two again. Then there's a tremble at the top, about as long as a blink, and a hundredth goes on it. And then nothing. It's flat. You shut everything." He looked up. "Five. Five hundredths for the night, and three of them were the go."

"Three hundredths," said Kellan. He had his own book out, and he wrote it under a ruled line. "One dram and ninety-seven left. I'd call that cheap."

"I'd wanted the cross," said Kiva. She had sat up, and she was looking at it. It was a pace away, scratched in the dirt, and she had never yet come down on it in that yard. "I could feel the door. It was there."

"I'm sure it was," said Perrin. "I'd my mouth open. I'd got as far as the *o.*" He lowered himself onto the spare crate with great care. "I'd like you to know that I've never been so pleased to be beaten to a word in my life. I'm putting it on the list of things I don't mind."

She laughed, which she had not meant to do, and found that the anger had a crack in it.

She got up by herself. She brushed off what dirt would come, and went and stood on the cross for a moment in her ordinary boots, with nothing open, to see what the yard looked like from there. It looked much the same. The post leaned over her left shoulder. Under her shirt the disk lay against her breastbone, cold as a coin, and she noticed that she had not thought of it once between the line and the ground.

"Friday," said Orla. "Not tomorrow. Tomorrow you'll come and sit with your back to that drum for an hour while Bexley runs it empty, until you can't tell it from a clock." She was already turning toward the shed. "You'll want to write it. I'd put it short."

Kiva wrote it under the lamp, on her knee, while the others packed the drum.

*Sanctioned go, the first. Old Yard, travelling mast. Called off at the top. Why: the pen. I listened for it, like the metal. The door was ready and the doorway wasn't.*

She thought. Then, in the column that asked what it had cost:

*0.03. An elbow. Nothing else.*

And beneath that, because she had been asked for it on a pavement at a quarter to six in the morning by somebody who would know anyway:

*I'd an answer ready for Orla before I'd stood up. She had hers first.*
