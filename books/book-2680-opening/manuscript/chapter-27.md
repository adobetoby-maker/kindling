# Chapter 27 — Kiva: One Crossing

The first tram on Sunday left Coper Street at twenty minutes past five, and there was nobody on it but the driver and two girls and a kit bag.

Kiva carried the bag. It had been settled on the laundry step without discussion, by her picking it up; it was slate blue, with a white wave stitched on the flap, and it was a good deal heavier than Orla's crate. Anwen had let her take it. She sat by the window with her hands in her lap, and for the first few stops she watched the street lamps going out one after another as the light came up behind them.

"Finch crosses that cord right four times in five," she said, somewhere past the bakery. "I had her do it till her fingers were raw after you'd gone up. Watch the fifth. She gets it wrong when she's talking, and she talks most when she's frightened."

"I'll watch."

"Don't correct her mid-cord. Call the check and let her find it. She'll be quicker next time if she's found it herself."

"You never told me that was what you were doing."

"No. You'd have minded." Anwen turned from the window. "The left pan's still slower than the right, when Kellan has it. He knows. He'll tell you that he's allowing for it, and he is, and it's still slower."

It went on like that along the whole of the Brickfields road. She gave the team to Kiva a piece at a time, the corners and the pans and which of Perrin's jokes meant that his ribs were hurting, and Kiva put each piece away as it came. Neither of them mentioned a gate. It rode between them on the slatted seat, and they both knew where it was.

The company hall was a long slate-blue front with its lamps still lit. There were six people on the pavement already, standing about with bags at their feet in the manner of people who have been told a quarter to six and have come at half past five, and at the top of the steps a broad grey man with a board under his arm was looking them over without appearing to.

"That's Mace," said Anwen. "He won't speak to you. He doesn't speak to anybody before seven."

They stopped at the foot of the steps. Kiva set the bag down on the flags.

"The culverts first," said Anwen. "A fortnight of nights. Then the Sag. We hold a lamp for the inspectors and stand reliefs on anything that opens. I'll be allowed a wall of my own by the fourth week if I don't flicker." She looked up at the blue front, and for a moment her face was not level in the least. It was seventeen, and it wanted this more than it could conveniently hide. "I've wanted to stand on those steps since I was twelve."

"I know. I've seen the poster on your door."

"Everybody's seen the poster on my door." She bent and took the strap. "Don't come out to the culverts. I'd never hold a lamp straight with you on the bank."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You'd have come on the Tuesday and stood behind a wall and thought I hadn't seen you." The strap went over her shoulder. "Don't."

She went up the steps. She did not look back from the top, and Kiva had not thought that she would. The broad man glanced at her, and nodded once, and she took her place at the end of the row with her back to the road.

Kiva stood a little longer on the pavement than there was any need to.

She had nothing to say. She had looked for something all the way from Coper Street and had found the place quite bare. It felt like standing in a doorway with nothing to carry. Then she turned, and went to wait for the tram going the other way.

---

Hollis Vane went up to the Wren House on Monday morning to see about a pair of ribs that he had said were his.

He had said it on a cinder floor, to four students and a keeper, and he had meant it. In his experience a thing like that was only meant if you went and found out afterward what it was costing. So he climbed the infirmary stair with his hat in his hand and asked at the sister's desk for whoever had the Stonehand boy.

It was Aske. Vane knew him a little, as a field officer knows the healer who has strapped him up more than once and has views about it.

"Cade's knitting," said Aske. He did not get up from his table. "He'll have been strapped twice this spring by the time he's done, and he'll go on telling everybody that he minds it. Stonehand will have him back for the autumn." He looked at Vane over his spectacles. "You've come about the ribs."

"I said they were mine."

"So Cade tells me. He tells everybody. He seems to think it was handsome of you." Aske took the spectacles off and began to polish them. "And the girl's fingers? Did you come about those as well?"

"What fingers?"

The healer stopped polishing.

"Nobody told you," he said.

"Told me what?"

"Rowan. The first two fingers of her left hand went white on your lever, Officer, and stayed white. Her team opened their sealed reserve at my cart inside the hour, all four of them agreed, and I eased them. They've come back to where they were in the spring, which is slow and late, and no better. They won't be better." He put the spectacles on again. "It's a nerve that's been burned once and starved twice. Each time she does whatever it is that she does, it's slower coming back."

Vane stood very still.

He had been on that floor. He had said his piece about the lid and the ribs, and turned to the grading, and behind him a ward-holder had knelt in front of a crate and asked when those fingers went white. He had not heard it. He had not been listening for it.

The Home Line asked its four things at the cart, and he had asked them himself on the Pit floor, as he had on a hundred floors before it. *Who was hurt:* Cade, ribs; Rowan, down after. Nobody had said a hand. Nobody had thought it a foreman's business. The cart was for the district's account, the pumps and the tins and the people carried off. What the work had cost the people who did it, they took home with them, and in all his years on the Line nobody had asked to see it.

He had given that hand a field direction because its owner had told him, across forty paces, that she had missed every try she had ever made. He had weighed that for about as long as it took to draw a breath, and he had thought he was weighing it rather well. He would not have done it for anybody he had not heard say she missed.

He saw now that he had never asked the other half.

"She'll tell you straight, if you ask," said Aske. "Nobody does, as a rule. They ask me." He turned back to his table. "Your Stonehand boy's in the second bed. Don't make him laugh."

Vane went down the stair afterward with his hat still in his hand, and did not notice that he was carrying it until he was out in the street.

Mara had the shop open late on Monday, because a pump-house regulator had come back from the Works with a fault nobody could find, and she would not leave a fault on her bench overnight if she could help it.

Kiva sat on the stool by the window, where she had done her school sums at fourteen, and watched her aunt work. Mara's left hand lay flat on the regulator's case. Her eyes were shut. Every so often the hand moved an inch, and stopped, and moved again, like somebody feeling along a wall in the dark for a door.

"She went, then," said Mara, without opening her eyes.

"At six. Mace didn't speak to her."

"He wouldn't. He doesn't speak to me, and I've known him twenty years." The hand moved. "And you came home and sat on that stool and didn't eat anything."

"I ate a bun."

"On the tram. I can see the sugar." Mara opened her eyes and took her hand away from the case, and flexed it. "Have you decided about the hill?"

"No."

"Good. I'd not trust it if you had, this soon." She reached for a screwdriver and did not pick it up. "I'll say one thing about it and then I'll leave it alone. I'm not going to tell you yes or no. I said no for you once, to Ilse Corran, on that step down there. You were six. And she gave you the thing anyway, three years after she was dead, by courier, and I opened the door to it in my apron." Her mouth went thin. "I've been angry with her ever since for not listening to me. I won't say no for you a second time and stand in the door while the world goes round me."

Kiva looked at her.

"You never told me you were angry."

"You never asked. And you were thirteen, and she was dead." Mara picked up the screwdriver. "Orla Dane went up that hill sixteen years ago and came down and said nothing to anybody. I'm going to go and see her about that."

"Don't."

"I shan't take you. And I shan't go till I'm ready, which isn't tonight, because I've a regulator with something in it." She bent over the case again. "I'm not going to shout at her, if that's what your face is for. I've never yet got a thing out of a person by shouting. I'll say two sentences and come home."

"Which two?"

"I'll know when I'm there," said Mara, and laid her hand back on the case, and shut her eyes.

The Circuit's travelling mast came into the Old Yard on Tuesday evening, in three pieces, and Perrin supervised.

He could do nothing else. He stood in the lane with his arms held a little away from his strapped sides, like a man in a barrel, and told Kellan and Noa where to put their feet. There was a brass head as big as a bucket, and a tripod of jointed legs taller than he was, and a drum in a felt-lined case that Noa would let nobody else touch. On the lid of the case hung a lead seal with the Placement Chair's stamp in it.

"A foot to your left," said Perrin. "No. The other left. You've got a post behind you, Renn; it's been there five hundred years and it has the right of way."

"It leans."

"It's leaning away from you. Take the hint."

They stood it at last on the east side of the lane, six paces from the scraped line and well clear of the cross. Noa broke the seal in front of all of them and threaded a tape with his tongue between his teeth.

"One pen," he said. "Set to her and nobody else. The tape goes to the Chair in the morning; that's what makes the instrument sanctioned." He hesitated. "It's also what makes the tape hers to show. Not to chartered parties in thirty days; that's for events. But she's got Dray's objection to hear, and she's told Dray she'll hear it on what the tape shows."

"So anything I do in front of that drum, Selka Dray can see," said Kiva.

"If the Chair thinks it bears. Yes."

"It was Anwen's condition," said Kellan. "A sanctioned mast, every go. I thought at the time it was for your safety."

"It was. It's for everybody's." Kiva looked at the brass head, which had begun to tick. "A meter's a meter. It doesn't choose who looks."

Orla had watched all of this from her crate without a word. Hollis Vane stood behind her with his hat in his hand. He had come down the lane at eight exactly, and had taken off the hat to Orla and nodded to the rest, and since then he had waited, as a man waits who has learned on tear watches how much of the work is standing still.

"Now, Hollis," said Orla.

He did not stand on ceremony. He came round the crate and stood in front of Kiva, a little nearer than he usually stood to anybody.

"At the Pit I asked the Line's four things," he said. "Was it closed. Who was hurt. What still works in the morning. What we tinned. I was told Cade's ribs, and that you went down after, and I asked no further. Yesterday I went to the Wren House about the ribs, and the healer asked me whether I'd come about your fingers too." His weathered face did not change. "Nobody had told me. I hadn't asked. The Line doesn't ask it at a cart, and it never has in my time. So I'm asking you now, and I'd like the whole of it. What did it cost you?"

Kiva felt Kellan's eyes come round to her, and then Perrin's. She kept her own on Vane.

Nobody on a floor had ever asked her that. They had asked her what a thing had cost the team, and the district, and the drum. She found that she had to look for the answer, because she had never had to carry it out of her own mouth.

"Two fingers," she said. "The first two, on the left. They went white on the lever and stayed white. The team opened the reserve at the cart, and Aske eased them inside the hour. They came back to where they'd been in the spring. Slow, and late. No better than that."

"And will they be?"

"Aske says not."

Vane nodded, once.

"Then that's a fifth thing at the cart," he said, "on any floor where I'm standing. What it cost, and to whom, before anybody's let go home." He put his hat on. "Dane. That's what I had. The rest's yours."

"Could we have it at ours?" said Perrin. "We haven't got a cart. We've got a handcart. I'm on it most of the time."

"You may have it at your handcart, Mr. Cade."

Orla got up.

She did it slowly, with the stick, and she stood in the lane between the mast and the cross and looked at each of them in turn, the two who had carried and the one who could not and the one who was going to jump.

"I've run this yard on a stick and two eyes," she said. "I'd no rule for this, because nobody's ever done it in front of me. So I'll make some, now, and you'll all hear them before anybody's in the air."

Nobody said anything.

"A step first, every night, off her own ration. A curb's height and a pace. If it isn't clean, there's no go, and nobody argues. And one go in a night. Never two. I don't know what two would cost her, and I've just been told what one did. I'll not learn the rest on her."

She shifted her weight on the stick.

"Before she leaves the line, she tells me whether the bottom thread is cold. If it isn't, she sits down."

She meant the lowest of the three raised lines on Kiva's chest, the one that warmed first whenever two doors wanted the same room. That was the meter. It had nothing to do with the disk, which hung on its cord beside it and was cold whatever anybody did.

"In the air: if that Ember isn't shut clean at the top, she calls *off* and comes down as herself, on the right leg, rolling. That isn't a miss to be ashamed of. That's the only part of this I can teach, and I mean to see it done before I see the other."

"And after?" said Kiva.

"After, you show me that hand before you show me your face. Any white in those two fingers and we're done for the night. White past a minute, and you see Aske before you see this yard again." Orla looked toward the mast. "Bexley says nothing till she's finished. That's Pryce's condition and it's a sound one. And there's to be one voice in this lane besides mine that may stop it."

"Anwen said I'm to call *off* for Seven," said Perrin. He looked rather surprised to hear himself. "While she's gone. Because I mind things."

"Then you call it for this as well. If you see what I don't, you say so, and I'll thank you afterward." Orla lowered herself back onto the crate. "The four of you have two drams in a glass at the ash office. You'd better know what they're buying. It isn't six landings. It's six that I'd ask for twice. The misses will cost you next to nothing, and they're most of what there is to learn."

---

Healer Aske passed the knee for landings on Wednesday morning, with his thumbs and a grunt, and told her that the strapping stayed on it until he said otherwise; and on Wednesday night Kiva stood on the scraped line with a week's ration in her and four-tenths of the team's on top of it.

It was the thirteenth. She had not lit so much as a strut in a fortnight.

The step came first. She pushed off the line, a curb's height and a pace, and shut the Ember at the top, and set the still door under her foot before the ground. It was like stepping onto a mattress the size of the yard. Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder and went through her and into the earth, and she held it there a breath, and gave the ground its weight back, and shut the door.

"Clean," said Orla. "Is the thread cold?"

Kiva laid two fingers of her right hand on her breastbone.

"Cold."

"Then you may have your go. To the cross. One low bound, as it was in the spring, and not an inch higher because there are people looking." The stick pointed. "Call it."

"Rowan. Ember, to still."

It sounded very small. She had last called it across a full Pit, with the terraces roaring. There were four here, and a brass head on three legs, and two rows of black posts going off into the dark.

The mast ticked behind her. She had her back to it, on Orla's terms.

The metal came off the cuff and ran down her sides and closed on her calves. She bent both knees, the good and the newly passed, and left the line.

It was four paces. It was nothing. She had crossed a brick sump on the same push with a Flask at her back.

And she could hear the pen.

She had not thought of that. On the shelf there had been a roar in the terraces and her own blood. Here there was a quiet yard, and behind her left shoulder a steel nib that had been scratching evenly and was now, as the struts took her weight, running up its paper with a small, thin, eager sound. She knew what it was drawing.

She shut the Ember at the top.

She shut it, and listened to hear whether the pen had heard.

It was no more than that. It was a quarter of a breath. But it was the same fault that she had spent a month unlearning with a tin and a stick, when she used to watch the metal run home along her arm to make certain it had arrived. The struts let go late. One of them clung for an instant, and she felt it cling. The metal was still running up her ribs when the top of the bound was already behind her and the cross was coming up out of the dirt.

She had half a breath in which to choose.

The still door was there. It was ready; she could feel it under her as she felt a pan under ash. If she opened it now it would open on a doorway that was not yet empty, and she knew precisely what that was called, and she had done it in this yard and in a cart shed and knew what it did.

*Is the Ember shut clean at the top?*

*No.*

"*Off!*" said Kiva. "Off, off—"

She let go of the pan. She let the whole of it go, and came down as nothing but a girl of seventeen travelling a good deal too fast, and she took the ground on her right foot and did not try to keep it. She folded at the hip as she had been shown and went over her own shoulder, and the Old Yard came round once and hit her in the back.

She lay a pace short of the cross and half a pace to the side of it, looking up at the lamp.

Nothing hurt. That was the first thing. The left knee had not been asked to do anything. There was dirt in her collar and her right elbow was skinned, and the bottom thread on her breastbone was as cold as it had been on the line.

"Hand," said Orla's voice, above her.

Kiva held up her left hand. The old fingers turned it over in the lamplight, and pressed the two tips, and let go.

"Pink. Good." The crooked face came into view against the sky. "Now your face, since you're so anxious I should have it. You're angry."

"I was listening to the pen."

"I know you were. I watched your head go." Orla straightened, with a grunt. "And then you did the only thing I've set you that you've done. I've set you fourteen before tonight, and the foreman's was his. You asked yourself the question at the top, you got the wrong answer, and you came down on it. I've seen that ordered a great many times. I've seen it obeyed about twice."

"It's another miss."

"It's the first thing you've done in this yard that I'd have paid for, and it turns out that somebody has." She looked over her shoulder. "Mr. Bexley. She's finished."

Noa came out from behind the mast with the tape across both palms. He was trying very hard to keep his face from saying anything, and not succeeding.

"The step. Two hundredths; it always is. Then the bound, two again. Then there's a tremble at the top, about as long as a blink, and a hundredth goes on it. And then nothing. It's flat. You shut everything." He looked up. "Five hundredths for the night, and three of them were the go."

"Three off the team's," said Kellan. "One dram and ninety-seven left. I'd call that cheap."

"I'd wanted the cross," said Kiva. She had sat up, and she was looking at it. It was a pace away, scratched in the dirt, and she had never yet come down on it in that yard. "I could feel the door. It was there."

"I'm sure it was," said Perrin. "I had my mouth open. I'd got as far as the *o.*" He lowered himself onto the spare crate with great care. "I've never been so pleased to be beaten to a word in my life. I'm putting it on the list of things I don't mind. It's a short list. It's mostly this."

She laughed, which she had not meant to do, and found that the anger had a crack in it.

She got up by herself. She brushed off what dirt would come, and went and stood on the cross for a moment in her ordinary boots, with nothing open, to see what the yard looked like from there. It looked much the same. Under her shirt the disk lay against her breastbone, cold as a coin, and she noticed that she had not thought of it once between the line and the ground.

"Friday," said Orla. "Not tomorrow. Tomorrow you'll come and sit with your back to that drum for an hour while Bexley runs it empty, until you can't tell it from a clock."

Kiva carried the crate in at a walk. When she came out again Orla was already at the gate with the lamp down off its hook, and had not asked for it.
