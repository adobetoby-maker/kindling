# Chapter 4 — Kiva: The Terrace Ring

By Tuesday night she could do it in nine and a half seconds.

She wrote that in the book. She wrote everything in the book now, in the seven ruled columns, in pencil that smudged when her hand sweated. Monday was ten seconds, then ten again, then twelve because she rushed the second gap and the bottom line lit and she had to stop and wait for it to cool. Tuesday was ten, nine and a half, nine and a half, and then a long, embarrassing fourteen, because Perrin had sneezed in the middle of her stance and she had lost the whole thing laughing.

*What went wrong:* Perrin.

*Who was hurt:* My dignity.

*Why:* Perrin.

Orla had read that page and snorted, and Anwen had read it and said, "That's not what the column's for," and written something in her own list.

Mara had not read any of it yet, and Kiva had not offered. The book sat on the kitchen table every night while they ate supper, closed, with the pencil on its string lying across the cover, and neither of them mentioned it.

On Wednesday morning Orla knocked at the shop before it opened and climbed the stairs as far as the kitchen doorway, where she stood leaning on her stick while Kiva ate.

"Open lane today," she said. "Stonehand Hall. I've put your name down."

Kiva put down her toast.

"You can't go into the Cellhouse having only done this in an empty yard with your friends," said Orla. "Your aunt's not going to sign for a shutdown path that's only ever been tested in the dark. You need to do it with someone trying to hit you."

"Who?"

"The draw decides," said Orla. "Unless somebody asks for you."

---

Somebody asked for her.

Kiva found out when she got to Stonehand Hall at noon and saw the board. The open lane was a Circuit tradition for the last week before placement trials, when anyone who was going into a trial could come and fight anyone else who was going into a trial, under a Circuit referee, in one of Stonehand's sanctioned rings. The bouts were logged, and the numbers went into your file. The gallery was free, and in placement week it was usually full, because recruiters liked to watch people fight when it did not count yet. They said you saw more.

The board was a long slate wall outside the rings, chalked with names in pairs. Most of the names had been drawn by lot. A few had the word *requested* chalked small beside one name, which meant that fighter had asked for the other.

*Ring Three, second bout. K. Renn (requested) against K. Rowan.*

"He asked for you," said Perrin. He was standing beside her with his hands in his pockets and a bruise already coming up on his cheekbone, because he had fought his own bout an hour earlier, against a Saltmere-sponsored Ember-door girl, and won it three to two. "He asked for you on purpose. In front of everybody. That's either very rude or very flattering, and I can't work out which."

"Both," said Anwen, who was reading the board with her list in her hand. "He wants to know what he's comparing. He said so on Classification Day. Now he's measuring."

"I'm going to lose," said Kiva.

"Probably," agreed Anwen. "So decide what you want to learn while you're doing it."

---

The Terrace Ring was the oldest of Stonehand Hall's practice rings, and the one Kiva liked least.

It was round and sunken, like a bowl cut into the floor of a great stone hall. Around the top ran a broad stone terrace with a rail and benches behind it for the gallery, and from the terrace three wide steps went down to the floor of the ring, each step knee-high and about a pace and a half deep, all the way around. The floor at the bottom was coarse, pale sand, raked smooth before every bout, about twelve paces across.

Stonehand built it that way on purpose. Their whole discipline was the body, which meant stance, contact, load, and footing, and they did not believe in flat ground. Flat ground, they said, taught you nothing about falling.

The rules were simple. Both fighters began on the top terrace, on opposite sides. Edge-light stayed dark, because a dark Edge was blunt metal and not a blade, and the referee would stop you if you lit it. A clean contact on the body or head counted a point, and a contact on an arm or leg counted half. Three points won. Any door was allowed. A sensing mast stood on the rim of the ring, a short steel pole with a cage of wire on top, wired to a draw board where the referee could see it. Before the bout, each fighter settled a single standard dram of clean ash into their reserve, and the draw board measured how much of it they spent, and that went into the file too.

Kiva sat on the bench at the top of the terrace and settled her dram.

It came in a small sealed glass vial. She broke the seal and tipped the grey powder into her left palm, where it lay fine and faintly warm, like flour from a mill. She closed her hand around it and let it go in, which was the only way to describe it. The warmth went into her palm and up her wrist and spread into her chest, and the ash in her hand went pale, then dull, then dead. It was like drinking something hot on a cold morning. It settled, and it filled the bottom of something inside her that she could feel but not name.

Across the ring, Kellan Renn did the same. He did not close his eyes. He just watched his hand until the ash in it went dead, and then brushed it off onto the stone.

The referee was a Stonehand master, a short, heavy woman with grey hair in a braid and a voice like a dropped crate. "Renn," she called. "Rowan. To your marks."

Kiva stood up and walked to the edge of the terrace.

From up here, the ring looked very deep, with the three steps going down like the sides of a bowl to the pale and perfect sand at the bottom. On the other side, twenty paces away, Kellan stood at his mark with his hands loose at his sides and his Ember cuff dark on his left forearm.

The gallery was full. Kiva could see Perrin and Anwen at the rail, and Orla on a bench at the back with her stick across her knees. Further along, under a slate-blue banner, two people in Graywater coats, a man and a woman, sat with their arms folded.

She made herself stop looking at them.

*What do you want to learn?*

She knew what she wanted. She wanted to win. She wanted to stand in the middle of that sand and be unmovable and let him break himself on her.

She had a plan for that, and she had been thinking about it since the board.

"Fighters ready," said the referee. "Begin."

---

Kiva went down the steps fast.

She did not try to Stride. She just went down them like stairs, three long drops, and landed on the sand in the middle of the ring, where she planted her feet.

Stance came up through her the way it always did, heavy and certain, the floor agreeing to take her weight. At the same time she reached for Edge, and the metal flowed off her slab and over her fist and closed into the bar.

Two doors. Stance and Edge. Stacked.

The bottom heat-line warmed, and she ignored it and held both. She stood in the middle of the sand, rooted, with the ugly bar raised in front of her, and waited for him to come down to her.

He did not come down.

Kellan walked to the edge of the top terrace and stepped down onto the second step, and only that far. He stood on it, a pace and a half above the sand, and watched her. His blade had formed on his right fist while he walked, long and narrow and dark, and he held it low.

She waited.

He waited.

It went on long enough for someone in the gallery to laugh. Kiva stood in the sand with both doors open and felt the bottom heat-line grow warmer, and then the second one begin. Holding two doors was not like holding one. It cost more, and she could feel it draining out of the dram she had settled, a slow, steady pull, like water out of a cracked jug.

He was making her pay for standing there.

She understood that a moment too late. Then he moved.

He did not come straight down. He came along the second step, quick and light, circling to her left, and she turned to follow him. Turning in stance was like turning a cart, with her feet heavy and her knees heavy. She swung the bar around and he was still above her, on the step, and the step put his blade at the height of her head while hers was pointed at his knees.

He feinted down at her. She raised the bar to block.

And the hand door woke up.

It wanted a ward. She was blocking, and blocking was what the hand door was for, and it lifted inside her, cool and quick, reaching to help. She felt the bottom line go hot, and then the second, and then the third.

She said nothing. She had forgotten to say anything.

The ward came up in front of her face, flat and wobbly, at the same moment the bar came up and the stance held, and all three doors pulled on her from three directions at once. The bar jerked in her fist. The ward splashed, and cold air burst against her face, and for one instant she could not see.

Kellan's blade tapped her on the collarbone, lightly, no more than a touch.

"One," said the referee. "Renn."

He was already back on the step, two paces along, his blade low again.

Kiva stood in the sand and breathed. Her chest burned, with all three lines lit and throbbing. She let the ward go completely. She let the stance go too, badly, without giving anything back, and her knees nearly folded.

*Stupid,* she thought. *Stupid, stupid.*

She had stacked. She had told herself she was going to stack two doors, stance and Edge, and hold them. And the moment there was pressure, the third had come in too, because it always did, because she had never in four years been able to hold two without the third deciding to join. She had known that. She had planned around it. And it had happened anyway.

And he had used the step. He had made her look up at him, and made her turn heavy, and made her pay to hold two doors while he paid for nothing at all.

She raised her eyes to him.

He was not smiling. He was watching her.

---

She decided to go up after him.

It was the wrong decision and she knew it while she was making it. She made it anyway, because she was angry, and because standing in the sand had cost her a point and most of a dram and she did not know what else to do.

She still had the bar on her fist. She went at the first step at a run, and as she reached it, she asked for Stride.

Stride was the Ember's other shape. It was struts of metal down the backs of the calves and along the soles of the feet, for a burst of speed or a hard jump, and Kiva could hold it for three bounds, maybe four. She had never been good at it. But she needed to get up a knee-high step fast, and onto the second step before he moved, and Stride was the only way.

The trouble was that Edge and Stride were the same door.

She knew that, as everyone did. An Ember held one shape at a time, blade or struts and never both, not below unbound mastery, and nobody in the Terrace Ring that afternoon was within a hundred miles of unbound mastery.

She asked for both anyway.

The metal tried, and she felt it try. It started to flow from the bar on her fist back toward her slab and down toward her calves, and at the same time it tried to stay a bar, and for a moment it was neither. The bar went soft and lumpy on her fist, and the struts started down her right leg and stopped at the knee.

Her right foot hit the edge of the first step with no strut under it and no weight behind it.

Her toe caught the stone lip. She went down.

She landed on her left knee on the edge of the step, and it hurt more than anything had hurt in a long time, a white flash straight up her thigh. The half-formed metal ran back into her slab in a rush. She rolled onto her side on the step with her hands empty.

Kellan's blade touched her ribs.

"One," said the referee. "Renn leads, two."

Kiva lay on the step and stared at the stone ceiling of the hall. Somebody in the gallery was laughing, not cruelly, just the way people laugh when somebody falls over.

She heard Perrin's voice say, "Shut up," quite loudly, and the laughing stopped.

"Up," said the referee. "Or yield."

Kiva got up.

Her knee was bad. It was not broken, and she could stand on it, but it was going to swell, and every step was going to tell her about it. She limped back down onto the sand and stood there with her hands at her sides and her chest aching and her breath coming hard, facing Kellan Renn on the second step above her.

He had not moved. He had not needed to.

---

She made herself stop.

Not stop fighting. Stop *doing*. She stood in the sand and let him stand on the step, and she did not open any doors at all, and she watched him.

She had been watching his blade, as everyone did, because it was long and narrow and steady and fast, and it was the thing that hit you. But Mara had taught her that if you wanted to know what a machine was doing, you did not study the part that moved the most. You studied the part that moved the least, and you waited for it to change.

So she watched his feet. And his left forearm, where the Ember cuff was. And his breath.

He came down the step toward her the way he had come before, quick, along the step and then sharply down onto the sand at her left side. And as he came, she saw his blade go.

It did not vanish. It flowed. The long dark metal ran off his right fist and back up his arm to the cuff, smooth and fast, and then, for a sliver of a moment, his right hand was empty. Then the struts came down his calves, and he took two long, light, springing strides across the sand to her left, and the struts ran back up to the cuff, and his hand was empty again for a sliver, and then the blade was back on his fist.

And he breathed out.

He did it each time, at each change, a small, sharp breath out through his nose, after the metal was home and before the next shape began. It was the same breath Orla took, the breath that meant *door closed*.

Kiva stood in the sand and felt her whole idea of him turn over.

She had thought he was fast because he could do everything at once. She had watched him for three years in the junior lanes and thought that. Seeing blade and struts and blade so quickly that it looked like one thing, she had thought that was what being good meant. All of it, together.

It was not together. It was one thing, then a gap, then another thing: blade, empty, struts, empty, blade. He sequenced, and he sequenced so cleanly and so fast that the gaps were almost invisible. But they were there, and she could see them now that she had stopped looking at the wrong part.

He was not fast because he stacked. He was fast because he never did.

He was doing exactly what Orla had been telling her to do for two years. He was just doing it about twenty times better.

He came at her from the left, blade back on his fist, and she turned to meet him, and as she turned she felt the bottom heat-line warm.

"One," she said, under her breath.

And she closed.

She did not reach for a ward, and she did not reach for stance. She had the bar on her fist, with the Ember door open, and she left it at that. One door. When the hand door lifted inside her to help, she did not let it in. She felt it at the doorway and she kept the door shut, the way you keep a door shut against a dog that wants to come in, gently, with your foot.

The bottom line stayed warm. The second line stayed cool.

His blade came at her ribs. She brought the bar across.

And she found out what the bar was for.

It was not a good blade. It had no point and no curve and it could not reach. But it was broad, as flat and wide and heavy as her forearm, and when his narrow blade met it, his blade did not slide off. It stopped dead against the flat of the bar as if it had hit a door.

He cut again, at her head. She brought the bar up, and it stopped him again.

He stepped back. For the first time all bout, he looked surprised.

---

The sand helped her. She did not plan that. She only noticed it.

He Strode away from her across the sand, two long springing bounds toward the far side of the ring, and on the second bound his struts dug in.

The sand was coarse and deep, and though it had been raked smooth, it had not been packed. When Kellan's strut-feet came down on it with all the force of a Stride behind them, they sank, only by a finger's depth, but it made his third bound a fraction late, and when he reached the bottom step and pushed off it, he had to push twice.

He knew it too. She saw him glance down.

After that, he did not Stride on the sand. He came at her on his own feet, with just the blade, and his own feet, on the sand, were not as quick as his struts.

They fought for a long time after that, longer than Kiva would have believed she could last.

It was not pretty. He was still much better than she was. He had a dozen ways to come at her and she had one way to stop him, which was to get the bar in the way. He came high and she got it there. He came low and she got it there, mostly. He came at her left side, where the bar was slow to reach, and she twisted and got the edge of it there, and the impact shook her to the elbow.

Every time the hand door woke, she kept it out. Every time she felt the bottom line warm, she said *one* under her breath and stayed where she was. One door. Only one.

It cost her, though not in heat. It cost her in everything else. Without stance, she could not root, and without a ward, she could not stop anything she could not reach with the bar. She was slow and heavy and one-handed and she could not hit him back, because hitting back meant opening the bar up, and every time she started to, he was already somewhere else.

But he could not hit her either.

She did not know how long it went on. It might have been a minute, or two. Her knee screamed at every step, her arms ached to the shoulder, and her breath sawed in and out, and the gallery had gone very quiet.

Then he changed.

She saw it a moment before he did it. He stopped coming at her bar and came around her left side instead, wide, on the sand, where she had to turn heavily to face him. Once. Twice. She turned, and turned, and the bad knee buckled a little on the second turn.

He had seen the knee. Of course he had. He was learning her just as hard as she was learning him.

The third time, he came wide again, and she turned, and her heel hit stone.

She had backed all the way to the edge of the sand, and the bottom step was right behind her, with its edge against her heel.

For an instant she wanted stance so badly it hurt. She wanted to drop into the still door and plant her feet and be unmovable, and the still door was right there, open under her, waiting. The bottom line went hot.

She did not open it.

Instead she put her heel back against the riser of the step, hard, and set her back foot flat against the stone, so that the step itself was behind her. It was not stance. It was not anything. It was just a wall to push against.

Kellan came in low, at her left hip, the side the bar could not reach.

She did not try to block. She shoved off the step with her back foot, with all her weight, and swung the bar not at his blade but at his arm behind it. Flat. Hard.

It hit his right forearm with a dull, heavy smack.

"Half," said the referee. "Rowan."

The gallery made a noise that was not a cheer. It was more like a whole room of people drawing in a breath at once.

Kellan stepped back. He glanced down at his forearm, and then at her.

Then the struts came down his calves, and he was not on the sand anymore. He took one long Stride straight up onto the bottom step to her left, where the stone was solid under him, and a second Stride along it, and came down off it behind her before she could turn.

His blade touched her back, between the shoulders.

"One," said the referee. "Renn wins, three to a half."

---

Kiva sat on the bottom step with her leg stretched out in front of her and breathed.

Her knee was already swelling, and her arms felt as though someone had beaten them with a mop handle. Under her shirt, the bottom heat-line ached, but only the bottom one. The other two had not lit since she fell on the step.

Kellan came over and stood on the sand in front of her. His blade had gone back into his cuff. He was breathing hard too, which pleased her more than she would have admitted.

"You stopped stacking," he said.

"You noticed."

"Halfway through." He rubbed his right forearm, where there was a red mark already. "You were slower after that. And it was harder to hit you." He frowned, as if the two things did not fit together in his head, and he did not like it. "You should be easier to hit when you're slower."

"I know," said Kiva. "I thought so too."

He considered her for a moment longer. Then he turned to the draw board on the rim of the ring, where the referee was writing up the numbers.

*Renn: dram spent, 0.31.*

*Rowan: dram spent, 0.88.*

It was nearly three times as much. And most of it, Kiva knew, had gone in the first minute, standing in the sand with two doors open, paying to stand still.

"Your draw's terrible," said Kellan.

"I know."

"Point eight eight against point three one, in one bout. The cell in the Cellhouse is Grade Second. At that rate, if you stack on it, you'll burn through your dram in a minute and the cell's charge in three." He read it out the way he might have read a meter. "That's a rate, not an insult. I've been put in teams with people who didn't know their own numbers, and it's worse than being put in teams with people who are bad."

"I know my numbers."

"Good." He nodded once. Then he hesitated, which she had never seen him do. "The half point," he said. "Off the step. Was that a plan?"

"No," said Kiva honestly. "I wanted to stance. I didn't let myself. So I used the step instead."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "Huh," very softly, and walked away across the sand.

---

Kellan Renn walked out of Stonehand Hall with his forearm throbbing and could not stop thinking about the step.

He went down the long front stairs into the afternoon light and did not stop at the Graywater table, though the woman in the slate-blue coat raised a hand to him, and he did not stop at the Halden table either. He walked to the end of the plaza and sat on the low wall by the fountain and examined the red mark on his arm.

It was a good mark, clean and square, the exact width of that ridiculous bar.

He had not been touched in a sanctioned bout since the autumn. He did not say that to anyone, because it sounded like boasting and it was not. It was a number, and numbers mattered to Kellan more than he could easily explain to people who had not grown up where he had.

His mother graded ash at the Halden refinery on the east bank, and she had graded ash there for twenty-two years. She sat at a long bench with a meter and a set of sample cups and decided, can by can, whether a batch was First or Second or Third. It was one of the most important jobs in the city and almost nobody knew it existed, even though, if she got it wrong, a cell somewhere in Meridian ran hot.

When Kellan was nine, a supervisor at the refinery had stamped a cut batch First to make his quarter, and when the batch failed in a hospital in the Lantern district, he had tried to say Kellan's mother had graded it. She had not. She had flagged it. She had written the true number in the grading book, in ink, with the date and her initials, and the supervisor had changed the stamp after she went home.

The number in the book had saved her, and nothing else would have. Her word against his would not have done it, and neither would her twenty years, or the fact that everyone on the floor knew who was honest. Only the number had done it, written down by the person who had measured it.

Kellan had believed in measurement ever since. It was not perfect, and he did not believe in it because it was perfect. He believed in it because it was the only thing that protected people who were not in charge.

The Circuit was built on measurement, on rank, hold, draw and call, on boards and numbers and logged bouts and files. It was not perfect either, and he knew that. But it meant that a refinery grader's son from the east bank could stand in the Measure Hall and have a board read him Fire, and nobody could say it said anything else. It meant that when he went to Graywater or Saltmere or any of them, his number would be his, and not a favor or a family name.

And Kiva Rowan did not fit on the board.

That was what bothered him. It was not that she was strange, because strange was fine. It was that the board could not read her, and if the board could not read her, then sooner or later somebody would have to decide what she was, somebody in a little room, and then her place on the roster would be a ruling and not a number. And every ruling like that made the numbers mean a little less.

He believed that. He had believed it walking into the Terrace Ring.

But he had not expected to learn anything from her.

He had expected her to stack, because everyone said she stacked. He had watched her in the junior lanes, all doors and no control, burning ash like a broken cell, and today he had stood on the step and waited for her to burn out, and she had nearly done it. And then, halfway through, she had picked one door and kept it, and every time he hit at her, that stupid broad bar was in the way.

And then the step.

In three years of fighting in the Terrace Ring, he had used the steps to stand on, to get above people, to Stride off. He had never once thought of backing against a riser to get leverage without spending anything at all. It was not a door. It would not show on any board. And it had cost him half a point.

Kellan sat on the fountain wall and rubbed his arm.

He did not like it. He did not like the idea that the thing that had touched him today was a thing no rig could measure. But he was honest with himself, the way his mother had taught him to be, and the honest account was that he had learned something in that ring, and he had learned it from somebody the board could not read.

He took out the pencil he kept in his jacket and a scrap of paper, and wrote on it: *Rowan. Bar blocks narrow blades flat. Uses risers for leverage. Slower on a single door but harder to hit. Dram 0.88 to my 0.31.*

He read it over once. Then he folded it and put it in his pocket, and went to find out which team the board had put him on for the Cellhouse.

---

Mara read the book that night.

Kiva did not offer it. She came home limping, with her knee wrapped in a cold cloth from the Stonehand infirmary, and sat down at the kitchen table, and put the book down in front of her plate the way she always did. And Mara, instead of not mentioning it, picked it up.

She read every page. She read them slowly, the way she read cell books at the bench, with one finger moving down the columns, while Kiva watched her eat nothing and turn pages.

At the last page, Mara stopped.

*Exercise:* Open lane bout, Terrace Ring. K. Renn.

*What went wrong:* Stacked stance and Edge at the start. Third door came in. All 3 lines. Splashed ward. One point to him. Then tried Edge and Stride at once, same door, can't. Fell on step. Knee. One more point. Then single door (Edge only). Kept hand door out. Held for about 2 min. Took a half off his arm from the step. Then he Strode behind me. Lost, 3 to a half.

*Who was hurt:* Me. Left knee. Kellan, right forearm (bruise).

*What it cost:* 0.88 of a dram. Mostly first minute.

*How long to come back:* Knee, a week? Lines, an hour.

*Second go:* Single door, said "one," closed. Harder to hit. Can't hit back. Too slow off the sand.

*Why:* He doesn't stack. Ever. He sequences, fast. I thought fast meant all at once. It means one at a time, clean, with no hurry in it.

Mara read the last line twice.

Then she put the book down and faced Kiva across the table.

"Show me," she said. "Not a door. Just show me what you do. When the line goes."

Kiva stood up. She stood in the middle of the kitchen, away from the wall cell and the stove and the cold-cupboard, and raised her right fist and formed the bar, which came ugly and dark. She held it. Then she let the hand door lift a little on purpose, just enough, and the bottom line went warm.

"One," she said aloud. And she held the hand door at the threshold and did not let it in, and let the bar run home into the slab, and breathed out, and felt both doors go quiet.

"That's my meter," she said. "The bottom line. I say it out loud. That's my shutdown path. One door home before the next one starts. And that's my book." She nodded at it. "Every time. Even when it's stupid."

Mara sat watching her for a long time.

"Now tell me what you can't do," she said.

Kiva had not expected that. She stood in the kitchen with her empty hands and her bad knee and thought about it honestly, because Mara would know if she did not.

"I can't stack," she said. "Not two doors, not three. Not on purpose and not safely. If I try, they fight, and I lose control of all of them." She took a breath. "I can't go fast. A full sequence takes nine and a half seconds. In a fight, I can only use one door and keep the others out. I can't tune a cell. I can read one. I can't tune one, not while the hand door and the Ember both want it. If something goes wrong near a cell, I can't fix it with a door. I'd have to use the quench, the same as anyone." She stopped. "And I can't promise nothing will go wrong. I can only promise I'll say it out loud when it starts."

Mara held her eyes for a moment longer.

Then she got up, went to the hook by the door where her apron hung, and took the countersignature form out of the pocket. She smoothed it flat on the kitchen table beside the field book and took the pencil off its string.

Under *Guardian*, in her square capitals, she wrote *M. ROWAN*. Then she signed it.

She did not hand it to Kiva at once. She sat holding it.

"Those are good limits," she said quietly. "Know them in the Cellhouse. Don't let anyone talk you out of them. Not Orla. Not your team. Not that boy with the steady eyes. Not the gallery. And not yourself, when you want to show them all."

"I won't."

"You will," said Mara. "You'll want to. Everybody does." She held out the form. "When you do, say it out loud."
