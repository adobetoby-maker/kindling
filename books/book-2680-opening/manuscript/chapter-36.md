# Chapter 36 — Kiva: Catch, Carry, Plant

Perrin hung Gerald from the shed hoist on the first Tuesday of the new month, and was very particular about the knot.

"He's a hundredweight," he said. "I've had him on the Hall's scale. From there," and he chalked a mark on the shed door, at the height of his own knee, "he comes in about as hard as Keld's shoulder. From there he's Moss. I'd not go higher than Moss on the first night. He's had a hard life, but he's got no judgment."

The sandbag turned slowly on its rope. Somebody at the Hall had inked a face on it since the spring.

The rope ran over the hoist beam and back to a cleat by the door. Let go, the bag swung down across the head of the lane and came through the scraped line from the left and a little behind, at the height of a shoulder. Kiva had stood on that line for twenty minutes while Perrin adjusted it, and had been struck twice, lightly, as herself.

"The step first," said Orla.

The step was clean. The thread was cold. She had a week's ration in her and nobody's ash but her own, and she said so before anybody could ask.

"Hand, to Ember. One crossing. It isn't on my paper. It isn't one of the six."

"It is not," said Orla. "Say what you're going to do with your body."

"Feet on the line, square to the cross. Left shoulder to the bag. Ward up at the shoulder, curved, for one tap. I shut it. Then the struts, and one low bound to the mark, and I come down on the Ember, as I'd come down from any bound."

"And if the ward isn't shut?"

"I stand still and take him as myself."

"Mr. Cade."

"On *hand,*" said Perrin. He had the rope in both hands. "And I'll point out that I've got the word *and* the bag. If I call *off,* I can't call him back. He's committed."

There were two people by the yard gate who had not been there the week before. Anwen stood with her back to the post, as she had on the twenty-fourth. Beside her, on an upturned pail, sat Pru Finch, who had been told that there would be a sandbag and had come to see it.

"Rowan, changing at the shoulder," said Kiva. "Hand, to Ember."

Her hands came up. The ward stood between them, tray-sized, faintly silver, and she bent it, as she had bent it a hundred times in that yard, into something like the bowl of a spoon.

"*Hand.*"

Perrin let go of the rope.

She heard Gerald come. She did not look. The ward was at her left shoulder, and the bag walked into it with a hundredweight of sand behind an inked grin, and the ward rang like a struck pan.

It held. That was the first surprise. It did not stop the bag. It turned it, as the curve of a spoon turns water, and the whole of that shove came round her shoulder and into her back and pointed her down the lane.

*Now shut it.*

She shut it. She brought her hands together and let the silver go, and reached for the cuff.

"*Ember.*"

The metal ran down her sides. The struts closed on her calves—

And the bottom thread on her breastbone went hot, like a wire laid across a stove.

"*Off!*" It came out of her before she knew she had said it. "Off, off, off—"

She let go of everything. The struts sprang back. Gerald's shove was still in her, and with nothing to take it she simply went, three stumbling steps down the lane with her arms out, and sat down hard in the dirt a pace short of the cross.

The bag swung back, and forward, and Perrin caught it against his chest.

"Hand," said Orla.

It was pink. Kiva held it up, and it was pink, and it was the other thing that was wrong. There was a line of heat from her breastbone to her collarbone, sharp and familiar. She had not felt it since the spring.

"That was a jam," she said. Her voice shook. "I shut the ward. I *shut* it."

"Mr. Bexley."

"Two for the ward," said Noa. He was frowning at the tape. "Then there's a hump. Eight hundredths. It's the mouse up the curtain again, like the Low Lane." He looked up. "There wasn't a gap. The first one never went down to nothing. There's a little shelf on it, about so high, all the way to where the second starts."

"A shelf."

"As though there was a bit of it left on."

Kiva looked at her hands.

She had brought them together. She had felt the ward go, in her palms and her wrists and the last two fingers of each hand. She had not felt it go in the first two fingers of the left. She had not felt anything there at all. They had told her that the ward was shut exactly as they told her that a cord was crossed, which was to say that they had reported nothing, late.

"There was a rim," she said slowly. "At the edge. Where these two were. I couldn't feel that it was still there."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then that's a thing I couldn't have taught you," said Orla, "and I'm glad you found it with a sandbag. That's cost you a tenth, and a line you'll feel till Friday." She turned her head toward the gate. "Miss Finch. You've been sitting on that pail like somebody with her hand up. Out with it."

Pru went scarlet.

"It's only that she doesn't put it out," she said. "I didn't like to say. She lets go of it. Everybody does; they hold a ward, and then they stop holding it, and it sort of falls off them. Mine used to. It's why they splash."

"And what do you do?"

"I finish it." Pru got off the pail. She came down the lane a little way, and held up her round hands, and a ward the size of a tea tray stood between them, humming. "I don't let go at all. I fold it. From the far edge, in toward me, like a cloth. That side first, then that, and when it's as small as a penny I shut my hands on the penny. So I always know where the last of it is. It's in my palms." The ward went out between one word and the next. "It's the only thing I'm any good at. Sorn said so. She said it was why my name was on her card."

Kiva was still sitting in the dirt. She looked up at a first-year who had run from a spaniel.

"Will you show me?" she said. "Slowly. I'd need to do it without those two."

"Without—" Pru looked at the hand. "Oh. Then you'd fold toward the others. Toward your little finger. And you'd keep those two right out of it, straight, so there's nothing for a rim to sit on."

"Passengers," said Kiva.

It was a catcher's word. Pike had said it to her in the Graywater hall yard, with a groove worn across his palm. *I took the cord the way I could hold it on my worst day.* She had learned that for a cord, and it had not once occurred to her that a ward had a grip as well.

They stayed until ten. Pru folded and Kiva folded after her, over and over, with two fingers held out stiff, until both their arms ached. It felt like wearing somebody else's hands. By the end of it a ward would go out of her as a cloth goes into a drawer, and the last of it was in her palms, where she could feel it.

Anwen had not said a word all evening. At the gate, going out, she stopped.

"The hand was pink," she said.

"It was a jam. There wasn't a gap."

"I know. I'm saying that I looked." She went on up the lane with Pru, who was already telling her about the spaniel.

---

The next Tuesday the bag came in at Keld's height, and Kiva folded the ward from the far edge to her palms with two fingers standing clear of it.

"*Ember.*"

She knew that it was shut. She had it in her hands like a coin.

For the length of a short word she was nobody. That was not new. What was new was that she was nobody going sideways, with a sandbag's shove in the bones of her back and nothing whatever to tell it what to do. The ash went out of her all at once, a quarter of a dram, like a breath.

Then the struts. They closed on her calves, and they did not push. They had nothing to push with; she had not bent a knee. They only caught what was already there, as a gutter catches rain, and turned a stumble into a bound.

It was low and long and a little crooked. She shut the Ember at the top out of habit, and opened it again on the way down, and landed on the cross on her struts, as she would have landed from any Stride. The knee held.

"Hand," said Orla, before she had straightened.

It was white. Both fingers, to the knuckle, and the nails blue.

"Mr. Bexley, while I've got this."

"Two. Then flat. Flat to the line." Noa's voice had gone up. "Then a cliff. Twenty-six. Then two for the bound. Thirty." He swallowed. "That's a crossing. Hand to Ember. Nothing dropped."

"So a ward can be passed," said Kiva. She found that she was shaking. "It went into the bound. I never pushed off."

"I saw your knees. They were straight as rails." Orla did not look up from the hand. "Be quiet, now. I don't like what this is doing, and I'd like to watch it without help."

They waited. The lamp-man came down the lane with his pole and lit the tram lamp at the foot of it, and went away again, and the shed's shadow went long across the dirt. Anwen had come away from the gatepost a long while before the first finger flushed. She stood in the lane with her arms folded hard across her chest until it was over.

Healer Aske saw it on Wednesday morning, and sat for a while with the hand on his knee.

"A ward is made in the hands," he said. "So whatever it is that goes, goes through there twice as hard, and I ought to have guessed as much. Tell me what the whole of this thing is. All three."

She told him. He heard it out with his eyes on the window.

"Two of those in one go," he said. "Then that's a fortnight's worth, and I'll have a fortnight after it with nothing. Every time. A single one, a week, as before. And no white on the day, not a trace, or you go home." He looked at her. "I'm telling you plainly, so you can't say you misheard me. I can't make you mind it. I've noticed that you do, which is why I go on troubling."

She took that to the drying yard that evening, since there was a thing that she was bound to tell them.

"Aske's ruled," she said. "A fortnight after any go with two gaps. So it's fewer than I said at the table. Between now and the Regional there's room for three, and that's if nothing's white."

Perrin was sitting on the mounting block with a pencil behind his ear. He had been very quiet for a fortnight.

"I've got a thing," he said. "I've been at it since the first."

He turned the pencil over in his fingers.

"My dad says Mrs. Cobb's had her dram. The Pellow's boxes have been lit on it since Saturday, and she's been up and down the stair to look at them like a hen." He scratched his jaw with the pencil. "So the street's had its dram. And you've gone at that bag twice out of your own thimble and sat down in the dirt, and you never once asked me to change my mind. I kept waiting for you to. I'd got my answer ready and everything."

"Had you?"

"It was very good. It had my dad in it." He put the pencil back behind his ear. "I can't use it now. So here's the other. A dram and eight-tenths out of the held, for three whole goes, and the misses out of the same. And I keep the word. If I call *off* on it two nights running, it's done till the season's read, and nobody argues with me, not Orla either." He looked round. "I'm not saying I've stopped minding. I'm saying I'd sooner be the one with his hand on the brake than the one who wouldn't let the cart out of the yard."

"That's a better line than mine," said Kiva.

"I know. I'm keeping it."

"I'll put a condition," said Anwen.

She had been leaning on the pump. She came off it.

"Before each one, I ask you. Out there, on the line, before you've moved. What it's for, that night. And what it's likely to cost you. And you answer me, and if it's changed since you last said it, you say how." Her face was level. "If I'm paying for a hand, I'll have it priced first."

"Every time?"

"Every time. You'll get sick of me."

"I shan't," said Kiva.

Kellan said yes on Thursday, in the passage outside the grading room, without asking to hear the rest.

---

The first whole one was on the fifteenth, and she called it off herself.

Anwen asked her on the line, and she answered, and Anwen went to the gate.

She had the ward folded. She had the first gap behind her, and the struts had caught, and she was in the air with a thing done that nobody she had ever heard of had done. She was so astonished to be there that she listened for it. It was a quarter of a breath. A strut clung.

"*Off!*"

She came down on the right leg, rolling, and the Old Yard hit her in the back. Thirty-one hundredths, and the fingers white till Orla had finished her tea. It was the oldest fault she had, and it was almost a comfort to meet it.

The second was on the twenty-second, and it was worse, because it landed.

It landed beautifully. The ward folded, and the gap came, and the bound; and at the top the cheap road was waiting, as it would always wait, and she was tired, and the hard part was done. The metal was nearly home. The pan wanted setting. She felt one thing begin as the other ended, as smoothly as a seam.

She came down on the cross like a cat on a cushion. Orla's ferrule went through her and into the ground.

"Miss," said Kiva.

She said it standing there, with the stance still open and the stick against her shoulder.

"That was the cheap one. At the second. I stacked it." She gave the ground its weight and shut the door. "The thread warmed at the top. I felt it and I went on. It's a miss."

Nobody moved. On the spare crate Perrin had his mouth open, and no word in it.

"Mr. Bexley," said Orla, very quietly.

"Two. A cliff. Two." Noa turned the tape to the lamp. "And then a hump. Six, with a round top. Then three. That's dearer than a crossing and cheaper than it ought to be." He looked at Kiva with something like wonder. "You said it before I'd found the place."

"I looked at my feet," said Kiva.

"So you did." Orla took her stick back. Her crooked face was doing something complicated. "On the eighteenth of the sixth month you made that same fault and went home thinking it the best of your life, and a boy had to tell you. Tonight you told the boy. I've been wondering since the spring what I was teaching you. I believe that may be it." She held out her palm. "Hand."

It was twenty minutes. The fingers were dull until the next morning, and Perrin walked her to the tram without making a single joke, which frightened her more than the hand did.

---

There was ash on Perrin's line for one more whole go, and one Tuesday left before Aske's fortnight ran into the Regional.

Anwen asked on the twenty-ninth, standing on the scraped line beside her.

"What's it for?"

"To find out whether it can be done at all, once, before a floor."

"What's it cost?"

"Both fingers, some hours. Possibly the hand. The ash I said at the table." Kiva did not look away. "It's the third try. That's what's changed. I'm more tired than the first time, and I know where the cheap one sits."

"Is that what's so?"

"Yes."

"Then I've nothing to add. I'll be at the gate."

The step was clean. The thread was cold, and there was no white.

"From Keld," said Perrin, at the rope. He was pale. "On *hand.*"

"Hand, to Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on the throw, and on the landing."

Her hands came up. Two fingers stood clear.

"*Hand.*"

The rope ran. She did not look.

Gerald walked into the ward, and it rang, and the curve of it took his whole weight round her shoulder and aimed her at the post.

"*Ember.*"

She folded it. Far edge. Near edge. A penny, in her palms. Shut.

Nobody. Sideways, in the dark behind her own breastbone, with a hundredweight of sand in her spine. The ash went, like a breath.

The struts caught it. She was up, low and long, and the metal had hardly closed before she was shutting it again, at the top, and this time she did not listen. She waited. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs and arrive. It was the longest thing that she knew how to do, and she had now to do it with one gap already paid and her left hand gone cold to the wrist.

Nobody, again.

It was different the second time. She had not known that it would be. The first gap had been a girl with a shove in her. The second was a girl with the same shove and nothing else left, like a purse turned out twice. The ash went out of somewhere that had already been emptied. For an instant she thought that there would not be enough of her to set anything.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the pan. Wide, ahead, by its corners.

"*Still.*"

Her right foot came down on the cross.

There was no jar. The yard took her. And then, a moment after, something else arrived that was not her. It came in at her heel, slow and heavy and a little stupid. It went down through the packed dirt and spread there, under the crooked post, four paces from where it had struck.

It was Gerald. It was the whole of a sandbag's swing, set down in the ground like a full pan on a table.

"Orla," she said. Her voice was a thread. "It's in."

"Hold." Orla had not touched her with the stick. She had her palm flat on the dirt beside the cross, like a woman feeling for a tram. "I've got him. I can feel him under my hand. Go on. Go on. That's enough."

"Give it back," said Orla.

She gave it back, and shut the door on an empty doorway, and stood.

Behind her the bag was swinging to and fro against Perrin's chest, light as a lantern, with nothing left in it.

"Mr. Bexley," said Orla.

"Two." He could hardly get it out. "A cliff. Twenty-six. Two. A cliff. Twenty-six. Three." He put the tape down on the drum case with both hands. "That's what she said it would cost, to the hundredth. There are two walls in it. You could cut yourself on either one."

Orla got up off her knees with the stick, and it took her some time.

"I'll say what I saw, and no more. A ward took a blow. A bound carried it four paces. A stance put it in the ground, and I had it under my palm. Nothing was open between." She breathed. "I've taught for one-and-twenty years, and I was the best part of twenty on the Line before that. I never saw it, and I never met anybody who had."

Kiva had not held out her hand. She could not find it.

It was there; she could see it, at the end of her arm. It was the color of a candle from the wrist down. Orla took it in both of her own and said nothing at all.

"There's a thing more," said Noa. His voice was odd. "It's on her pen. After the second cliff, when she's shut everything and it's flat. A little way on, there's a notch. A hair." He looked from one of them to another. "I've one pen. If she so much as twitched, that's all it is. I can't tell her from the mast with one pen."

"I didn't twitch," said Kiva. "I don't think. I couldn't swear."

"The tram lamp winked," said Perrin. "The one at the foot of the lane. I thought it did. I was looking at her, not at it."

"Then it's a hair, and it stays a hair till we've more than one pen to look at it with," said Orla. "Rowan. Is that thing at your neck warm?"

Kiva put her right hand to her collar.

"Cold."

"I thought it would be. Sit down before you fall."

---

Orla Dane sat on in the yard after they had gone, as she had sat on after most things that mattered.

The mast was packed. The bag hung from the hoist with its inked face to the shed. Cade had walked the girl home with her arm across his shoulders; he was passed for it, to the shoulder, and had mentioned the fact several times. Her hand had not come back. It had not so much as begun.

She had said, in the spring, *Show me six,* and had been rather pleased with the sound of it. It was a good rule. She had not foreseen that it would meet a healer. A go a fortnight, and each one emptying a hand for longer than the last. Six of those was a year. It was very likely more hand than the girl had.

So there would be no six. Not of this. There would be one, on a tape with two cliffs in it, and a Regional in seventeen days.

She turned that over, and looked at what was underneath.

It was a Regional. It had not escaped her. She had last brought a candidate with more than one door to that floor twenty years ago, certain of him, with a plan to the last step and a reason for every one. She had explained each drill to him beforehand. He had never once surprised her, and she had taken that for trust.

*What's different?*

She made herself answer it as she would have made Rowan answer, with no shine on it.

The girl was not at the Bench. That was Dray's doing, and Crest's, and Orla had been glad of it in that office and ashamed of how glad. There was a pen on her, held by a boy who had twice told a Torch that she was wrong. There was a word that a Stonehand lad with cracked ribs could say. There was a ward-holder who asked the price on the line before every go, and had not finished being angry, and paid anyway.

And the girl had stood on a cross a week ago, with a stick against her shoulder, and called her own miss.

Jessamy had never once done that. Orla had not known that it wanted teaching.

Safety was not on offer. She had stopped expecting that there would be a morning when it was. What there was, was a meter and a shutdown path and a hand, as Mara Rowan had told her months ago, in a work apron, standing beside the spare crate, in two sentences.

*What do I say, if I'm asked?*

She would be asked. She had sat on enough floors to know it. There was a foreman at every board, and a string at one end of the last of them, and in a bad spring nothing kept its time. Somebody would turn to the rail and say, *Sponsor. What's she passed for?*

"Recovery," said Orla, to the post. "Dark hands. One crossing, Ember to still, to a mark, on a week's rest."

And the other?

*I have seen it once. I have seen it missed three times, and the last miss she called before her reader did. It costs better than half a dram and a hand. It is not on her paper and I'll not put it there. Cade can stop it. So can I. So can she.*

That was all of it. It was not much to say across a floor. It had the merit that every word had happened.

She would not spend the week of the sixth on another, nor the thirteenth. Aske forbade the one. The other she would forbid herself. A hand bought a yard or it bought a floor, and she was done pretending that she might have both.

She took up her stick. The post leaned over her in the dark, split and black, with its two iron bands and the bar through its top, as it had leaned over every teacher who had ever sat there and wondered what it was for.

"You're no help," said Orla, and went in.

---

The hand came back on Thursday, at about noon, the best part of two days after it had gone.

It came back to less. Kiva knew that before Aske told her. The first finger found the pin, slowly. The second did not find the pin at all. It found that something had pushed.

"That's not the same nerve reporting late," said Aske. "That's a different one reporting something else. I'd not say it's finished coming back. I'd not say it hasn't." He let go. "Nothing till the thirteenth. You know that already."

She went home by the river, the long way, since she was not wanted anywhere.

At the Coper Street corner she stopped and put her right hand to her collar. It was a thing that she had begun to do without thinking, the way one pats a pocket for a key. The disk lay where it always lay. It was cold. It had been cold through three doors in a row, and she found that she minded about as much as one minds a clock for not striking between the hours.

---

Mara sat her practical on the seventh of the ninth month, in the examiners' room at the Municipal Works, with her left entered as her reading hand.

She came home at four with her bag over her shoulder. She did not say anything. She went down the hall to the dead panel, and took off the red tag that had hung there since the spring without looking at it, and dropped it in the stove. Then she fetched a can and her tools, and Kiva stood at the kitchen door, well back, and watched her aunt fill a cell and tune it, slowly, with the wrong hand, which was now the right one.

The panel lit. It was a small, steady, yellow light. It had been dark for the best part of four months.

"He said the tune was a hair slow," said Mara. "He said he'd rather have it slow from somebody who knew which hand she was using. He's a dreadful old man." She wiped her fingers on her apron. "You're still not to touch it."

"I know."

"I know you know. I only like saying a thing that I'm licensed to say."

They had the three Kennel floors in those weeks. Jude cut on Seven's cloth for the first time, and came down heavy, and took a full second at the top. Kiva set his mark a pace short of where she would have set it for Kellan, and they sealed a little quicker every floor. Perrin was let carry Gerald out of a tank on his shoulder. He told Gerald beforehand.

Kellan came to the shop on the Wednesday before the Regional. He would not come up.

"I'll say it on the step," he said. He was in his own coat. "The draw's posted. If you get past Brask, and I get past a Saltmere four, it's us in the afternoon. I'm going to use what I know."

"I'd supposed you would."

"You'd better hear it from me, all the same. I know you call a change before you make it, because Rule Six says a change is declared when it's called. I know there's a moment at the top when there's nothing of you open and you can't so much as lean." He was looking at the doorpost. "I told you to shut it there. I'm the one who knows it's there."

"It's the only place to hit me."

"Yes." He let out a breath. "I won't go near Cade's ribs. That isn't a point; that's a hurt, and I've told Birch so, and he said it was my lane. The rest I'm going to take. I'd not think much of either of us if I didn't."

"Kellan."

He looked at her.

"Go for the gap," said Kiva. "I'd sooner lose to you than be let off by you. I've been let off. It's worse."

Something eased in his face. It was not a smile.

"I'm told Talley's coming for my feet," he said.

"He says they're in love with themselves."

"They were. It was the best advice I ever had." He went down the step. "Friday, then."

On Thursday night Jude chalked it on the Low Lane's brick, for the five of them, as two boxes and a great many arrows.

"Sixteen fours. Twelve by yield and four houses. Four boards." He tapped the first box. "Friday, the Great Floor. The Lane from the morning bell, knock-out, three carries, Circuit rules: three for the litter over your own line, one for a down, and the most over the three carries goes through. And Rule Six for anybody who changes, which is to say for her. The Bench at five, when the lanes are down to their last two. Pryce leads that; Rowan sits in the tiers and doesn't so much as look at a cell." The second box. "Saturday, the Pit. The Carry in the forenoon. Every board's marked by place, top marks for the first four down to one for the last, and the three are added. The best four on the three boards make the first company for the Call."

"What's the Call?" said Pru.

"It's new. It's what they've made of the Weighbridge." Jude rubbed his jaw with a chalky thumb. "A string out of the Kiln, and husks let out behind it, and a row of lit boxes along the east wall for a street, and dummies to be fetched. A rung and a husk and a casualty to each four. A Home Line foreman judges it, the way the Line judges a call at the cart."

"Then it's judged properly," said Kiva.

"And every dram's combed at the door. And Sorn's put her name to our entry." He dusted his hands. "Under her hand. Rowan for recovery. Finch for reserve, and a lamp."

"Still not a wall," said Pru. "I've gone down in the world."

Kiva had the watch's word that a thing on a cord was not equipment. She had Aske's: *one, in the week, if the lane wants it. Then nothing.* She had one crossing that Orla would answer for and three doors that she would not. She had ten fingers, of which eight reported, and a reader with a flag.

It did not feel like being ready. It felt like a sheet laid down with every corner called.
