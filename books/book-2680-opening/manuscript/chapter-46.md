# Chapter 46 — Kiva: Call the Turn

"Then say it," said Hollis Vane.

He had not looked surprised. He had his chalk in his hand already.

"I'd like it in the order that Sorn asked for." Kiva kept her back against the post. It was something to stand against. "What it is. What it costs. What I do if it's refused. And who has every piece of it that isn't mine."

Rhea Sorn's head came round.

"Who told you that I'd asked for an order?"

"Orla. On the tram. She said that it wasn't a yes."

"It wasn't." The pale eyes rested on her. "Go on. I'm listening for the last part hardest."

"That arm's a hem under load. It's got a slack; it hauls, and then it eases. I stand at the hole. I put a ward under it for one tap, on the slack, and it comes off the water. It throws me. I fold, and I go with the throw four paces, and I put it down in dry ground and stand on it. Hand, Ember, still." She heard her own voice, thin in the cold yard. "Then it's hauling on me and not on the drain. The slot's clear. The gate can come down behind it. And there's nothing driving the main, so my aunt can stop her pumps and have the water lie."

"For how long?" said Vane.

"I held a corner in the Pit, off three posts. This is an arm of ten, and longer. I'll not hold it. That's the first piece that isn't mine." She drew a breath. "I plant it. Once it's planted, it goes to somebody who can stand under it, and I shut. That was to be Brask."

"It still is," said Lowen Brask, from the gap.

"Not if the bar wants you. Four fitters have had that gate since Tuesday, and it's stuck short of home. If the last of it is still-doors' work, that's you and Cade. *Two to the bar.* Then the corner's Wace's, with Moss at his other end. A plank. I asked them on Monday." She made herself say it slowly, as she would have called a mark. "I'm saying it now, before, so that if it turns, it's a turn that's been called."

"Who calls its time?" said Rhea.

"Not me. I'll be under it. I need somebody who can see the hole and call its time, *out* when it pulls and *in* when it gives, and not hurry. Brask, if he's free. If he's at the bar—"

"Then it's mine," said a voice from the lane.

They had come in by the side gate. Kiva had not heard them for the bell. There was a thin boy in Wren House green, and a porter, and between them a wooden chair with a rail, such as the infirmary used on its stairs. A red head was in it. A leg in a board stuck out in front like a bowsprit.

"I heard that bell from my bed," said Selka Dray. She was the color of tallow, and she had a blanket round her shoulders. "I said that I'd come. I'd terms. There's a needle on her; I can see Bexley's tripod. Put me down by that post, Marten, where I can see the hole. Not so near. I've been near."

They put her down beside Kiva, against the eighth post, the last of the north row, five paces from the shaft.

"The cord on Seven's rung is Finch's, on her own cloth," said Kiva. "The wax is Pryce's. The word's Cade's; he may say *off* from the bar, and Orla may, and I may, in two places." She had come to the end of it. It was a long list. There was hardly anything left on it with her name. "That's who has what. I've only the middle."

"The price," said Rhea. "I'll hear that from him."

Healer Aske had walked down the lane. He had no coat on. He took her left hand without a word and turned it to Pru's lamp, and pressed the palm, and the wrist, and looked at the nails.

"No white," he said. "I looked for it. I'd have been glad of it." He did not let go. "I'll say this to the foreman's face and to the officer's, since I said that I would. I don't know. The two fingers paid the last time, since they'd been hurt first. They've nothing left. So it comes out of the next thing, and the next thing's the hand. I think it goes to the elbow."

"And comes back?" said Vane.

"I think so. I've no case to think it from. Nobody has done this three times and let a healer watch." His dry voice did not rise. "It may be that she keeps a hand that grips and doesn't feel. Like her aunt's right, only the whole of it. That's the price as near as I can say it, and I'll not round it down to be obliging."

Nobody spoke. Along the foot of the wall the white cord drew up, and let down.

"And if I say no?" said Rhea.

"Then you cut from the bottom, and it's paid out at the top, and it stands on this hill with its arm in the main." Kiva had that ready. It was not a good answer, and she had made it on purpose. "The pumps go on running in step. My aunt can't fill over moving water. It's as it was this morning, with a string added, until a cell fouls or somebody thinks of a thing that I haven't. I'd not blame you. I was glad on Sunday when you said it."

"Were you?"

"For about an hour."

Rhea Sorn looked at her for a long moment.

"Did anybody look at you?" she said. "Just now. Before you spoke."

"No. I watched you all not."

"Hm." Rhea looked at the hand in the healer's grip, and not at Kiva. "The left hand, to the elbow. Her healer doesn't know that it comes back. Offered. Not asked." She was quiet a moment. "Under my hand, then. Twenty-three years I've cut first and asked the price after. It seems I can ask it first."

"I answer for the hand," said Orla.

She had come down from her crate. She stood with both palms on the head of her stick.

"As her sponsor. I've seen it clean twice. I've seen it missed three times. I'll not pretend it's more than that." She did not look at Kiva. She looked at the cord. "And I don't know that it will work. I said that at the Pit and I was ashamed of how new it felt. It's a little less new."

"I am a witness," said Ysra Crest, from the shed. "I am standing where I can see. Mr. Bexley has two flags."

"I've the pen on her," said Noa. His voice cracked. "I've had it on her since she put her hand to that post."

Vane put his chalk away. When he spoke, it was in the flat field voice he had used in the Pit, and he took longer over it.

"Direction to Kiva Rowan, catcher, Team Seven, attached, under Rhea Sorn's hand. Three doors in sequence, hand, Ember, still, declared. Cost, as her healer said it before: the left hand, to the elbow, and he does not know that it comes back. For: an arm of a string of ten in the Stopgate sluice, driving the Ferren main, and a bank that cannot be filled while it drives. No other hand on this hill takes it off the water. Offered by the candidate. Not asked. Selka Dray calls its time. The corner, once it's planted, is Lowen Brask's, or Wace's." He looked at her. "Do you take it?"

There was a thing to be done first, and the person it belonged to was not in the yard.

"Pryce asks me first," said Kiva. "Before each one. What it's for and what it costs. It's her condition. She's on the wall."

"Then somebody fetch her," said Rhea. "Mace has Stroud."

It was three minutes. Anwen came through the gap with frost on one sleeve and her face shut. She did not ask what had been said. She looked at the cord, and the half-covered hole, and the hand in the healer's two. Then she came and stood square in front of Kiva.

"What's it for?" she said.

"To take an arm off the Ferren main. So a gate can be shut and a bank filled."

"What's it cost?"

"My left hand. To the elbow. Perhaps for good."

Anwen held her eyes. "Is that what's so? All of it?"

"Yes."

"Then I've nothing to add." Her level look did not move. "Ask me."

"Pryce," said Kiva. Her throat had closed. "The wall. For whatever comes up after."

"Mine," said Anwen, and went.

"I'm frightened," said Kiva.

Vane looked at her for a moment. Then he nodded, once, as if she had told him the wind had changed.

"I'd begun the word before you said it," he said. "Do you take it?"

"Yes."

---

It took four minutes to set.

"Ten blades," said Vane. "One to a knot, from outside, dark. You light after the foot. You cut on one word, and the word's hers. Sorn has the knot in my gate, since that's the one with the arm. Renn the one below her. Talley below him. The rest as the Torch sets you."

"The word's *back,*" said Kiva. "Not before the gate's down, and not before my aunt says that the water's lying. On the slack."

"Ten cloths," said Pike. "Over the planks, where there are planks. Finch has hers under Renn's knot."

"The wire. Bexley can't work a key and a pen." Vane looked round. "Three words, to the pump house, as they're said. *Planted.* On that, the Works stops its pumps and quenches its bank; a dead cell can't be cooked. *Down,* when the gate's seated. *Back.* I want a runner at the lodge door who can shout."

"I can shout," said the boy called Ned.

He had got up from the shed wall. He had silt to the hair.

"I'm told that you think a bell's for other people," said Vane. "You'll do. Go."

The fitters had the bar. They stood two a side, in their shirtsleeves, with the ash pole lying fore and aft along the wall like a boat's tiller.

"On *planted,*" said the man in the apron. "Not a breath sooner. I'll not swing a bar through a girl."

Kiva walked to the hole.

The iron lid lay half across it. The arm went down through the foot of dark that was left, at the lane side of the post, humming. She stood on the lid. It rocked a little under her boots, and settled.

"The step first," said Orla.

"I know."

She had not chosen her ground. She knew which way the thing would throw her. It had its whole weight to the east of the post, where the black gate lay beyond the gap, and ten grey shapes on a hill beyond that. A ward under it would be flung along that line, back toward the gap, as a cork goes down a stream. She had gone with the throw in the Pit, and it had served.

Four paces east of the post was packed dirt, under the wall. She stepped off the lid, and went to it, and laid two fingers of her right hand on her breastbone.

"Cold."

She opened the still door.

She set it by its corners, wide, as she had learned to over a cistern. It went down through the dirt.

There was a room underneath.

It lay eight feet down, bricked, and full of something that moved. Her stance found the top of it and rang, faintly, as a foot rings on a cellar flap. And through the brick came a pull and a give, slow and even, and it wanted her in step with it.

She shut the door. She had been open for less than a breath.

"That's not clean," said Orla.

"No. It's the main. It comes in under the gate and along the wall to the post. I felt it on Sunday, on my knees. I'd have put the arm down on top of the drain." She was cold all over. "It'd have gone straight back in."

"Then there's no go."

"There's no go *there.*"

She went back to the post. She stood with her hand on the iron band, and made herself think it through like a fault on Mara's bench, and not like a fright.

The main came in from the east. It went out to the west, along the south row, toward the sheds. And from the north-east, slantwise, a small mouth came in from the core house. That was three roads into one hole. There was a fourth side.

She walked four paces north from the lid, out into the middle of the lane, between the two rows. She opened the door again.

It went down through packed dirt, and clay under the dirt, and it found nothing at all. There was only ground. It took her as a wharf takes a mooring.

"That's clean," said Orla. Her palm was flat on the dirt beside Kiva's boot. "That's as dead as a doorstep."

"Then that's the mark." Kiva shut the door, and scraped a cross with her heel. Her heart was going very fast. "North. Across the lane. Not along it." She raised her voice. She pitched it for the gap, and the shed, and a square shape standing east of the post where the Pit had taught him to stand. "*The mark's turned! It isn't east! East's the drain! I'm carrying it north, to here! Brask! Wace! Say it back!*"

"North," said Lowen Brask. He was already walking. "Not along. To the cross."

"North," said Wace. "To the cross. Moss, with me."

"It'll throw you east," said Rhea Sorn. She had not gone to her knot. She stood at the low wall, with her blade arm down. "I watched the Pit. You went where it sent you."

"I bent the ward to send it. It's a spoon. If I curve it the other way, it'll turn the shove a quarter round." She did not know that. She had done it with a sandbag, from the left. "I think it will."

"*Think,*" said Rhea.

"I can't weigh it."

"No." The white-streaked head went down, a half inch, and came up. "Nor could I have. Go on."

There was a wall of frost to be thought of, and nobody had thought of it.

She saw it then. If the corner went north, the cord would run from a gateway to a cross in the lane, and the hem would hang from it. It would cut the Old Yard slantwise, from the low wall to her heel. The post would be on the far side.

"The bar's behind it," she said. "When I've planted. The hem comes across between the cross and the post. Whoever's at the bar is shut in against the wall, and can't come to me. Whoever has the corner mustn't be at the bar."

Brask had stopped.

"She's right," he said. "I'd have been turning a screw and looked up to find a wall between me and my own job." He looked at the four fitters. "Can you finish it?"

"We've had the best part of two days to finish it," said the man in the apron. He did not sound proud. "I'd not wager a girl's arm."

"Then it's called," said Kiva. "Brask and Cade to the bar, now, south of where the hem will be. Wace and Moss to the cross. *Two to the bar.* I want it said back."

"Two to the bar," said Perrin. He had come up beside Brask. His big face was grey. "Cade and Brask. I keep the word from there. And I'd like it known—"

"It's known," said Kiva.

"I hadn't said what."

"I know what."

He nodded. He went and put his hands on the ash pole, and the fitters gave it to him as men give up a thing that has beaten them.

She went back to the lid.

---

"*Out,*" said Selka Dray.

Her voice was thin, and exact. It came across five paces of dark from a chair against a post, and it did not hurry.

Kiva put up her hands.

They came up low, at her right hip, over the foot of dark beside the iron. Two fingers stood clear of the rest, straight and stiff. She bent the ward into the bowl of a spoon, and she bent it the wrong way round, with the curve to the east and the open side north.

It was cold. She could feel the cold through the silver.

"Hand, to Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on the throw, and on the landing."

The arm drew up. The lid shivered under her boots. She felt it reach the top of its pull and hang there, and she felt the hang begin to give.

"*In*—" said Dray.

Kiva was already moving. She had felt the give come before it was called, a hair early, as she had promised she would.

"*Hand,*" said Kiva, and slid the pan under.

For the length of a breath it lay there. It was the whole arm of a thing that stood ten abreast down a hill, and it lay in a tray between her palms, light as a clothesline, humming.

"*Out,*" said Dray.

It hauled.

It was not three posts. It was ten. The cord came up out of the dark like a hawser taking a ship, and found a spoon under it, and the whole of that pull went round the curve of her ward and into her right side.

It did not throw her east.

It turned. She felt it turn, a quarter round, as water turns in the bowl of a ladle, and it struck her in the ribs and the hip and pointed her out across the lane.

And it had a beat in it. It was slow and even and many, and it was stronger than anything that she had ever had her hands on, and for the length of one tap she felt her own breath begin to go in time with it.

"*Ember.*"

She folded. Far edge. Near edge. A penny, in her palms. Shut.

Nobody.

She was nobody, going sideways over the packed dirt of the Old Yard with a hill's worth of weight in her spine. The ash went out of her like a breath. And the rhythm went with it. It was left on the far side of a shut door, as mud is left on a step, exactly as it had been once before.

The struts caught her. Low, and long, and north. She was over the lane. Her left hand was cold to the wrist already, and she shut the Ember at the top and did not listen for it. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs and arrive.

Nobody, again.

It came out of a place that had been emptied in the Pit, and had only half filled. She had thought that she knew what that was like. There was a moment in the air when it seemed to her quite reasonable that there should be nothing to pay it with. Something paid. It went out through her left arm. She felt it pass the wrist, and go on, and she felt it pass the elbow.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the pan. Wide. By its corners.

"*Still!*"

Her right foot came down on a cross in the dirt, and the yard took her.

Then the corner came.

It came in at her heel like a tide coming up a slip. It was ten things leaning back on a line, and they went on leaning. It went down through her and into the clay and spread there, and the whole of the old ground under the wall took the weight of it, and held.

There was a white cord running down to the dirt a foot from her right boot. It was as taut as a stay. It ran away from her, east and a little south, over a low wall, to a black gateway, and from it a wall of frost was already dropping, thin as breath.

"*Planted!*" said Kiva.

"*PLANTED!*" It was a boy's voice, from the lodge door, and it would have carried to the Sag. "*Planted, planted, send it!*"

"*Bar!*" That was Brask.

She did not look. She had her heel on the thing, and it was doing something she had not been ready for.

It was pulling her into step.

Every time it drew, her weight wanted to brace with it. Every time it gave, her weight wanted to sink. That part had no strength in it, only patience. It was the same slow even beat that had come up through the brick, and she was going with it before she knew it, the way you fall into step with somebody on a pavement without meaning to. And each time she went with it, the corner sat a little heavier, and she was a little less herself and a little more one end of its line.

She had stood in her aunt's shop and watched a meter needle nod in time with the one beside it. She had heard the Ferren bank breathing with one breath for nineteen days.

*Don't nod.*

She broke step.

When it gave, she did not sink. She let the give go by, all of it, and stood on nothing in particular, and she took the weight down late, in the middle of the next pull, when every part of her said it was the wrong moment. The corner jarred against her. It was like missing a stair in the dark. Then it went down into the clay like a stone into mud, and it had not had her.

She did it again, and not in the same place. Late, and then later. Then once almost with it, and away again, as you would dodge somebody who was trying to dance with you.

"*Out,*" said Dray, from her chair, thin and exact. "*In.*"

Kiva did nothing on either. Dray was calling its time, and its time was the one place Kiva would not be.

It could not get hold of her. It pulled and gave and pulled, and every time it went looking for her in its own time, she was somewhere else.

"Wace," she said, between two of its beats. "The plank. Your end."

*Boom.*

A still door opened on her left, a yard off, and the lane rang with it. It was a great deal wider than hers. Wace had been a post before.

*Boom.* Another, beyond him. Hendry Moss stood there with his thick neck sunk between his shoulders, and between the two of them something hummed, like a plank laid from trestle to trestle.

"Mine," said Wace.

She leaned it.

She had never done that. She had watched it done to her, on a floor that boomed, by a boy who pushed one man and moved another. She let the corner go over, a little at a time, from her own heel to the end of that plank, as one lets a full tin down onto a shelf.

It went. She felt it go off her. She felt two still-doors take it and sink a finger's breadth into the dirt.

And she felt them start, at once, to go with it.

"*No,*" she said. "Don't go with it. It wants you in step. That's how it gets you. Listen to Dray. Wherever she says, don't be there. Take it when I call, and I won't call it the same way twice." She felt the thing give under her boots, and let the give go by, and waited, and waited. "*Now.*"

"Now," said Wace, through his teeth, a beat late, and the corner jarred against him and went down.

"Hendry," said Kiva. "You're with it. You're nodding."

"I'm not—" said Moss. Then, through his teeth: "I am."

She shut the door.

She shut it on an empty doorway. She was standing on a cross in the Old Yard with nothing open, and a cord a foot from her boot that was not hers any longer. She could still feel the thing through the soles of her boots, without a door, the way you feel a tram coming through a rail.

She did not sit down. She had meant to. She had two people beside her now, and then three; a long incurious boy in black and gold had walked out of the dark without a word and set his feet at Moss's shoulder, and the plank had become a thing with three ends.

"Yarrow," he said. "Halden. I'm told it goes round."

"It goes round," said Kiva. "Don't let it take you with it. *Now.*"

Five paces off, a girl in a chair was calling the creature's time, and a yard from her a girl with nothing open was calling everything else.

Perrin Cade had the end of an ash pole in both hands, and he had never wanted so much to say that he could not.

It was not heavy. That was the insult of it. He had held three hundred and eighty kilos over his head on Thursday, long enough for the whole Hall to get to its feet. This was a bar as thick as his wrist, stuck through a post, and it would not go round.

"It's not weight," said Brask. He was at the other end, with his feet set. "It's a screw. You can't lift a screw. You lean, and you wait for it."

"I've leaned."

"You've pushed. Open the door, Cade. Wide. And don't push at all."

Perrin opened it.

The dirt of the Old Yard hummed under him. It was a narrow place to stand. There was thirty feet of brick a yard behind his heels, and in front of him, three paces off, a wall of frost had come down across the lane like a sheet let fall from a line. He could see through it. There were shapes on the far side, and a white cord, and a small figure with one arm hanging.

*You don't look up.*

He looked at the bar.

"On my word," said Brask. "*Give.*"

He let his weight go into the pole. He did not shove. He stood there, wide, and let the screw have the whole of him, as he had let the Long Terrace have a plate.

Something groaned under the ground.

"*Take.*"

The bar moved a hand.

"*Give.* It's coming. Don't chase it. *Take.*"

They walked it. It was the slowest walk of his life. A quarter turn, with the pole coming round toward the wall, and his knuckles a foot from the brick. A half. The post creaked in its iron bands. Down the half-covered hole between them there was a sound like a ship's timbers.

His ribs were telling him something. They had been strapped from the armpits to the hips for a spring and a summer, and they had a good memory.

*That's not pain,* he thought. *That's only where pain was.*

"It's near," said the man in the apron, behind him. He had both fists clenched, as if that would help. "It's near. A little more, and it's in the groove."

"*Give.*"

It stopped.

It stopped as it had stopped for four fitters since Tuesday. The pole stood across the lane, and the two of them stood at its ends with everything they had in the ground, and nothing moved.

"There's a hand's breadth of silt in the west groove," said Perrin. His voice came out squeezed. "The boy said. He was on it when I had him out."

"Then it's a hand's breadth of silt against two still-doors," said Brask. "I'd back us. *Take.*"

It did not take.

Through the frost he could hear two voices going. There was a girl calling *out* and *in*, thin and exact. There was another calling *now*, and never once where the first one did, and her voice was not steady, and she had two people answering her, and then three.

She had planted it and given it away. He had heard her do that. She had stood on that lid and named every part that was somebody else's, and his was this.

"Brask," he said. "I'd like it known that I mind."

"What?"

"All of it. Everything since the fifth month. I've been saying that I don't, since Thursday, and it was a lie; I was only pleased." He shut his eyes. "I mind that I had my back to the Pit. I mind that I carried an old man past Pryce holding a string. I mind that there's a wall of ice between me and my own team and I'm pushing a stick."

"Then mind it into the bar," said Lowen Brask, "and stop telling me."

He did.

He had no more strength. He only stopped keeping any of himself back for afterward. The still door went down through the dirt, and the clay, and the old brick of the wall's footing, and the whole of the bottom of the School came up under his boots.

The screw shrieked.

The bar went round. It went a quarter, all at once, and the two of them walked after it, stumbling, until it came up hard against its stop and would go no further. Under the ground an iron plate as broad as a door went down into its groove and struck bottom with a sound like a struck tank, and the whole Old Yard heard it.

"That's home!" said the man in the apron. "She's seated!"

"*DOWN!*" Perrin had a voice like a tram, and he had been saving it. "*Gate's down! Kiva! It's shut!*"

He let go of the pole. His hands would not open at first.

He found that he was sitting on the dirt with his back against thirty feet of brick. There was a line of something warm down his side, under the tunic, where two ribs had been.

"That's the strapping," he said, to nobody. "I'd forgotten that I'd left it off."

"Did it go?" said Brask.

"No. It's only telling me."

Through the frost, a girl was still calling *now*.

Mara Rowan had never liked that room. Twenty-four cells down one wall, and every one of them leaning on its neighbor.

She had hands for them, all the same. She had got the last of them just before noon, off a tram, in a body: five students in Wren House green who had stood a Bench in the ninth month and had been waiting at the Brickfields shed with the carts. There was an operator to every cell that she could find one for. There was a student at the rest, with a red handle under one hand and a fresh can at his feet.

Rusk had cell one. He had taken his coat off.

"I've not pulled a handle since I was made examiner," he said. "It seems I know where it is."

The three pumps stood at the far end, turning. The long room was lit off its own bank, a row of lamps down the ceiling fed by the same twenty-four cells. Old Lumb, the keeper, sat on an upturned bucket beside the sump hatch with a notched stick down the hole and an oil lamp on the floor beside him, lit, though nobody needed it yet.

"When they stop, the water comes up," he said. "It rained Sunday." He reached into the hatch and rapped a brass plate set in the sump wall, a foot over Mara's chalk, green with age and stamped with the Works' mark. "When it's at the brass, it's at the sill, and then it's the Hythe's cellars, and I'll have a hundred people at my door in the morning with wet coal. I've told you that before."

"I heard you the first time, Mr. Lumb."

"I'm only saying."

Her chalk mark was a hand above the water. The water came up to it, and fell.

It had come up to it for nineteen days.

There was a sounder on the keeper's desk, a small brass arm on a spring. A Works clerk sat over it with his head bent to it. Beside him stood a narrow woman with a grader's seal on a cord and a brass glass like a clock with no hands; and behind her, with his hat in his hand, a pleasant man in a black coat who had asked very civilly whether he might watch.

The sounder chattered.

"*Planted,*" said the clerk.

Mara did not allow herself to think about what that word had cost, or whom. She had told a girl in a yard that she could only manage it from two miles off.

"Mr. Lumb. Stop your pumps."

He threw three levers, one after another. The slow breathing at the end of the room faltered, and ran down, and ceased.

It was very quiet. She had not heard that room quiet in her life.

"Handles!" said Mara. "Hands on. On my word, and not on your neighbor's. *Quench!*"

Twenty-four red handles came over in one flat sound, like a shutter.

All down the long wall the needles stopped nodding. They did not go one after another. They went as they had lived for nineteen days, together, and lay against their pins.

And the lamps went out.

She had known that they would. It was still a shock. The long room went from white to nothing, and then to the small yellow circle of Lumb's oil lamp on the floor, and the one in her own hand, and a row of faces in green standing in the dark at their handles with their eyes very wide.

"Dead," said Rusk, out of the dark. "That's a bank that can't be cooked. Whatever comes down that drain now, there's nothing here for it to foul."

"There's the water," said Mara.

She went to the sump hatch. She knelt, in her good skirt, and held the lamp down.

It came up to the mark. It fell. It came up again, a little short. And it fell less far than it had, each time, because the whole of it was rising.

"It's still moving."

"It's less," said Lumb. He had his stick in. "A finger. It was two." He did not say the other thing. He did not need to. Her chalk was going under.

"It's being let go of. Something's had hold of it, and it's been taken off, and the water's only rocking with what was in it." She did not take her eyes from the chalk. "I want it flat. I'll not fill over a rock."

The sounder went again.

"*Down,*" said the clerk.

That was a gate. That was an iron plate in a groove at the head of a hill, and a core house on the dry side of it. She had not quite believed in it until this breath.

She watched the water.

It rose toward the brass, and rocked, and rose. It was like watching a cup that somebody had set down too hard. She put her left hand flat on the surface, her reading hand, and felt the last of the rocking go out of it, and the black water lay in the lamplight as flat as a slate, with her own face in it, a hand below the sill.

"It's lying," she said. Her voice was not quite her own. "Send that. One word. *Still.*"

The clerk's hand rattled on the key.

It was a long way to send a word, and a long way back. She knelt at the hatch and watched her own face in a drain. She had no notion what was being done on a hill by a School. She had arranged it so. She had told herself that it was so that she could do her work.

Somewhere down the culvert, beyond the hatch, there was a sound that she did not like. It was water talking to itself in the dark. It had been a long way off when the pumps stopped. It was not so far now.

"It's past your chalk, missus," said Lumb. He was not chatting any more. "Two fingers off the brass."

The sounder spoke.

"*Back,*" said the clerk. And then, since it went on: "*Hem down. Ten cut. It is out. Fill. Vane.*"

"*Fill!*" said Mara.

She was on her feet. She did not know when she had got up.

"Draw your dead cans! Fresh home! Every one's had its pinch; I salted them myself. Give each one its full minute before you touch the tune, and I don't care if you have to say your prayers to fill it. Then tune. And *not* to the lamp tune. I'll give each of you your tune at the drum, and there aren't two alike in this room."

They drew. They filled. In the dark the long room filled with people waiting out a minute, some of them praying, one of them humming, all out of time with one another, and it was the most beautiful noise that she had ever heard in a pump house.

She went down the row with her lamp.

She had known that she would have to. Twenty-four fresh cells stood side by side on one wall, and a cell was a patient thing, and it leaned to its neighbor. Somebody had to stand between each and the next while its tune took hold, and feel which way it was leaning, and tell the tuner. Somebody had to make sure that no two of them took the same step.

She laid her left palm on the first drum.

"That's yours, Mr. Rusk. Three. It's holding three. Good."

The second. "Five. It's leaning down toward three. Bring it up. More. There."

It was like standing in a doorway between two rooms of people who each wanted her to come in. She held her hand on the iron and felt for both. She had watched a girl do something like it on the cinders of a pit, and had not understood until now what it had been taking out of her.

"Seven. Good. Four."

"That one's going back," said the narrow woman at the glass. She had not raised her voice. "Third from the end. It's fallen in with its neighbor. I have it on the dial."

"I felt it. Quench it. Fill it again." Mara did not stop. "There's a second can under your stool, boy. That's what it's for."

Halfway down the wall she noticed that her fingertips were white.

Further on she could not feel the iron. She could feel that there was a drum. She had a girl at home who used those words.

The lamp in her right hand had begun to gutter. She had filled it that morning and forgotten that she had used it at the manhole too.

"Mr. Rusk," she said. "You'll take the odd ones from here. Your thumbs are as good as my palm; I've said otherwise for years out of pride. I'll take the even."

He came without a word.

"Lumb. The first pump. It's on the first eight cells, and no others. Start it."

One pump drew, and let go, in the quiet. It had a slow, private, limping sound. Down the culvert, the water went on talking.

"That's one," said the keeper. "I want three."

The second pump took its cells and drew. The third would not. The cell third from the end had fallen in a second time, and a third, and it was a white-faced Wren House student who said at last, in a very small voice, that he thought the can was cut.

It was. It was civic stock, and somebody had put soot in it.

"Missus." Lumb's voice had gone thin and high, like an old man's, which she had never once heard it be. "It's at the brass. There's water going over. I can hear it."

"I can hear it too. Give me a can off the spare shelf. Not that one. The one with the wax unbroken." Her lamp went out. She did not stop for it. She had her left hand on the drum, and she could not feel it, so she bent to Lumb's lamp on the floor and watched the needle in its light instead, as she had taught a child to do. "Its full minute. Tune it to six. Hold it. *Hold.*"

It held.

"Start your pump, Mr. Lumb."

The third pump drew.

Three pumps took their strokes in the long brick room, each in its own time, like three people walking who do not know one another. Along the left wall four-and-twenty needles trembled in the yellow light, and stood, and not one of them nodded to the next. Down the culvert, the talking water went quiet. Overhead, the ceiling lamps came back, one at a time, and not together.

"Seven cellars," said Lumb. He had his ear to the hatch, and his voice had come back to its own size. "Perhaps eight. A hand of water in each. I've known the Hythe have worse from a blocked gutter." He looked at her. "You're holding that arm oddly, missus."

She was. She had it against her chest, with the right hand over it.

It was white to the wrist, and painless. It lay there like a thing that she was minding for somebody.

"That's starved," said the woman with the glass. She had come round the desk. She took the hand, quite briskly, and turned it to the lamp. "I've seen it on graders who hold a reed too long over a loud tin. It's not burned. It wants ward-trace, plain, inside the hour, or it'll sulk for a month." She frowned. "The shelf's pinches were plain of everything, ward and all, and a hand can't feed on that. There's not a pinch of plain ward-trace in Meridian. It's all had the hair in it since the thaw."

"Then it will have to sulk," said Mara.

She was looking at the needles.

"I said to a watch officer that everything in my house had a meter and a way to shut it down," she said. "I've been nineteen days finding the second half." She was very tired. "I should like somebody to tell me what it cost."

Nobody in the pump house could.

"*Back!*" said Kiva.

She said it in the middle of a pull, off the creature's beat, where it least expected anybody. A boy had shouted *still* from the lodge door, and a watch officer had said, quite quietly, *Rowan, it's your word,* and she had let the thing go round once more to be certain.

"*Give it to the ground!*"

Three still-doors let go together. The lane boomed.

There was light.

It came over the bottom wall. She could not see the street. She saw the brick, thirty feet of it, go white along its top, and the underside of the mist over Stopgate lit like a ceiling. One flash was blue-white and no longer than a blink. One was yellow, and level, and lasted most of a second, in a black gateway to the east. There were eight more.

Then the cord beside her boot lay down.

It went loose without snapping, from her cross to the gate and away down a hill that she could not see, and the frost that hung from it came after, slowly, folding as it fell. The Old Yard was whole again. There was a post on the far side of where a wall had been, and two people sitting against the brick.

And every window in the School showed a point of light.

She saw it. They had been brown, and dark, a hundred of them, on their boxes or on nothing. For the space of a blink each had a spark in it. Over the wall, down the whole length of a dark hill, six hundred more had done the same.

"*Dark!*" It was a square flat voice from the gateway.

And nearer, on the other side of the brick, a smaller one. It shook.

"*Dark! Corners!* Not you, the warden's corner, she's proud, bring her in a hand—"

That was Pru. That was a first-year who had run from a spaniel, calling a hem for a Fire's rung.

"There's a notch," said Noa.

He was at the shed. He had a white flag in one fist, as high as his arm would go, and he had not put it down to read.

"Two cliffs. Twenty-six, and twenty-six. Nothing between but the bound. And after the second, there's a notch." His voice had gone to nothing. "I've laid the Pit tape on it. The notch sits on the Pit's notch, to the width of the pen. It's the third time."

"That's the lamps," said Orla.

"No. That's what I can't get past. It isn't." He lowered the flag at last. "The notch was five minutes ago. It was while she was saying *Wace.* The lamps didn't so much as wink then. I was looking; I've done nothing but look." He turned his narrow face up to a hundred dark windows. "They jumped just now. At *back,* when the hem came down. At the Pit those fell in the same instant. I've had them as one thing for four months."

"They're two," said Kiva.

"They're two. One of them keeps time with you. The other keeps time with that." He nodded at the wall, and the street beyond it. "I don't know what either is. But I know now that I've been calling two things by one name."

"I watched you," said Selka Dray.

She had stopped calling. She sat in the infirmary's chair with her blanket round her and her leg stuck out, and her sunk eyes were on Kiva.

"I called its time for you the whole while, and you never once came in on it. Not with the plank. Not with nothing open. Not with three boys who'd never stood under a hem in their lives." Her jaw worked. "Once you nearly did. Near the end. I'd not mention it, only that you'd want it right."

"I would."

"I know. It's the worst thing about you."

Kiva found that she was sitting down. Somebody had her by the shoulders. It was Orla, on her knees in the dirt, with the stick thrown aside.

"Hand, Rowan."

She looked for it.

It was at the end of her arm. She lifted the arm from the shoulder, and it came. It was the color of a candle. It was that color to the wrist, and past the wrist, and past the elbow, to a place a hand's breadth above it where the sleeve began.

It did not feel like anything. It was only a thing that she could see.

She put her right hand to her collar.

"Cold," she said.

Then she put the same hand flat on the packed dirt of the Old Yard, beside the cross.

She had had something under that palm for ten days. It had come up through a doorpost, and an iron plate, and a flagstone, slow and even and many.

There was nothing. It was only ground.
