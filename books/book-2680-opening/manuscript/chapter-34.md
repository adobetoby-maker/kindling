# Chapter 34 — Kiva: On What Grounds

The Placement Chair's hearing room had been built for quarrels between schools, and it had benches for forty.

There were not forty. There were perhaps five-and-twenty, and they had sorted themselves along the two sides of the long table as water sorts itself in a trough. On the left, by the windows, sat a pleasant man in a black coat with a clerk at his elbow and a flat leather case before him. On the right sat the wardens of Weighbridge Street. There were three of them. The lamp warden had put on a collar. Warden Cobb had not put on anything that she did not wear every day, and she sat with her small dry hands folded on the table as if she meant to keep them there till she had what she came for.

Perrin's father sat behind her, on the wall bench, with his cap on his knee. He had not been asked. He had walked her up the steps and had not seen his way to leaving.

Grader Halloran had the middle of the table to herself, with a cup and the comb. At the far end sat Rhea Sorn, in a plain coat, and beside her Pike, who had folded his broad hands on the wood.

Team Seven had the bench under the clock. Kiva sat with Kellan on one side of her and Perrin on the other, and Pru beyond Perrin, very upright, with her yellow card held in both hands as if somebody might ask to see it. Noa was at the end with a roll of tape across his knees. Orla had a chair by the door, and behind Orla, where she could see everybody and be asked nothing, sat Mara.

Ysra Crest came in on the hour. Everybody stood, and she waved them down.

"I'll say first what I am not," she said. "I am not a court. I can't construe a patent, and I shan't try. I am the Chair of a Circuit, and a bag of ash was tinned by a Circuit hand on a chartered company's shift, and the Compact's ash office answers to me for where such a bag lies and who may touch it. That is the whole of my business this morning. I'd be obliged if nobody asked me to do more." She sat. "Mr. Adlam. You asked to be heard first."

He rose. He did it easily, with one hand on the leather case.

"The Halden Works is grateful, ma'am, and it will be brief. It claims nothing new. In 2391 it was granted the Step, and all ash exhibiting the Step, however produced. There is such an ash in the Wren House cold room. A grader of the House has put a comb over it, and so has the Works' own, and they agree to a hair." He inclined his head to Halloran, who did not incline hers. "The Works does not say that anybody did wrong on that wharf. It asks one thing, and it asks it civilly. A hundred and thirteen drams were held back from a street that was owed half of them that night. They were held back on the word of a provisional candidate and a field comb. The Works would like to know on what grounds, so that it may know what grounds to bring."

"And what does the Works propose?"

"That the bag go where such ash has gone for three hundred years, into a bank built to carry it. The Works will pay the street its ground-first half today, at par, in plain civic stock, and the company and its attached team their shares on the same footing. Nobody is the poorer. The ash is put to the only use for which anybody has ever found it safe." He smiled a little. "I'm told that a shed foreman on that wharf offered a week's wages for it. He had the right of the matter."

Perrin's father shifted on the wall bench, and Kiva heard his cap fall off his knee.

It was a fair offer. She could feel how fair it was. Everybody was to be paid, and the red tag was to become a matter of bookkeeping, and the only thing that would have happened on a wharf at four in the morning was that a bag had been a little delayed.

"Warden," said Ysra.

Mrs. Cobb did not rise. She was not much taller standing.

"I've been offered ash by a rule before," she said. "I said so to the Torch, there, and she'll bear me out. I don't want that bag. I'd not have it in my cellar for a pension. But I'll tell you what I do want, and it isn't fifty-six and a half drams of civic stock from a gentleman in a good coat." She did not raise her voice. "That bag came up out of a hole in my road, over forty-eight flats, and a man of ours had his shoulder under a leg to get a stranger out. Before it's the Works' or the House's or anybody's, it's Weighbridge Street's. I'll not have it called anything else in this room. I've had my street called *the installer's* once this year, and I didn't care for it."

"Does the Works dispute where the bag was made?" said Ysra.

"Not for an instant, ma'am."

"Then that much stands. Grader."

"The reference shelf wants a dram," said Halloran. She did not get up either. "One. Every pinch I've ever heard speak on the seventh reed came out of a swept bank. This didn't. I can't learn a thing I'm not let touch. I've no opinion on who owns the rest and I'd thank everybody not to ask me for one."

"Catcher."

"I tagged it," said Pike. "Red. Not for the ground. On her word and a comb."

"Whose word?"

"Hers first." He nodded down the room. "She had the north hem. She told me before I'd finished saying *sealed.* I put my hand on it and felt nothing; I never have. Then I held the reed over it, and the reed knocked."

"Miss Rowan."

Kiva stood up. Her knee let her, though it was still strapped.

"What did you weigh?"

"Nothing, ma'am. I can't weigh it." She had been told to go on saying that, by somebody who had cause to know, and she found that it came more easily in a full room than it had on a wharf. "I'd my hand on the hem. It came up through the canvas, all of it together, like the hum off the tram sheds. I haven't an instrument for that. I'd not have asked anybody to hold a bag back on it."

"What did you ask?"

"I said it was keeping time. He held the reed."

"So that your word was a reason to look, and the comb was the looking."

"Yes."

"And the caution? You gave a tuner a caution from a patent, I'm told, across a trestle, at four in the morning."

"A friend had read it to me. *Not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under load.* I didn't know what six boxes on one landing were. I thought they might be a bank." Her face was hot. "I still don't know. I said so then."

"Mr. Adlam. Does the Works say that ash is safe in a tenement box?"

There was the smallest pause.

"The Works' own caution says that it is not, ma'am. We find ourselves in the curious position of agreeing with the catcher."

"Then you have your grounds, and you brought them yourself." Ysra folded her hands. "You asked why that bag was kept from the ground. It was kept on a catcher's word, a chartered catcher's comb, and a caution the Halden Works put its own name to in 2391. I find that sufficient."

Somebody on the wardens' side let out a breath. Adlam bowed, quite gracefully, like a man acknowledging a point at cards.

"May I be heard?"

It was Kellan. He had got to his feet without Kiva feeling him move.

"You're not a party, Mr. Renn."

"No, ma'am. I held a corner of that bag. I'm entered for the Regional on the Works' license team, and from this Thursday I draw its stipend. I'd sooner you had that from me than found it on a list." His voice was level, and his ears were white. "I heard the shed foreman offer his week's wages. I also heard what he said next, and it's been left off. He said he'd not put a thimble of it in his own kitchen. He said his cold-cupboard had no business keeping time with next door's."

"Is the foreman here?"

"He is," said a deep voice from the wall. Perrin's father had stood up with his cap in both hands. "I said it. I'd say it again. It's good ash. It's the best I ever had my hand on. I'd not have it in the house."

"Thank you. Sit down, both of you." Ysra looked at Adlam. "Does the Works object to that being heard?"

"The Works would expect nothing else of Mr. Renn," said Adlam pleasantly. "It said so to him a week ago."

Kellan sat. His hands were shut on his knees. Kiva did not look at him, since it was the only thing that she could do for him.

The Chair was quiet for some time, looking at the table. Then she spoke, slowly, and the room was so still that Kiva could hear the lamp warden's collar creak when he swallowed.

"The bag lies where it lies, in the Wren House cold room, under three seals: the Works', the wardens' of Weighbridge Street, and this office's. It is opened with all three present or not at all. It was made in Weighbridge Street on the 24th, off a call answered by the Graywater Company, and whoever comes to own it will own it knowing that."

Mrs. Cobb's chin went up a fraction.

"The street is owed its ground-first half, and has waited long enough. The casualty reserve will advance fifty-six and a half drams of plain stock to the wardens today. Whoever is found to own the bag will repay the reserve."

"Plain," said Mrs. Cobb.

"Combed in front of you, Warden, by anybody you care to name."

"I'll name her." The old woman pointed with her chin at Halloran, who looked resigned.

"One dram is drawn for the reference shelf and one for the Halden technical office, under the three seals, and no more. A thing that nobody may test is a thing nobody will ever agree about." Ysra turned her eyes to Adlam. "The Works' claim under its patent is heard. It is not ruled on. The Works may take it to the Compact's court, where it belongs, and I expect that it will."

"It will, ma'am," said Adlam.

"I'd think the less of it otherwise. Now the part that is mine." She sat back. "A catcher of any step, on any Circuit floor or any shift a Circuit team is attached to, may tag a bag red on grounds stated. A bag so tagged is combed before it is panned. A catcher's word is sufficient to withhold. It is not sufficient to prove. It may stop a bag. It may not name one."

Pike's square head came up a little.

"And until the court has spoken, no ash that speaks on the seventh reed above a trace is to be issued, bought, burned or settled on any floor of this Circuit. Every dram brought to the Regional Gates will be combed at the door, the houses' with the rest." She looked at Adlam. "That binds the Works' own license team, Mr. Adlam, as it binds the others. I take it that your yard has plain stock."

"Its yard has. I can't answer for what comes out of the Kiln, ma'am. Nor, I think, can you."

"No," said Ysra. "I can't. It's why I've made the rule about tags."

She let them go at ten, all but four. The wardens went down the stair in a body, the lamp warden saying the figure over to himself as though he might drop it. Mrs. Cobb stopped in front of Kiva on her way to the door.

"I've been up to Two-C every morning since," she said. "The light comes on when the door's opened. It's not so much as winked." She looked Kiva up and down, as she had on the wharf. "I'd have known whose door to knock at. I'm rather pleased not to have had to."

She took Perrin's father's arm and went out.

---

There were four of them left, and the room seemed a good deal larger.

"Now the other matter," said Ysra. "It won't take long. Mr. Bexley, I was in a yard last night, and I've heard about the eighteenth from three people. I've one question for you, and one for the sponsor. Can a Torch with a stick tell a crossing from a stack?"

Noa stood up. The roll of tape slid off his knees and he caught it.

"No, ma'am."

"You're quite certain."

"She leaned on it with her whole weight. She said there was nothing there to find. There wasn't. It's only on the tape." He swallowed. "I don't think anybody could. I don't think it's her fault."

"I didn't ask whose fault it was. Orla?"

"He's right," said Orla. She had not risen, and nobody asked it of her. "I'd have passed it. I'd have called it a crossing, and I'd have been as wrong as I've ever been in my life but once. I can tell you now when she waits at the top. I couldn't tell you that she hadn't."

"Then here is what follows from it, and you'll not care for it." Ysra did not look away from her. "No crossing stands toward the sponsor's six, or toward anything, unless the tape shows the cliff. The sponsor's eye is not sufficient. Mr. Bexley is reader to this office for the candidate. At the Regional he sits at the Circuit's mast with a flag." She turned to Orla. "Your name stays on her line. I'd thought of taking it off. I'd very nearly made up my mind."

"I'd supposed you would have."

"A sponsor who can't see a fault is no use to me. A sponsor who walks into this office on the Monday and tells me that she couldn't is the only kind I've ever been able to use, and I had to wait twenty years to meet one. You'll keep the stick. He'll keep the pen. Neither of you passes a crossing without the other." She stood. "And I want one of the three that are left made on my own floor. I've no doubt of your yard. I should simply like to know that it wasn't the yard."

Orla laughed, one short bark, and did not explain it.

Kellan Renn had never liked the Halden refinery gate.

It was iron, and thirty feet high, and it had the Halden Works' device on it in gilt, a hand with a flame standing in the palm. He had walked under it with his mother's dinner every school holiday since he was eight. When he was small, he had waited outside it for four hours on the day that they nearly took her stamp away, and he had stared at its rivets until he could have drawn them in the dark.

He looked at them again on Tuesday afternoon while the porter found his name. They had it on a list now.

The grading room was at the top of the old still house, under a skylight, with no lamp in it and no cell within thirty paces. His mother was alone at the long bench. She had a tray of pinches in front of her, and the comb, and the brass seal on its cord, and she did not look round until she had finished the cup that she was on.

"You've been in Crest's room," she said. "Your collar's wrong. You pull at it when you've stood up in front of people."

"I said what the foreman said. About his kitchen."

"I thought that you might." She held the comb over the next cup. Nothing spoke but the third reed, a thin clear note, and under it the hair that was in everything now. "Was it true?"

"I was four paces from him."

"Then you'd no choice in the matter, and I don't know why you've come up three flights to tell me." She moved the cup along. "Sit down, Kellan. You've your father's way of standing about."

He sat on the stool at the bench end.

"Your seal's at the foot of their claim," he said.

"My seal's on the grading. I held a reed over a pinch that a Wren House woman brought across the river in a cold tin, with two wardens watching her, and it knocked. I said *Step, full,* and I put my seal to that. I'd do it again." She laid the comb down. "I didn't seal *whose.* I've never sealed whose in two-and-twenty years. That's said by people on the second floor, and they say it over my seal because my seal is what makes them believed."

"Doesn't that trouble you?"

"It troubled me a great deal when I was your age." She looked at him at last. She had his eyes, or he had hers, and she was not smiling. "Then one morning a man came up those stairs with a tin that was stamped First, and it was soot. He told me to put my seal under it, and I wouldn't. Every grader on this floor put her cups down until the Works had heard the two of us on the open floor. I learned that if I only ever put my name to what the reed said, there'd be one thing in this building nobody could make me unsay. It's a narrow sort of safety, and I've never found another."

"I told Adlam I'd not keep quiet for him."

"And what did he say?"

"That they'd expect nothing else. He's said it twice now. Once to me, and once in front of the Chair."

"Then he's cleverer than the man with the tin." She took up the next cup. "It doesn't make him your friend. It makes him a person who's understood that he can't use you for anything but cutting. I should cut very well for them, if I were you, and be exactly as much trouble as you were this morning, and no more."

He went down to the yard at four.

It was a bigger floor than the Kennel's, paved in blue brick, with a lattice house of its own at one end and a ward line in black standing easy along the wall. They were paid. One could tell it by how they stood. There was a string in the lattice already, a six-knot, grey and patient, and a square man with a foreman's slate was calling rungs.

The boy called Pettit was at the rail, lacing his cuff. He was a second-year, fair and heavy in the leg, and he nodded as one nods to somebody who has been given half one's dinner.

"They've given you my rung," he said. "The top. I'm to carry."

"I didn't ask for it."

"I know. Birch decided on your feet. He told me so himself; he's that sort." Pettit pulled the lace tight. "I ought to mind it more than I do. I watched you light after the foot on Thursday. I've been lighting on the way up since the thaw, and nobody ever told me it was wrong, since the tin always came in."

"Nothing wrong with it. It only prints if your catcher's close."

"Ours kneels at eight paces. That's the yard's rule." Pettit looked at him curiously. "How near does yours kneel?"

Kellan thought of a north hem, and a voice that said *not yet,* and a bound that he had once spent dark because of it.

"Near enough to be asked," he said.

There was a jacket on the peg with his name chalked over it. It was black, and good cloth, with a narrow gold cord at the cuff. He put it on. It fitted him across the shoulders as nothing he owned had ever fitted, and he stood for a moment with his arms a little out, like Cade in his strapping, and disliked how much he liked it.

Then the foreman said his name, and he went to cut.

The sister came down from the hill house on Wednesday, in grey, by the same road the Warden had come.

She was young, not much older than Kiva, with a plain scrubbed face and her hands folded in her sleeves. She would not sit. She stood on the shop floor between the counter and the stove, and looked at the cells on the shelves as a person looks at dogs she has been told are friendly.

"The house thanks your niece for her answer," she said, "and won't press it. I'm sent with one thing more, and then I'm to go."

"Say it," said Mara.

"When she was up at the house, the Warden saw her left hand, and knew it. Our old wall-holders carry theirs so." The sister's eyes went, briefly and without embarrassment, to the hand. "Such a hand isn't burned. It's spent. It fills with a long rest and with nothing else that we've found. Some of ours have come back whole in a year. Not all." She drew a breath, as if she had been made to learn the rest and wanted it right. "The offer stands. A year in the house, as a guest. Silence at table and one door. Every other kept shut, and we know how to keep them shut; it's the only thing we've ever studied. We'd teach her nothing she didn't ask for. We don't say she ought to come. We say the door's there, and it would be shut behind her gently."

Then she bobbed, oddly, as though to a much older person, and went out; and they heard her sandals go away up Coper Street toward the tram.

Kiva sat down at the kitchen table.

It was a kind thing. That was what she had not been ready for. She had expected to be wanted for something, as the Halden Works wanted a bag, and here was a house on a hill that had looked at two fingers lying on a table and had offered her the one thing that Healer Aske had said he trusted.

"A year," she said.

"They've had a good deal of practice at years." Mara came and sat across from her.

"I asked Aske yesterday. Whether rest would fill it. He said that it was the only physic he believed in, and that he couldn't tell me what a month would buy, since nobody had ever given him one."

"So they're not lying to you."

"No."

Mara waited. She had her left hand flat on the table, and her right in her lap, where she generally kept it.

Kiva looked at her own. The first two fingers lay beside the others, a little straighter, a little apart. She could see them perfectly well. They were slow to tell her that the table was there.

She did not have an answer ready. She noticed that, and it frightened her rather more than having one would have done.

"If I went," she said slowly, "I'd come down in a year with ten fingers and one door. I'd not be on a roll. There'd be three on a mast and three never shown, and Orla's name on a line with nothing under it. And the next time a foreman had nobody south of a string, there'd be nobody." She stopped. "That's the good reason. It's a very good one. I can hear how good it is."

"And the other?"

"I want it." It came out small. "I want the six. I want to find out what the third one does. I've wanted to know what I am since I was thirteen, and I'm nearer than I've ever been, and I'd be going up a hill to be kept from it by kind people." She made herself look up. "I think I'd be choosing to keep the fingers so as not to have to find out. I don't know that that's right. I know it's what's true."

Mara did not say anything for some time.

Then she turned her right hand over on the table, palm up, where the lamp could reach it. Kiva had seen the scar ten thousand times. It ran white and shining from the heel of the hand to the roots of the fingers, and in the middle of it there was a place the size of a coin that had felt nothing since a cell on Linden Row.

"Nobody offered me a year," said Mara. "I'd have been told to rest it if there'd been a soul to tell me, and I'd a shop, and a girl of thirteen in the house who had just been told that nobody had a name for what she was. So I went on, and I taught the other hand. I've never known whether it would have come back." She closed the fingers. "I'm not going to tell you that I'd have refused. I don't know. I'm telling you that I know what it is to go on with a piece of yourself not answering, and that it can be done, and that I'd not wish it on you for all the doors in Meridian."

"That isn't advice."

"No. I've none. You'd not have asked me for it a year ago; you'd have told me what you'd decided, very clearly, with reasons." She got up and put the kettle on. "If they send again, I'll say that you thank the house and keep its door in mind. I'll not say *no.* I'd leave a thing like that on the latch."

That night Mara took the left hand at the stove, as she did every night, and pressed the first finger and then the second, and waited.

"The truth," she said.

"Late," said Kiva. "Both. The second later."

Mara let it go, and said nothing, and went up.

The Great Floor was the largest room that Kiva had ever stood in.

She had sat in it. Everybody in Meridian had sat in it, on some holiday or other, up in the banked oak tiers that held four thousand, to watch the Regional's lanes. She had never before come in by the fighters' door at the north end and walked out onto the boards with nobody in the seats. It was Monday, and her week was up.

It was an oval, sixty paces long. The floor was not boards. It was oak blocks set on end, like the paving of a quiet street, black with two hundred years of wax, and when she stepped on it something answered from below.

"It's hollow," she said.

"It's sprung," said the Chair's clerk, who had the keys. "There's an undercroft. It was a cistern before the Compact had the building."

A brass stud the size of a penny was let into the blocks near the middle, and four paces from it somebody had laid a strip of white tape. On the rim above the south tier stood the Circuit's own mast, a great dark thing with four-and-twenty pens, and Noa was up there already, a small figure bending over the one that had been set to her.

Ysra Crest sat in the front row, by herself. Orla had been given a chair on the floor.

"The step first," said Orla. "As ever."

Kiva stood on the tape. She pushed off, a curb's height and a pace, and shut the Ember at the top, and set the still door under her foot before the wood.

It boomed.

The whole floor boomed, like a hand laid on a drum. The stance went down through the blocks and found a room underneath where the ground ought to have been, and for half a breath she had her weight on nothing. Then it caught, somewhere lower, with a lurch. Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder and she went back a full step.

"That's not clean," said Orla.

"No."

"Then there's no go. That's the first rule, and I didn't make it for yards only." She turned her head. "I'm sorry, Ysra. You've come down for nothing."

"I've come down to see a rule kept in front of me when it was inconvenient," said the Chair. "I shouldn't call that nothing. When?"

"Tomorrow. If the step's clean."

Kiva stayed when they had gone. The clerk wanted his dinner, and said so, and she asked him whether he would show her the undercroft first.

It was down a stone stair behind the north tier. He held a lantern up for her, grudgingly, and she stood at the bottom and looked along a low brick vault that ran the whole length of the hall. It was dry, and it smelled of dust. Square brick piers stood in rows down the dark, each as thick as a tram's wheel, with oak beams across their heads.

"How far apart?"

"Four paces. Each way."

"Which one's under the stud?"

He did not know. They paced it out together, from the foot of the stair, with the lantern. The stud was not over a pier. It was in the very middle of four, with two paces of nothing under it in every direction but a beam.

*What's behind you,* Rhea Sorn had asked, in a van. Kiva had learned that one in a street, at a cost that was not hers. It had not crossed her mind until this afternoon that a floor might have a behind as well, and that it might be underneath.

"Could the mark be moved a pace?" she said. "Over that one."

"Moved?" The clerk was shocked. "That stud's been there since the Compact was signed. They put the litter on it. Nobody moves the floor for a fighter, miss. Not at the Regional."

"No," said Kiva. "I didn't think so."

She walked the blocks for an hour in her ordinary boots, with nothing open. She stood on the stud and rocked. She learned where the beams ran by the sound, and where the four piers stood round her, a pace and a half off at each corner, like the weights in the hem of a sheet.

That was what did it. She had been setting the still door as she set a pan, flat and under her, and waiting for the ground to come up and meet it. There was no ground. There were four corners. A sheet did not lie on its middle.

On Tuesday evening Kellan was not there. It was the first go that he had missed. Tuesday was a refinery yard now.

The step was clean. She set it wide, out to the four piers and down them, and the floor hummed under her and held.

"Rowan, changing at the top. Ember, to still."

Her voice went up into four thousand empty seats and came back a little late.

Up. The Ember shut. She waited for it, at the top, for as long as it takes to say a short word, and the ash went out of her like a breath.

Nobody. A girl in the air over a cistern.

She set the pan by its corners.

"*Still.*"

Her right foot came down on a brass stud, and the Great Floor rang once, deep, like a struck tank. The bound went in at her heel and out four ways, along the beams and down the brick. She felt each pier take its share. Then it was quiet, and she was standing on it, and Orla's ferrule came and leaned and found nothing to move.

"Give it back."

She gave it back to four corners, and shut the door.

"A cliff," said Noa's voice, from very high up. It cracked. "Thirty-one. And there's nothing on the other pens. Not a hair. I've three-and-twenty of them idle and I've watched them till my eyes ran."

"So it wants more than a great many pens," said Kiva.

"Or it wants a ring of lamps that's dead. Or it was only ever the first." He leaned over the rail. "I still can't choose. I'm sorry."

"Don't be."

Orla had the hand already. It was white. The Chair came down out of the front row and stood where she could see, and the clerk locked the north door and came back jingling, and stood too. Nobody said anything. The light from the high windows went off the oak and up the south tier while they waited, and Kiva heard Perrin, behind her, stop shifting his feet.

The first flush came at last. Orla's mouth was thin.

"That's past what I'd let go," she said. "So it's Aske before the yard, and I'll take it to him myself."

"Sixty-three left in the glass," said Perrin. He said it very carefully, as if it might break. "Two more is sixty-two. And one hundredth. I'm told I'm to say *and one hundredth* every time."

Ysra Crest was still looking at the hand.

"I'm content that it wasn't the yard," she said. "I'm rather less content with the rest." She put on her gloves. "It was quicker last week."

"It was," said Orla. "She knows it. She was watching the window, same as you."
