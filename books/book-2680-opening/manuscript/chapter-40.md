# Chapter 40 — Kiva: The Grip

Hollis Vane gave the account at five, standing on the cinders, since there were people in that Pit whose floor it had been.

The terraces had not emptied. The stewards had said twice that the Regional Gates were struck and that people might go home, and perhaps a third of them had gone. The rest sat on in the low sun, quietly, like people at the end of a long service who are not sure that it is over.

The bags stood in two rows along the tinning table. One row had white tags. The other had red.

"The Line asks five things at the cart," said Vane. He had his outdoor voice, flat and whole. "I'll ask them here, since this was a call. What's closed. A tear on the open floor, outside the lattice, held by four husks. Struck at twenty past two. Closed at ten past three, by the Home Line."

He let that stand a moment.

"Who's alive. All of us. Who's hurt. Dray, blade, the left leg above the knee, the bone, and a burn of cold. Rowan, catcher, the left hand, as directed. What's still lit. The race was dropped by the keeper and stayed dropped. The Row was quenched, twelve boxes, and none fouled. And across the road, the Ferren bank has been in step since twenty past two and has not come out. That's the Municipal Works' to answer, and I've sent to tell them so."

There was a man in a black coat standing by the load board with his hat in his hand. It was Halden's glass that had found it. He inclined his head.

"What's tinned. Clean, five hundred and twelve drams. Put by, red, on catchers' words and a comb, near as much again. And a ring of something that isn't ash, swept under the Chair's seal, which nobody here has a name for." He turned to the tiers, and then back. "And what I think of you after. One thing. A blade cut a husk inside her step, on her catcher's word, before the foreman had read the floor. The foreman had not called a stand. The safety officer called one to that team as the catcher's hand fell, too late to govern. That's the floor's fault, not hers."

"That's not right," said a voice from the cart.

They had not yet taken Selka Dray away. She had refused to go until it was said.

"It was my cut. I'll have it as mine."

"You'll have the cut as yours, Miss Dray. It's in the kill line with the others, and it was as clean as any I saw today." Vane did not look round. "What I think of you after is for a thing done wrong. You did what a second-step blade is taught to do with a Handful that has its back to her. A Torch keeping a floor safe may stand any student on it, and Sorn did, and it reached you with your foot already going down. The floor was wrong. I'd a company on it and I hadn't read it. That's whose it is."

Dray said nothing. After a moment the thin boy on the cart's step put his hand on the edge of the stretcher, and she let him.

"Now the pot," said Vane. "It was struck. A struck floor's shared the Line's way, and I'll say what that is, since three of you belong to a house and will want to know. It isn't shared by who was kneeling where. Everything clean goes in one, and it's divided by the standing rule. The civic half, two hundred and fifty-six. The casualty reserve, sixty-four. The schools, ninety-six. The teams, ninety-six. That's four-and-twenty to a team. Halden's four tinned more of it than anybody. They'll draw the same as Seven, who tinned a rung that's wearing a red tag."

"The Halden Works does not object," said Mr. Adlam.

"I'd have been surprised," said Birch, beside him, rather sourly. "It was my four tinned it."

"And the red row?" That was Jude. He had Seven's own bag at his feet, with its red tag knotted on the neck.

"The red row goes where the last red bag went. To a cold room, under three seals, on every catcher's word who tagged it. There's a rule, as of the third of the seventh month. I was glad of it today." Vane hung his slate on his belt. "That's a response. It goes to the district as the Line's. I'd sooner it had been a Regional."

He came across to the table afterward.

Kiva was still sitting with her back to it. Aske had bound the hand in dry linen, loosely, more to keep it from being knocked than for any good that it would do, and it lay in her lap like a parcel.

"The cost was said before," said Vane. "Your healer said it and you said it. I'll want it again, when he knows what it came to. I'll come and ask you myself."

"Yes."

He nodded, and went back to his string.

Kiva looked along the table. Perrin was sitting on an upturned crate with his shoulder in his other hand, very pleased with it. Jude was standing over the red bag as if it might get up and leave. Kellan had come back from wherever Birch had gone and stood a little apart, in a black jacket with the cord torn off one cuff. Anwen was coming across the cinders from her gate with her arms hanging. Pru was on the ground beside Kiva and had not let go of her shoulder since the ring.

"Seven," said Kiva.

They came. They came as they would have come for Orla, and stood in a loose half ring, and Perrin looked surprised at himself.

"What went wrong?" said Kiva.

Nobody answered at once. Up at the east rail she saw a crooked shape on a stick turn its head, and stop, and not come down.

"I came down heavy on our rung," said Jude. "I'd a tenth more at the top than I needed. I knew it in the air."

"I had my back to all of it," said Perrin. "That isn't wrong. That's only the worst thing that happened to me."

"Edda was short on the outside and I didn't hear it till it dipped," said Anwen. "I'd have heard it on the Sag."

Kellan was looking at the bags.

"I left Birch's line in the middle of the Pit and told him after," he said. "He'd every right to stop me. He didn't. That was him. It wasn't anything I'd done to deserve it."

"I called *take a corner* across the Great Floor yesterday as if it were the answer," said Kiva. "It was half of one. Who was hurt?"

"Dray," said Pru.

"And you," said Anwen.

"And me." Kiva did not look at the linen. "What did it cost?"

"Four-and-twenty to the team, from the pot," said Kellan, "and a red bag we'll never see. And what she burned out of her thimble." He stopped. "And the fingers."

"Aske says in a week," said Kiva. "Second go?"

"I'd cross it right first time," said Pru, scarlet.

"I'd take the tenth off," said Jude.

"I'd have Edda's outside before she went short," said Anwen. "Not after."

"I'd tell Birch first," said Kellan.

Perrin thought about it.

"I'd do the same," he said. "All of it. I'd have my back to it again. I'd only mind it a bit less, perhaps, with practice."

"Why?" said Kiva.

Nobody had anything. Pru opened her mouth and shut it again. A long way up, at the east rail, Orla Dane leaned on her stick and did not say a word.

"I don't know either," said Kiva. "That's all, then."

Up at the rail, Orla turned away and went to find the stair.

---

Rhea Sorn was sitting on an upturned tub at the end of the table.

She had cut five rungs since ten past three, each longer than the last, and she looked it. She had her elbows on her knees and her hands hanging. She did not get up.

"I told you on a wharf to go on saying that you couldn't weigh it," she said. "You said it again today. I heard you. *It's only what comes through a neck.* Then you tied a red tag on it."

"Pike held it out."

"Pike's no fool." The pale eyes came up. "I'll tell you what I saw that I'd not seen. You stood on this floor and had the cost said before you'd done a thing. You had a healer say it, and a foreman give it you, and you said you were frightened. I've been twenty-three years at this. I cut first, and I ask the price after, and I'm thought very sound for it."

Kiva did not know what to say to that.

"I'm not offering you anything," said Rhea. "You're seventeen, with a hand in a cloth. Seven's still mine. The board will want a Flask stood on my own van before it takes the brackets off, and it's right to." She looked at her own hands. "I'm only telling you because nobody told me. That's the whole of it."

Lowen Brask came by with a canvas roll on his shoulder. He stopped, and nodded at the linen.

"Did it come easy?"

"No."

"Good." He nearly smiled. "Look at your feet anyway. Vane wants me on the Line in the spring. A hold crew, for wages. I said I'd need to think, and then I went and stood by that ring for a quarter of an hour, and I found that I'd thought." He shifted the roll. "They'll not be standing holds for a while, I shouldn't wonder. Somebody's been watching how it's done."

"You said nobody showed them."

"I said I wasn't saying anybody did." He went on toward the west gate. "That's not the same. I had that off a catcher who's grown particular about guesses."

Noa came last, down the keeper's stair, with the tape rolled against his chest in both arms.

He did not unroll it. He sat down on the cinders in front of her, cross-legged, like a boy at a lesson.

"Two cliffs," he said. "Twenty-six, and twenty-six. They're a little under two seconds apart. There's nothing between them but two hundredths for the bound. I held the white up before you'd landed."

"And after?"

"You know what's after. You saw the lamps." His narrow face was grey with tiredness. "It's on all sixteen. Every pen on the drum. A hair, at one instant, about a dozen seconds on from the second cliff. That's when you said *back.* Three of those pens were blades cutting a post. Four were wards bringing up a gate. Two were running with a casualty, and one was the casualty. Five were standing dark against a wall. One was you, with everything just shut. It's the same hair on every one, lit and dark alike."

"Who saw it?"

"The Chair." He swallowed. "She came along the rim while the drum was still turning. She wouldn't have me read it to her. She put her own finger on the first cliff, and the second, and the hair, and stood there for a long while with Adlam behind her and me beside her and didn't say a word. Then she said, *Thank you, Mr. Bexley,* and went back to her rail."

"And Adlam?"

"He wasn't looking at his own glass. He was looking over my shoulder, and he said, *There,* before I did." Noa rubbed his eyes. "He's going to the Chair himself, in his own coat and not Halden's, to say that he was present and saw sixteen pens move together on hands lit and dark alike, and that he knows of no fault in a mast that would do it. He says he'd like to be second, when I've something to show anybody."

"Have you?"

"No." He looked at her. "You've been asked what it is all day. What do you call it?"

"I don't know."

"Nor do I," said Noa. "That's the first true thing either of us has said about it."

He got up off the cinders with the tape in both arms, and went to find out whether the Chair would let him go home.

---

There was no dream that night.

She had expected one. She had lain down early, in the narrow bed, with a basin on the chair and the hand propped on a folded towel, and Mara had sat in the doorway, as she did on fever nights. They had both waited. The fever did not come. The lights did not come by any road.

Late on, Mara got up and came and sat on the edge of the bed, and took the left hand off the towel, as she had taken it every night since the spring. She pressed the first finger. She pressed the second.

"The truth," she said.

"Nothing," said Kiva. "I can see you doing it."

Mara held the hand a moment longer. Then she laid it back on the towel, as one lays down a thing that might wake.

"The plate's cold too," said Kiva.

"So I'd supposed. You'd have told me." Mara did not go back to the doorway. She sat with her back against the bedpost and her knees drawn up, which she had not done since Kiva was small. "I didn't come down."

"I saw."

"I got as far as standing. I'd my hand on the seat in front. I'd said that I wouldn't, and I thought, if I go to that rail now she'll look at me and not at the foreman, and it won't be hers any longer." Her voice was quite level. "I told you once that I wanted you safe without owning what you chose. I'd no notion how much it would cost to mean it. I minded. I'll say that the once."

"I looked up. Before I said yes."

"I know you did. I'd my hand on my coat like a woman who's lost her purse." She got up, stiffly. "A man was saying something about the Ferren bank, by the load board, as they carried you past. I heard two words of it. I shall go across to that pump house on Monday and put my hand on the bank."

"What did you hear?"

"*In step.* And *not come out.*" Mara turned down the lamp. "Go to sleep. It isn't yours. It's the Municipal Works', and for once I'm glad to be the one of us who's licensed."

The wrist came back on Sunday, toward evening, like a tide coming up a slip. The palm came on Monday morning, and the thumb with it, and the last two fingers. By the time she had her boots on she could feel the lace in them.

She could see the first two. She laced round them.

---

The board in the Placement Hall basement had the result on it by ten on Monday, in chalk, and there was a crowd.

Halden License first. Old Four second, with its brackets. Twenty-Eight third. Seven fourth, with its brackets, and under the four of them a line in a clerk's hand to say that the Call had been struck.

"Fourth," said Perrin. He read it with his lips. "Of sixteen. With brackets." He considered. "I've decided I don't mind the brackets. I'd got so used to them. It'd be like losing a tooth."

Kiva was aware of the people round her. Nobody moved their kit away. A first-year whom she did not know looked at the linen on her hand, and then at her face, and then, rather quickly, at the board again.

She was sent for before noon.

Ysra Crest sat behind her desk with her hands folded on nothing. Orla was in the chair by the door, with her stick.

"Sit," said Ysra. "You may put that hand where it's easiest. I've a number of things to say, and only one of them is a ruling."

Kiva sat.

"In 2676 an assessor of this office stood in the Old Yard, with you on the ground, and told your aunt that she had no name for what that was. It came to me, and I let it stand." The long plain face did not alter. "I have let it stand every spring for four years. I told myself that it was caution. Some of it was. A good deal of it was that I had once passed a boy for a Bench because his sponsor was certain, and I had sooner have no name than a wrong one."

"I know about the Bench," said Kiva.

"I imagine that you do. Orla tells a story to its end, these days." Ysra unfolded her hands. "I still have no name for it. I'll not pretend otherwise to oblige anybody. I can't rank you. I don't know what your Hold is, or your Draw, with three things that take turns. I don't know what you are, and you are not to take a line on a notice board for my saying that I do."

"No, ma'am."

"But I had been using *no name* as a reason to do nothing, and that was wrong." She did not look away. "So on Saturday I went up to the mast. I didn't have it read to me. I put my own finger on the tape while the drum was still turning. Two cliffs, and nothing open between them. And a hair on every pen, lit and dark, at the instant you said *back.* I saw that. Nobody told it me."

"What does it change?"

"Your place? Nothing. You're provisional, Third Division, on a sponsor's line, with the conditions that you had on Friday. I've added none and I've taken none away." She almost smiled. "It changes what I know. That isn't a great deal. It's more than I had on Friday, and I got it myself."

Kiva found that she had nothing to say. She kept her hands still.

"Miss Dray has sent word from an infirmary bed," said Ysra. "She asks that the mast condition stand for any candidate ever seated with more than one door. She says that it worked, and that she objected to you twice and would again. Then here is the ruling. From today, on every Circuit floor, any candidate seated with more than one door goes nowhere without a sanctioned mast on her and a reader at it. It is Miss Dray's rule. It goes out with her name on it." She paused. "And Mr. Adlam came to this office yesterday, in his own coat, to tell me what he saw over Mr. Bexley's shoulder. I have not the least idea what to do with that."

"Nor has Noa."

"Then we are three." She turned her eyes to the door. "Orla. You've come to tell me something."

"As her sponsor," said Orla. She did not get up. "What I'd seen and what I hadn't. What I said on that rail, and that I said I didn't know it would work. And what it cost her." The stick did not move. "I answer for the hand. I didn't, the last time. A board settled it for me, and I let them."

"I was on that board."

"I know you were."

They looked at one another, the Chair and the Torch, across twenty years and a girl's bandaged hand. Kiva kept very still.

"Then I'll answer for it with you," said Ysra. "Not as Chair. As the ward-holder who passed him for that Bench. It ought to have two of us."

Orla nodded, once. Neither of them said anything more about it, and after a while the Chair rang for her clerk.

---

Healer Aske gave it the week that he had promised, and on Saturday he took the linen off for the last time.

He had the towel, and the pin. He had also a penny, and a cup of water from the kettle, and a scrap of the waxed cord that catchers used.

"Eyes shut."

She shut them.

She felt the pin on her little finger, and the third, and said so. She felt it on her thumb. She felt it, sharp and at once, in the middle of her palm, and at the heel of her hand, and on the wrist.

Then there was a long time when nothing happened.

"I've had the pin in the first finger all this while," said Aske's voice. "I'm pressing rather hard. I'd be bleeding you if I pressed harder."

"Something's pushing."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

"And here?"

"The same. Less."

He put the penny between her two fingers and her thumb. She could feel it against the thumb. Against the fingers it was only that there was something there, as one knows through a boot that one is standing on a stone.

"Edge or flat?"

"I don't know."

"The cup. Is that hot?"

She had two fingers in water. She knew that from the sound.

"I can't tell."

"The cord. I'm laying it over them. Tell me if it's crossed."

She could not have told him that there was a cord. She opened her eyes. It lay across the two fingertips, over and then under, as plain as print, and she felt nothing of it whatever.

Aske took it away. He dried her fingers himself, one at a time, on the towel.

"Then I'll tell you," he said. "I said that I should, when I'd a week to do it with. They've come back as far as they're coming. They'll bend. They'll grip. They'll tell you when something's pushing them and when it's stopped, which is a good deal more than nothing, and you're to use it. They'll not tell you edge from flat, or hot from cold, or one thread from two. That's gone."

"For good."

"I've never seen it come back from there. I looked for a case. I went up the hill on Thursday and asked the Order's infirmarian, since they'd been civil enough to offer, and she was civil enough to see me." He folded the towel. "She says that their year's rest is for a hand that's hungry. It does nothing for one that's been emptied. She says that she's sorry, and that she thought I'd want it plainly."

"So the door on the hill—"

"Was for before. It was a real door. You chose not to go through it, in the seventh month, with your eyes open, and you told your aunt why. She told me." He looked at her. "I don't say that you chose wrong. There's a girl with her leg in a board this morning who'd say otherwise, and she's no friend of yours. I say that it was a choice, and it had a price, and this is the price. I've had patients who wanted me to tell them it was bad luck."

"It wasn't luck."

"No." He put the pin away. "There's two drams of ward-trace sealed at the ash office for your team, against need. I'll save you the asking. It's no use for this. Keep it for somebody whose hand's only starved."

She looked at the two fingers.

They lay beside the others, a little straighter. They were not white. They were the same color as the rest of her. Nobody who saw that hand on a table would know that there was anything the matter with it, unless they had sat near it in a room and knew how the old wall-holders carried theirs.

No rank would change it. She had never thought that one would, and she found that she had wanted to be told so all the same.

"What do I do?"

"What your aunt did. What that catcher of Sorn's has done for six-and-twenty years." Aske stood. "You find out which of the other eight will do each thing, and you ask somebody for the rest. I'm told that you've been practicing."

Pike had them throw the sheet in the Graywater hall yard that evening until he had stopped wincing, and it took most of the evening.

It was not a shift. Rhea Sorn had sent a boy down to say: the yard, at six, no van. They had come as they were, the four who had stood the Regional and Pru, with the purse sheet on Perrin's handcart, and found a square grey man under a hooded lamp with his hands behind him.

"Up," said Pike. "Again. Rowan, you're setting that left on the hem as though it could tell you something."

"It can tell me there's a hem."

"Then use it for that and nothing else. Heel of the hand. Last two fingers hooked. The first two straight out, clear of it; they're passengers, and passengers don't hold the rail." He walked round. "You had that grip for a ward, I'm told. From the first-year. It's a catcher's grip, whoever taught it. Up."

She threw it again. The canvas lay flat. She brought the neck in at her elbow, slower than she used to.

"Now the cord," said Pike. "All three turns. By yourself."

"I can't feel the cross."

"I know that you can't. Do it."

She did it. She made the first turn, and the second, and at the third she had the cord over her two fingers and no way on earth of knowing which side of itself it lay.

"Cord," she said. "Pru. Yours. All of it."

"Mine," said Pru, and had it, and crossed it right.

"There," said Pike. He had stopped beside her. "That's the grip. That's all of it. It isn't in your hand. It's that word, said before you've fumbled and not after." He held out his own scarred right palm, with the groove worn across it and the one finger that had been a passenger since he was thirty. "I've caught six-and-twenty years on this. I've been thought a great hand by myself. I never sealed a neck in my life that somebody wasn't kneeling at the other hem to take it if I slipped. I only didn't say so." He closed the hand. "You'll have to say so. Every time. You'll hate it for about a year."

"And then?"

"Then you'll be a catcher. You'll have noticed that there's no other kind."

They went on. On the last throw he walked the whole way round and said nothing.

There were people on the hall steps who had come out to watch. One of them came down into the yard when the sheet was rolled. She had a broken nose, and her tunic was buttoned to the neck.

"Well," said Greta Stroud.

"Miss Stroud."

"I asked you a thing on a stair. In the sixth month. You said you'd tell me when you knew."

Kiva had not forgotten. She had thought about it, on and off, for three months, and she had never once got an answer ready, since every one that she tried had a shine on it.

"What the dram was for," she said. "I think it was so that I could feel I'd paid. For your hands. It didn't pay for anything. Your company had it covered." She looked at the linen, which she was still carrying in her pocket, out of habit. "This isn't for your hands either. I wish it was. It was for a leg, and it was mine to spend. I only know now what it's like when a thing's actually paid, and it wasn't that."

Stroud looked at her for some time.

"Mm," she said. "That's two things out of you I've half liked. You're getting to be expensive." She jerked her chin. "Your cuff's undone."

It was. Kiva had dressed that morning with eight fingers, and the left cuff had beaten her. A button wanted an edge found, and a hole, and one thread told from two.

"Give it here," said Stroud.

She did up the button. She did it quickly and without gentleness, with two hands that had been six weeks in linen, as one does up a child's.

"I'd somebody do mine till the seventh month," she said. "My coat, and my boots, and a spoon held for me the first fortnight. I've never hated anything so much. I'd have bitten the girl if I'd had the use of my teeth." She let go of the wrist. "Let them. That's all I've got. It's not advice. It's only that I was no good at it, and it took me a month longer than it need have."

She went back up the steps.

Anwen was folding the last of the cord. She had watched all that from the handcart, and she waited until the yard was nearly empty.

"I saw Mace on Monday," she said. "After the board was read. He'd told me to."

"I know. You said he would."

"I haven't told my mother yet. I haven't told anybody." Anwen put the cord down. She looked, for a moment, exactly seventeen. "He's got a relief's place on the night van's line for next summer, a whole season of it, and it's mine. Not Stroud's seat. One of my own. He said he'd watched a first-year hold a Flask's face from the fall of that hold until the tear was shut, the best part of forty minutes, with three students beside her. He said it dipped once, at the south end, by a hand, and came up."

"It did. I saw it dip."

"You weren't to be watching."

"Finch told me that as well."

Anwen laughed. It was the first time that Kiva had heard that laugh since a kitchen table in the spring, and she had not known until then how much she had been listening for it.

"I wanted to tell you first," said Anwen. "I've been wanting to since Monday. I found that I could. So that's a thing that's changed." Her face was level again, and very clear. "I'm done minding. I said in the yard that I wasn't, and I wasn't. You asked me on that floor. I asked you what it was for and what it cost, and you told me, with a thing on your shoulder that was going to take two fingers off you, and then you asked me for the gate. I'm done." She took up the cord again. "I've not forgotten. I looked, and those aren't the same."

"No."

"You're not to say anything handsome."

"I haven't got anything."

"Good." She held out the folded sheet, and Kiva took the other end of it, with her right hand and the heel of her left, and they walked it in.

---

Jude Talley came to the Rowan kitchen on Sunday for his share, and stood by the dresser with his cap in his hand, as though the share might change its mind if he sat.

"Four-and-twenty from the Pit," said Kellan. He had worked it out on the tram and did not need to look at anything. "Clean. Six to you, by name, as Kill. Eighteen to Seven. The reserve we held back for the day wasn't drawn, so it goes back to held. So does what's left on Perrin's line, half a dram and a hundredth."

"Six drams," said Jude. He said it carefully, as a man says a figure that he has not expected to be let keep. "And my name on a yield line. I'll have that before a draft board in the spring." He looked round the table. "I didn't cut your rung clean. It's in a cold room with a red tag on it."

"You cut a post," said Perrin. "On a word. I had my back to it, and I'm told it was a full second and very ugly."

"It was a second and a tenth."

"Then you've earned the tenth."

Kellan had come in his own coat. Birch had come up to the grading room on Friday to tell him himself: released from his entry, the Regional having been struck, with the Works' thanks and his stipend to the seventeenth. *The Works would expect nothing else of you,* Birch had said, and had said it as though it hurt.

"They want the jacket back," said Kellan. "I've had the cord sewn onto the cuff again first. Birch has asked whether I'd take the yard for the winter. Tuesdays and Thursdays, a contained string, nothing owed." He was looking at the table. "I haven't answered. I'm thinking it over. I learned more in that yard in ten weeks than in two years of being excellent by myself. I also turned my back on it in the middle of the Pit."

"What did your mother say?" said Anwen.

"That I'd put my name to what the reed said." He nearly smiled. "She says that it's a narrow sort of safety and she's never found another."

"I'd take the yard," said Kiva.

They all looked at her.

"You asked what I'd do. No. You didn't. I'm sorry." She had heard it as it left her. "I had that ready. I've no idea whether you should."

"That's better," said Anwen.

"And nobody's to ask me for six-tenths of anything until the spring," said Perrin. "I've a plate to lift on Thursday. I'm told I'm passed over my head."

---

Mara took her across to the Ferren pump house on Monday, the twenty-sixth, at four, and made her stand in the doorway.

"You're not to come past that sill. It's a bank of four-and-twenty, charged, and you've a certificate that says three paces. I only want you to see what I've been looking at for a week."

It was a long brick room, very clean, that smelled of oil. The three pumps stood at the far end, turning, as they had turned for three hundred years, with a sound like slow breathing. Along the left wall ran the bank. Four-and-twenty cells in a row, each with its drum, each with a needle under glass.

Every needle in the row was nodding. They went up together, and came down together, like a line of people breathing in a church.

Kiva had seen twelve needles do that once, on a load board, for two minutes.

"Since the seventeenth," said Mara. She had gone down the row and laid her left palm on a drum near the middle. "It fell in at twenty past two that day. The keeper says it hasn't missed a beat since. The pumps haven't dropped a stroke; I'll say that first, since it's worth more than the rest. The low streets are dry."

"Then what's wrong with it?"

"Nothing, yet. That's what's wrong with it." Mara took her hand away. "A bank that keeps one time is one engine. That's very fine in a refinery, where there's an operator with his hand on it and a spare bank behind. This is a municipal bank. It was built so that a cell could fail and its neighbors never know. Now if one drifts, all four-and-twenty drift. If one's fouled, I don't know what the other three-and-twenty do." She came back up the room. "Rusk has had the best of the Municipal Works in here since Sunday week. He's had a man from Halden's technical office, who was very civil and of no use at all. They can't take it out. Nobody's ever taken one out. They can quench the lot and fill them fresh, and that's four hours with no pumps, in a wet autumn."

"And they won't."

"They'll have to. But that isn't what I brought you to see."

She stood in the doorway beside Kiva and looked out, up the Brickfields road, toward the hill.

"I've been walking the Brickfields trunk with my hand," she said. "Every drum on it, from this door to the Compact Quarter. The drum by the tram sheds went on Wednesday. I've had my palm on it every week for six years, and on Wednesday it stopped being itself and started being a beat. The one by the Corn Exchange went on Friday. The one at the foot of Hanley Street, last night." She did not raise her voice. "It's walking. A drum every two days, near enough. It isn't in the copper; they've had links open the whole length. It goes all the same."

"Where does the trunk end?"

Mara looked at her.

Kiva knew before she answered. She had sat beside that drum for six months, on a bench by a cart-shed door.

"The core house," said Mara. "At the Crooked Line School." She was quiet a moment. "Eighteen days, if it keeps its time. I'd not wager that anything will, this year."

Somewhere over the roofs, toward the Sag, a bell had begun to ring. It rang two strokes and a pause, and two again. As they listened another took it up, farther off, three and three; and then a third, from the river, on a code that Kiva did not know.

"That's mast five," said Mara. "And the weigh-house. And I think that's the weir." She turned up her collar. "The Home Line's stopped holding tears for licensing, as of Saturday. They've not said why."

"They wouldn't. Lowen says they're not saying anybody showed them."

"No. I shouldn't, either, if I were they."

Kiva put her right hand flat on the brick of the doorpost.

She could feel it. It came up out of the floor of the pump house, through the sill, slow and even. It was a bag on an iron bridge at four in the morning, and a white cord across a girl's leg.

She put her left hand beside it.

The palm felt it, and the heel, and the last two fingers. The first two lay on the brick. They told her that there was a wall.

"I don't know what it is," she said.

"No. Nor do I, and I've a license." Mara looked down the long row of nodding needles. "I told a watch officer in the summer that every device in my house had a meter and a way to shut it down. I've found the meter for this. I've had my hand on it every day since the seventeenth." She stepped back from the sill. "I've not found the other."

They walked back along the Brickfields road together, toward the tram. Mara walked on her left, without being asked, where a hand that could not tell one thread from two would not have to find the curb by itself.

The bells were still ringing when the tram came.
