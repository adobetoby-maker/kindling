# Chapter 40 — Kiva: Hand as Before

Hollis Vane read his slate at five, standing on the cinders, since there were people in that Pit whose floor it had been.

The terraces had not emptied. Eleven hundred people had been told twice by the stewards that the Regional Measure was struck and that they might go home, and perhaps a third of them had gone. The rest sat on in the low sun, quietly, like people at the end of a long service who are not sure that it is over.

The bags stood in two rows along the tinning table. One row had white tags. The other had red.

"*The Pit, Saturday the 17th,*" said Vane. He had his outdoor voice, flat and whole. "*The Call, opened at two. A tear on the open floor, outside the lattice, held by four husks, at twenty past. Struck, twenty-one past. Closed, ten past three, by the Home Line.*"

He turned the slate a little to the light.

"*Lives: none lost. Hurt: Dray, blade, the left leg above the knee, the bone, and a burn of cold. Rowan, catcher, the left hand; see the direction. Works: the race dropped an hour and ten minutes by the keeper. The Row quenched, twelve boxes, by the keeper; none fouled. The Ferren bank in step since twenty past two and not come out; that is the Municipal Works' to enter, and I have sent to tell them so.*"

There was a man in a black coat standing by the load board with his hat in his hand. It was Halden's glass that had found it. He inclined his head.

"*Tinned, clean: five hundred and twelve drams. Put by, red, on catchers' words and a comb: five hundred and three and seven-tenths. And a ring of something that is not in the yield book, swept under the Chair's seal, which nobody here has a name for.*" He turned the slate over. "*Remarks: one. A blade cut a husk inside her step, on her catcher's word, before the foreman had read the floor. The foreman had not called a stand. The safety officer called one to that team as the catcher's hand fell, too late to govern. The remark is the event's.*"

"That's not right," said a voice from the cart.

They had not yet taken Selka Dray away. She had refused to go until it was read.

"It was my cut. I'll have it under my name."

"You'll have the cut under your name, Miss Dray. It's in the kill line, with the others, and it was as clean as any I saw today." Vane did not look round. "The remark is for a thing done wrong. You did what a second-step blade is taught to do with a Handful that has its back to her. A Torch keeping a floor safe may stand any student on it, and Sorn did, and it reached you with your foot already going down. The floor was wrong. I'd a company on it and I hadn't read it. That's whose it is."

Dray said nothing. After a moment the thin boy on the cart's step put his hand on the edge of the stretcher, and she let him.

"Now the pot," said Vane. "It was struck. A struck floor's shared the Line's way, and I'll say what that is, since three of you have a house's paper and will want it said. It isn't shared by who was kneeling where. Everything clean goes in one, and it's divided by the standing rule." He read the figures off the back of his hand. "The civic half, two hundred and fifty-six. The casualty reserve, sixty-four. The schools, ninety-six. The teams, ninety-six. That's four-and-twenty to a team. Halden's four tinned more of it than anybody. They'll draw the same as Seven, who tinned a rung that's wearing a red tag."

"The Halden Works does not object," said Mr. Adlam.

"I'd have been surprised," said Birch, beside him, rather sourly. "It was my four tinned it."

"And the red row?" That was Jude. He had Seven's own bag at his feet. *Stepped. On a catcher's word and a comb. K. Rowan.*

"The red row goes where the last red bag went. To a cold room, under three seals, with every catcher's name on it. There's a rule, as of the third of the seventh month. I was glad of it today." Vane hung the slate on his belt. "That's a response. It's on the district table by morning under the Home Line's name. I'd sooner it had been a Regional."

He came across to the table afterward.

Kiva was still sitting with her back to it. Aske had bound the hand in dry linen, loosely, more to keep it from being knocked than for any good that it would do, and it lay in her lap like a parcel.

"I've a ledger to write tonight," said Vane. "The back of it. I'll put the direction as I read it. Under *Result* I'll put that it was taken off her, and carried, and held for ten, and given back, and that the hold came down." He looked at the linen. "There's the third column. I filled it before. I'd like to fill it after as well, when your healer knows. E.K. did. He went back in the autumn and wrote two words."

"*Hand as before,*" said Kiva.

"I'll come and ask you, when it's time. I'll not put what I've only been told."

Rhea Sorn was sitting on an upturned tub at the end of the table.

She had cut five rungs since ten past three, each longer than the last, and she looked it. She had her elbows on her knees and her hands hanging. She did not get up.

"I told you on a wharf to go on saying that you couldn't weigh it," she said. "You said it again today. I heard you. *It's only what comes through a neck.* Then you wrote a tag on it."

"Pike put the pencil down."

"Pike's no fool." The pale eyes came up. "I'll tell you what I saw that I'd not seen. You stood on this floor and said what it would cost before you'd done it. You had a healer say it, and a foreman write it, and you said you were frightened. I've been twenty-three years at this. I have never once named the price first. I cut, and I count after, and I'm thought very honest for it."

Kiva did not know what to say to that.

"I'm not offering you anything," said Rhea. "You're seventeen, with a hand in a cloth. Seven's still mine. The board will want a Flask stood on my own van before it takes the brackets off, and it's right to. I only wanted it said by somebody who'd know." She looked at her own hands. "I burned that card. The one with two words on it about you."

"You said you would."

"I did it the same night. It's the only thing I did that week that I'd do again without thinking."

Lowen Brask came by with a canvas roll on his shoulder. He stopped, and nodded at the linen.

"Did it come easy?"

"No."

"Good." He nearly smiled. "Look at your feet anyway. I'm told Vane wants me on the Line's roll in the spring. A hold crew, for wages. I said I'd need to think, and then I went and stood by that ring for a quarter of an hour, and I found that I'd thought." He shifted the roll. "They'll not be standing holds for a while, I shouldn't wonder. Somebody's been watching how it's done."

"You said nobody showed them."

"I said I wasn't saying anybody did." He went on toward the west gate. "That's not the same. I had that off a catcher who's grown particular about guesses."

Noa came last, down the keeper's stair, with the tape rolled against his chest in both arms.

He did not unroll it. He sat down on the cinders in front of her, cross-legged, like a boy at a lesson.

"Two cliffs," he said. "Twenty-six, and twenty-six. They're a little under two seconds apart. There's nothing between them but two hundredths for the bound. I held the white up before you'd landed. I didn't need the glass."

"And after?"

"You know what's after. You saw the lamps." His narrow face was grey with tiredness. "It's on all sixteen. Every pen on the drum. A hair, at one instant, eleven seconds on from the second cliff. That's the instant you said *back.* Three of those pens were blades cutting a post. Four were wards bringing up a gate. Two were running with a casualty, and one was the casualty. Five were standing dark against a wall. One was you, with everything just shut. It's the same hair on every one, lit and dark alike." He swallowed. "Adlam saw it. He wasn't looking at his own glass. He was looking over my shoulder, and he said, *There,* before I did."

"What does he want?"

"Nothing. That's the thing about him." Noa rubbed his eyes. "He's written to the Chair. He showed me. It's four lines, and it's under his own name and not Halden's. It says that he was present and saw sixteen pens move together, on hands lit and dark alike, and that he knows of no fault in a mast that would do it. He says that he'd like to be second, when I've something to print."

"Have you?"

"I've a page with no heading. I've had it since the sixth month." He looked at her. "You've been asked what it is all day. What do you call it?"

"I can't weigh it."

Noa took his pencil from behind his ear. He unrolled a hand's breadth of the tape's blank margin against his knee, and wrote three words on it, small and square.

*Not yet weighed.*

"That'll do for a heading," he said. "It's the first true one I've had."

---

There was no dream that night.

She had expected one. She had lain down at nine, in the narrow bed, with a basin on the chair and the hand propped on a folded towel, and Mara had sat in the doorway with the pencil and the single sheet, as the household rule required. They had both waited. The fever did not come. The lights did not come by any road.

At eleven Mara put the sheet away, with nothing on it but the date.

"*Nothing. No fever,*" she said. "I've written that. I'd as soon have it on the page that there was a night when you did the largest thing you've ever done and it had nothing to say about it."

"The plate's cold too."

"So I'd supposed. You'd have told me." Mara did not go. She sat with her back against the doorpost and her knees drawn up, which she had not done since Kiva was small. "I didn't come down."

"I saw."

"I got as far as standing. I'd my hand on the seat in front. I'd said that I wouldn't, and I thought, if I go to that rail now she'll look at me and not at the foreman, and it won't be hers any longer." Her voice was quite level. "I told you once that I wanted you safe without owning what you chose. I'd no notion how much it would cost to mean it. I'd like you to know that I minded. I shan't say it twice."

"I looked up. Before I said yes."

"I know you did. I'd my hand on my coat like a woman who's lost her purse." She got up, stiffly. "A man was saying something about the Ferren bank, by the load board, as they carried you past. I heard two words of it. I've the trunk books in the shop, and Rusk's leave to read them. I shall go across to that pump house on Monday and put my hand on cell nine."

"What did you hear?"

"*In step.* And *not come out.*" Mara turned down the lamp. "Go to sleep. It isn't yours. It's the Municipal Works', and for once I'm glad to be the one of us who's licensed."

The wrist came back on Sunday, toward evening, like a tide coming up a slip. The palm came on Monday morning, and the thumb with it, and the last two fingers. By the time she had her boots on she could feel the lace in them.

She could see the first two. She laced round them.

---

The board in the Measure Hall basement had the result on it by ten on Monday, in a clerk's hand, and there was a crowd.

*The Regional Measure, 2680. Read on three boards; the Call struck.*

*1. Halden License. 2. Old Four (att.). 3. Team Twenty-Eight. 4. T7 (att.).*

"Fourth," said Perrin. He read it with his lips. "Of sixteen. With brackets." He considered. "I've decided I don't mind the brackets. I'd got so used to them. It'd be like losing a tooth."

"There's a line under it," said Kellan.

There was. It was in a different hand, level and upright, and people were reading it over one another's shoulders as they had once read three sealed papers.

*Entered by the Chair, for the Circuit's record. On the floor of the Pit, on the 17th, under a recorded field direction, after the event was struck: three doors opened in sequence, one after another, nothing open between. Declared. Witnessed by this office and by eleven hundred others. Read on two instruments. The candidate is not thereby classified. She is not thereby certified. It is not to be asked of her. It is written here because it occurred. Y. Crest.*

Kiva read it once.

She was aware of the people round her. Nobody moved their kit away. A first-year whom she did not know looked at the linen on her hand, and then at her face, and then, rather quickly, at the board again.

"*Because it occurred,*" said Kellan, under his breath. "That's the best reason I ever saw anybody give for anything."

She was sent for at eleven.

Ysra Crest had three papers on her desk and her hands folded on them. Orla was in the chair by the door, with her stick. She had brought a paper of her own, and it lay on the corner of the desk, unsigned for by anybody yet.

"Sit," said Ysra. "You may put that hand where it's easiest. I've a number of things to say, and none of them is a ruling."

Kiva sat.

"In 2676 a clerk of this office wrote *Classification deferred* against a girl of thirteen, and under it, since something had to go there, *There isn't a column for this.* The sheet came to me, and I let it stand." The long plain face did not alter. "I have let it stand every spring for four years. I told myself that it was caution. Some of it was. A good deal of it was that I had once signed a boy's entry to a Bench because his sponsor was certain, and I had sooner have no column than a wrong one."

"I know about the Bench," said Kiva.

"I imagine that you do. Orla tells a story to its end, these days." Ysra unfolded her hands. "I still have no column. I'll not pretend otherwise to oblige anybody. I can't rank you. I don't know what your Hold is, or your Draw, with three things that take turns. I don't know what you are, and you are not to take a line on a notice board for my saying that I do."

"No, ma'am."

"But I've been using *no column* as a reason to write nothing, and that was wrong, and I've been shown that it was. By a catcher's tag, and a night clerk called R., whose margin Mr. Bexley copied into the note that I have on file, and a watch that put *cost* back into a ledger." She laid her finger on the first of the three papers. "So I've opened a page. It isn't a class. It has a heading, which is *Witnessed,* and it has ruled lines, and what goes on them is what was done, where, on whose direction, what it cost, and who read the tape. It has one entry. I'll not have a second written on it by anybody who wasn't there."

"What does it change?"

"Your place? Nothing. You're provisional, Third Division, on a sponsor's line, with the conditions that you had on Friday. I've added none and I've taken none away." She almost smiled. "It changes what the next Chair finds in the drawer. I'm told that's what a record is for."

She touched the second paper.

"Miss Dray has written to me from an infirmary bed, in pencil. She asks that the mast condition stand for any candidate ever seated with more than one door. She says that it worked, and that she objected to you twice and would again. I've granted it. It's her rule now; I've put her name on it." The third paper. "And a Mr. Adlam has written, under his own hand, about sixteen pens. I've filed that beside Mr. Bexley's. I have not the least idea what to do with either."

"Nor has Noa."

"Then we are three." She turned her eyes to the door. "Orla. You've brought me something."

"My account," said Orla. She did not get up. "As her sponsor. What I'd seen and what I hadn't, what I said on that rail, and that I said I didn't know it would work. And what it cost her. If a hand's the price of a thing my candidate does, I sign for the hand. I didn't, the last time. A board sealed it for me and I let them."

"I was on that board."

"I know you were."

They looked at one another, the Chair and the Torch, across twenty years and a girl's bandaged hand. Kiva kept very still.

"I'll countersign it," said Ysra. "Not as Chair. As the ward-holder who put her name to his entry. It ought to have two of ours on it." She drew the paper toward her, and took up her pen, and stopped. "You wrote to him. I heard that you had."

"In the seventh month. Four lines."

"Has he answered?"

"No," said Orla. "I didn't write it to be answered. I wrote it so that it would have been written."

---

She went up the stair that creaked on Tuesday, since it was within the week.

It was the Ferren who witnessed her. Kiva had not met the old woman before. She sat at the oak table with her river pilot's squint, and watched a girl of seventeen take up a steel pen in her right hand and hold a page flat with the heel of her left.

*Cold. Saturday the 17th, at the Pit, about half past two in the afternoon. After three doors in sequence, under direction, with a hold's hem carried and planted for ten counts. Cold before, during and after.*

*I did not ask it anything. It did not do anything. K.R.*

"You've a tidy hand for somebody writing round two fingers," said the Ferren.

"It's the other hand. I only hold the paper with this one."

"So I see." The old eyes went down the column, past *Cold,* and *Cold,* and *Cold,* to the one long entry at the head of it, and back. "I sat four-and-thirty years in this chair hearing *nothing* read. I laid my fist down against recalling it in the summer, and I'd do so again. But I'll tell you what I told the Sag. A plate that does something is not the plate we've been sitting over." She blotted the line herself. "I'd begun to wonder whether we'd all been asking it the wrong thing. *What does it do.* It's on the first leaf, in print. I've never thought to ask what else was happening when it did."

Kiva looked at her.

"Don't mind me. I'm seventy, and I've no business having notions." The Ferren closed the canvas book. "Go down by the left of the stair. The third step's been loose since Corran's day. She used to jump it."

---

Healer Aske gave it the week that he had promised, and on Saturday he took the linen off for the last time.

He had the towel, and the pin. He had also a penny, and a cup of water from the kettle, and a scrap of the waxed cord that catchers used.

"Eyes shut."

She shut them.

She felt the pin on her little finger, and the third, and said so. She felt it on her thumb. She felt it, sharp and at once, in the middle of her palm, and at the heel of her hand, and on the wrist.

Then there was a long time when nothing happened.

"I've had the pin in the first finger for a slow ten," said Aske's voice. "I'm pressing rather hard. I'd be bleeding you if I pressed harder."

"Something's pushing."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

"And here?"

"The same. Less."

He put the penny between her two fingers and her thumb. She could feel it against the thumb. Against the fingers it was only that there was something there, as one knows through a boot that one is standing on a stone.

"Edge or flat?"

"I don't know."

"The cup. Is that hot?"

She had two fingers in water. She knew that from the sound.

"I can't tell."

"The cord. I'm laying it over them. Tell me if it's crossed."

She could not have told him that there was a cord. She opened her eyes. It lay across the two fingertips, over and then under, as plain as print, and she felt nothing of it whatever.

Aske took it away. He dried her fingers himself, one at a time, on the towel, and capped his pen before he had written anything.

"Then I'll tell you," he said. "I said that I should, when I'd a week to do it with. They've come back as far as they're coming. They'll bend. They'll grip. They'll tell you when something's pushing them and when it's stopped, which is a good deal more than nothing, and you're to use it. They'll not tell you edge from flat, or hot from cold, or one thread from two. That's gone."

"For good."

"I've never seen it come back from there. I looked for a case. I wrote to the Order's infirmarian, since they'd been civil enough to offer, and she was civil enough to answer." He folded the towel. "She says that their year's rest is for a hand that's hungry. It does nothing for one that's been emptied. She says that she's sorry, and that she thought I'd want it plainly."

"So the door on the hill—"

"Was for before. It was a real door. You chose not to go through it, in the seventh month, with your eyes open, and you told your aunt why. I've seen the line; she showed me." He looked at her over the card. "I don't say that you chose wrong. There's a girl with her leg in a board this morning who'd say otherwise, and she's no friend of yours. I say that it was a choice, and it had a price, and this is the price. I've had patients who wanted me to tell them it was bad luck."

"It wasn't luck."

"No." He wrote at last. It was a short line. "There's two drams of ward-trace sealed at the ash office with your team's name on it. I'll save you the asking. It's no use for this. Keep it for somebody whose hand's only starved."

She looked at the two fingers.

They lay beside the others, a little straighter. They were not white. They were the same color as the rest of her. Nobody who saw that hand on a table would know that there was anything the matter with it, unless they had sat near it in a room and knew how the old wall-holders carried theirs.

No rank would change it. She had never thought that one would, and she found that she had wanted to be told so all the same.

"What do I do?"

"What your aunt did. What that catcher of Sorn's has done for six-and-twenty years." Aske handed her the card. It had six figures in a column, and under them a line ruled across. "You find out which of the other eight will do each thing, and you ask somebody for the rest. I'm told that you've been practicing."

Pike had them throw the sheet in the Graywater hall yard that evening until he had stopped wincing, and it took eleven times.

It was not a shift. Rhea Sorn had sent a boy down with a card, four words long. *Yard. Six. No van.* They had come as they were, the four who had stood the Regional and Pru, with the purse sheet on Perrin's handcart, and found a square grey man under a hooded lamp with his hands behind him.

"Up," said Pike. "Again. Rowan, you're setting that left on the hem as though it could tell you something."

"It can tell me there's a hem."

"Then use it for that and nothing else. Heel of the hand. Last two fingers hooked. The first two straight out, clear of it; they're passengers, and passengers don't hold the rail." He walked round. "You had that grip for a ward, I'm told. From the first-year. It's a catcher's grip, whoever taught it. Up."

She threw it again. The canvas lay flat. She brought the neck in at her elbow, slower by a count, as she had since the summer.

"Now the cord," said Pike. "All three turns. By yourself."

"I can't feel the cross."

"I know that you can't. Do it."

She did it. She made the first turn, and the second, and at the third she had the cord over her two fingers and no way on earth of knowing which side of itself it lay.

"Cord," she said. "Pru. Yours. All of it."

"Mine," said Pru, and had it, and crossed it right.

"There," said Pike. He had stopped beside her. "That's the grip. That's all of it. It isn't in your hand. It's that word, said before you've fumbled and not after." He held out his own scarred right palm, with the groove worn across it and the one finger that had been a passenger since he was thirty. "I've caught six-and-twenty years on this. I've been thought a great hand by myself. I never sealed a neck in my life that somebody wasn't kneeling at the other hem to take it if I slipped. I only didn't say so." He closed the hand. "You'll have to say so. Every time. You'll hate it for about a year."

"And then?"

"Then you'll be a catcher. You'll have noticed that there's no other kind."

They went on. On the eleventh throw he walked the whole way round and said nothing.

There were people on the hall steps who had come out to watch. One of them came down into the yard when the sheet was rolled. She had a broken nose, and her tunic was buttoned to the neck.

"Well," said Greta Stroud.

"Miss Stroud."

"I asked you a thing on a stair. In the sixth month. You said you'd tell me when you knew."

Kiva had not forgotten. She had thought about it, on and off, for three months, and she had never once got an answer ready, since every one that she tried had a shine on it.

"What the dram was for," she said. "I think it was so that I could feel I'd paid. For your hands. It didn't pay for anything. Your company had it covered." She looked at the linen, which she was still carrying in her pocket, out of habit. "This isn't for your hands either. I'd like to say it was. It was for a leg, and it was mine to spend. I only know now what it's like when a thing's actually paid, and it wasn't that."

Stroud looked at her for some time.

"Mm," she said. "That's two things out of you I've half liked. You're getting to be expensive." She jerked her chin. "Your cuff's undone."

It was. Kiva had dressed that morning with eight fingers, and the left cuff had beaten her. A button wanted an edge found, and a hole, and one thread told from two.

"Give it here," said Stroud.

She did up the button. She did it quickly and without gentleness, with two hands that had been six weeks in linen, as one does up a child's.

"I'd somebody do mine till the seventh month," she said. "My coat, and my boots, and a spoon held for me the first fortnight. I've never hated anything so much. I'd have bitten the girl if I'd had the use of my teeth." She let go of the wrist. "Let them. That's all I've got. It's not advice. It's only that I was no good at it, and it took me a month longer than it need have."

She went back up the steps.

Anwen was folding the last of the cord. She had watched all that from the handcart, and she waited until the yard was nearly empty.

"I saw Mace on Monday," she said. "After the board was read. He'd told me to."

"I know. You said he would."

"I haven't told my mother yet. I haven't told anybody." Anwen put the cord down. She looked, for a moment, exactly seventeen. "He opened the drawer. He's got a relief's place on the night van's line for next summer, a whole season of it, and he's put my name on it. Not Stroud's seat. One of my own. He said he'd watched a first-year hold a Flask's face from the fall of that hold until the tear was shut, the best part of forty minutes, with three students beside her. He said it dipped once, at the south end, by a hand, and came up."

"It did. I saw it dip."

"You weren't to be watching."

"Finch told me that as well."

Anwen laughed. It was the first time that Kiva had heard that laugh since a kitchen table in the spring, and she had not known until then how much she had been listening for it.

"I wanted to tell you first," said Anwen. "I've been wanting to since Monday. I found that I could. So that's a thing that's changed." Her face was level again, and very clear. "I'm done minding. I said in the yard that I wasn't, and I wasn't. You asked me on that floor. You said *Pryce, the gate, on back,* with a thing on your shoulder that was going to cost you two fingers, and you still asked. I'm done." She took up the cord again. "I've not forgotten. I looked, and those aren't the same."

"No."

"You're not to say anything handsome."

"I haven't got anything."

"Good." She held out the folded sheet, and Kiva took the other end of it, with her right hand and the heel of her left, and they walked it in.

The four of them signed the book on Sunday, at the Rowan kitchen table, and there were five in the room.

Jude Talley had come for his line. He stood by the dresser with his cap in his hand while Kellan read it out.

"*Credited from the Pit, the 17th, by the foreman's pot: 24.0. Clean; W, full; I, a trace. To Talley, J., Kill, by name, a head's share: 6.0. To the book: 18.0.*" Kellan turned the page. "*Regional reserve, not drawn, returned to held. Three-door line, fifty-one hundredths not spent, returned to held. Held: 5.62. Sealed, medical, W: 2.0. Not opened.*"

"Six drams," said Jude. He said it carefully, as a man says a figure that he has not expected to be let keep. "With my name on a yield line. I'll have that before a draft board in the spring." He looked round the table. "I didn't cut your rung clean. It's in a cold room with a red tag on it."

"You cut a post," said Perrin. "On a word. I had my back to it, and I'm told it was a full second and very ugly."

"It was a second and a tenth."

"Then you've earned the tenth."

Kellan had come in his own coat. He had a letter in the pocket of it, on good paper, and he had read it to them before the book was opened. *The Halden Works releases Mr. Renn from his entry, the Measure having been struck, with its thanks, and encloses his stipend to the 17th. It would expect nothing else of him.*

"They want the jacket back," he said. "I've had the cord sewn onto the cuff again first. Birch has asked whether I'd take the yard for the winter. Tuesdays and Thursdays, a contained string, nothing owed." He was looking at the green cover. "I haven't answered. I'm doing the sum. I learned more in that yard in eleven weeks than in two years of being excellent by myself. I also turned my back on it in front of eleven hundred people."

"What did your mother say?"

"That I'd sealed what the reed said." He nearly smiled. "She says that it's a narrow sort of safety and she's never found another."

"I'd take the yard," said Kiva.

They all looked at her.

"You asked what I'd do. No. You didn't. I'm sorry." She had heard it as it left her. "I had that ready. I've no idea whether you should."

"That's better," said Anwen.

"It's on the list," said Perrin. "Under things I don't mind." He signed, and pushed the book along. "I'd like it put that nobody's to ask me for six-tenths of anything until the spring. I've a plate to lift on Thursday. I'm told I'm passed over my head."

Kiva signed last.

She held the page with the heel of her hand. Her name came out as it always had, square and plain. There was nothing in it to show that anything was different.

---

Mara took her across to the Ferren pump house on Monday, the twenty-sixth, at four, and made her stand in the doorway.

"You're not to come past that sill. It's a bank of four-and-twenty, charged, and you've a certificate that says three paces. I only want you to see what I've been looking at for a week."

It was a long brick room, very clean, that smelled of oil. The three pumps stood at the far end, turning, as they had turned for three hundred years, with a sound like slow breathing. Along the left wall ran the bank. Four-and-twenty cells in a row, each with its drum, each with a needle under glass.

Every needle in the row was nodding. They went up together, and came down together, like a line of people breathing in a church.

Kiva had seen twelve needles do that once, on a load board, for two minutes.

"That's nine days," said Mara. She had gone down the row to the ninth, and laid her left palm on its drum. "It fell in at twenty past two on the seventeenth. I've the keeper's book. It hasn't missed a beat since. The pumps haven't dropped a stroke; I'll say that first, since it's worth more than the rest. The low streets are dry."

"Then what's wrong with it?"

"Nothing, yet. That's what's wrong with it." Mara took her hand away. "A bank that keeps one time is one engine. That's very fine in a refinery, where there's an operator with his hand on it and a spare bank behind. This is a municipal bank. It was built so that a cell could fail and its neighbors never know. Now if one drifts, all four-and-twenty drift. If one's fouled, I don't know what the other three-and-twenty do." She came back up the room. "Rusk has had the best of the Municipal Works in here since Sunday week. He's had a man from Halden's technical office, who was very civil and of no use at all. They can't take it out. Nobody's ever taken one out. They can quench the lot and fill them fresh, and that's four hours with no pumps, in a wet autumn."

"And they won't."

"They'll have to. But that isn't what I brought you to see."

She had the trunk book under her arm. She opened it on the sill, between them, at a page ruled in her own hand.

"The Brickfields trunk. Every drum on it, from this door to the Compact Quarter. Nineteen went on Wednesday; I've its baseline for six years, and on Wednesday it stopped being a number and became a beat. Twenty went on Friday. Twenty-one last night." Her finger moved down the column. "It's walking. A drum every two days, near enough. It isn't in the copper; they've had links open the whole length. It goes all the same."

Kiva followed the finger.

There were nine more lines below it. At the foot of the page, in Mara's small exact print, was the name of the last drum on the trunk. She knew it. She had sat beside it for six months, on a bench by a cart-shed door.

*Core house, the Crooked Line School.*

"Eighteen days," said Mara. "If it keeps its time. I'd not wager that anything will, this year."

Somewhere over the roofs, toward the Sag, a bell had begun to ring. It rang two strokes and a pause, and two again. As they listened another took it up, farther off, three and three; and then a third, from the river, on a code that Kiva did not know.

"That's mast five," said Mara. "And the weigh-house. And I think that's the weir." She shut the book. "The Home Line posted a notice on Saturday. No tear's to be held for a licensing until further word. I read it twice. It didn't give a reason."

"It wouldn't. Lowen says they're not saying anybody showed them."

"No. I shouldn't, either, if I were they."

Kiva put her right hand flat on the brick of the doorpost.

She could feel it. It came up out of the floor of the pump house, through the sill, slow and even and many. It was the hum of the sheds. It was a bag on an iron bridge at four in the morning, and a white cord across a girl's leg.

She put her left hand beside it.

The palm felt it, and the heel, and the last two fingers. The first two lay on the brick. They told her that there was a wall.

"I can't weigh it," she said.

"No. Nor can I, and I've a license." Mara looked down the long row of nodding needles. "I told a watch officer in the summer that every device in my house had a meter and a way to shut it down. I've found the meter for this. I've been reading it for nine days." She tucked the book under her arm. "I've not found the other."

They walked back along the Brickfields road together, toward the tram.

Vane had come to the shop on Sunday, with his hat off, and had asked her what he should write in the third column, now that her healer knew. She had told him. He had written it standing at the counter, in the back of a ledger older than the school, under the direction that he had given her and the cost that he had put first.

*Cost, after: the first two fingers of the left hand, without fine feeling. Not returned.*

He had held the pen a moment longer. Then he had said that E.K. had gone back in the autumn, and added a line about a roll, and that he had no roll to add her to.

"I've one," Kiva had said.

She had it under her arm now. It was a canvas field book, soft at the corners, with a spring and a summer in it. She had written the line on Sunday night, at the kitchen table, where it would be read. She had put it on the right, in the column that asked for no reason at all, and Mara had laid the brass weight on the page without a word.

*Rowan, K. Catcher. Hand as before.*

The bells were still ringing when the tram came.
