# Chapter 20 — Kiva: Mine, Yours

Lowen Brask sat down on the away line and let Hendry have the water first.

He always did. A captain who drank first had a team that noticed. He had been taught that at thirteen by a woman with one eye who ran the junior lanes at the Hall, and it was one of the few things he knew for certain.

"Seven to four," said Keld. She was pleased, and trying not to show it. She had never been good at that. "And she's finished. Did you see it go? She can't stand on that."

"She can stand on it. She can't land on it." He looked up the lane. "Those are different."

The girl was sitting against the fourth post on the far side, with her leg out and a healer's boy kneeling over it, and she was looking at nothing at all, which he respected. Her captain stood over her with his arms folded, and the small one in the scarf was talking, and nobody was listening to the small one in the scarf.

He had moved a ladder. That was all.

He turned it over again, honestly, as he would have turned over a first-year's fall. He had read Rule Six on a tram a fortnight ago and laughed out loud, and an old man opposite had looked at him. A school that told you what it was going to do, the moment it was doing it. No wonder his Hall had been beating it for thirty years.

So he had planned for the call. If she called a change on the move, she would be coming at something to stop it, and a stop wants a thing to stop. Take the thing away. He had drilled it with Keld on Thursday, with a bench. *When you hear her call, lift.* There was no cruelty in it. If you ran at a door, and the door was opened, the floor on the other side was your own affair.

It had worked to the inch.

And that would have been the end of his thinking, and a pleasant end, if he had not spent six years on a mat teaching children to come down.

He had watched more landings than he could have said, and he knew a late door when he saw one. He knew what it looked like when a boy opened his stance in a panic with something else still running, because he had seen her do exactly that on a run plate, twice through, from the back of the viewing room. The Ember's metal had been on her calves in the Cellhouse when she planted. It had been ugly, and it had held once, and he had told Keld on the stair afterward, *don't expect it again.*

There had been nothing on her shins just now.

He had been looking. Nobody else on that floor would have been; they were looking at her face, or the flag. He had been looking at her legs, because that was where a landing told the truth. The struts had gone before she was halfway down. She had come through the air with nothing open at all, a plain girl falling fast, and as her foot found the brick one door had opened, the right one, by itself.

It had been late by about the width of a breath. And it had been aimed at his sandbag.

"What are you chewing?" said Keld.

"I'm trying to think whether I've ever seen anyone get that part right and fall over anyway."

"What part?"

"Never mind." He took the water from Hendry. "It's usually the other way about."

He had a Hold that had stopped at seven and a half two springs ago and would not be coaxed. He was not strong, and he had come to terms with that. Stonehand's upper teams wanted plates lifted, and he could not lift them, and so he was a third-year in a Third Division tunic who taught twelve-year-olds to argue with the floor. He did not mind it as much as people supposed. But the Home Line took its hold crews from hands with a second step, and Old Four had been eighteenth on a list for two years.

On Wednesday week he would stand in a clay pit with sixteen people and a string of eight knots. One of the sixteen was a girl who had just done a new thing wrong and fallen over it.

He ought to think about that, and he would, on the tram.

"Third carry," he said. "They're three behind. They have to come and get it. Talley's proud, and the Bexley boy can't keep still to save his life, so they'll run. We divide at the fifth posts, same as the first. Hendry, the ward. Idony, the boy. Arrand, you stay on my shoulder and stop apologizing to people."

"And Rowan?"

"Leave her the sandbag."

He stood up, for across the lane the healer's boy had finished, and the girl was getting her good leg under her with a hand on the post.

*I should have liked to see it land,* thought Lowen Brask. It surprised him. He put it away, with the other things he would think about on the tram, and went to the mark.

---

"I'm not going to say it," said Jude.

"Say it. I'd rather."

"You told her you wouldn't. In front of me. With my book on the bench." He had his arms folded so tightly that his knuckles were white. "And I'm three behind in my own lane, with a fighter on one leg. No. I'm not going to say it. I haven't the time."

The knee was the size of a small loaf. The healer's boy had strapped it and told her that she could stand on it and should not, and gone away. Kiva held the post and tried her weight, and it bore, though it felt as if somebody had packed the joint with hot gravel.

"I can plant," she said. "I can walk a few paces. I can't bound. I'm sorry, Jude. I mean it, and I know it isn't any use to you."

"It isn't." He breathed out through his nose. "So. Third carry. He'll do what he did in the first. He'll leave you the sandbag and hunt."

"He won't hunt."

They all looked at her. Even Pru stopped.

"He didn't hunt us in the first one," said Kiva. She had been going over it against the post, because it was better than going over the other thing. "It looked like it. But think where they went. Round the sides, slowly, where we'd have to come to them. And then in the second he came straight down the middle the moment I had struts on. He isn't walking round me because he's afraid of the stop. He's waiting for me to travel. The whole afternoon. Every point they've had came off one of us moving."

"Noa bounded," said Pru slowly. "Into Keld."

"I ran at three of them," said Jude. "With my back to nothing."

"And I went eight paces through the air and told him when I'd arrive." It tasted of brick. "He hasn't beaten anybody who was standing still. I don't think he's tried."

Noa had his tape. He had been to the board. He looked from the strip of paper to the chalk dots on the floor of his mind, and she watched it reach him.

"Then don't," he said. "Any of us."

"We're three behind," said Jude. "If we sit on it, it lies in our half at time, and that's one. We lose by two instead of three."

"So we take it home." Kiva pointed down the south row. "Along the posts. One post at a time. I plant at a post with my heel on its shoe, and that's a riser. Pru ties the gate across from oak to oak, so there aren't any ends. When it's clear, I let go and you two carry it four paces, and I walk, and I plant at the next. I never change doors. Nobody's ever in the air. And every time it changes hands, somebody calls it. *Yours. Mine.* So there's never a moment when it's nobody's."

"That's four paces at a time," said Jude. "With four still-doors on the other end of it."

"They have to come and take hold first. That's the part I like." She met his eye. "You said it on Monday. Our book says your own side ought to know first."

Jude Talley looked down the lane, at the nine black posts and the white line beyond them. Then, for the first time since the fall, something eased in his jaw.

"Nobody runs," he said. "Nothing changes. And we say everything. Pru, can you tie that wall to wood?"

"I've never tried. It's always had ends. I've always thought the ends were the ward's own fault." Pru considered. "I could lean it on the posts. Like a plank across a ditch."

"Then be a plank."

"Rowan," said Kiva to the referee. "Still."

The grey-braided woman looked at the strapped knee, and at her face, and nodded.

---

"Bell."

Noa went once. It was the only run of the carry, and he made it a good one. He had Donny off the mark and four paces west, hard against the foot of the southern fourth post, before the brown tunics had left their line. Then he let go and stood still, with his hands open, and it was plainly the hardest thing he had done all year.

Kiva came down the lane on her own two feet. It took a long time. She sat rather than knelt, with the bad leg out, and set her right heel against the iron shoe of the post, and took the head poles with her right hand and the crook of her left elbow. She did not trust the two fingers. She had not trusted them since the Terrace.

She let her weight go down.

"Mine," she said.

"Plank," said Pru. The ward came up across the lane a pace east of Donny's feet, low and leaning, and its two ends came to rest against black oak. It hummed there. It had never hummed before. "Oh. It likes that. Jude, it *likes* it."

Jude stood at the mouth of the south aisle with his blade down. Noa stood at the north.

Stonehand reached the fifth posts and divided, two and two, and went into the aisles.

They came along both walls at that slow, rolling walk. They came round the ends of the rows, looking for somebody to hunt, and found four people standing still behind a wall with a sandbag.

Lowen Brask stopped.

He put up one hand, and the other three stopped with him. He looked at the gate, and at the heel on the post, and at Jude Talley, who was not running anywhere. Kiva saw him read it. It took him about as long as it took to read a tram ticket.

Then he folded his arms.

For the space of ten breaths, eight people in the New Lane looked at one another across six paces of brick, and nobody did anything at all. The gallery had begun to mutter. Somebody in brown called out to get on with it.

"He's ahead," said Jude, without moving his lips. "He can stand there till the glass runs."

"Then we go home," said Kiva. "Call it."

"Yours?"

"Yours."

She gave the ground its weight back. Jude and Noa had the poles as her hands opened, and Donny came up off the brick and went west, four paces, at a walk. Pru's ward folded and rose again at the third posts. Kiva limped after it, and sat, and set her heel.

"Mine."

Lowen Brask unfolded his arms. He said one word to his team, and all four came down the lane.

---

They did it properly. She would say that for them afterward.

Moss and the boy with the ears walked up to Pru's wall and leaned on it, side by side, like men at a bar. Keld went into the north aisle after Noa. And the captain came down the south, behind the posts, to where Jude stood.

He did not try to pass him. He stopped a pace short. Then he reached through the gap between two posts with both broad hands, and took hold of Donny's foot poles, and pushed.

Kiva felt it arrive in her wrists.

She had been waiting three weeks to feel that again. It was the Terrace. It was a strong man leaning on a door. "*Now!*"

Jude's blade was already going.

It met nothing. Brask had pushed with one hand, she saw, too late, and kept the other back, and he had been stepping away before the word was out of her mouth. His open palm went past the dark edge, as a person goes past a branch on a path, and came down flat on Jude's forearm behind it.

"Half! Stonehand. On the arm."

Jude swore. "He wasn't there. You said *now* and he wasn't there."

"There was nothing in it." She was staring at her own hands. "Jude, it stopped at my wrists. It never went through. At the Terrace I felt him in the step. That was a knock on a door by somebody who'd already walked off."

Lowen Brask, three paces away behind an oak post, inclined his head to her very slightly.

"Kiva," said Pru. Her voice was climbing. "There's two of them on it now, it's going twice as quick, I can feel it going soft in the middle—Jude—"

"Pru." Kiva did not look round. She kept her eyes on the poles. "Don't let them break it. Drop it yourself. When you choose."

"Drop it? They'll fall on you!"

"Yes. Call it. Call it to me."

There was a small, frantic silence.

"*Finch! Gate off!*" said Pru, in a voice that cracked.

The silver went out like a blown candle.

Hendry Moss had been leaning on it with the whole of his considerable self. He came through the place where it had been as a man comes through a door that somebody has opened from inside. Two great stumbling steps, with his arms spread, and his chest wide, straight down upon the head of the litter and the girl sitting at it.

She had the poles in the crook of her left arm. She took her right hand off the wood. She held it up, open, and he arrived on it.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

He knelt. He went down on the brick an arm's length from her, a large placid shape folding at the edge of her eye, and everything in her that had ever knelt at a cloth turned toward him.

"Staying," said Kiva, through her teeth.

She had her hand back on the pole before the line judge had his arm up.

It was the first time she had ever put anyone down. She noticed that some while afterward. In the moment there was only the boy with the ears standing alone where a gate had been, with his mouth open, and nobody on the foot poles at all.

"Yours!"

"Ours," said Jude, and they had it up, and down the lane to the second posts. Pru's plank dropped across the lane behind them and hummed.

"Mine."

Kiva set her heel against the iron shoe of the post and felt the old oak take her.

---

That was where it stuck.

Idony Keld had got Noa. Kiva heard it go, in the north aisle, the long arm and the flat call. "*Down!* Crooked Line!" Then Keld came round the end of the gate by the wall, where there was just room, and she and the captain and the boy with the ears all laid hold of Donny's feet together.

They did not push. Brask had finished with pushing. They pulled.

It came on slowly, three still-doors leaning back from the poles with their heels set, and then four, when Moss had sat out his time. Kiva felt it come through her arms and her ribs and her hips, and through her right heel into the iron shoe of a post that had stood there three hundred years. It stopped there. She was not going to be moved.

But the poles were wood, and her hands were hands.

"Fifty seconds," called the referee.

Jude could not get a touch. Each time his blade went toward one set of ribs, another's palm was waiting behind it. Pru had her gate up, between nobody and nothing. The board said eight to five.

Brask pushed. A short, quick shove, with the weight of a breath.

"No," said Kiva. Jude's blade, which had twitched, stayed where it was.

"Ah," said Lowen Brask. He sounded like a man whose pupil has given the right answer a term early. Then he leaned back with the others and went on pulling, and his calm brown eyes came down to the crook of her left elbow.

He was watching the hook. He had told her that he would.

Her right hand had slipped a finger's width. The left arm was shaking. He would have her in a fourth carry, he had said. There was not going to be a fourth carry. He would have her in thirty seconds instead.

Kiva looked at the four of them.

She looked at them properly. Four people, leaning backward at the far end of a sandbag. All their weight was hung on those two poles. They were not standing on the brick. They were standing on her.

*As a man leans on a door he expects to open.*

She had been the door, once, at the Terrace. She had been shut, and he had put everything on her, and for one instant none of it had been in him.

It was the other way about now. She only had to open.

"Jude," she said. "Get a hand on the head. Pru, the other. When I call it, it's theirs."

"It's *what*?"

"Yours first. Then theirs."

She did not whisper it. A call was no good whispered.

"*Rowan! Still to dark!*"

It went down the lane and up into the lantern, and Lowen Brask's eyes came up from her elbow to her mouth.

She saw him understand. It took him half a second, which was half a second quicker than anybody else in brown. He took both hands off the poles, cleanly, and stepped back, and stood on his own feet. His mouth opened.

"Let—"

She gave the ground its weight back.

Donny went east as if he had been kicked. He went a full pace, with nothing whatever holding him, and at the far end of him, three still-doors who had been leaning their whole selves on a rooted girl found that there was no girl there.

The boy with the ears sat down. Moss went back four steps, windmilling. Keld kept her feet, because she was Keld, but her long arms were flung wide to do it, and her ribs were as open as a book on a table.

Jude Talley had started on the call.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

"Mine!" That was Pru, with both fists on a head pole. "Is it mine? I've got it! Kiva, I've got one!"

Kiva had the other. She had no door open at all. She was a girl on one good leg, hauling seventy kilos of sand backward with a first-year who was narrating.

They made three paces. Then the litter stopped as if it had been nailed.

Lowen Brask had bent, unhurried, and taken the foot poles in his two hands, and let his weight go down. One still-door. Seven and a half. He looked along the length of Donny at her, and there was nothing unfriendly in it.

"That's mine," he said.

"I know."

Jude's blade came for his ribs.

He had to take a hand off the poles to meet it, and he met it late, with his wrist. "Half! Crooked Line." With one hand he could not hold both poles. The litter slewed, and Kiva and Pru dragged the head round like the hand of a clock. Jude came again, and stood between.

Brask looked at the dark edge. He looked at the girl walking backward behind it, with her hands full and her leg dragging. One step would have put his palm on her. It would have put his own ribs on Jude's flat on the way.

"No," he said gravely. "Thank you. Not that one."

He let go.

They went over the white line backward, the three of them, and fell down in a heap on the far side of it, with Donny on top.

"Litter home! Three points, Crooked Line!"

"*Time,*" said the referee, a few seconds later, to a lane in which nobody could hear her.

The board turned over, and Crooked Line had it by a point, and the western rail, where the first-years sat, had gone entirely quiet.

---

They shook hands at the fifth posts, and Kiva did it sitting on the brick.

Hendry Moss shook her hand with great goodwill and told her that nobody had ever opened a door on him before. Keld took it briefly, looked at the knee, and said, "I told you I'd strap that." The boy with the ears wished to be assured that it had been allowed. Noa assured him.

"One point," said Lowen Brask. "I'll have to take your lane card down from over my bed and put up a worse one."

He lowered himself to the brick beside her, quite naturally, as a man sits down next to someone on a wall.

"You told me," he said. "*Still to dark.* I heard it. I got my own hands off. I couldn't get theirs off, and I've been teaching Idony for three years." He shook his head. "She didn't hear a word of it. It wasn't said to her. But your captain had his blade moving before you'd let go. Your little one had her hands out. Your whole side went on a thing I'd heard as plainly as they had."

"That's what the rule's for."

"So I've found. I read it as a rule that tells me where you'll be. It tells them as well. I only read the half I liked." He was silent a moment. "I'm not going to apologize for the ladder."

"No."

"But I've taught landings for six years, and I'll tell you what I saw, since nobody else in this building was looking at your shins." He held up a finger and thumb, a hair apart. "You'd nothing on them. The metal was home. You came down on one door, and it was the right one, and it was late by about that much. I've seen a great many people get that wrong. I've never seen anybody get it right and fall over."

Kiva did not know what to say, and her knee was beating like a second heart.

"Where was it meant to go?" he said. "The speed."

"Into the litter. Into you."

"Mm. And I moved the litter." He looked at the strapping. "I wonder where it would have gone if I hadn't. All of it. That's a good deal to put into a sandbag." He got up, slowly. "That's all I've got. I don't know your doors. Wednesday week, Miss Rowan. I'd take it kindly if you didn't try that on my rung."

"I won't."

"Good. I believe you, which is more than your captain does at present." He nodded, and went away after his team.

The referee came over before she went, and stood with her grey braid over her shoulder and her hands behind her back.

"Door declared each carry," she said. "One change, called under home Rule Six, and made on the call. A fall. No undeclared change. No incident." She looked down at the strapping. "A fall isn't an incident. A fighter who's flagged from door to door is supervised; I said so last time and I'll say it to the board again. And you were on your face in my lane with nothing lit, and you called your own *off*. I hadn't asked. The board will hear that as well. It isn't *safe*, mind. I don't want it mistaken."

She went back to her table without waiting for an answer, which was as well, because Kiva had none. She sat on the brick with her knee beating and watched the gallery empty, and did not see Orla anywhere in it.

---

Orla was waiting in the passage behind the lane, where the buckets were kept.

She had sent Mara on to the tram, and Kiva, who saw that and knew what it meant, came down the passage on Anwen's arm, and then let go of the arm.

"I'll be outside," said Anwen. She went.

Orla Dane stood with both hands on her stick. Her voice, when it came, was quiet.

"On Monday you told me you would do it the slow way."

"Yes."

"In front of your captain."

"Yes."

"I answer for you in Ysra Crest's office. If your line had lit out there, that's an incident, and it's my standing and my contract. I'd have paid. I said I would the day I took you on, and I meant it." The lamp-oil eyes did not leave her. "But you don't spend it without asking me. Not once. Not because it's eight paces and it's right there. It will always be right there, Rowan. That's what it's going to be like for the rest of your life."

Kiva had nothing to hold. She held the wall.

"I'm sorry."

"I know you are. You're a great deal sorrier than you were on your knees with that leg, and so you ought to be. I'm not finished." Orla breathed. "The yard's shut till the healer's seen that. And I'll tell you now, so you needn't wonder, that I've not yet seen one I'd stand behind."

It was fair, and that was the worst part of it. She would almost rather have been shouted at by somebody who was wrong.

"Now," said Orla. Her voice had changed. It had gone dry and exact, the voice of the crate. "You've paid for a lesson. I'll not have you go home without it. Your shins were bare. I saw. The order was right, for the first time. So what did you do wrong?"

"I was late."

"By a hair. That isn't what put you on the brick. Where was the speed to go?"

It was the question Brask had asked. She gave the same answer, and heard it this time.

"Into the litter."

"And when he moved the litter?"

"Into my knee."

"Into the *ground*," said Orla. "It was only ever going into the ground. Through you. That's what a stance is; you've known it since you were thirteen. The litter was in the way." She tapped the ferrule once on the stone. "You tried to spend it on him. A sequence isn't a thing you do to somebody. He hadn't to be there for it at all. You proved that. He left, and it went into the ground anyhow, by the worst road there was."

Kiva stood in the passage among the buckets.

She had thought of it as a blow, and she saw that now. From the first night on the line she had thought of the speed as a thing to hit with, a Stride made heavy, because that was what would have been wonderful.

It was not a blow. It was only weight, arriving. It had to be given somewhere to arrive.

"Go home," said Orla. "Ice it. And Rowan. That last carry." She had turned to the door, and she did not turn back. "That sandbag changed hands the whole way home, and it was never once nobody's. Think about why that worked, and the other didn't, before you think about anything else."

---

The healer at the school infirmary gave her a fortnight.

"No landings," she said. She was a stout, unhurried woman who rolled bandage the whole time she talked and never once looked at it. "None. You may stand on it. You may walk on it, with the strapping. If you come down on that leg from any height before the second of next month, I shall hear of it, and I shall send the account to your sponsor."

"There's a Flask on the twenty-ninth."

"Are you the blade?"

"The catcher."

"Then kneel on the other one."

So when the yard opened again on Monday night, with a new week's thimble of ash in her, Orla took away the bound.

"A step," she said. "One. Off the line. Struts for a single push, no higher than a curb and no longer than a pace. If you miss, you've taken a step. I'd like to see your healer make a bill out of that."

Perrin Cade had come, and he sat on the spare crate with a hand on his ribs and watched Kiva stand on the line and look at the cross. It was four paces off still, for nobody had moved it. It was only that she was not going there tonight.

"Can I say a thing?" he said. "It's about falling. It's the only subject I'm any good at."

"Say it," said Orla.

"Brask taught me. When I was twelve. A month, I told you. And the trick was that you don't land and then let go. You're let go already when you get there. He used to say it every morning, till I could have hit him." Perrin put on a mild, patient voice that was not at all a bad likeness. "*Arrive before you're there, Cade.* I thought it was the stupidest thing a person ever said. Then I fell off the practice wall onto my back and didn't feel it. You set the door for the weight that's coming. Not the weight you've got."

"I can't feel the ground till it's there."

"No. Nobody can. You don't wait to feel it. You'd be late." He shrugged, and winced. "It's like. I don't know what it's like. It's like holding out your arms for a baby somebody's about to hand you."

Kiva stood very still.

"It's a pan," she said.

"A what?"

"You don't chase ash." She was speaking slowly, because it was coming slowly, from a long way down. "It falls where it's going to fall. If you wait to feel it land, you're already behind. You put the pan under first. Before. You set it where the fall's going, and then the ash arrives, and it's in the pan." She looked at her own two hands. "I've done that four hundred times. With the bad fingers. In the dark. I've never once waited for it."

Orla said nothing at all.

*Ember.* The struts closed on her calves. She pushed off the line, a curb's height and a pace, a thing a child might have done over a puddle. She shut the Ember at the top of it. The metal ran home.

And she set the still door under herself, as she would have set a pan.

Her right foot came down into it.

There was no jar. There was no ground, exactly. It was like stepping off a curb onto a mattress that was the whole of the Old Yard. The step went through her and out, and she had never in her life landed so softly on anything.

*Caught,* she thought, and felt her hands do what they had always done after a catch.

She put the lid on.

The stance shut. The stick arrived. She went over sideways into the dirt.

"Well," said Orla, after a moment. Her voice was not quite even. "You caught it. And then you tinned it. I asked for a stance, Rowan, not a seal check."

"It was soft." Kiva lay on her back and looked at the lamp. "Orla. It was *soft*. My knee didn't know."

"I saw. Is your line cold?"

"It never moved."

"Then that's the first that didn't hurt, and it's a miss. You caught the speed and stood on nothing afterward. Up."

The next was the same. She caught it, and this time kept the door open by main force, and the stick moved her a foot across the lane as a hand moves a pudding. She had taken the landing in and had nothing left over to stand with. Orla called that a miss as well.

On Wednesday night she made two more.

The first put her back two steps.

The second she caught, and stayed open, and stayed. The iron ferrule came in at her left shoulder with all of Orla's arm behind it. And it went through her. She felt it go, as she had felt Lowen Brask go at the Terrace: in at the shoulder and down, through her hip and her right heel, and into the dirt of the lane, and gone.

She had not moved.

*I've done it,* thought Kiva, and the joy of it went up through her like a struck match.

The bottom line lit.

"*Ember! Off!*"

She stood with her hands on her knees while it cooled. She did not need to be told. She had felt the Ember come up through the middle of the gladness, without being asked, to see what the fuss was about.

"For a breath," said Orla. She had got to her feet. She had not done that for any of the others. "The work went across. I felt it go; it nearly took the stick out of my hand. And then you were pleased, and I watched you being pleased, and your other door came to see." She sat down again, heavily. "You'd stopped calling."

"I was listening for the ground."

"You've told me yourself what happens when you stop calling. The door's just standing there, and it comes to see." A pause. "That's a miss."

"It was nearly—"

"Nearly is a miss with a better seat. I've watched every one of them, and I'd put my name to none. Not for a lane. Not for a floor." Orla's crooked face was grave in the yellow light. "You'll go to the Pit on the paper you've got. Recovery. Dark hands. You'll kneel."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Orla, and reached up for the lamp.

---

Between those two nights in the yard, on the Tuesday, there had been the second floor.

They spent it at four, on the Kennel's brick, and it was the last thing Team Seven had left to spend but the sealed dram. Kiva had walked the floor, and set the borrowed sheet between the first tank and the second, and then she had stood up and said the thing she had been turning over since the lane.

"When I call dark, I mean everybody. Not inside three paces. The whole floor. Nothing lit anywhere till I say sealed."

"The rule's three," said Kellan.

"The rule was three on the Sag Line. Dray's ash went forty and found a pump." She looked along the floor toward the grates. "The Pit's a bowl. I don't know what the air does in a bowl. And there's Saturday. We stopped feeding him. Every point they had came off one of us moving. When nobody ran and nothing changed, he had nothing to hunt, and he had to come to us."

"That's a man," said Anwen. "At the weir they kept the wall lit. Ten paces off, the whole way through. And Bettan told us to leave it something to lean on."

"At the weir they had eight Home Line wards round the outside and a meadow. We'll have a floor and three teams of students." Kiva had her answer ready, and she gave it. "If nothing's lit, there's nothing for open ash to lean at. That's all I mean. It's the same thing. Give it nothing. And the string can have the lattice for its gate. That's what the one on the Sag did when we all went dark. It went and leaned on the hold like a cow."

Anwen did not answer that. She had asked, and been answered, and she sat back on her heels with her arms folded. Nobody else could find the hole in it, and Kiva, who had looked, could not find one herself.

So they did it her way. The husk that came up out of the dark was a plain one, for once, and Kellan cut it at the foot of Anwen's ward. *Dark,* said Kiva, and the ward went out, and there was not a lit thing on the settling floor from the yellow line to the gutter. Four people came to four corners. She had told Anwen beforehand that the cord's third turn was hers.

"Yours."

"Mine," said Anwen, and crossed it over, not under.

"Sealed."

It was quicker than the first floor by a long way, and it weighed twenty-three and six-tenths, and there was no hair-bright line in a grain of it.

"Hm," said Warden Druce. He turned the bag's neck in his grey fingers. "Whole floor dark. Whose notion?"

"Mine."

"And if there'd been seven more of it standing behind you with nothing to look at?"

"It would have gone and leaned on your lattice."

"On my floor, it would. I've a lattice sixty years old in the next room, and it's the loudest thing in this building." He set the bag on the scale and did not look at her. "I'd ask myself what the loudest thing in the Pit is, Miss Rowan."

"The Kiln."

"I'd have thought so. Mind, I've not stood a Flask in that hole for years, and nobody's stood one there with a company of first-years. Put it to your foreman. It's his floor." He lifted the bag off the scale. "That's a tidy catch. I'll say so. I'd only say that a tidy catch on a Handful has never yet told anybody anything about a string."

Kiva carried that home on the tram, turned over and over.

She had a catch that was quicker than the last, and a reason that had won a lane, and now a question from a warden who had said *hm*. She did not have an answer to it. On Thursday she would put it to a Home Line foreman, and see whether he had.
