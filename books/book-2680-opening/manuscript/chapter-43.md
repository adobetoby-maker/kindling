# Chapter 43 — Kiva: A Drain in a Box

The Halden Works lent its license yard on Sunday afternoon, and Foreman Birch made it very plain whose yard it was.

"My floor," he said, at the rail. "My ward line. My string, that I've had in that cage since the thaw, and I know its temper. The Municipal Works has asked for a floor that keeps step, and there's only the one in Meridian with a rift on it. It's got it. Nobody lights so much as a pipe on that brick until I've said."

It was a bigger floor than the Kennel's. It was paved in blue brick, with a lattice house at the north end and a paid ward line in black standing easy along the wall. Kiva had not been inside the refinery gate before. It was riveted from top to bottom like a ship's side, and she walked under it looking up.

What Mara had built stood down the middle of the yard.

It was a trough. It was twelve paces long, knee high, made of new planks and lined with lead, and it had a foot of water in it. At the south end stood a brewer's hand pump with its handle off, and beside the pump, on a bench, six cells in a row with their needles under glass.

All six needles were nodding. The pump drew, and let go, as they did. Down the whole length of the trough the water rose a finger on the lead, and fell.

"There's your main," said Mara. "And there's your pump house. Mr. Adlam's people stepped that bank for me at eight this morning, with a pinch of their own salt, by the patent. It took them four minutes. They were very civil about it."

"And that?" said Kiva.

At the north end of the trough, twelve paces from the bench, there was a stool. The stool stood with its feet in the water. On it sat a single cell with a needle of its own.

"That's a drum on Stopgate. One plain cell, filled at eight, on its own time. There isn't a wire to it. There's nothing between that cell and the bench but twelve paces of water going up and down." Mara looked at it with dislike. "At eight it stood. At ten it had a tremble. Come to the rail and tell me what it's doing now. Not past the rail."

Kiva went to the rail.

The needle on the stool went up, and came down. It did it with the other six.

"It's in," she said.

"It went in just before noon. Mrs. Renn had her glass on it."

There was a woman at the bench whom Kiva had never met and knew at once. She was narrow and upright, with a grader's seal on a cord round her neck, and she had Kellan's eyes. In front of her stood a brass instrument like a clock with no hands.

"It didn't go all at once," said Kellan's mother. She did not look up from the glass. "It leaned for an hour. Then it went. I'd say that a cell is a patient sort of thing, and it's been pushed from underneath, quite gently, a great many times."

"Then it's the water," said Rusk.

He stood behind the bench with his thumbs in his waistband. He looked like a man who has slept in a chair.

"It's the water in a box," said Mara. "I'll not say more than that until tomorrow night, and nor should you." She put both hands on the bench. "But I'll say what we've all been assuming since Thursday, as though it were in the patent, so that we can watch it be right or wrong. The water moves because the pumps move it. Stop the pumps and the main lies still. Then a bank may be quenched and filled fresh, and it will keep the time that it's given."

"That's the plan," said Rusk. "That's the whole of the plan, if the fence fails. I stop my three pumps for as long as the sump will bear, and I put every hand in the Works on that bank at once, one to a cell. That brings my four hours down to half of one."

"It's a good plan. I'd have drawn it myself. I'd sooner see it work once in a box before I trust a district to it. And Officer Vane wants to know what a string makes of a floor like this one. Mr. Birch has been kind enough to say that we may ask it."

"I said you might ask," said Birch. "I didn't say it'd answer politely."

There were a good many people along the rail. The Placement Chair had come, since there were students on a floor with a rift at one end of it, and she stood by the gate with her hands folded. Hollis Vane was there with his hat on. Rhea Sorn had brought Pike, and Greta Stroud, and Ruddock with his bag, and nobody else.

The fours were along the east wall, dark. Old Four had come, and Twenty-Eight with its reserve blade, and Halden's own three in their black and gold. Seven stood at the end.

"Gate," said Birch.

Four of his paid wards walked out onto the blue brick. They brought a wall up twelve paces west of the trough, as the Circuit's rules had required since the spring, silver and square.

"That's the brightest thing on my floor. It's lit, and it's worked, and it's nearer than the bench. Any string I ever let out of that cage has gone to a gate like a dog to its dinner." He raised his arm. "Lattice. Give me the door."

The cage at the north end opened with a rattle.

Kiva had seen a good many strings by now. This one was small. It had six knots and not eight, and it stood no higher than a tram's window, on four legs. It had frost on it from crown to knee.

It came out onto the brick and stopped. Its blunt top turned, slowly, a quarter of a circle, as a weathercock turns.

It turned past the gate.

It did not pause on it. Four people were burning ash to make the brightest thing in the yard, and the grey head went by them as a man's eyes go by a stranger's in a street. It came round to the south, and stopped, pointing down twelve paces of lead-lined trough at a bench.

"That's not right," said Birch.

The string walked. It walked to the north end of the trough and stood there, astride the stool, with one pair of legs on either side of the water. The cell on the stool went on nodding between its knees.

Then the shine on it moved. It ran down off the shoulders, as Kiva had seen it run once before, in the Pit, in the spring. Two white cords went down from the lowest knot, as thick as a wrist, and into the water.

"Sorn," said Vane.

"I see it." Rhea had not moved from the rail. "It did that on the bus, in the fifth month. I said then that it had found something better than a gate. I didn't know what the something was."

"It's found the beat," said Mara. Her voice was very quiet. "It doesn't want the bright thing. It wants the thing that keeps time."

"Then I'll have a rung off it while it's standing still to be cut," said Birch. "That's what a string in my yard is for. Renn."

---

Kellan Renn had come through the refinery gate that afternoon in his own coat, and for the first time in his life he had not counted the rivets.

He had been thinking about his feet.

Birch had said nothing about his answer. Birch did not say much. He had been standing at the rail when the students came in, and he had looked at each of them as a grader looks at a tin. When he came to Kellan he had looked down.

*They've not got worse,* he had said. *Tuesdays and Thursdays. Bring your own cuff.*

That was the whole of it. It was a place, and nothing was owed for it, and there was no jacket on a peg.

"Top rung," said Birch now. "Yarrow has the cloth. He kneels where you put him, and you ask him. I'm told that's how it's done by people who mean to bag anything."

"Yes, sir."

He walked out onto the blue brick, dark.

He disliked it at once. He knew the string; he had cut its top rung a dozen times since the seventh month. What he disliked was the floor. There was something coming up through his boot soles, unhurried, like the tread of a great many people going past on the other side of a wall. His feet wanted to fall in with it.

He had the best feet in the School. He had been told so since he was fourteen. It was a new thing to find that they could be asked to do something by a floor.

*No,* he thought, as he would have said it to a dog that wanted to walk him. He put his weight down where he meant it to go, and a little after the floor's time, and then a little before. His feet steadied.

Yarrow was laying the cloth. He was a still-door, long and incurious, and he threw a sheet as a man lays a table that he has laid before. He put it on the east side of the trough, hard up against the planks, under the string's top knot.

"That's close to the water," said Kellan.

"It's where it'll fall."

"It's a pace from a thing that's got two cords down in it."

"I've time," said Yarrow, patiently. "Ash doesn't run. It lies. It lies where it lands and leans to the nearest lamp, and I've put myself between it and the bench. What else is there?"

It was a fair answer. It was what every catcher was taught. Kellan looked at the trough, and at the white cords going down into it, and could not think of anything he had ever been taught that said otherwise.

He looked along the east wall instead. He did it without quite meaning to. There was a catcher there who was particular about ground.

She had her eyes on the cloth. She was frowning. But she was not his catcher today, and she had not been asked, and he saw her shut her mouth on whatever it was.

"Renn," said Birch. "While we're young."

He went up the back of it dark, on his struts, in two bounds.

"Now?"

"Not yet," said Yarrow. "It's swaying. Now."

He lit.

It was four-tenths. He had it to a hair. The edge went through the top knot level and came out the other side, and he was dark again before his feet had found the brick. He had never made a cleaner one in that yard.

Sixty drams fell like sand out of a split sack.

"*Dark!*"

It did not lie.

He saw it from three paces. The heap hit the canvas and spread, as a heap does, and then the near edge of it began to move. It did not drift. It ran. It went across the waxed cloth toward the trough in a grey tongue, as flour runs to the low side of a tilted board, and over the hem, and up the planks.

It was climbing the cord.

"*Corners!*" Yarrow had the hem in both fists. His voice had gone up. "Corners, *now,* it's going—"

Pettit and Hesketh hauled. The neck came in. It closed on what was left, and Yarrow's cord went round it three times as fast as Kellan had ever seen a cord go.

"Sealed," said Yarrow. He weighed the bag in his two hands, as a catcher will. "That's light. That's a great deal light."

There was a grey scum on the water.

It lay all down the trough, from the stool to the pump, moving with the rise and fall. Half a rung of open ash had gone into a foot of water in less time than it takes to say so.

"The bench," said Kellan's mother.

She said it quite quietly. He turned his head.

All six needles had jumped. He saw them do it, together, like six hands flung up. They stood for a moment against their upper pins, quivering. Then they lay down, all at once, as no bank's cells ever die, and the glass over the third cell cracked across with a small flat sound.

The pump stopped.

"That's fouled," said Rusk. His voice was thick. "That's one pinch of open ash in the water, and six cells cooked inside a breath. All six. There wasn't one of them that could say no to the others."

"There were six," said Mara. "I've four-and-twenty."

Nobody answered her.

Kellan stood on the blue brick with his blade dark on its cuff. He had cut a rung as well as he knew how, on the word of a catcher who had done what every catcher was taught, and it had killed a bank.

*I asked,* he thought. *I asked him and he told me, and it was a good answer.*

He had learned to ask. It had cost him a season. It had not until this moment crossed his mind that asking the catcher was no use at all when the catcher did not know either.

---

"The pump's dead," said Kiva.

She had not been asked. She said it all the same, since it was the thing that was there.

"We can see that the pump's dead," said Birch.

"The water isn't."

They looked.

The brewer's pump stood with its plunger down. The six cells on the bench lay cold. Nothing in that yard was drawing on the trough, and all down its twelve paces the grey water rose a finger on the lead, and fell.

Mara walked the length of it. She walked slowly, with her eyes on the wet line. She stopped at the north end, where the string stood astride the stool on its four legs, with five knots left and two white cords going down between them.

The cords were moving. They drew up, a hand, and let down. Three grey knots above them leaned back a little as they did it, and eased, like three people hauling a neck shut on a called word.

"It's pumping," said Mara.

She turned round. Her face was perfectly composed, and Kiva had never seen it so white.

"There. That's what I said at the bench. *Stop the pumps and the main lies still.* It doesn't. If there's one of those standing in it, the pumps may stop and the bank may cook, and the water goes on breathing, since the thing's learned the stroke and has two arms down the hole." She looked at Rusk. "And I'd have filled four-and-twenty cells fresh, in the dark, on the strength of that sentence."

"So would I," said Rusk. "It was my plan before it was yours. Fill the one. The stool. Show me the rest of it. I'd sooner see the whole of what I've got wrong."

A Works fitter did it, at arm's length, with the string's legs on either side of him and a ward of Birch's between. He quenched the cell on the stool, and drew it, and slid a fresh can home.

"Open," he said.

The needle rose and stood, on a time of its own. The fitter let out his breath.

Then it trembled.

Then it went up, and came down, with the water.

"That's a fresh can," said Pike. He was looking at it as he looked at a neck. "It takes the first pattern that touches it. I've said that to every corner-hand I ever trained, about a lit blade." He shook his square head. "I never thought to say it about a puddle."

"So I can't quench it while it's in the water," said Rusk. "And I can't stop the water while it's in the water. And I can't cut it while it's in the water, or the rung runs down its own arm and cooks my bank." He ticked them off on his thumbs. "Then it must come out of the water. How does one take a thing like that by the arm?"

"You don't put a ward on it," said Greta Stroud.

She said it from the rail, hoarsely, without being asked.

"I've said that on two floors now. I put a wall against a cord like that in the spring, and I meant to keep hold. It went up me to the elbows."

"Iron, then," said Rusk. "Dead iron. Something with nothing in it to take."

They tried iron. It was a fair thing to try.

The fitter had a boat-hook. He stood well back, on the west side, and reached across the trough with the full length of it, and got the hook under the nearer cord where it left the water.

Frost ran up the shaft like a fuse.

"*Let go!*" said Rhea Sorn.

He had let go already. He was not a fool. The boat-hook hung in the air for a moment, white from end to end, with nobody holding it. Then the cord drew up, a hand, and the hook went with it and was laid down in the water as gently as a spoon.

The fitter stood looking at his glove. The palm of it had gone stiff, and cracked when he bent it.

"That's my left," he said. "And me right-handed, which is the only luck I've had today."

"I'd shake on it," said Perrin, with feeling, from the wall. "But."

Nobody laughed much.

Kiva had been watching the cord.

She knew that rise and fall. She had had it in a tray between her palms, in the Pit, for one slack, as light as a clothesline. She had not held it. That was the whole point of what she had done. She had put a pan under it at the instant that it stopped pulling, and had been somewhere else before it began again.

"It's got a slack," she said. "It hauls, and then it eases. A ward for one tap, on the slack, and folded before the haul comes back. It doesn't take a ward that isn't there any longer."

"That's a catcher's notion," said Pike.

"It's what I did. I only did it once."

"I know what you did. I was behind three posts with three cloths." His eyes had gone along the east wall, at a small round first-year with a cord wound round her fingers. "It wants the best fold in the yard. That isn't yours."

"No," said Kiva. "It's hers."

Pru Finch looked behind her, to see who was meant.

"Me?"

"You fold from the far edge. You don't let go. You've always got the last of it in your palms." Kiva heard that she was talking quickly, and slowed. "I learned it from you on a pail. I do it with two fingers stuck out like a fork. You'd do it properly."

"I can't go anywhere with it. I haven't an Ember. I can't put it down."

"Nobody's asking you to go anywhere. I only need to know whether it can be lifted off the water at all. For a breath. By a ward that isn't taken."

Pru looked at Rhea Sorn.

"You're entered for a corner and a lamp," said Rhea. "And the cord. I gave you the cord on Friday." The pale eyes rested on her. "This isn't in it. So it's yours to refuse, and I'll think none the worse. Stroud will tell you what it costs if it's late."

"Six weeks," said Stroud. "And a spoon held for you."

Pru swallowed. She unwound the cord from her fingers and put it away in her tunic, and buttoned the pocket.

"I'd want it called," she said. "The slack. I can't watch the cord and my hands."

"I'll call it," said Kiva.

It was out before she had thought. Of course she would call it. It was a hem under load, with a time in it, and she was the catcher. She had called the *in* on every sheet that Seven had thrown since the fifth month.

She went and knelt at the rail of the trough, a long pace from the planks. She laid her hands on the blue brick, the right palm and the heel of the left.

It came up through the brick. She could feel the pull in it. She could feel the easing.

Pru knelt on the east side, three paces from the string's near leg, with a ward of Birch's standing over her like a porch. She put up her round hands. The silver stood between them, no bigger than a tea tray, and she bent it into the bowl of a spoon.

"*Out,*" said Kiva. She felt the pull top out, and hang. "*In*—"

She knew as she said it.

It was late. It was not much late. It was the width of the difference between two hands, of which one told her the truth and the other told her that there was a floor. She had taken the middle of them, as she had for four years, and the middle was wrong.

Pru slid the pan under.

The cord was not slack. It was just beginning to draw. It came up into the bowl of that small bright ward with the whole weight of three knots behind it, and Kiva saw the silver go grey at the rim, like a window breathed on.

"*Fold!*" That was Pike, and Stroud, and Anwen, all at once.

Pru folded.

She did it faster than Kiva had ever seen anything done with a ward. Far edge. Near edge. A penny. Shut. The cord fell back into the water with a slap.

She sat down on the brick with her hands held up in front of her face.

They were white. They were white to the first joint, all eight fingertips and the two thumbs, as though she had dipped them in milk.

"They've gone to sleep," she said, in a high surprised voice. "They've all gone to sleep. I'm narrating. I can't feel my buttons. I had buttons."

Ruddock was over the rail already, with a towel. Anwen was a pace behind him.

Kiva had not moved. She was still kneeling, with her hands on the brick.

"Who called that?" said Pike.

"I did."

"I know that you did. Now the rest of it."

She made herself say all of it.

"I called the *in* late. I had it through two hands and I took the middle. One of them can't hear." Her mouth was dry. "I told a yard on Friday night that I couldn't take a thing's time any longer by myself. I said that somebody else would have to have it. Then I knelt down here and took it, since it's what I've always done. That's mine. It's her hands."

"It's the tips," said Ruddock, over his shoulder. "It's a nip. She folded inside it. Ten minutes, and she'll be able to pick her nose."

"I don't," said Pru faintly.

Kiva stood up. Her knees were not quite steady.

"Brask," she said. "You called the Pit's. Would you call this?"

Lowen Brask had been standing with Old Four, with his arms folded. He came along the wall without hurry.

"I'd call it," he said. "I'd sooner you'd asked me five minutes ago. I was standing there with it in my mouth." He looked at her, quite kindly. "You said it on Friday in the Graywater yard, I'm told. It's all over the Hall. *A thing's time. Somebody else has that.* It's a good rule. You'll have to keep it on the days that you're frightened as well."

"I know."

"I know you know." He turned to the trough. "Finch. When you're ready. Not before."

It was twelve minutes before the color came back. Pru would not be hurried, and nobody hurried her. Then she knelt down again in the same place, with her jaw set, and put up her hands.

"*Out,*" said Brask.

He let the pull go by. He let it top out, and hang, and he let it hang a while longer than Kiva would have.

"*In.*"

The pan went under.

For a breath the white cord lay in a small silver spoon, a hand's breadth above the water.

"Fold."

It was gone. The cord dropped.

And down the whole length of the trough, the water missed a beat.

Kiva saw it. Everybody saw it. The grey scum rose a finger on the lead, and fell, and rose, and then for the space of one slow breath it only lay there and rocked.

On the stool, between the string's knees, the single needle checked. It stood. Then the water drew again, and it went on.

"There," said Kellan's mother, from her glass. "It fell out. For as long as that arm was off the water, that cell had nobody to follow. It came back in as soon as it was asked."

"Then it wants more than a tap," said Rusk slowly.

"It wants the arm kept off," said Mara. "Lifted, and taken away, and put down in dry ground, and held there. For as long as it takes a Works crew to do the rest." She did not look at the east wall. "I've seen that done. I sat in a gallery and watched it. I don't know of any other hand that can."

Nobody in the yard looked at Kiva.

It was a strange thing to be at the middle of. Forty people had all at once found something to study in the brick, or the lattice, or their own boots.

"I'll say it, since it's mine," said Ysra Crest, from the gate. "*It is not to be asked of her.* I said that at the Chair and I meant it. Nobody in this yard will ask."

"Nor will it be offered," said Rhea Sorn. "Not on my floor."

She had turned her back on the trough. She was speaking to Vane, and to Rusk, and to a woman in a work apron, and not to a girl of seventeen.

"She's attached to me. Whatever becomes of her on a call is mine. I had her spend two fingers on a floor of mine three Saturdays ago, with the price said first, and I told her afterward that I'd never once named a price before I cut." Her voice was flat. "I've named this one to myself. It's too dear. I won't have a catcher of mine go up in the dark over a hole in the road and come down with half a hand, for a plan that had a wrong sentence in it an hour ago. Find me another way."

"I'd be glad to," said Mara. "What is it?"

"Plank the holes. Every one. Give it no water to put an arm in. Then it's a string on a street, and I take it down a rung at a time on a gate, as I've taken two hundred."

"And if it finds a hole that we didn't?"

"Then I'll cut it where it stands and bag what I can."

"With its arm down my drain. You watched what half a rung did to six cells."

"I watched."

They looked at one another. They were not angry. Kiva saw that, and it frightened her more than anger would have. They were two women who were each right about a thing, and the things did not fit.

She had an answer.

It was a good one. It was about a foreman with nobody south of a string, and a hand that was already spent, and what a catcher was for. It would have sounded very well on that brick.

She looked at Pru's fingers. They were pink again, and Pru was holding them up to the light one at a time, as though she had been given them for a present.

*I'd an answer ten minutes ago,* thought Kiva. *It was to kneel down and call it.*

She put the good one where she had put the others.

"Sorn's right that it isn't hers to have offered," she said. "I've nothing to say. I'd rather know how you get that one back in its cage."

Birch grunted.

"That's the first sensible question since two o'clock. I don't. Not with its arms in a trough." He jerked his head at the fitter. "There's a bung in the south end of that. Pull it."

The fitter pulled it.

The water went out across the blue brick in a grey sheet and found the yard's drain. The trough emptied in a slow minute. The wet line went down the lead, and down, and there was a foot of air where the water had been.

The two white cords hung in it.

They hung there a while, reaching, with nothing to reach. Then they simply let go. They came apart from the bottom, softly, into loose grey ash that pattered on the lead like rain.

The shine went back up onto the string's shoulders. Its blunt head lifted. It swung round, and found a wall of silver twelve paces to the west, and walked to it and leaned.

"Now it's a string," said Birch. "Now I know what to do with it."

"You took the water away," said Kiva.

"I pulled a bung. I can't pull a bung on the Ferren main."

"No." Mara had bent over the empty trough. She was looking at the lead, and the wet, and the little heap of grey sweepings where two arms had been. "No, nobody can drain a main. But one might shut a piece of it. A sluice. A gate, let down across the brick, with the hill on one side and the low streets on the other. They had them on every drain in this city before there were pumps." She straightened. "I've never seen one on that plan."

"The plan's been tidied," said Kiva.

"So it has."

"Why is it called Stopgate?"

She had not known that she was going to ask it. It had been lying in her head since Tuesday, with a great many other things that she did not know the use of.

Mara opened her mouth. She shut it again.

"I've no idea," she said. "I've walked up it for thirty years. I supposed that it was somebody's name."

---

Drum twenty-eight fell in at eighteen minutes to eight on Monday evening, with thirty people at its rail to watch it.

Kiva was on the pavement. She had been told not to come nearer, and she did not need to. She saw it through the green door, past her aunt's shoulder.

The needle stood. It trembled. Then it went up, without any hurry, and came down. It had done it before anybody had drawn breath to say so.

Below it on the hill, behind two more green doors, two needles lay against their pins in a pair of drums that had been dry since Wednesday midnight.

Rusk stood at the rail with his hands in his pockets. He did not say anything for some time.

"Well," he said at last, to Mara. "There it is." He looked down the street, at the dim brown windows of the middle stretch. "I've had six hundred families on boxes for five days, in the wet, to learn that I was wrong. I said that I'd carry that. It's heavier than it looked on Wednesday."

"A sixth time," said Mr. Adlam. He said it very quietly. "I shall have to tell the technical office that there is a thing which its patent does not describe. They will not like it. I rather think that I do."

"You stopped nothing," said Mara, to Rusk. "And you did no harm. I'd have done the same in your chair, and I said so on Wednesday."

"You did." He took off his linen cuff guards, one after the other, which she had never seen him do, and put them in his pocket. "It's two days to the next drum, and two more to a school. Mrs. Rowan. You called the drum. You stood at Birch's bench yesterday and showed me that my plan was wrong." He held out the trunk plan, rolled. "It's yours. The hill, the bank, and the stopping of it. I'll back whatever you say, and I'll put my forty operators where you put them."

Mara did not take it at once.

"I don't want it," she said.

"No. I'd noticed. That's a good part of why."

She took it. She held it for a moment in her left hand, the reading one, as she would have held a can that might be cut.

"Then I'll have the manhole at the head of the hill lifted," she said. "Tonight, with a ladder down it. I mean to have my hand on that water where it goes under the gate."

---

The Home Line ran its lanes dark, and Hollis Vane ran the Old Yard dark late that night, with the lamp over the first shed put out and the yard as black as the inside of a boot.

"The Line's way," he said. He stood at the sheds' end with five of Seven in front of him in a row, and five strips of black cloth over his arm. "You've all run lanes lit. This isn't that. You run it from the sheds, dark. You touch every post as you pass, so you know where you are. Before the eighth, the one in front calls the turn, and which way, so the one behind turns with him. At the last post there's a bar. You put your hands on it and you say who has it."

"Why?" said Pru.

"Because that's how the Line trains. You learn a road with your feet." He gave her a cloth. "I've a post in my casualty road and a sponsor who says I've never looked at it. I'm looking at it now."

Kiva was not in the row.

She was at the far end, forty paces off, at the foot of the leaning post, on the cross. Vane had put her there. *You read ground. Aske's passed you for a stance. Stand on it and tell me what that post does when somebody puts a hand on it.*

She had told him about Sunday. He had listened, and said nothing, and put her there.

"Cold," she said, to her own collar, and opened the still door.

She set it by its corners, wide. It went down through the packed dirt. The yard took her, as it always had.

And under the yard, a long way down, the thing that she had followed on her knees came up into her heels. It was stronger than on Sunday. It drew, and gave, slow and even, and it was right underneath her, as near as the floor of a cellar.

She knelt, keeping the stance, and laid both hands flat on the dirt beside the cross.

Orla was on her crate by the first shed, a dark shape against a darker one. Kiva could not see her. She knew where she was by the stick, which tapped once on the dirt, as if to say that she was there and had nothing to add.

"*Go,*" said Vane.

They came.

She heard them before anything. A great many boots on packed dirt, at a jog, and somebody's knuckles striking wood, and a grunt.

"Post," said Perrin's voice. "With my shoulder. Found it."

"Keep going," said Vane, behind them.

"Post," said Perrin, a little later, more thickly. "Face. That one was my face."

"It was," said Kellan, a little behind him, quite calm. "I heard it."

There was laughter, cut off.

They were coming down the lane in a string, five of them, each with a hand out. Kiva could hear the posts take them: the dull knock of a palm on old wood, left side, right side, as the rows went. She could hear Anwen breathing, very even, and Pru saying *sorry* to a post.

"*Turn!*" Perrin, before the eighth. Loud. "*Turn left!*"

"*Left,*" said Jude, behind him, and she heard his boots change.

They came round the eighth post. Perrin came on, wide, with both hands out in front of him like a man looking for a door in a dark room. She heard him come. She did not say anything. He was not hers to steer.

His hands found the bar.

He found it with his forearm first, at the height of his chin, and said *oof,* and then he had both hands on it, one each side of the post.

"Cade has the bar," he said.

He stood there. She could feel him through the ground, a big weight set wide, a yard from her knees.

"Why does it lean?" said Perrin. He said it into the dark, as one asks a thing that has been bothering one for a year. "Sir. Why does this one lean?"

"Because the road isn't straight," said Vane.

He said it quite flatly, from somewhere near the eighth post, as a man gives the name of a tram stop.

"Which road?"

"Two to the bar, Cade."

Boots. A second weight came round the eighth post and arrived at the other side of the iron, and a hand met it.

"Talley has the bar," said Jude.

"Then lean on it," said Vane. "It's a bar through a post. Let's see what it does."

"It's a post," said Perrin. "It's in the ground. It's—" He leaned. Kiva felt him lean. "It doesn't do anything, sir. It's a post."

"Open your door, Cade."

Perrin opened it.

She felt that, too. A still door came open a yard from her, wide and heavy and quite without hurry, the way he had opened it on the Long Terrace under a plate, and his weight went down into the dirt beside hers.

Something under the ground groaned.

It came up through the soles of her boots and through her palms. It was iron, a long way down, moving against something that had held it for a very long time.

"It's moving," said Jude. His voice had gone high. "Cade. It's moving."

"I know it's moving. I'm the one moving it." Perrin's breath came short. "Lean, Talley. Don't shove. Lean."

The bar went round. Kiva heard it go: a slow grinding, like a millstone, and the old wood of the post creaking in its iron bands. A hand. Another hand. Under her own hands the water in the ground drew, and gave, and drew.

Then the bar came up hard.

It came up without a ring, against something dense that gave a hair and would give no more, as a spade goes into wet clay and stops, and the two of them stopped with it.

"It's stuck," said Jude.

"It's not stuck. It's—" Perrin leaned. "It's like leaning on a sandbag. There's something in the way down there. Mud, or worse."

And under the ground, under the post, eight feet down, something answered.

It was a sound. It was deep and hollow, like a cellar door let fall at the bottom of a house, and the whole Old Yard heard it. Kiva felt it go up through her knees. Somebody at the sheds' end said *what was that* in a voice that was not Seven's. Orla's stick struck the dirt once, hard.

And the water under Kiva's hands faltered.

It had been drawing. It drew half its way, and hitched, as a breath hitches when somebody comes into the room. It stood a moment on nothing. Then it came on again, short. It gave, short. It drew again, and it was not lying still, and it was not what it had been either. It was a thing that had been going one way for a long time and had been leaned on.

"*Off,*" said Kiva. "Hands off. Both of you. *Off.*"

Perrin had been given that word, and he knew what it was for. She heard his hands come off the iron, and Jude's after.

"Kiva?" said Anwen, from the dark by the eighth post. "What is it?"

"The water," she said. "It's the main. It's right under the post. When the bar went round, it—" She did not have a word. "It stumbled."

Nobody said anything.

There was a sound at the gap in the low wall, of somebody coming through it too fast in a skirt. Then a lantern. It came along the lane with its shutter open, and its light went over five people with black cloth round their eyes, and an old woman on a crate, and a watch officer with his hat in his hand, and came to rest on a black post with a bar through its top that stood a quarter round from where it had stood that afternoon.

Mara was wet to the shoulder of her left arm. She had come up a ladder.

"Who touched it?" she said.

"Cade," said Vane. "And Talley. Two to the bar. It's a Line drill."

"I had my hand on the water at the head of the hill," said Mara. Her voice was not quite her own. "Where it goes in under the gate. It was rising and falling as it's done for a fortnight. Then there was a sound under the road, a long way off, like a door. And the water faltered under my hand. It came up halfway, and stopped, and came on short." She looked at the post. "It's still short."

She knelt down beside Kiva, in the dirt, and put her wet left hand flat on the ground beside the cross.

She kept it there for a long moment.

"It's a gate," said Mara.

Kiva had known it since the sound went up through her knees. She had not had the word for it.

"It's a gate," she said. "The post. It's the handle of a gate."

"And it isn't shut." Mara did not move her hand. "It's gone down part of the way and stuck on something. It's hanging in the water. I can feel the water going round it." She drew a breath. "Stopgate. Heaven help me. I've walked up it for thirty years."

Somebody took the cloth off their eyes. It was Perrin. He stood by the post in the lantern light and looked at the bar, and at his own two hands, as if they had done something without asking him.

"I only asked why it leaned," he said.

"I know," said Vane. He put his hat back on. "I've a man with a saw coming in the morning. I shall send him home and ask him whether he's a shovel."
