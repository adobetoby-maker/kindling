# Chapter 43 — Kiva: A Drain in a Box

The Halden Works lent its license yard on Sunday afternoon, and Foreman Birch made it very plain whose yard it was.

"My slate," he said, at the rail. "My ward line. My string, that I've had in that cage since the thaw, and I know its temper. The Municipal Works has asked for a floor that keeps step, and there's only the one in Meridian with a rift on it. It's got it. Nobody lights so much as a pipe on that brick until I've said."

It was a bigger floor than the Kennel's. It was paved in blue brick, with a lattice house at the north end and a paid ward line in black standing easy along the wall. Kiva had not been inside the refinery gate before. She had counted the rivets on the way under it, since somebody had once told her the number, and had lost count at two hundred.

What Mara had built stood down the middle of the yard.

It was a trough. It was twelve paces long, knee high, made of new planks and lined with lead, and it had a foot of water in it. At the south end stood a brewer's hand pump with its handle off, and beside the pump, on a bench, six cells in a row with their needles under glass.

All six needles were nodding. The pump drew, and let go, as they did. Down the whole length of the trough the water rose a finger on the lead, and fell.

"There's your main," said Mara. "And there's your pump house. Mr. Adlam's people stepped that bank for me at eight this morning, with a pinch of their own salt, by the book. It took them four minutes. They were very civil about it."

"And that?" said Kiva.

At the north end of the trough, twelve paces from the bench, there was a stool. The stool stood with its feet in the water. On it sat a single cell with a needle of its own.

"That's a drum on Stopgate. One plain cell, filled at eight, on its own time. There isn't a wire to it. There's nothing between that cell and the bench but twelve paces of water going up and down." Mara looked at it with dislike. "At eight it stood. At ten it had a tremble. I'd like you to come to the rail and tell me what it's doing now. Not past the rail."

Kiva went to the rail.

The needle on the stool went up, and came down. It did it with the other six.

"It's in," she said.

"It went in at ten minutes to twelve. Mrs. Renn had her glass on it."

There was a woman at the bench whom Kiva had never met and knew at once. She was narrow and upright, with a grader's seal on a cord round her neck, and she had Kellan's eyes. In front of her stood a brass instrument like a clock with no hands.

"It didn't go all at once," said Kellan's mother. She did not look up from the glass. "It leaned for an hour. Then it went. I'd say that a cell is a patient sort of thing, and it's been pushed from underneath, quite gently, a great many times."

"Then it's the water," said Rusk.

He stood behind the bench with his thumbs in his waistband. He looked like a man who has slept in a chair.

"It's the water in a box," said Mara. "I'll not say more than that until tomorrow night, and nor should you. I've a paper under seal." She took a half sheet from her apron. "But I'll say what we've all been assuming, since Thursday, as though it were written in the patent. I've put it down so that it can be wrong in front of everybody."

She read it out.

"*That the water moves because the pumps move it. Stop the pumps and the main lies still. Then a bank may be quenched and filled fresh, and it will keep the time that it is given.*"

"That's the plan," said Rusk. "That's the whole of the plan, if the fence fails. I stop my three pumps for as long as the sump will bear, and I put every hand in the Works on that bank at once, one to a cell. That brings my four hours down to half of one."

"It's a good plan. I'd have drawn it myself." Mara put the half sheet down on the bench, under the brass weight that she had brought from her own counter. "I'd like to see it work once in a box before I trust a district to it. And Officer Vane wants to know what a string makes of a floor like this one. Mr. Birch has been kind enough to say that we may ask it."

"I said you might ask," said Birch. "I didn't say it'd answer politely."

There were a good many people along the rail. The Placement Chair had come, since there were students on a floor with a rift at one end of it, and she stood by the gate with her hands folded. Hollis Vane was there with his slate. Rhea Sorn had brought Pike, and Greta Stroud, and Ruddock with his bag, and nobody else.

The fours were along the east wall, dark. Old Four had come, and Twenty-Eight with its reserve blade, and Halden's own three in their black and gold. Seven stood at the end.

"Gate," said Birch.

Four of his paid wards walked out onto the blue brick. They brought a wall up twelve paces west of the trough, as the Circuit's book had required since the spring, silver and square.

"That's the brightest thing on my floor. It's lit, and it's worked, and it's nearer than the bench. Any string I ever let out of that cage has gone to a gate like a dog to its dinner." He raised his arm. "Lattice. Give me the door."

The cage at the north end opened with a rattle.

Kiva had seen a good many strings by now. This one was small. It had six knots and not eight, and it stood no higher than a tram's window, on four legs. It had frost on it from crown to knee.

It came out onto the brick and stopped. Its blunt top turned, slowly, a quarter of a circle, as a weathercock turns.

It turned past the gate.

It did not pause on it. Four people were burning ash to make the brightest thing in the yard, and the grey head went by them as a man's eyes go by a stranger's in a street. It came round to the south, and stopped, pointing down twelve paces of lead-lined trough at a bench.

"That's not right," said Birch.

The string walked. It walked to the north end of the trough and stood there, astride the stool, with one pair of legs on either side of the water. The cell on the stool went on nodding between its knees.

Then the shine on it moved. It ran down off the shoulders, as Kiva had seen it run once before, in the Pit, in the spring. Two white cords went down from the lowest knot, as thick as a wrist, and into the water.

"Sorn," said Vane.

"I see it." Rhea had not moved from the rail. "It did that on the bus, in the fifth month. I said then that it had found something better than a gate. I didn't know what the something was."

"It's found the beat," said Mara. Her voice was very quiet. "It doesn't want the bright thing. It wants the thing that keeps time."

"Then I'll have a rung off it while it's standing still to be cut," said Birch. "That's what my paper says a string in my yard is for. Renn."

---

Kellan Renn had come through the refinery gate that afternoon in his own coat, and for the first time in his life he had not counted the rivets.

He had been thinking about his feet.

Birch had not answered his letter. Birch did not write. He had been standing at the rail when the students came in, with his slate, and he had looked at each of them as a grader looks at a tin. When he came to Kellan he had looked down.

*They've not got worse,* he had said. *Tuesdays and Thursdays. Bring your own cuff.*

That was the whole of it. It was a place, and nothing was owed for it, and there was no jacket on a peg.

"Top rung," said Birch now. "Yarrow has the cloth. He kneels where you put him, and you ask him. I'm told that's how it's done by people who mean to bag anything."

"Yes, sir."

He walked out onto the blue brick, dark.

He disliked it at once. It was not the string. He had cut that string's top rung nine times since the seventh month, and he knew how it stood. It was the floor. There was something coming up through his boot soles, slow and even, like the tread of a great many people going past on the other side of a wall. His feet wanted to fall in with it.

He had the best feet in the School. He had been told so since he was fourteen. It was a new thing to find that they could be asked to do something by a floor.

*Count against it,* he thought. He had watched a girl do that on the cinders of the Pit, with a hold's corner planted under her heel. He had not understood what she was at until a blade with a broken leg had explained it to a cart.

He counted in threes. It was the first number that came. His feet steadied.

Yarrow was laying the cloth. He was a still-door, long and incurious, and he threw a sheet as a man lays a table that he has laid before. He put it on the east side of the trough, hard up against the planks, under the string's top knot.

"That's close to the water," said Kellan.

"It's where it'll fall."

"It's a pace from a thing that's got two cords down in it."

"I've sixty counts," said Yarrow, patiently. "Ash doesn't run. It lies. It lies where it lands and leans to the nearest lamp, and I've put myself between it and the bench. What else is there?"

It was a fair answer. It was what the book said. Kellan looked at the trough, and at the white cords going down into it, and could not find the place in the book where it said anything else.

He looked along the east wall instead. He did it without quite meaning to. There was a catcher there who was particular about ground.

She had her eyes on the cloth. She was frowning. But she was not his catcher today, and she had not been asked, and he saw her shut her mouth on whatever it was.

"Renn," said Birch. "While we're young."

He went up the back of it dark, on his struts, in two bounds.

"Now?"

"Not yet," said Yarrow. "It's swaying. Now."

He lit.

It was four-tenths. He had it to a hair. The edge went through the top knot level and came out the other side, and he was dark again before his feet had found the brick. He had never made a cleaner one in that yard.

Sixty drams fell like sand out of a split sack.

"*Dark! One!*"

It did not lie.

He saw it from three paces. The heap hit the canvas and spread, as a heap does, and then the near edge of it began to move. It did not drift. It ran. It went across the waxed cloth toward the trough in a grey tongue, as flour runs to the low side of a tilted board, and over the hem, and up the planks.

It was climbing the cord.

"*Corners!*" Yarrow had the hem in both fists. His voice had gone up. "Corners, *now,* it's going—"

Pettit and Hesketh hauled. The neck came in. It closed on what was left, and Yarrow's cord went round it three times as fast as Kellan had ever seen a cord go.

"Sealed," said Yarrow. "Twenty—" He stopped. He weighed the bag in his two hands, as a catcher will. "That's not thirty. That's not anything like thirty."

There was a grey scum on the water.

It lay all down the trough, from the stool to the pump, moving with the rise and fall. Half a rung of open ash had gone into a foot of water inside ten counts.

"The bench," said Kellan's mother.

She said it quite quietly. He turned his head.

All six needles had jumped. He saw them do it, together, like six hands flung up. They stood for a moment against their upper pins, quivering. Then they lay down. They did not come down one after another, as cells die in a bank. They went as one, and the glass over the third cell cracked across with a small flat sound.

The pump stopped.

"That's fouled," said Rusk. His voice was thick. "That's one pinch of open ash in the water, and six cells cooked inside a breath. All six. There wasn't one of them that could say no to the others."

"There were six," said Mara. "I've four-and-twenty."

Nobody answered her.

Kellan stood on the blue brick with his blade dark on its cuff. He had cut a rung as well as he knew how, on the word of a catcher who had done what the book said, and it had killed a bank.

*I asked,* he thought. *I asked him and he told me, and it was a good answer.*

He had learned to ask. It had cost him a season. It had not until this moment crossed his mind that asking the catcher was no use at all when the catcher did not know either.

---

"The pump's dead," said Kiva.

She had not been asked. She had said it aloud all the same, since it was the thing that was there.

"We can see that the pump's dead," said Birch.

"The water isn't."

They looked.

The brewer's pump stood with its plunger down. The six cells on the bench lay cold. Nothing in that yard was drawing on the trough, and all down its twelve paces the grey water rose a finger on the lead, and fell.

In. And out.

Mara walked the length of it. She walked slowly, with her eyes on the wet line. She stopped at the north end, where the string stood astride the stool on its four legs, with five knots left and two white cords going down between them.

The cords were moving. They drew up, a hand, and let down. Three grey knots above them leaned back a little as they did it, and eased, like three people hauling a neck shut on a called word.

"It's pumping," said Mara.

She turned round. Her face was perfectly composed, and Kiva had never seen it so white.

"There. That's my half sheet. *Stop the pumps and the main lies still.* It doesn't. If there's one of those standing in it, the pumps may stop and the bank may cook, and the water goes on keeping time, since the thing's learned the stroke and has two arms down the hole." She picked the paper off the bench. "And I'd have filled four-and-twenty cells fresh, in the dark, on the strength of that sentence."

"Fill the one," said Rusk. "The stool. Show me the rest of it. I'd sooner see the whole of what I've got wrong."

A Works fitter did it, at arm's length, with the string's legs on either side of him and a ward of Birch's between. He quenched the cell on the stool, and drew it, and slid a fresh can home.

"Open," he said. "One. Two."

At forty the needle rose and stood, on a time of its own. The fitter let out his breath.

At fifty-one it trembled.

At fifty-eight it went up, and came down, with the water.

"Inside the count," said Pike. He was looking at it as he looked at a neck. "Fresh ash is open for a slow sixty. It takes the first pattern that touches it. I've said that to every corner-hand I ever trained, about a lit blade." He shook his square head. "I never thought to say it about a puddle."

"So I can't quench it while it's in the water," said Rusk. "And I can't stop the water while it's in the water. And I can't cut it while it's in the water, or the rung runs down its own arm and cooks my bank." He counted them off on his thumbs. "Then it must come out of the water. How does one take a thing like that by the arm?"

"You don't put a ward on it," said Greta Stroud.

She said it from the rail, hoarsely, without being asked.

"I've said that on two floors now. I'll say it on every one I'm ever stood on. I put a wall against a cord like that in the spring, and I meant to keep hold. It went up me to the elbows."

"Iron, then," said Rusk. "Dead iron. Something with nothing in it to take."

They tried iron. It was a fair thing to try.

The fitter had a boat-hook. He stood well back, on the west side, and reached across the trough with the full length of it, and got the hook under the nearer cord where it left the water.

Frost ran up the shaft like a fuse.

"*Let go!*" said Rhea Sorn.

He had let go already. He was not a fool. The boat-hook hung in the air for a moment, white from end to end, with nobody holding it. Then the cord drew up, a hand, and the hook went with it and was laid down in the water as gently as a spoon.

The fitter stood looking at his glove. The palm of it had gone stiff, and cracked when he bent it.

"That's my left," he said. "And me right-handed, if anybody's writing."

"It's noted," said Perrin, with feeling, from the wall.

Nobody laughed much.

Kiva had been watching the cord.

She knew that rise and fall. She had had it in a tray between her palms, in the Pit, for one slack count, light as a clothesline. She had not held it. That was the whole point of what she had done. She had put a pan under it at the instant that it stopped pulling, and had been somewhere else before it began again.

"It's got a slack," she said. "Four counts hauling and four easing. A ward for one tap, on the slack, and folded before the haul comes back. It doesn't take a ward that isn't there any longer."

"That's a catcher's notion," said Pike.

"It's what I did. I only did it once."

"I know what you did. I was behind three posts with three cloths." He was not looking at her. He was looking along the east wall, at a small round first-year with a yellow card. "It wants the best fold in the yard. That isn't yours."

"No," said Kiva. "It's hers."

Pru Finch looked behind her, to see who was meant.

"Me?"

"You fold from the far edge. You don't let go. You've always got the last of it in your palms." Kiva heard that she was talking quickly, and slowed. "I learned it from you on a pail. I do it with two fingers stuck out like a fork. You'd do it properly."

"I can't go anywhere with it. I haven't an Ember. I can't put it down."

"Nobody's asking you to go anywhere. I'd like to know whether it can be lifted off the water at all. For a count. By a ward that isn't taken."

Pru looked at Rhea Sorn.

"Your card says a corner and a lamp," said Rhea. "And the cord. I underlined the cord on Friday." The pale eyes rested on her. "It doesn't say this. So it's yours to refuse, and I'll think none the worse. Stroud will tell you what it costs if it's late."

"Six weeks," said Stroud. "And a spoon held for you."

Pru swallowed. She put her card away in her tunic, and buttoned the pocket.

"I'd want it called," she said. "The slack. I can't watch the cord and my hands."

"I'll call it," said Kiva.

It was out before she had thought. Of course she would call it. It was a hem under load, with a count in it, and she was the catcher. She had called the count on every sheet that Seven had thrown since the fifth month.

She went and knelt at the rail of the trough, a long pace from the planks. She laid her hands on the blue brick, the right palm and the heel of the left.

It came up through the brick, slow and even. She could feel the haul in it. She could feel the easing.

Pru knelt on the east side, three paces from the string's near leg, with a ward of Birch's standing over her like a porch. She put up her round hands. The silver stood between them, no bigger than a tea tray, and she bent it into the bowl of a spoon.

"*Out,*" said Kiva. "Two. Three. Four. *In*—"

She knew as she said it.

It was late. It was not much late. It was the width of the difference between two hands, of which one told her the truth and the other told her that there was a floor. She had taken the middle of them, as she had for four years, and the middle was wrong by half a count.

Pru slid the pan under.

The cord was not slack. It was just beginning to draw. It came up into the bowl of that small bright ward with the whole haul of three knots behind it, and Kiva saw the silver go grey at the rim, like a window breathed on.

"*Fold!*" That was Pike, and Stroud, and Anwen, all at once.

Pru folded.

She did it faster than Kiva had ever seen anything done with a ward. Far edge. Near edge. A penny. Shut. The cord fell back into the water with a slap.

She sat down on the brick with her hands held up in front of her face.

They were white. They were white to the first joint, all eight fingertips and the two thumbs, as though she had dipped them in milk.

"They've gone to sleep," she said, in a high surprised voice. "They've all gone to sleep. I'm narrating. I can't feel my card. I had my card."

Ruddock was over the rail already, with a towel. Anwen was a pace behind him.

Kiva had not moved. She was still kneeling, with her hands on the brick.

"Who called that?" said Pike.

"I did."

"I know that you did. I want the rest of it."

She made herself say it to the whole yard, in a carrying voice, as she would have called a mark.

"I called the *in* half a count late. I had it through two hands and I took the middle. One of them can't hear." Her mouth was dry. "I told a yard on Friday night that I couldn't take a count any longer by myself. I said that somebody else would have to have it. Then I knelt down here and took it, since it's what I've always done. That's mine. It's her hands."

"It's the tips," said Ruddock, over his shoulder. "It's a nip. She folded inside it. Ten minutes, and she'll be able to pick her nose."

"I don't," said Pru faintly.

Kiva stood up. Her knees were not quite steady.

"Brask," she said. "You called the Pit's. Would you call this?"

Lowen Brask had been standing with Old Four, with his notebook shut. He came along the wall without hurry.

"I'd call it," he said. "I'd sooner you'd asked me five minutes ago. I was standing there with it in my mouth." He looked at her, quite kindly. "You said it on Friday in the Graywater yard, I'm told. It's all over the Hall. *The count of a thing. Somebody else has that.* It's a good rule. You'll have to keep it on the days that you're frightened as well."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm only saying it as it was said to me." He turned to the trough. "Finch. When you're ready. Not before."

It was twelve minutes before the color came back. Pru would not be hurried, and nobody hurried her. Then she knelt down again in the same place, with her jaw set, and put up her hands.

"*Out,*" said Brask. His voice was as even as a clock. "Two. Three. Four. *In.*"

The pan went under.

For one count the white cord lay in a small silver spoon, a hand's breadth above the water, light as a clothesline.

"*Two.* Fold."

It was gone. The cord dropped.

And down the whole length of the trough, the water missed a beat.

Kiva saw it. Everybody saw it. The grey scum rose a finger on the lead, and fell, and rose, and then for the space of one slow breath it only lay there and rocked.

On the stool, between the string's knees, the single needle checked. It stood for a count. Then the water drew again, and it went on.

"There," said Kellan's mother, from her glass. "It fell out. For as long as that arm was off the water, that cell had nobody to follow. It came back in as soon as it was asked."

"Then it wants more than a tap," said Rusk slowly.

"It wants the arm kept off," said Mara. "Lifted, and taken away, and put down in dry ground, and held there. For as long as it takes a Works crew to do the rest." She did not look at the east wall. "I've seen that done. I sat in a gallery and watched it. I don't know of any other hand that can."

Nobody in the yard looked at Kiva.

It was a strange thing to be at the middle of. Forty people had all at once found something to study in the brick, or the lattice, or their own boots.

"I'll say it, since it's on my board," said Ysra Crest, from the gate. Her level voice carried the whole floor. "*It is not to be asked of her.* I wrote that under a result, with my name. I meant it. Nobody in this yard will ask."

"Nor will it be offered," said Rhea Sorn. "Not on my card."

She had turned her back on the trough. She was speaking to Vane, and to Rusk, and to a woman in a work apron, and not to a girl of seventeen.

"She's attached to me. That's my hand on her entry and my name against whatever becomes of her. I had her spend two fingers on a floor of mine three Saturdays ago, with the price read out first, and I told her afterward that I'd never once named a price before I cut." Her voice was flat. "I've named this one to myself. It's too dear. I'll not have a catcher of mine go up in the dark over a hole in the road and come down with half a hand, for a plan that had a wrong sentence in it an hour ago. Find me another way."

"I'd be glad to," said Mara. "What is it?"

"Plank the holes. Every one. Give it no water to put an arm in. Then it's a string on a street, and I take it down a rung at a time on a gate, as I've taken two hundred."

"And if it finds a hole that we didn't?"

"Then I'll cut it where it stands and bag what I can."

"With its arm down my drain. You watched what half a rung did to six cells."

"I watched."

They looked at one another. They were not angry. Kiva saw that, and it frightened her more than anger would have. They were two women who were each right about a thing, and the things did not fit.

She had an answer.

It was a good one. It was about a foreman with nobody south of a string, and a hand that was already spent, and what a catcher was for. It would have sounded very well on that brick.

She looked at Pru's fingers. They were pink again, and Pru was holding them up to the light one at a time, as though she had been given them for a present.

*I'd an answer ten minutes ago,* thought Kiva. *It was to kneel down and call it.*

She put the good one where she had put the others.

"Sorn's right that it isn't hers to have offered," she said. "I've nothing to say. I'd like to know how you get that one back in its cage."

Birch grunted.

"That's the first sensible question since two o'clock. I don't. Not with its arms in a trough." He jerked his head at the fitter. "There's a bung in the south end of that. Pull it."

The fitter pulled it.

The water went out across the blue brick in a grey sheet and found the yard's drain. The trough emptied in a slow minute. The wet line went down the lead, and down, and there was a foot of air where the water had been.

The two white cords hung in it.

They hung for perhaps ten counts, reaching, with nothing to reach. Then they simply let go. They came apart from the bottom, softly, into loose grey ash that pattered on the lead like rain.

The shine went back up onto the string's shoulders. Its blunt head lifted. It turned, a quarter of a circle, and found a wall of silver twelve paces to the west, and walked to it and leaned.

"Now it's a string," said Birch. "Now I know what to do with it."

"You took the water away," said Kiva.

"I pulled a bung. I can't pull a bung on the Ferren main."

"No." Mara had bent over the empty trough. She was looking at the lead, and the wet, and the little heap of grey sweepings where two arms had been. "No, nobody can drain a main. But one might shut a piece of it. A sluice. A gate, let down across the brick, with the hill on one side and the low streets on the other. They had them on every drain in this city before there were pumps." She straightened. "I've never seen one on that plan."

"The plan's been tidied," said Kiva.

"So it has."

"Why is it called Stopgate?"

She had not known that she was going to ask it. It had been lying in her head since Tuesday, with a great many other things that she did not know the use of.

Mara opened her mouth. She shut it again.

"I've no idea," she said. "I've walked up it for thirty years. I supposed that it was somebody's name."

Noa was on the Library step at eight on Monday, as he had said, and Kiva was beside him.

The Reader let them in herself. She was small and upright, with two pairs of spectacles, and she looked at Kiva as one looks at a borrower who has brought a book back clean.

"The post," she said. "I've been waiting for somebody from that School to ask me since 2649. I'd begun to think that I should have to put it in my will." She took a lamp from its hook. "Three flights. Mind your heads on the second."

The superseded stacks smelled of cold iron and paste. She went along a row without reading the labels and drew out a thick grey volume with a broken spine.

"The *Standard Lane Manual,* eleventh edition. Withdrawn 2603. There's the lane."

It was halfway down a page. It had the exercise set out in six ruled columns, in print, and a seventh squeezed in at the right.

*The lane with the crooked posts. Why: because the road isn't straight.*

Beside it, in pencil, in a very small hand, somebody had written a line. *Retired from standard ed., 2603. Committee vote 11–6. Original reason retained here.*

"That's a junior archivist," said the Reader. "She'd no leave to write in it. I've blessed her for it rather often."

"*Because the road isn't straight,*" said Noa. He read it twice, with his lips. "That isn't a reason. It's—"

"A riddle. So the committee said. It's in the minutes." The Reader turned back a leaf. "Now read me the other six columns."

Kiva read them. The first was only the lane's name, and where it lay. *What:* nine posts in two rows. *How:* run dark, on a count. *Went wrong:* a collision at the ninth. *Cost.* *Second try.* It was all there, tidy and complete.

"It says posts," she said slowly. "*The lane with the crooked posts.* There's only the one."

"Is there?" The Reader looked at her over the upper spectacles. "In the eleventh edition there's a plate. I'll show you."

It was an engraving, brown with age. It showed a strip of ground under a wall, with two rows of posts down it, and not one of them stood square to its neighbor. The whole lane curved. It bent like a river, from the near end to the far, and the last post of the south row stood a pace out from all the rest with something drawn across its top.

"That's a bar," said Noa. "Through the post. Like a capstan."

"The School straightened the lane in 2390, when it built the wall higher. It's in the bursar's accounts. *To setting the posts fair, eleven days.* They couldn't shift the last one. It doesn't say why." She shut the book. "And that's all that my shelf can give you. A plate, and a riddle, and a clerk's pencil. I can tell you that a reason was there. I can't tell you what it was. This is the manual. It kept the why and lost the what."

Somebody was coming down the iron stair.

He came at a walk. He had his hat in one hand and a long flat tin case under his arm.

"Then I've the other half," said Hollis Vane. "I was until two this morning finding it. It was in the chest under the bearer's box, with the Line's lane sheets, which nobody has unrolled since my grandfather was a boy." He laid the case on the reading shelf. "It's in the record hand. I can't read a word of it. I've brought it to the only person in Meridian who can."

The Reader put on the second spectacles over the first.

It was a single sheet, soft as cloth, ruled in seven columns. She bent over it for some time.

"This is older than my manual," she said. "It's a copy, in the record hand, of something a good deal older again. It's the same entry. It isn't the same words." She read it out, slowly, a column at a time.

"*The lane with the crooked posts. At Meridian, under the south wall, on the line of the hill drain.*

"*What: nine posts, set as the drain runs, which is not straight. The ninth is the sluice post. It stands out from the wall for the bar.*

"*How: run it dark, from the sheds. Call the turn before the eighth, aloud, and say who has the bar. Two to the bar. Nine turns shuts the hill from the low streets.*

"*Went wrong: ran it straight and silent, and met the ninth. Also: two came to the bar, and nobody had said which two.*

"*Cost: a collarbone. A cellar in the Hythe, the night that nobody had said.*

"*Second try: called it, and said which two. Shut in nine turns.*

"*Why: because the road isn't straight. S.V.*"

Nobody spoke.

Kiva found that she had her right hand flat on the shelf, as she would have laid it on a pavement.

"It's a gate," she said. "The post. It isn't for practice. It's the handle of a gate." She could hear her own voice, and it sounded very young. "The drain turns there. I felt it turn. They put the lane on top of the drain so that everybody who ever trained in that yard would know the way to it in the dark. And they made you say it aloud. Which way, and who."

"*Call the turn before the eighth,*" said Vane.

He was looking at her.

"I stood in that yard on Saturday night and watched you make a carry team shout *post, left* over its shoulder. I thought that it was neat. I thought that you'd made it up." He put his hat down on the tin case. "You had. That's what I can't get past. Somebody wrote that down five hundred years ago, and a school lost it, and a committee voted on it, and you made it up again in a quarter of an hour because two litters hit a post."

"It was Perrin who said it was a lane call."

"I dare say it was." He turned to the Reader. "May that sheet go to the head of Stopgate? Today? I'll sign for it under the watch's name."

"It may go wherever the Line carries it. It's the Line's. I've only read it to you." She took off the second spectacles. "You'll want a spade, Officer. That plate shows the bar a hand above the ground. It's had a good many centuries of yard swept over its feet."

"I've a man with a saw," said Vane. "I told a sponsor so. I shall send him home and ask him whether he's a shovel."

---

Drum twenty-eight fell in at eighteen minutes to eight that evening, with thirty people at its rail to watch it.

Kiva was on the pavement. She had been told not to come nearer, and she did not need to. She saw it through the green door, past her aunt's shoulder.

The needle stood. It trembled. Then it went up, without any hurry, and came down. It had done it before anybody had drawn breath to say so.

Below it on the hill, behind two more green doors, two needles lay against their pins in a pair of drums that had been dry since Wednesday midnight.

The Chair's clerk had the paper. He broke the wax in front of Rusk and gave it to him.

Rusk read it aloud. He read it in a flat voice, to the wardens, and the fitters, and a pleasant man in a black coat who had taken off his hat.

"*If it is the drain: drum twenty-eight, on Monday the third of next month, with two dry drums between.*" He folded it along its crease. "It's the third. That's twenty-eight. Those are two dry drums." He looked down the street, at the dim brown windows of the middle stretch. "I've had six hundred families on boxes for five days, in the wet, to learn that I was wrong. I said that I'd carry that. It's heavier than it looked on Wednesday."

"A sixth time," said Mr. Adlam. He said it very quietly. "I shall have to write to the technical office that there is a thing which its patent does not describe. They will not like it. I rather think that I do."

"You stopped nothing," said Mara, to Rusk. "And you did no harm. I'd have done the same in your chair, and I said so in that room."

"You did. I'd it written." He took off his linen cuff guards, one after the other, which she had never seen him do, and put them in his pocket. "It's two days to twenty-nine, and two more to a school. Mrs. Rowan. You called the drum. You found the drain. I'm told that you've a sheet of paper, as of this morning, with a sluice on it." He held out the trunk plan, rolled. "It's yours. The hill, the bank, and the stopping of it. I'll sign whatever you draw, and I'll put my forty operators where you say."

Mara did not take it at once.

"I don't want it," she said.

"No. I'd noticed. That's a good part of why."

She took it. She held it for a moment in her left hand, the reading one, as she would have held a can that might be cut.

Then she looked round for her clerk.

"Kiva," she said. "The book. And find me a spade."
