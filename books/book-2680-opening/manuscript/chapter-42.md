# Chapter 42 — Kiva: The Ninth Post

Healer Aske saw the hand on Friday at seven, and for the first time since the spring he did not take out the pin.

"There's no use my pricking those two," he said. "I know what they'll say. I'll look at the rest." He turned the hand over under the lamp, and pressed the palm, and the root of the thumb, and each of the last two fingers in turn. "When. When. When. Good. That's a hand with eight parts and it's had a fortnight, all but a day."

"So I'm passed."

"For what?" He let go. "You're passed to catch. You always were. You're passed for one door at a time, dark hands, from this morning; a step, a stance, a ward for a tap. I'm told that you test the ground before you'll stand on it. I approve of that." He sat back. "From tomorrow, the first, the fortnight's up. So I've to say something about the other, and I've thought about what."

He took his time over it.

"A crossing, one, in seven days, as before. The longer thing I won't put a word to. If anybody means to direct it, he sends for me, and I say the price to him myself, before, with you listening. I won't have it carried up a hill by a runner." He folded his hands. "I don't know what the price is now. That's the truth of it. The two fingers have nothing left to lose. It took them first because they'd been hurt first. I don't know what it takes second."

"You could guess."

"I could. I shan't, at seven in the morning, to a girl who'd believe me." He stood. "I'd as soon that nobody sent for me. I've no curiosity in the matter whatever."

Mara had forty corks in a string bag, and she went up Stopgate with them like a woman delivering eggs.

She had begun at the pump house sump at six, with Old Lumb watching from his bucket, and worked uphill from there a manhole at a time. A cork went down the upper hole. Then she walked back down to the one below and watched for it. Every one had come through, turning a little on the water, in no hurry. By ten she was at the foot of Stopgate, and Kiva met her at the rail of the twenty-fifth drum house.

It was a clear cold morning. The hill was quieter than it ought to have been. The two dry drums had six hundred households on their boxes, most of them in the streets behind. On the hill itself it was the middle stretch, and one could tell which houses by the windows, which had the dim brownish look of lamps turned down to save a can. A cart from the casualty reserve stood at the rail of the twenty-sixth drum house with its tailboard down.

"You'll watch," said Mara. "I'll drop. You've the better eyes and I've the better knees, which is a thing I never expected to say."

They did it the same way at each grating. Mara walked up to the next one, between the tram rails, and knelt, and let a cork go through the bars. Kiva knelt at the one below with the lamp let down on its chain, and watched the black shine at the bottom of the brick.

The cork came. It came out of the dark under the hill, bobbing, carried down on the slow give of the water, and stopped, and went back a little when the water drew, and came on again.

"There," said Kiva.

"Good," said Mara, from up the hill. "Again."

A tram went by them, clanging, and the motorman leaned out to look at a Grade Two operator on her knees in his road.

"I'd a thought in the night," said Mara, at the twenty-eighth. She was a little out of breath. "I'll tell it you, since you'll not make more of it than it is. If I'm right, and the water's doing it, then every one of these holes is a place where what's underneath can get at what's on top. I don't like a hole. I shall ask Rusk for planks."

"For every grating?"

"For every one on this hill, and sandbags to weight them. It'll flood the gutters if it rains. I'd sooner have wet boots." She let the cork go. "What did your flagstone say on Thursday?"

"That something was pushing. Very small."

"It's not small up here." She watched Kiva's lamp. "Is it coming?"

It came.

The last drum house was near the top, where the houses grew taller and had brass on their doors. Above that there was only the last grating, at the very head of the street, and beyond it the wall.

Kiva had come up that hill every morning for six months and had never properly looked at where it ended. Stopgate did not turn, or open into a square. It simply stopped, against thirty feet of old dark brick. The tram line bent away to the east along the foot of the wall. In the middle of the wall was a cart gate of black oak, with a wicket in it, and over the gate a stone with the School's device, which was a line with a kink in it.

"Now," said Mara, at the last grating. She got up, with a hand on the rail. "By the Works' plan the main goes on under that gate, straight as a pipe, across the bottom yard and up the School's hill. There's a rain gully in the yard that sits right over it. I'll drop a cork in that gully, and you'll stay here, at this grating, and watch for it to come down under the gate."

The wicket was open. There was a short woman standing in it with a ring of keys at her belt.

"I thought you'd be up," said the keeper, Ansty. "I've had the kettle on since eight. You'll want the gully by the cart shed. Mind the trunks."

Mara went in. Kiva knelt at the last grating and let the lamp down.

The water at the bottom of the brick rose, and fell. It was stronger here than at the foot of the hill. She could see it move without trying.

She waited.

Through the wicket she could hear the bottom yard. It was full of first-years going home: trunks scraping, a carrier's dray, somebody's mother. She could hear Ansty saying that a rain gully was dry when it wasn't raining, and Mara saying that it ought not to be, and then the slosh of a kettle, and then a bucket.

"Gone in!" said Mara's voice. "With a bucket behind it!"

Kiva watched.

The water rose and fell under the lamp. A leaf came down out of the dark, and turned, and went on. Nothing else did.

"Anything?"

"No."

Another bucket. Kiva could hear it go down the gully on the other side of the wall, a hollow gargle, twenty paces off.

The black water breathed in and out at the foot of the brick, and nothing came down on it.

Mara came out through the wicket after a while, with the empty bucket in one hand and the plan in the other, and stood looking down at the grating as if it had been rude to her.

"It's on the main," she said. "That gully. It's drawn on the main, as plain as a rule. There ought to be a foot of water under it, moving, and a cork ought to come down that main and through this grating inside a minute." She did not raise her voice. "I've put two buckets of Mrs. Ansty's kettle down it, and a cork, and the cork's gone somewhere. It hasn't come here."

"Where's the gully go?"

"I asked her. She said down, and then away." Mara looked at the black gate. "I can't tell you where the main goes after this grating. I'd have said yesterday that I could draw it with my eyes shut."

Kiva got up off the setts. Her knee told her about it.

She went in through the wicket. She did not mean to walk anywhere in particular.

There were a great many trunks. The bottom yard was a cobbled square, thirty paces each way, and that morning it was full of first-years in their walking coats, with the look of people who have been told that they are not in trouble.

Kiva knew the yard as she knew her own stair. On the east side stood the core house, square and windowless, with its one iron door and the hum coming off it like warmth off a stove. The Low Lane was built against its south wall. Behind it, to the north, was the long slate roof of the New Lane. On the west side there was only a low wall with a gap in it, and through the gap one could see packed dirt, and a shed, and the end of a row of black posts.

She found that she had gone through the gap and was standing at the east end of the Old Yard, in daylight, which she had hardly ever done.

It looked smaller by day. It was a long strip of packed ground under the bottom wall, forty paces from the gap to the sheds at the far end, with two rows of black posts down it. Somebody's scraped line was there still. So was the cross, four paces beyond it, scratched over and over in the dirt.

And the last post of the south row leaned over the cross, as it always had.

"Why's that one crooked?"

Kiva looked round.

It was a child. She was perhaps thirteen, small and sandy, with a plait and a junior's grey smock, and she was carrying a quench box in both arms that was plainly not hers. She had the fixed, careful look of somebody who has been Kindled since the spring and has not yet set anything on fire.

"I'm to take this to the shed," said the child. "They're sending the first-years home and nobody's told the juniors anything. I've been coming to this yard since the fifth month. All the others are straight. That one isn't. I asked Miss Dane, and she said she didn't know."

"She says that to everybody."

"Do you know?"

Kiva considered the post. It was black, and split down one side, and bound with two iron bands. A short bar of old iron ran through its top, crosswise, worn bright at both ends, so that it looked like a man with his arms out. It stood a full pace out of its row, and it leaned into the lane: to the left, if one stood at the sheds and looked along the row.

She had put her hand on it when she was thirteen. She had come down beside it, with nothing open, and paid a quarter of a dram each time. She had never once been able to answer that question.

"No," she said. "I don't. I've wondered for four years."

"Oh." The child shifted the box. She seemed, if anything, relieved. "I thought it was only that I was new."

She went on down the lane toward the sheds. Kiva watched her go round the leaning post without looking at it, as everybody did, by a little sideways step that she had plainly learned in her first week.

Pike had them in the Graywater hall yard that evening, and it was the worst hour that Kiva had spent on a sheet since the spring.

The new one had come up from the ash office at four. It was stiff, and it smelled of wax, and Pru had carried it the whole length of the Brickfields road on her shoulder rather than put it on Perrin's handcart.

"Finch catches," said Pike. "Rowan, you're a corner."

"Which?"

"That's hers to say. You'll have noticed that it's her cloth."

Pru stood in the middle of the yard with the sheet at her feet and her round face perfectly white.

"North-east," she said. "Please. I mean, north-east. Rowan."

Kiva went to the north-east corner. She knelt, and took the hem.

It was a strange place to be. She had been the one in the middle since the fifth month. She had set the mark and thrown the cloth and called the corners, and four people had done as she said. Now she had a lead weight in her right hand and the heel of her left on a hem, and a first-year behind her was reading the ground.

"Cade's the rung," said Pike. "Gerald, I should say. Off the hoist, from the height of a man. When he lands, he's sixty drams and he's open, and fresh ash is open for about a minute. Finch."

"Mark," said Pru. Her voice shook. "Here. A pace south, for the wind. There isn't any wind. I'm saying it for practice." She threw the sheet.

It did not lie flat. One corner had folded under.

"Again," said Pike.

She threw it again, and again. At last it lay like a tablecloth, and Perrin let Gerald go from the hoist, and a hundredweight of sand hit the middle of the canvas with a sound like a dropped mattress.

"*Dark!*" said Pru. It came out as a squeak. "*Corners!*"

Kiva hauled.

She did it as she had done it a hundred times. She brought her corner in toward the heap, low, with her right hand, and she felt for the slack of the hem along her left side to tell her when the next corner was coming up to meet her.

There was no slack. There was nothing there at all. Her two fingers lay on the canvas and told her that there was canvas.

"*Rowan!*" That was Pru. "Your left! It's proud! It's standing up like a tent!"

She looked. Along her left side the hem had lifted off the ground in a long loose fold, a hand high, where a corner that had not waited for its neighbor had pulled the cloth up. Anything open would have run out under it.

"That's a lost rung," said Pike. He did not raise his voice. "Not hers. Yours. What happened?"

"I felt for the hem. On the left."

"With what?"

Kiva did not answer.

"With what, Rowan?"

"With two fingers that can't tell me there's a hem."

"So you asked a thing of a hand that it hasn't got, and you didn't say so to your catcher, and she found it out with the bag open." Pike squatted down beside her, heavily. He did not look angry. He looked like a man testing a knot. "I told you that you'd hate it for a year. This is what the year's made of. You knew about the cord, since that's the one everybody's watching. What else is there? Say them. To her, not to me."

It was not a comfortable minute. She knelt in a hall yard, in front of four people and a sandbag, and went through her own left hand like a person turning out a pocket.

"The cord. All three turns. That's yours already." She made herself look at Pru and not at the ground. "The slack of a hem on my left side. I can't feel it come. So it's a right-hand corner for me, every time, where the slack's on my good side. Or somebody on my left who calls it to me."

"North-west, then," said Pru. "Or south-east. Got it."

"Tin lids. The cold-room kind, with the lip. I can't find the lip." She thought. "The pin of a seal. Whether wax has gone off. I'd have to look, and at night I can't."

"Wax is mine," said Anwen. She was sitting on the handcart with her hands in her sleeves. "I've done wax since I was eight. It's laundry."

"And a thing's time." Kiva had not known that she was going to say it. "If a thing has a beat in it, a hem or a cord, I used to feel it in both hands and take the middle of the two. I've only the one now that hears properly. I got the hold's breath at the Pit because Brask called it for me. I'd not have had it by myself."

Pike's square head came up a little.

"That's the one I was waiting for," he said. "I'd not have thought you'd find it tonight. Who has that?"

"I don't know. Nobody. Brask, if he's there."

"*If he's there* isn't a name. Find one. Before you want it." He stood. "Finch. Throw it again. Rowan, south-east. And this time, when you want her corner in, you tell her so. You've a voice. I've heard it called shrill."

"I'm not shrill," said Pru. "I'm carrying."

They threw it until the lamp was lit. It grew a little less dreadful. Late in the hour, Pru said "Rowan, in a hand" as calmly as anybody passes the bread, and Kiva brought her corner in a hand. The neck closed. Pru took the cord at her elbow and made three turns, and looked at the third, and did not think about it.

"Sealed," she said. Then she sat down on the stiff new canvas and put her face in her hands.

"That's a catcher's bag," said Pike. "On her own cloth. Rowan was a corner in it and did as she was bid." He rolled the cord. "I'd not have wagered a penny on either of those in the fifth month."

Kiva's knees ached. She had been corrected all evening by a girl who had once run from a spaniel.

She found, somewhat to her surprise, that she could bear it. It was a good deal like being a still door. One was not the thing that was happening. One was only what it stood on.

Hollis Vane walked his plan on Saturday night, and he began it at ten with every lamp on Stopgate turned down.

"I'll say the reason for the dark," he said. He had the wardens at the foot of the hill with their handbells, and behind them the fours. "If a tear opens on this street, I'll want one thing lit on it, and that's a gate. Everything else that's bright is a thing for it to walk to. So you'll learn the road with your feet. There's a white tape up the east curb. There'll be a voice at every corner."

There were sixty-two houses on the hill itself. The rest of the dry drums' six hundred households lay in the streets to either side, and had roads of their own. Fourteen of the hill's houses had somebody in them who could not walk. For tonight a Circuit dummy lay in each of those fourteen beds, by leave of the household, and in most of them the family had stayed up to watch.

"The bottom forty go down. To the Brickfields road, and the tram shed, where the Wren House has its carts. The top twenty-two can't come down past whatever's in the road. They go up. Through the School's gate, across the yard, along the old practice lane under the wall, and out by the side gate to the square." He looked at the bursar. "I've been shown it by daylight. I'm told that it's the shortest way off that hill that doesn't cross a drain."

"It doesn't cross the main at all," said Mara. She was there for Rusk, with the plan. "If the main's where it's drawn."

"Is it?"

"I put a cork down it yesterday and never saw it again. Ask me tomorrow."

The gate stood at the head of the street, a hundred paces below the wall, silver from curb to curb. It was Doran Mace's. He had brought six of the night van's wards up from the Brickfields road and four students to stand among them, and he walked behind the line with his board under his arm, looking at boots.

Anwen was at the end of it.

Kiva saw that from where she stood. It was the east end, with the curb on one side of her and Tibb on the other, and nothing beyond her but an area railing. She had her hands low and her face shut, and the silver between them did not flicker.

"Rowan," said Vane. "You're recovery. So you've no litter and no wall. I've a use for somebody who reads ground. You'll stand in the School's yard, at the turn, and tell me what goes wrong there. Not mend it. Tell me."

"Yes, sir."

"Finch has the lamp, shuttered. Bexley's on the School's mast, on the core house roof, with his eyes on a drum and not on a bell. If that bell rings and he doesn't shout, it's the mark and not the drum, and nobody's to stop walking." He raised his voice. "*Carry! On the wardens' bells! Go!*"

The handbells went off down the hill all at once, like a drawer of spoons upended.

She stood in the gap of the low wall, with Pru beside her, and listened to a street being emptied in the dark.

It was a queer, slow, shuffling noise. There were doors, and feet on steps, and a great many people saying *mind* to one another. Somewhere a child was laughing, since nobody had told it not to. A warden's voice went along the top of the hill, cracked and steady. *Odd numbers, by the tape. Evens, wait for your bell.*

The first litter came through the black gate at a trot.

It was Pettit at the head and a Stonehand first-year at the foot, and a dummy between them in a blanket. They had been told the road. Across the cobbles, through the gap, along the lane.

"Through here," said Kiva. "Lane's ahead. Posts both sides."

"I've got it," said Pettit.

He had not got it.

He went through the gap fast, with his head down, and he did what anybody does who is carrying a weight along a wall in the dark. He kept to the wall. The south row of posts stood in its line, a yard out from the brick, and he meant to run along the lane side of it as a man runs along a fence.

The nearest post of that row was not in the line. It was the last of the row, as the lane was reckoned from the sheds, and so the first that anybody met from the gap. It stood a pace out, eight paces in, leaning into the road at the height of a man's hip.

There was a sound like an axe going into a log.

"*Down!*" said Pettit, from the ground, in a voice of pure surprise. The dummy had gone over his head. The first-year at the foot had sat down on it. "Who put a post there? There's a post in the road!"

"It's always been there," said Pru.

"Then it's always been in the road!"

He had it on the shin. He got up, swearing in a careful undertone like a boy who has been taught not to, and they gathered the dummy and went on, limping.

The second litter was two third-years of the School, who had walked past that post for three years. They went round it without a check. The third was Old Four's.

Lowen Brask had the head of it. He did not trot. He came through the gap at his long, even walk, with the dummy lying in the poles as though it were asleep, and he stopped.

He stopped dead, two paces short of the post, and stood there.

"There's something here," he said, to the dark. "I can hear the wall's come nearer. Wace, a pace right. Slowly." They went round. "Rowan. Is that yours to tell?"

"It's a post. It's out of its row. It leans."

"I know which it is. I've fought in this yard." He was already past. "I'd not thought of it with a litter. Somebody ought to say so at the gap."

"I did say posts."

"You said posts both sides. That's true of all of them." His voice came back along the lane, quite without malice. "It's the one that isn't like the others that wants saying."

The fourth litter hit it.

It was two Wren House second-years with a real person, an old man from number fifty-eight who had declined to be a dummy and insisted on being carried in his own chair. He was not hurt. The chair's leg was. He made a great deal of noise about it, and a bird in a cage on his knee made more.

"You could stand at it," said Pru. "With the lamp."

"He said nothing lit."

"Then you could just stand at it. And say."

It was what she wanted to do. It was exactly what she had done at the Pit, and on a street, and in the lane on her first morning. She would plant herself where the trouble was and call it for everybody, in a carrying voice, until the last litter was through. She had her mouth open to say *I'll take it.*

*Tell me what goes wrong. Not mend it.*

She shut her mouth.

It was not that she could not do it. It was that if she stood at that post, then the post was hers, and on the night that mattered she would be somewhere else. She would be four paces off with a hand gone, as she had told a kitchen table on Wednesday, and there would be a post in the road and nobody at it.

"No," she said slowly. "It's not a thing for one person to stand at. It's a thing for whoever's carrying to say."

The fifth litter was Perrin's.

She heard him before she saw him. He was telling the dummy that it was being rescued.

"Perrin! Stop at the gap!"

He stopped. He had Hendry Moss of his own Hall behind him, a boy with a neck as thick as his head, and they stood there in the dark with the poles on their shoulders.

"There's a post," said Kiva. "The last of the south row. It's a pace out, into the lane, on your left as you go in. I'm not going to stand at it. Call it before you're through the gap. *Post, left. Keep right.* Loud enough for Moss to turn with you."

"Post, left. Keep right," said Perrin obediently.

"And when you're past it, call it over your shoulder. To whoever's behind. Whether you can see them or not."

"What if there's nobody behind?"

"Then you've told a wall, and it's cost you nothing."

Perrin thought about that.

"It's a lane call," he said. "It's *Yours* and *Mine.* It's only that it's for a post." He hitched the poles. "I call that rather good. *Post, left! Keep right!*"

They went through. They did not touch it. She heard him bawl it backward down the lane, in the voice that he had been saving since the spring, and a moment later she heard somebody at the black gate take it up who had not been told to. *Post left. Keep right.*

Before long it was coming up the hill ahead of the bearers. By the last of the litters a warden with a handbell was calling it at the wicket, and had no idea why.

Then the bell rang.

It was the School's own, on the core house roof, and it rang right over their heads, two and two and two, as loud as a dray on cobbles. Every foot in the yard stopped.

There was a litter halted in the gateway, and a warden with her bell held up and her mouth open, and forty people on a dark hill all turning their faces the same way.

"*It's the mark!*" That was Noa. His thin cracked voice came down off the roof. "*It's the mark, not the drum! I've got the needle! Walk! Go on walking!*"

"*Walk!*" said Vane, from somewhere below. "*You heard the roof! That's what you've a roof for!*"

They walked.

It was done a little before midnight. Vane looked at his watch at the black gate, with the wardens round him and the fours sitting on the cobbles.

"Too long. With nothing chasing us." He did not sound displeased. He did not sound pleased. "I'd want it in half the time. We'll walk it again on Tuesday. Now. What went wrong? Bottom of the hill first."

They told him. A warden had rung evens before odds. The tape had come off the curb at number thirty. Twenty-Eight's ward had heard a join in Mace's gate and had said so, and had been right.

"The yard," said Vane. "Rowan."

"A post," said Kiva. She stood up. "The last of the south row in the old lane. It's a pace out of line, and it leans into the road at hip height. Two of the first litters struck it. The ones that missed it were people who'd known it for years, so it wasn't that they saw it. Brask stopped short of it and felt his way. Then I had one team call it at the gap and call it back over the shoulder, and after that nobody struck it. I didn't stand at it. I thought I shouldn't be there to."

"A post," said Vane.

"Yes, sir."

"In the middle of my casualty road." He turned his head. "Bursar. What is it doing there?"

The thin man in the gown had been sitting on a trunk. He got up.

"It is retained," he said, "for ceremonial continuity. It's the oldest thing in the School. It's said to be older than the School."

"Is it holding anything up?"

"I shouldn't think so."

"Then it can be ceremonial somewhere that I'm not carrying an old man in a chair. I'll have it out by Tuesday. I've a man with a saw."

"No," said Orla Dane.

She had been in the yard the whole night, on her crate, by the first shed, where she had sat for one-and-twenty years. Nobody had asked her for anything. She had her stick between her boots, and she did not raise her voice.

"You'll not put a saw to that, Hollis. Not on a bursar's word, nor on mine."

"Give me a reason."

"I haven't one." The crooked face was grave. "That's what I've to say, and I've said it to every first-year who ever asked. I've sat under that post since before you had a hat. I've wondered what it was for most nights. I've never once thought that the answer was *nothing.*"

"That's not a reason. That's a feeling about one."

"It is. I'm not a girl; I know the difference." Orla looked along the fours until she found the one she wanted. "You run your lanes dark on the Sag. You've never run this one. It was laid for running dark. Run it, the Line's way, before you cut it. If there's nothing, I'll hold the lamp for your man myself."

Vane was silent.

He was a watch officer. Kiva saw him weigh the one thing against the other, a post and a litter, and she saw that he did not enjoy it.

"Monday," he said. "After dark. I'll put a four through it the way the Line runs a lane, and we'll see what it's for. It's in the road, Dane, and a girl of seventeen has just shown me how to get forty people round it with their voices, which I'll grant you is more than the saw would have taught them. That buys it till Tuesday noon." He put his watch away. "Seven. You're the four. Bring your boots."

It rained a little before dawn, and Mara was on the hill by seven on Sunday to see what the rain had done.

It had done nothing to the water. A wet drain breathed exactly as a dry one had. She went up the street from grating to grating with her lamp, not dropping anything this time, only looking, and Kiva went beside her.

"It's stronger at the top," said Kiva.

"It's stronger at the top and no worse at the bottom. Like a tide making up a creek." Mara stood up from the last grating. "Tomorrow's the third. If that drum does what I told Rusk it would, I shall be believed, and I find that I've no stomach for it."

Ansty let them in at the wicket. The yard was empty and wet, and the trunks were gone.

Mara went straight to the gully by the cart shed and knelt.

"Wet," she said. "From the rain. There's an inch of rainwater in the bottom of that, lying as flat as a plate." She did not get up. "It isn't moving. Not a hair. I've a main that breathes a hand's width on one side of that gate, and ten paces inside it I've a puddle."

"Then it isn't under here."

"It's drawn under here." She had the plan out. She laid it on the wet cobbles and put her knee on a corner of it. "There. A blue line, from the gate, north, across this yard, past the core house, and on up the hill. It's as straight as if somebody had done it with a ruler."

Kiva looked at the line. It was very neat.

"Drains don't go straight under old ground," said Mara. "They go round what was there before them. That line's been drawn where a drain ought to be, by somebody who never put a lamp down a hole." Her voice was quite level. "Somebody's tidied my drain."

Kiva had her palm on the cobbles already.

There was nothing. There was wet stone, and under it the dead flat silence of ground with no hollow in it. She moved a pace toward the gate, and knelt again. Nothing. A pace west. Nothing.

She worked along the inside of the bottom wall. She did it on her knees, in the wet, with her right palm and the heel of her left, as a person feels along a shelf in the dark for a thing that has rolled. Mara watched her and did not say a word.

At the gap in the low wall she felt it.

It was very small. It was the least thing that she had ever called a feeling. It came up through the packed dirt on the far side of the gap, slow and even, and it was not coming from the north.

She went through on her knees. She followed it. It was like following the warmth of a pipe along a wall. It ran west from the gate, close under the brick, and it grew a little as she went, and then, eight paces into the Old Yard, it stopped being ahead of her.

It was underneath. It was directly underneath, and stronger than anywhere, and beyond that place it went off at a slant, along the lane, toward the sheds.

She took her hands off the ground and looked up.

There was a cross in the dirt in front of her knees, scratched over and over. There was a black post beside it, split, with two iron bands and a bar through its top.

"It's here," she said. Her voice came out oddly. "It doesn't go north. It comes in under the gate and turns. It runs along under the wall to here, and then it goes off down the lane, on the south side, about where the posts are." She sat back. "I can't weigh it. It's what comes through dirt. You'd have to dig."

"I can't dig up a school on a palm."

"No."

"And I can't put a cork down it, since I've no hole to put a cork down." Mara had come to the gap. She stood looking at the lane, and the two rows of posts, and the one at the end that was a pace out of its line. "Then the plan's wrong, and I don't know where it's right. That's the whole of what I've got."

She went back to her gully and stood over it with her hands in her apron pockets, looking down at a puddle as if she might yet shame it into moving.

Kiva stayed where she was.

She had come down on that cross again and again since the summer. She had stood on it in the still door, with the whole of the Old Yard under one heel, and felt the ground take her. It had never boomed. It had never been anything but ground. Whatever was down there was a long way down, and bricked over, and older than anybody's plan.

She looked at the post.

It leaned out over the lane. She had always thought of it as leaning away from its row, like a tooth gone bad. A child with a plait had gone round it on Friday by a little sideways step, without looking, and so had every student who had ever used that yard.

It struck her now, for the first time, that it might not be leaning away from anything. It might be leaning toward.
