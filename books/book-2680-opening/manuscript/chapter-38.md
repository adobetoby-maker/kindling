# Chapter 38 — Kiva: The Call

Healer Aske had his cart at the Pit's west gate by seven on Saturday, and he saw her hand on the tailboard, standing up, with the pin.

"When," said Kiva, for the first finger. It had taken five breaths.

She did not say it for the second. She waited until he took his thumb away.

"That's twice now it's told you about my thumb and not my pin," said Aske. "Yesterday's didn't even land, I'm told. A boy touched you in the air." He did not wait to have it confirmed. "No go before the twenty-third. What's today?"

"The seventeenth."

"Then we understand one another." He put the pin in his lapel. "You're catching. I've no quarrel with catching. You've eight fingers that report, and you've a first-year who crosses a cord right four times in five, and I'm told that you've the sense to hand it to her. Kneel all you like. Open nothing." He looked at her over the tailboard. "I can ease a hand that's starved. I've done it for you once. I can't ease one that's been spent to the bottom, because there's nothing left in it for the ash to speak to. That's the last thing I'll say to you this week."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"*Yes,* she says." He turned away to his linen. "They always say yes at seven in the morning."

---

The Pit had been altered since the spring, and Kiva could have told whose hand had been in it.

She came down the keeper's stair with her kit on her shoulder and stood at the east rail, and looked. It was the same yellow oval, and the same grey cinders, seventy paces from the Kiln at the north to the sump at the south, and forty across. The race ran down the middle of it still, a line of iron gratings as straight as a tram rail.

But there was a green iron box now on the wall above the sump, where the south link had stood naked for a hundred years. A rod ran from it along the foot of the east wall to a long handle beside the load board. A man could open the branch from the floor in the time that it took to say so. Nobody need cross anything.

"They've buried a feeder to the lattice house as well," said a voice beside her. "Round by the road, in a pipe. So the race can be dropped and the cage stays lit."

It was Mara. She had a seat in the east terrace, two rows up, and she had come down to the rail in her good coat.

"That's what you asked for," said Kiva.

"I wasn't the first to ask for it, on that link. I was only the one who asked the week after a girl had stood on the lever." Mara's eyes went along the rod, joint by joint, as they would have gone along a drum. "They've done it properly. I could wish that they'd done it for a better reason than being frightened."

Along the foot of the east wall, under the reserves' rail, there was something else that was new. Twelve oak posts stood in a row, and on each post was a cell box no bigger than a biscuit tin, with a small lamp burning on its lid.

"What are those?"

"Those are a street," said Mara. "Somebody on the Circuit's board has been talking to Graywater." She put her hand on the rail. "I'm told that I may sit in a gallery, these days. I'm going to do it. I'll not come down to this rail again whatever I see, and you may hold me to it."

She went back up to her seat.

The Carry was run in the forenoon, and for most of it Kiva had nothing to do but watch.

It was a still-door's board. There were three dummies to be fetched, one from the foot of the sump by a ladder, one from the top terrace, and one from the loft of the lattice house, and the whole four had to bring them to the west gate by the clock. Perrin had been passed to the shoulder for a fortnight and more. He went down the sump ladder as if it had been his own stair.

"You're being rescued," Kiva heard him say, from the bottom. "I've not done this since the spring, so we're both to be patient."

He brought it up on his shoulder. He had gone a very odd color by the top of the ladder, and he was grinning like a boy with a prize marrow.

They were sixth. Old Four had it by a clear half minute. Lowen Brask carried the lattice-house dummy down a loft stair in his arms as though it had been asleep, and did not hurry, and beat everybody.

"He taught me that," said Perrin, afterward, with his hands on his knees. "He says the casualty can tell if you're frightened of the clock. I said it was a sack of sand. He said, *So was Cade, the first year.*"

When the aggregate went up on the great board at noon, Seven's was the fourth name in the first company for the Call, under Halden License, Old Four and Twenty-Eight.

"Fourth," said Jude. He stood looking at the marks under it for a long time. "By one mark. We're in by one mark, over a Saltmere house four that beat us on the Bench. And Old Four's second, that we put out yesterday. A first on any board's the top mark, and they had one this morning."

"That's Pryce's four minutes," said Perrin.

"It's Cade's ladder," said Anwen. "And it's a corner. I'm not having it all."

---

Hollis Vane had the first company on the cinders at half past one, sixteen hands and four reserves, and he gave them the Call in his flat field voice.

"A string out of the Kiln at two. Skinned; they all are. Eight rungs. The top four are yours, one to a team, in the order that you stand on that board. Graywater has the bottom four and the legs." He nodded across the floor. On the west side eight purse sheets had been laid in a row, from north to south, and beyond them a line of people in slate blue stood easy. "The gate's theirs. It walks the string down the west side, a sheet at a time. That's a rule now. You know why."

Nobody in the fifth company of the spring had forgotten.

"Behind the string, the lattice house lets out four husks, one after each rung. One to a team. They'll come out of the chute at the Kiln's east side, and they'll want the brightest thing they can read, and the designers have been good enough to give them twelve." He pointed his chalk at the row of lamps under the east wall. "That's a street. Those are kitchens. I'll want to know about every one that's still alight at the close. And there's a casualty for each of you on the east side, to be at the west gate before your rung's sealed."

He looked round the sixteen.

"It's judged as the Line judges a call. What's closed. Who's alive. What's still lit. What's tinned. And what I think of you after. The tin's the fourth thing you're for." He hung the slate on his belt. "I down one of every team. You know that. Now you'll each tell me what's behind you, before I've asked."

They told him. Halden's ward, the tall girl called Hesketh, said *the Row, at thirty paces, and the terraces.* Edda of Old Four said the same, and added the race. When it came to Seven, Anwen said it, since the gate was hers.

"The Row east of us. The race between. The lattice house north. And a full terrace of people who haven't a cell among them, so they're not a lamp, they're only in the way of the legs."

"Good. Roles."

"Talley. Seven. Kill."

"Pryce. Seven. Hold. I call the step for our rung."

"Cade. Seven. Carry. To the shoulder."

"Rowan. Seven. Catch. Door declared: none." Kiva held up her left hand, since he was a man who looked at hands. "I've two fingers that don't report. Finch takes the cord at the third turn, when I call it, every time. That's arranged. It isn't a thing going wrong."

"Finch. Reserve. A corner, and the cord. And a lamp if I'm told." Pru swallowed. "Not a wall."

"So I hear." Vane's eyes rested on Kiva's hand for a moment longer than on anybody's. "Settle your drams at the keeper's gate. They've been combed. Then stand at the east wall, dark, until your rung's called."

The safety crew came across from the west gate while they were settling.

Rhea Sorn walked first, with her coat over her arm. Pike had a roll of cord. There were four wards behind them in blue, and the third of the four was a woman of about thirty with a broken nose, who was doing up the top button of her tunic with her own two hands as she walked.

Kiva had not seen her since a stair.

Greta Stroud saw her at the same moment. She finished the button. She did not stop, and she did not nod; she only looked, levelly, at Kiva's left hand hanging at her side, and then at her own, and went on to her place in the line.

At the west gate, on a camp stool beside the Wren House cart, a long-legged young man sat with a stick across his knees. He lifted it an inch when he saw Seven look.

"Nairn," said Perrin. "He's walking."

"He's sitting," said Pru. "But he walked to sit."

---

The lid went up at two.

Kiva had seen it rise before. It was the same cast-iron slab on its chains, with the same four still-doors of the Hall leaning into the capstan, and what came out from under it was what had come out in the spring. A grey bulk, and another above it, and another, on legs as thick as lampposts. It stood as high as a tram's roof, and it had frost on it from crown to knee.

The gate was lit already. Four wards in blue had a wall of silver across the head of the first sheet, twelve paces west of the race, and the top knot swung to it as a weathercock comes round, and the string walked.

"Halden," said Vane. "Rung one."

She watched Kellan cut it from the east wall, forty paces off.

He went up the back of it dark. She saw him ask. She could not hear the words, but she knew the tilt of his head, and she saw the Halden catcher's hand go up, flat. *Not yet.* He came down dark. He went up again, and there was a blue-white line at the top for about as long as it takes to blink.

"Sealed," said the Halden catcher's voice, across the cinders.

"He asked," said Jude, beside her. "They kneel at eight paces in that yard. Somebody's moved their catcher in."

The first husk came out of the chute a minute after. It did what the designers had asked of it. It trotted south-east across the cinders toward twelve small lamps, with nothing in it but direction, and Hesketh lit a lure in its road and Kellan took it on the cloth. Pettit carried a dummy across the race to the west gate with his eye on the string's legs.

It was a good drill. It was almost exactly a street at half past two in the morning, with the fear taken out.

Old Four had the second rung. Gower went up dark, and waited for a quiet man called Wace to say *now,* and lit for a full half second, which was long for him. The skin did not break. It slid down a knot, as it had every time since the spring, and lay there thicker.

The second husk went for the Row. Edda leaned a ward across its road, and Lowen Brask walked the dummy over the gratings as if he had all day.

"Twenty-Eight," said Vane. "Rung three."

Selka Dray was standing four places along the wall. Kiva saw her roll her shoulders once, and look at the thin boy beside her, and wait.

---

Rhea Sorn stood a long pace behind a Flask's hind legs, with her hands in her pockets, and disliked how easy it was.

She had been asked to walk the Circuit's board through this in the seventh month. She had sat at their table and told them what a call looked like, this year. A string, and things let out behind it, and something that mattered at its back. They had listened. They had built her worst night of the spring out of oak posts and biscuit tins, and scheduled it for two o'clock.

It was a sound piece of work. She would have passed it. She had passed it.

*What's there?*

A string, two rungs down, on her own gate, where a string belonged. Stroud was in that gate. Stroud's hands were whole. Rhea had looked at them that morning for rather longer than was polite.

Sixteen students along the east wall, dark, as they had been told. A foreman who had left a gate lit. A keeper with his hand a yard from a lever that had not existed in the spring. A race that could be dropped in a breath, and a lattice that would stay up when it was.

Every single thing that had gone wrong at this Pit on the twenty-ninth of the fifth month had been found and mended. She distrusted that more than anything else on the floor. A spring that had surprised her seven times was not going to be stopped by having its last surprise fenced.

*And what's behind me?*

She turned her head and looked. She did it every minute or so now. She had not done it for twenty years. Finch had taught her to, on the Weighbridge, sitting on a curb with wax all down her back.

The Kiln. The lid, up on its chains. The lattice house against its flank, with the chute beside it, shut.

"Twenty-Eight's rung," said Imrie, from the gate. "Step."

The Dray girl went up the back of the string in two bounds. Rhea watched her narrowly. She had seen that blade on a mudbank in the spring, a breath from sight, with the river taking the ash. She had not liked it then and had said so to nobody.

The girl hung at the top, dark, with her blade arm low.

"*Not yet,*" said a boy's voice, from the north hem of the third sheet.

She came down. She came down with her teeth shut and her whole body shaking with wanting to cut. She went up again.

"*Now.*"

Yellow. Four-tenths. Sixty drams fell like sand out of a split sack.

*Well,* thought Rhea. *Somebody's taught that one to wait.*

The boy at the hem drew the neck in and called it sealed, well inside his minute.

"*Sedge!*"

It was a shout from the Kiln, and the name was the shouter's own, given as a hand gives it before a report. She had never heard the lattice-house warden's voice before; nobody had; the woman lived in her shed like a snail. It came down the floor cracked and high.

"*Foreman! There's two in the throat that I've not let! I've four against the gate and I've only sent for one!*"

Rhea was already turning.

The chute at the Kiln's east side had opened. It was meant to open; it was time for the third husk. One came out, grey, skinned, at a trot.

Then another. It was two minutes early, and it was Seven's.

Then two more, shoulder to shoulder, that were nobody's at all.

Four Handfuls, on an open floor, with a string at her back that she could not leave and a rung only just sealed on the third sheet. It was more than had been ordered. It was not more than a company could kill. There were four student blades on that wall with a first step apiece.

She waited for them to turn east. There were twelve lamps under the east wall, and a husk went to the brightest thing that it could read.

They did not turn east.

They did not so much as lift their heads toward the Row. They trotted south, down the middle of the floor, along the race, in a file. Where the cinders were emptiest, a little past the middle, they stopped.

Rhea had killed something over two thousand husks. She had never seen one stop in the open with nothing lit near it and not begin to seek.

They sought nothing. They moved apart. Two went west of the gratings and two east, as deliberately as four men pacing out a plot, until they stood at the corners of a square eight paces on a side, with the race running through the middle of it.

Then they turned to face one another, and were still.

"Vane," said Rhea. She did not shout it. "What's that?"

"I don't know." He was forty paces off, at the load board, and his voice was as flat as her own. "I've never seen it."

The shine on the nearest husk had begun to move. It did not stay on the shoulders, where a skin belonged. It ran outward, along the line toward its neighbor, like frost crossing a pane; and from the neighbor a line of the same ran to meet it.

They met. Where they met, there was a cord. It was white, and as thick as a wrist, and it hung in the air at the height of a man's chest with nothing holding it but the two grey things that it joined.

*There's no door in it.* She had said that at this Pit, in the spring, about a string that would not turn. She found the words in her mouth again.

There was a boy laying a cloth.

He was the thin one, Twenty-Eight's catcher, and he had done it properly. The third husk was his team's, and it was standing still at the south-east corner of that square with its back to him. He had put his canvas down two paces behind it. He was kneeling.

And the Dray girl was walking to it. She was dark. She was coming from behind, as a blade comes at a skin, without hurry, with her eyes on her catcher's hand.

*That's inside her step,* thought Rhea. *It's her husk. It's a Handful, with its back turned. I'd have sent her myself.*

"*Twenty-Eight!*" She had it out. "*Stand! Stand where you are!*"

Somebody else was shouting as well. It was a girl's voice, from the east wall, high and carrying, and it had a word in it that Rhea did not understand the use of.

"*Dray! Don't take the corner! It closes!*"

The thin boy's hand came down.

---

Kiva saw it from eighteen paces, and she saw all of it.

She had known what it was a breath before she knew that she knew. Four of them, square, facing in. A line run from each to the next. It was a box. It was a plank on four men, and she had stood in front of one yesterday morning with her heel on a pier.

*Take a ward-holder off a Home Line square and there isn't a gap. It shuts on you like a gate.*

She had turned that over in the interval and not seen what use it was.

"*Dray!*" It tore her throat. "*Don't take the corner! It closes!*"

Selka Dray heard her. Kiva saw the red head check, a quarter of a turn. She saw the eyes come round, and find who it was, and go back to a thin boy's hand.

It was not contempt. It was worse than that, and fairer. Dray had lit after the cloth for three months. She had stood at the top of a string with her blade out like a lamppost until her catcher said. She had been taught, at a great cost to her temper, that there was one voice on a floor which a blade listened to. It was not the voice of a catcher from another team, shouting about corners. Nor did the Torch's *stand* reach her as an order. It came across the floor tangled in Kiva's own shout, when the boy's hand had already begun to fall.

"*Now,*" said Wilf Marten.

Her left foot came down. Her right came down. There was a line of yellow light, low and level, as long as a breath.

It was the cleanest cut that Kiva had ever seen her make. The husk fell in through its own shoulders, and four-and-twenty drams went down in a soft heap, and more than half of it was on the canvas.

"*Dark! Bag's open!*"

She was dark before he had said it. She had learned that as well.

For a breath, nothing happened. There were three grey shapes standing at three corners, and a heap of ash at the fourth, and the white cord hung between them. It sagged a little at the south-east, where it had nothing to rest on.

Then it came off.

It came off the place where the husk had been like a hawser coming off a bollard. The loop of it dropped. Dray had turned to step back to her catcher, as a blade ought, and it fell across her left leg above the knee and bore her straight down onto the cinders.

She did not cry out. She said, "*Oh,*" in a small, surprised voice, like somebody who has put a hand on a cold pipe.

And the three that were left took up the slack.

Kiva saw them do it. They did not move their feet. They only leaned back a little, all three together, like three people hauling a neck shut on a called word. The cord came taut from the north-east corner down to the ground, and from the ground up to the south-west, with a girl's thigh under the bend of it.

There was a sound. It was not loud.

"*Marten!*" That was Dray. Her voice had gone thin and very exact. "*You've a bag open! Shut it!*"

"Selka—"

"*It's open and it's mine! You finish it!*"

The thin boy was a pace and a half from her. He had the hem of his cloth in both fists, and his face was the color of the canvas.

"Corners," he said. "There aren't any corners. I'll do it. I'll do it."

"*This is a response!*"

Vane's voice went across the Pit like a shutter coming down.

"*The Call's struck! It's a response and it's mine! Every student dark, and stand! Keeper! Drop the race!*"

The long handle by the load board went over. Kiva heard it go, a deep, oiled *clack* that ran along the foot of the wall and ended in a green box above the sump. Forty lamps round the rim went out together. It was the middle of the afternoon, and it made no difference to the light, and it felt like a hand being taken off her shoulder.

"*And the Row!*"

Dunmore had a master handle for that too. Twelve small lights along the east wall went out in a breath.

"*Lid!*"

At the north end the four of the Hall leaned into their bars. The chains ran. Sedge was still shouting something from her shed, and nobody could hear what.

On the west side the string stood where it had been left, with its blunt head against the gate. It had not turned. The gate was the nearest lit thing in the Pit, and now it was the only one.

"I've got this," said Rhea Sorn's voice, level, carrying. "It's on my wall and it stays on my wall. I'm not cutting it until I know what's behind me. Hollis. That's yours."

Kiva had not moved from the east wall. Nobody had. Sixteen students stood dark in a row, as they had been told, and looked at the middle of the floor.

It had finished.

The cord ran the whole way round now, from corner to corner to the ground and up again. And from the cord a wall had come down. It was the same frost as a skin, thin as a breath on glass. It hung from the white line to the cinders like a sheet from a rope, and it stood up above the line as well, to twice the height of a man, and it shone.

Four walls. Three grey posts, and at the fourth corner, where the line came down to the ground, a girl lying on her back with her leg under it.

She was outside. That was something. The wall rose from the cord across her thigh, and her head and shoulders and her right leg lay on the open cinders, a pace from a heap of ash that a thin boy was drawing shut with shaking hands.

"*Sealed!*" said Marten. It cracked. "It's sealed. Selka. I'm sorry. I had to—"

"I told you to." She had her head turned on the cinders. She was looking at the thing on her leg. "It's cold. It's like a pipe in winter. Marten, don't touch it. Don't put anything on it."

Kiva found that she had both hands up in front of her, the left one too, with nothing in them.

Inside the square, through the frost, something was a different color.

It was in the very middle, over the gratings of the race. It was a thin upright stroke of blue, taller than a man, like the gap at the edge of a door.

"That's a tear," said Jude. His voice had gone. "That's a tear. On the floor. That's not in the Kiln."

"The lattice is up," said Anwen. "I can see the lamp on the shed. It never dropped."

"It isn't in the lattice," said somebody behind them. "It's in that."

It was Lowen Brask.

He had come along the wall from Old Four's place. He was not looking at the blue. He was looking at the white cord, and at the three grey shapes leaning back from it, and his square quiet face had an expression on it that Kiva had never seen there. It was the face of a man who has come home and found a stranger sitting in his chair.

"Foreman," he said. He said it in a carrying voice. He had learned that from a rival. "Brask. Old Four. I stood the Ferren hold in the seventh month, and the flats in the eighth. That's a hold."

Vane turned his head.

"Say it again."

"It's a square. It's four posts and a line leaned from each to the next, and the weight goes round. That's how we stand one. That's how we keep a tear open for a day so that first-years can be licensed at it." He swallowed. "They're doing what we do. I don't know who showed them. I'm not saying anybody did. But I've had my shoulder under that for ten weeks, and I'd know it in the dark."

"A hold keeps a tear open," said Vane slowly.

"And as wide as it's wanted. Ours we keep to a Handful's width." Lowen looked at the blue stroke. "I don't know what width that one's wanted."

As he said it, the blue opened a little, like an eye.

Something began to come up out of it. It came a knot at a time, as a rope is paid out of a hatch.

On the cinders at the south-east corner, Selka Dray lifted her head an inch.

"Rowan," she said. She did not raise her voice. It carried, since nobody else in the Pit was speaking. "You said *it closes.* What closes?"

Kiva opened her mouth.

She had it. She had the whole of it, standing ready: a plank, and a post, and what a Stonehand captain had told her on a booming floor. She had shouted two-thirds of it across eighteen paces a minute ago. It had not been enough, and it had been all that she had.

"A hold," she said. "If you take a corner, the others shut on it. I only learned it yesterday. I didn't know it was that until it stood up." She made herself go on, to a girl on her back with a cord across her leg. "I called *take a corner* on the Great Floor as loud as I could, and half of what anybody here knows about holds, they had off me, and it was the wrong half. That's mine."

"I wasn't listening to you," said Dray. "I was listening to Marten. I'd do it again." Her head went back onto the cinders. "It's tightening. It does it in time. Somebody tell me what I'm under."

Kiva looked at the white line.

It ran from a grey shoulder down to the ground, and across a leg, and up again. It had a wall hanging from it. Three things were leaning back on it together, in a slow time that she could almost hear.

She had seen that before. She had not seen it standing on end.

It was a cord run through a hem. It was a neck, being drawn shut. She had knelt at a great many of them since the spring, with the cord at the crook of her elbow, and she knew to a hair what a hem did when one corner of the sheet was let go.

"It's a purse sheet," said Kiva. "And she's the weight in the corner."
