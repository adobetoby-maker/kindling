# Chapter 29 — Kiva: The Night Van

The watch room at the Graywater hall had one wall that was all map, and one that was all sums.

They had been told six, and they were there at a quarter to, the four of them in a row on a bench with the sheet between their feet. It was a plain room. There were hooks for coats, and a stove, and a long table scarred by forty years of tin lids. On the map wall every sensing mast in the eastern districts had a small glass lamp beside its name, and every lamp was dark, and under them hung a brass bell with a cord that nobody touched.

Kellan was reading the other wall.

It was a painted board, ruled in columns, and somebody chalked it fresh each week. *District table. The season to date.* The companies went down the left side, and across the top ran five words that Kiva had last heard read out from a slate on a cinder floor, with one more added at the end.

*Closed. Lives. Works. Tinned. Remarks.*

"Saltmere's first," said Kellan, in the voice he kept for a sum that would not come out. "Twenty-seven calls, twenty-seven closed. Nobody lost, two hurt. Not an hour dark. Two thousand eight hundred and forty drams." His finger moved down. "Graywater, thirty-one calls. All closed. Five hurt. *Six hours dark, one branch.* Three thousand nine hundred and ten. One remark."

"That's the Pit," said Perrin. "Six hours is us."

"And third is Halden's First Company, with four thousand four hundred. They've tinned more than anybody on the wall. They've one call *held over* and two remarks, and they're third." Kellan turned round. "Graywater's eleven hundred drams ahead of Saltmere, and it stands behind. How is the table reckoned?"

"From the left."

The man who had spoken was sitting at the end of the long table, mending a cord. He was square and grey-headed and he had been there when they came in. Kiva had known him at once by his back. She had last seen it on a meadow, four paces from a headless Flask, not turning round.

"You read it as you'd read a line of print," he said. He did not look up from the cord. "Was it closed? If one company's closed them all and another hasn't, that's the end of it; you never get as far as the tin. Then lives. Then what was still working in the morning. You only come to the fourth column when the first three are level." He drew a knot tight with his teeth. "You can't buy a column with the one to its right. There's houses that keep trying."

"Then a clean yield—"

"Is the fourth thing we're for. I've been catching twenty-six years, and I'm the fourth thing we're for." He laid the cord down, finished. "Pike. You'll be Rowan. Sorn says you kneel like somebody who was taught by a tinsmith."

"By a Torch with a bad knee."

"Same thing." He looked at the bundle between their feet. "Is that wet?"

"It was trodden. An hour, in a trough."

"Who told you to?"

"A laundress."

"Then she's forgotten more about canvas than the Circuit ever knew," said Pike, and almost smiled, and the door opened.

Rhea Sorn was not as tall as the poster.

That was Kiva's first thought, and it was a stupid one. She had seen this woman on a door for six years, and across a gallery, and behind a grey column with a white line going up from her fist. She had never been in a room with her. The streak at the temple was real. The eyes were paler than they had been painted. She came in with her coat over her arm, and she looked at the four people on the bench as Mara looked at a tray of instruments that had come back from somebody else's repair.

"Paper," she said. "Each of you. What you're passed for and what you're not. Renn."

Kellan stood up. "Kill. First step, full. Second step, attached. I've cut one rung of a Flask under a foreman, and a Handful in the field."

"Cade."

"Carry. And I'm not to." Perrin got up more slowly. "Ribs. Nothing over my head, no stance under a load. The Hall's healer wrote it out and made me sign. I can take a corner and a pole. I can walk somebody out who can walk."

"Finch."

"Hold. First step." Pru's voice went up a full tone. "A corner and a lamp. Not a wall. You underlined it."

"I did. I'll say why, later, when it matters." The pale eyes came to the end of the bench. "Rowan."

It was the first word that Rhea Sorn had ever said to her.

"Catch," said Kiva. "Recovery only. Dark hands, under a declared team call. No door opened within three paces of open ash or any charged cell. I'm not certified to kill, hold, carry, or tune."

"And the other thing? I was on that floor."

"It isn't on my paper. I've two on a sanctioned mast. My sponsor wants six." She kept her hands still. "I'll not do it tonight for anybody."

"Good. I'll not ask." Rhea hung her coat on a hook. "Hollis Vane asked, and he'd a reason, and he's written it down in two books. I've read the second one; he sent it up. I've no intention of writing a third." She turned to face them, with her back to the map. "You're attached. I'll tell you what that means on my van, since the board's wording is very fine and no use. It means that you fetch, and carry, and catch behind Pike. It means you kill nothing above a Handful, and that only when I've said the word. Nairn's your officer on the ground. If he tells you to stand, you stand, and you ask him why on the way home."

A young man had come in behind her, tying back his hair. He was perhaps three-and-twenty, long in the leg, with a cheerful, sunburned face, and there was dark metal on a cuff at his wrist.

"That's me," he said. "I was you four years ago. I've not forgotten what it was like, so I'll try to be less terrible than mine was."

"You'll be exactly as terrible as is wanted," said Rhea. "Counter. Each of you settles a field dram on the company's meter before we do anything else. Then Pike will have that sheet down in the yard and up again until he's stopped wincing."

---

It took eight times before Pike stopped wincing.

He did not say what was wrong. He stood in the hall yard under a hooded lamp, with his hands behind him, and each time the sheet was down he walked round it once and said, "Up," and they took it up.

On the fifth he stopped beside Kiva.

"Your cord hand. You change your grip at the second turn."

"I've two fingers that don't tell me anything. I start in the hand, and then I have to hook the elbow to—"

"I know why. I watched you do it at the Pit, from under a string." He held out his own right hand. It was broad and scarred, and there was a groove worn across the palm. "I'd a tendon cut at thirty. That finger's been a passenger ever since. I don't ask it for anything. I took the cord the way I could hold it on my worst day, and I've held it that way every day since, the good ones too." He closed the hand. "A grip you change is two grips, with a moment between when you've got neither. You know about moments between. I'm told that you pay a quarter of a dram a time for one."

Kiva looked at him.

"So choose the bad-day grip. Elbow from the first turn, not the second. It's slower by a count. Take the count." He walked on. "Up."

She took the cord at the elbow from the first turn on the sixth throw, and the seventh, and the eighth. It was slower by a count. It felt like wearing somebody else's boots. On the eighth, Pike went all the way round the sheet and said nothing whatever, which she understood to be the most that anybody got.

After that there was nothing to do, and Kiva learned that this was most of the trade.

They sat. The stove ticked. Four ward-holders in blue played a slow game of cards at the far end of the table, and one of them was Imrie, who had stood in a line at the Pit and had not once looked round. Nairn slept in a chair with his boots on a second chair. A bald man called Ruddock, who drove the van and was the crew's healer, went through a satchel of linen and counted it twice.

At twenty minutes to ten the bell rang.

It rang two strokes and a pause, and two again, and a lamp came up on the map wall, out on the edge of the allotments. Nobody ran. The cards were laid face down. Nairn's eyes opened. Within about ninety seconds twelve people and their kit were in the back of a long blue van on two facing benches, and the hall doors were open on the Brickfields road.

"Sag Line, mast five," said Rhea, from the front. "That's a recalibrated mast, on a baseline that's been creeping all spring. It's rung false twice this month. We go as though it hadn't."

It had.

There was nothing on the Sag but a wind and a smell of turned earth. They stood on the berm for twenty minutes in the dark while Pike walked the cut with a hooded lantern and Rhea watched the place where a tear might have been. Then she took a slate from her coat and wrote on it, and read it out, in the voice of somebody reading a laundry list.

"*Closed: nothing open. Hurt: none. Works: all. Tinned: nil.* That's a call. It's on the table by morning, the same as the Pit."

Down the berm, where the big Ferren culvert came out under the dyke, there was a light.

It was a small one, the silver of a held ward, steady as a lamp in a window. Two men in Works oilskins were going into the culvert's mouth by it with a measuring rod. Behind them stood a row of people in blue with their hands at their sides, and at the end of the row, a little apart, a figure with both arms raised was holding the light up for the men to see by.

Kiva knew the way she stood.

She was sixty paces off, across a drainage cut. The light did not waver. It was not a wall and it was not holding anything back; it was only a lamp, lifted so that two inspectors should not put a foot wrong in the dark. She had been given that in her second week, and Kiva did not know enough to tell whether it was early.

Anwen did not look round. She could not have told one dark shape on a berm from another, and Kiva did not call. It was Mace's line. They had come about a mast.

"Somebody you know," said Rhea Sorn. She had come up beside Kiva without a sound.

"She was our Hold."

"I know who she is. I've read her card as well." The pale eyes were on the small steady light. "That's a good lamp. He'll have a wall on her inside the month." A pause. "Van."

She asked them on the way back. The van hummed along the embankment, and she turned on the front bench with one arm along its back.

"There was nothing there. Suppose there had been. One husk, plain, in that cut. Renn. What do you do?"

"Ask my catcher for the mark. Come at it dark. Light after the foot." Kellan answered as he would have answered the Chair. "I'd expect to have it bagged inside forty-five."

"Rowan?"

"Read the wind and the ground. Set the cloth in the lee of the berm. Call *dark* at the cut."

"Finch?"

"Stand where I'm put and light when I'm told and drop when she says." Pru considered. "And narrate, I expect. Quietly."

"Cade."

"Find out who can't walk."

"Mm." Rhea looked at them for a moment longer. "There's an allotment pump forty paces east of that cut. Agnes Roake's. A team put its cell out of order in the spring with a four-second kill on that very bank, and she waited six weeks for the reserve to fill it." She let that lie. "You've each told me what you'd do. They were good answers. Not one of you has told me what's behind you."

Nobody said anything. The van went on through the dark.

"I'll not ask it again tonight," said Rhea, and turned to the front.

---

Anwen Pryce heard the second bell from the mouth of a culvert, at fourteen minutes past two.

It came across the flats from the east, very small and clear, three strokes and a pause and three again. She had learned the codes in her first week. Three and three was the weigh-house.

"Weighbridge," said somebody along the line.

"Not ours," said Mace. He did not turn his head. "Night van's. We're lamps. Pryce, you're dipping."

She was. She brought it up. It was the fifth hour she had held a light over a brick mouth for two men with a rod, and her arms had stopped being arms a long while ago and become two things that she balanced a lamp on. *A wall takes it out of your wrists,* Stroud had told her, with her own hands in linen. *A lamp takes it out of your patience. He'll leave you on a lamp till he's seen which you run out of first.*

The night van. She had seen it at ten, up on the berm, a long dark shape with its lamps hooded, and a row of people beside it against the sky. She had not been able to tell one from another. She had known all the same.

They would be on the benches now, with the sheet between their feet. Finch would be talking. Kellan would have a watch out. Perrin would be saying that he minded something, to keep from saying what he was afraid of.

There was a half sheet in her coat, under the list.

It had come up by Mace's runner on Thursday, with no beginning and no name at the foot. She had read it standing in a culvert in the rain. *Monday. Why: it was easy and I was pleased, and I didn't look at my feet.* And lower, under a ruled line, in the same square pencil: *A thing L. Brask said in a passage. I put it on the left. It belonged on the right.*

She had read that six times. It was not an apology. It had none of the roundness of one. It was only the counts, as she had asked for them, with the part left in that made the writer look a fool.

She was still angry. She had examined it that night as she would have examined a ward for a thin place, and it was all there, as sound as the day she had turned her list over on a kitchen table. Greta Stroud could hold a spoon now, and not a cup.

And she wanted very badly to be on that van.

It seemed that a person could have both of those at once and hold a lamp. Nobody had told her so. She had thought that when she finally stood on a Graywater line she would be one whole thing, with nothing left over.

The bell had stopped. Whatever it was had been heard by the people whose business it was.

"There's the low arch, miss," said the inspector, from inside the brick. "A foot to your left, if you'd be so good."

She moved the light a foot to the left, and held it there, and it did not flicker.

---

The van took the turn off the tram road at twenty-seven minutes past two, and stopped with its nose to the top of a street that had no lamps in it.

"Weighbridge Street," said Nairn, and dropped the tailboard.

Kiva had been down it once or twice by day. It was an ordinary street. It ran south for a hundred and fifty paces and fell a little all the way, between two rows of yellow brick tenements five stories high, to a wharf on the canal. There were railings, and front steps, and iron gratings set into the pavements over the cellars. At the bottom stood the old weigh-house with the mast on its roof. Beyond it, across the water, the Lantern lay along the far bank in a long low wash of steady white light, as it had lain every night for four hundred years.

On this side, every window was dark.

It was not an empty dark. She could feel that as she got down. There were people behind those windows, four or five hundred of them, in back rooms with their doors shut and their hands in their laps, and not one had so much as a candle. A woman's voice was calling something down a stairwell, flat and steady, a floor at a time.

"They've done a dark drill," said Ruddock, pulling out the satchel. "The wardens ring it on the bell. Lamps out, doors shut, nobody at the front. This street's had the same drill since before there were trams." He nodded at the high wall on the right, where a pair of iron gates stood open on a yard full of sleeping cars. "And that's the sheds."

"That's my father's shed," said Perrin.

He said it quite quietly. There were five or six people at the gates, in leather aprons, standing well back in the shadow with their arms folded, and one of them was a short, wide man with a grey mustache who had lifted his hand about an inch when the van stopped and put it down again.

"Is he on nights?" said Kiva.

"He's always on nights. He says the cars are better company." Perrin had gone a curious color. "He's never seen me on a floor that wasn't behind glass."

Just inside the gates a woman sat on an upturned crate with a child asleep against her shoulder, and a man in his shirt stood over the pair of them with a blanket he had not thought to unfold. They had come out of a ground-floor window by the back. Somebody from the sheds had given them a mug of tea, and none of the three had touched it.

A lamp warden came up out of the dark with a dead lantern in his hand. He had the street's switch already pulled, he said, and the wardens rung. Two were out. The first had come with the bell, at fourteen minutes past, and gone in at a ground-floor window halfway down on the west side, and the family had got out the back and pulled their red handle as they went. The second had come at twenty-four past. It was sitting on the Hythe Building's step.

"Who's in this street that can't be got out by the back?" said Rhea.

"Two that I know of. There's an old carter at the top of the Hythe on a breathing bellows; he's not been down those stairs in a year. And a child in the Pellow, two up, at the front, whose physic has to be kept cold. The mother wouldn't leave the cupboard. Warden Cobb's shut her in the back room with it." The lamp warden rubbed his jaw. "They're both on the cores, ma'am. The cell boxes are only the reserve."

"Until a core trips. Then they're on a box the size of a biscuit tin." Rhea's eyes went down the dark fronts. "That's two more things behind us. And the thing itself?"

"Below the Pellow. Two-thirds down, plumb in the middle." He pointed with the dead lantern at a tall front on the east side, with a crack over its door that somebody had stapled long ago with iron. "It's kept its time so far, ma'am. One, and ten minutes, and one."

"So far," said Rhea.

She stood at the top of the street with her hands in her pockets and looked down it for about as long as it would have taken to count twenty. Kiva looked where she looked. The cobbles ran away downhill, grey in the shine from the far bank. Two-thirds of the way down, in the very middle of the roadway, there was a place where the dark was the wrong color. It was a thin upright stroke of blue, taller than a man, like the gap at the edge of a door.

"Listen," said Rhea. She did not raise her voice, and twelve people heard her. "This is a Handful tear, in a street with a core under every doorstep and a cell box on every kitchen wall. That's what's behind you. So nothing dies within reach of a grating, and nothing's lit that I haven't put there. The reason is that every one of those cellars is a lamp."

She took her hands out of her pockets.

"Mine's the one on the step. Imrie lights for it at the Hythe; Pike has the cloth. Line stands dark at the forty, in case it gives us something that isn't a Handful. Seven." The pale eyes came round. "The one on the west pavement is inside your step. Cloth, lure, cut, catch, as you were passed. In the middle of the road, and no nearer a railing than you can help. Nairn stands over you. Go."

---

Kiva set the mark by the crown of the road.

She knelt and put her palm on the cobbles. They fell away south, and a little to either gutter. There was no wind. The air lay in the street like water in a trough. She chose the high middle, fourteen paces above the blue stroke, where the stone was dry and nothing sloped toward a grating.

"Here. Pru, two paces down from the south hem. On my word."

The sheet went down. It lay as Pike had made it lie, flat, at the first throw.

The husk was on the west pavement, eight or nine paces off, with its blunt head pushed through the area railings of a house whose ground-floor window was only a frame. It was the size of a big dog and the grey of a wet slate. It was working at a grating, patiently, as a dog works at a rabbit hole.

"Lure," said Kiva.

Pru's hands came up. The ward that stood between them was the size of a tea tray, and it hummed.

The blunt head came out of the railings. It turned, and it came straight, down off the curb and across the cobbles at a trot, as they always came, with nothing in it but direction. Kiva did not watch it. She watched the hem. She heard Kellan go past her on his struts, dark, and she heard his foot come down.

There was a line of blue-white light, as long as a breath.

"Dark!" said Kiva. "One."

Pru's ward went out like a pinched wick. Twenty-odd drams lay on the middle of the canvas, as soft as flour.

It went as it had gone on a dozen floors. Corners up at five. Perrin on the west hem with his arms held stiff, Kellan north, Pru south. The neck came in. The cord went round at her hooked elbow, slower by a count, once, and twice, and at the third turn her two dull fingers told her nothing whatever about the cross and she said, "Cord check," and Pru said, "Crossed right. Over, then under toward you," and it was.

"Sealed. Forty-one."

"Tidy," said Nairn. He had stood three paces off through all of it with his arms folded. "That's tidier than mine was at twenty. Get it on a pole."

Up the street, at the Hythe's step, there had been a longer line of light, and a white one. Kiva had seen it at the edge of her eye. She had heard a flat voice say *thirty-three.*

"Forty-one," said Kellan, low. He was unhooking the weights with quick fingers, and his eyes were bright. "Theirs was thirty-three. With a Torch, and a man who's done it nine hundred times. We could be under forty on the next. Did you see how it lay? It never lifted."

"I saw."

She had. She was pleased. She knew it for what it was, and gave it a number, and it went on sitting in her chest like a warm stone all the same.

Perrin took the bag on a pole across his shoulders and started up the hill toward the van at a careful walk. The sheet lay open on the crown of the road. Pru was blowing on her hands.

Ten minutes, the warden had said. *It's kept its time.* The next was not due until twenty-six minutes to three.

Down the street, the blue stroke opened a little, like an eye, and a third husk stepped out of it onto the cobbles.

It was two minutes early.

"There," said Kellan. "That's ours. Same mark. It's fourteen paces from the cloth and the cloth's down."

Nairn had his back to them. He had turned to look up the hill, to where a woman with a white streak in her hair was standing in the roadway with one hand raised, flat, at the height of her shoulder. He began to say something. It began with *wait.*

Kiva looked at the sheet.

It was down, and it was flat. The ground was the same ground. The air had not moved. Three minutes ago she had set a lure two paces below that hem and a Handful had come across the road to it like a tram on a rail, and they had sealed at forty-one.

"Cloth's down," she said. "Pru. Lure."

The tea tray came up, humming.

The third husk turned its head toward it.

And behind the husk, the blue opened as a mouth opens.

A fourth came through at a scramble, sideways, with a shine on its shoulders. And after it, before it was clear, something began to rise out of the gap that was not the size of a dog. It came up a knot at a time, as a rope is paid out of a hatch: a grey bulk, and another above it, and another, on legs as thick as lampposts, until its blunt top stood level with the second-floor sills and there was frost on it from crown to knee.

It did not look about. It had nothing to look with. The top knot swung, slowly, through a quarter of a circle, as a weathercock swings when the wind comes round.

It stopped on a tea tray.

"*GATE!*"

That was Rhea. Kiva had never heard her shout. Up the hill four people were running, and their boots were loud on the stones.

"Seven, *dark!*"

"Pru!" said Kiva. "Dark, dark—"

The ward went out. Pru was good at that; it was the thing she was best at in the world. It was out before the second word.

But the Flask had already lifted a leg.

Kiva saw it come. It came over the south hem of her own sheet, a grey column against the shine from the far bank, quite slowly, with all the time there was. It was coming down on the place where the light had been. Pru was standing in that place with her hands still raised and her mouth open, and she was not narrating anything.

Something hit her from the side.

It was Nairn. He took her at the waist with his shoulder, running, and she went over like a ninepin and slid on her back across the wet canvas. He went down on one knee with the force of it.

The leg came down.

There was a sound. Kiva had heard a brake shoe let go once, in a tram yard, when she was small. It was like that, and a good deal shorter.

Then light broke across the street behind her, silver and enormous, and the top knot lifted off the place where Pru had been and went to it. The Flask took one step north. It took another. Its blunt head came to rest against the gate, eight paces up the hill, and it leaned there, and was still.

It was standing over them.

Kiva was on her knees at the north hem with six grey legs round her like the posts of a pier. The lowest knot hung an arm's length above her head, slack and heavy, turning a little. She could feel the cold coming off it. Kellan lay flat on the cobbles with both hands spread. Pru was on her back on the sheet, staring straight up.

Nairn was beside the south hem. He had got up onto his elbows. One of his legs was under him, and the other went away beneath a column of grey as thick as a barrel, and did not come out the far side.

He was not making any noise. His face was the color of the canvas.

"Nobody," he said, quite clearly, "light anything."

Nobody did.

The third husk trotted past them up the hill, to the wall. The fourth had not followed. Kiva heard its feet on the pavement below her, quick and light, going toward a door with a crack over it.

She knelt under a Flask with her hands flat on her own new sheet. Three drams, and four signatures. It had lain so well.

*Lure,* she had said. She had read the wind and the fall of the ground. She had not left one thing out that a catcher is taught to look for. It had not once crossed her mind to ask who else in that street might be able to see a light.

*Not one of you has told me what's behind you.*

Up the hill, behind the silver, a pair of boots was coming down at a walk. It was a heavy, careful walk, the walk of somebody who has been forbidden to run and has dropped whatever he was carrying.

"That's a remark," said Rhea Sorn's voice, from somewhere beyond the legs. It was perfectly level. "It goes on the table, and it's mine. Seven, stay as you are. Don't speak. I'm coming round."
