## Verdict

The book works as one novel. It needs no structural repair, no shortening, and no change of voice. It makes a clear promise in chapter 1 ("There isn't a column"), keeps it honestly in chapter 48 ("I still have no column. I have a page"), and earns almost everything in between by showing its working.

Three small, targeted repairs are worth doing before anyone calls it finished. One of them (three record contradictions on payoff beats) I would treat as required, because the novel's whole argument is that records must agree. They are listed at the end. Everything else is taste, listed separately.

## What the book promises, and whether the prologue helps

**The promise.** A girl who opens all three doors cannot be measured by the instruments her city trusts, and the book will find out what she is by writing down, honestly, what she can and cannot do. Underneath that: reasons get tidied out of records, and somebody pays when they do.

## Who matters and why

- **Kiva.** Not because she is powerful. Because she writes down the part that makes her look a fool.
- **Mara.** The emotional center. The license letter in the drawer (ch 2, 7), the left hand that learned to read (ch 9), "I can only manage it from two miles off" (ch 45), and her own hand starving at the bank (ch 46–47). Her arc mirrors Kiva's without commentary.
- **Orla.** Jessamy Hart makes her caution cost something; "Show me six" and J.H.'s card (ch 48) close it without a hug.
- **Anwen.** A friend with a want of her own. "I'm not done" (ch 26) and "Ask me" / "Mine" (ch 39, 46) are the best friendship writing in the book.
- **Perrin.** Comic voice and conscience at once. "You're being rescued" becomes a shared tool.
- **Kellan.** Belief in measurement tested from both sides: his best habit prints a yield (ch 12); he goes for the gap as promised (ch 37); he walks off Halden's line on rule nineteen (ch 39).
- **Selka Dray.** The strongest choice in the cast: an objector who is right in part, never converts, and still calls the count from a chair. "I can hold both."
- **Rhea, Vane, Pike, Aske, the Reader, Mrs. Cobb, Pru, Noa, Brask.** Each has one clear function and one line that defines them. Pru (the spaniel, the fold, the red tag) and Noa ("They're her numbers"; "Not yet weighed"; "Not one thing") grow most.

The cast is large. For the adult lens it reads as a working city. For the younger lens, and for audio, the sheer number of named officials in Movements Four to Six is a load (see read-aloud).

## Do training, competition, records, home life, and live danger build rather than repeat?

They build. The reason is that each strand changes kind, not just size.

- **Training.** Drill at eleven seconds (ch 3) → the tin drill (ch 10) → no door at all (ch 11) → fourteen priced misses (ch 17–20) → six crossings with a hand count of 41, 58, 75, 95, 130 (ch 28–35) → one three-door sequence (ch 36). Every step has a cost and a witness.
- **Competition.** Each lane teaches one distinct thing, and the lanes that teach nothing are summarized in a paragraph (Wren House, ch 18; Stonehand, ch 28). Kiva never wins a lane by being strong. That restraint holds to the end.
- **Records.** Cell book → field book → yield book → request book → bearer's book → the Line's ledger → a tenement's ledger → a keeper's book → the Chair's "Witnessed" page → the manual. Each is a different kind of record and each changes an outcome. The count of ledgers is itself a load by Movement Six.
- **Home life.** The kitchen table is the spine: the book that must lie on it, the license, the relit panel (ch 36), the cuff.
- **Live danger.** Contained husks → a field Handful → a Flask on a bus → a street with people in it → an exam that turns into a response → a district's drains. The threat class changes every time.

One rhythm does repeat, and a whole-book reader will see it coming by Movement Four: Kiva over-applies the last lesson and someone else pays (Mara's license; Stroud's hands; Nairn's leg; Pru's fingertips). Movements Five and Six break it properly. At the Pit and at Stopgate the price is named first and is hers.

## Is Kiva's growth visible and earned?

Yes, on two tracks, and the second is the better one.

- **Skill.** Earned by arithmetic. The book never gives her a free step. The single most persuasive beat is ch 28, where the landing everyone wants to count is called a miss by a boy with a strip of paper.
- **Character.** From fixing the thing in front of her with an answer already prepared, to asking, naming what she cannot do, and handing pieces to other people. Ch 46 completes it: she lists everything that is not hers ("I've only the middle").

**Adult lens:** the character track is over-labeled in the second half. The narrator names the "answer ready" beat explicitly about 28 times, 24 of them in chapters 25–48. It is earned every time and still starts to feel like a tally.
**Thirteen-year-old lens:** the labels probably help, and the physical cost (two fingers that cannot tell edge from flat) will land hard. A reader hoping for a power payoff should know the book ends with her "certified to kneel." I think that is the right ending; it is not the expected one.

## Do the movement joins feel like one novel?

Structurally, yes. The calendar runs unbroken from the third of the fourth month to the sixteenth of the tenth, and I could not find a date that slips. Threads cross the joins cleanly: mast six (ch 2 → 13), the plate in the meadow (ch 18 → 22 → 41), rule nine (ch 19 → 23), "post, left" (ch 42 → 43).

One join is audible: **Movement One to Movement Two, in diction.** Chapters 1–8 are plain and close to contemporary ("I got you lunch"). From chapter 9 the narration and nearly every speaker move into a period-English idiom that then thickens. Counts by movement, from a post-read search:

**Forgotten, or deferred without a marker**

**Overexplained**

**Unfairly withheld**

## Does the ending satisfy this book and create honest desire for Book Two?

**It satisfies this book.** The three things the book asked are answered at their true size: why the post is crooked (a gate handle, and the reason belongs in print); what Kiva is (seated, a catcher, not classified, on a page rather than a column); what it cost (two fingers, for good). The last scene refuses the obvious speech twice. Kiva adds to "Went wrong" and "Second try" and leaves "Why" alone, then lets a child find out by running the lane. The final sentence is earned.

**It creates honest desire for Book Two.** No cliffhanger. The reasons to go on are things the book has already shown to be real: banks in step two hundred miles away at the same minute; a National Measure at Saltmere; a pump-fitter with one hand who wants to read what she wrote; Dray's promise to beat her fairly; the Ferren's question, "what else was happening when it did?"

**The tail is long.** The climax lands in ch 46; ch 47 is mostly inventory; ch 48 recovers completely. **Thirteen-year-old lens:** the cuff and the juniors will land; the unanswered "what is it?" may frustrate. **Adult lens:** the restraint reads as integrity.

## Repair priorities (three, in order)

1. **Reconcile the three record contradictions on payoff beats (items 1, 2, 3).** Required, in my judgment, and each is a matter of a phrase. They sit on the closing beat of Rhea's arc, on the scene that identifies the plate, and on the backstory that ties Rhea to Corran. In a novel whose case is that records must agree, a reader who catches one will go looking for others. Items 5 and 6 can ride along.
2. **State the lane scoring-call rule where it is first needed (item 4).** The referee's own explanation already exists at ch 19:255. A first-time reader, and especially a listener, can misread who scored through the whole of the first Stonehand lane (ch 15–16), which is one of the book's best sequences.
3. **Mark the planted clocks the book walks away from.** The anniversary, the "lid," and the Mars rumor are set down early and prominently, then left without a word. They do not need answers in Book One. They need to be visibly still open, the way the book marks every other unknown ("Why: unknown"; "Not yet weighed"). This is the one change that most improves honest desire for Book Two, because at present a careful reader cannot tell deferred from forgotten.

## Optional tastes (not required)

- **Diction seam at Movement One.** If one hand is wanted throughout, the cheaper direction is toward the later voice. I would leave it unless a listener stumbles.
- **Growth labels in the second half.** Let some of the "answer ready" beats pass without the narrator naming them, where the action already shows it.
- **Third telling of the in-air sequence (ch 46).** Its newness is in the turn and the hand-off; the reprise could carry less of the earlier wording.
- **Prologue for younger readers.** A presentation decision (how the listening or reading edition signals that Kiva is coming), not a text cut.
- **Chapter headings.** "Kiva:" on all 48 is inaccurate for about half.
- **Name clusters for audio.** Noted; renaming is invasive and I do not recommend it this late.
- **Items 7 and the 2661 pin.** One clause each would settle them.
- **Ledger recitals aloud.** The share arithmetic is right every time I checked it and is still the hardest material to hear.
