# Chapter 25 — Kiva: Asked Nicely

Orla's crate weighed about as much as a cat, and Kiva carried it out of the shed on Monday night as though it had been a full tin.

That was the bargain. She was to carry it at a walk, out at eight and in at ten, until the thirteenth, and in between she was to sit on the other one and do nothing that a healer could put a price on. She set it down in its old place, four paces short of the crooked post, and squared it to the lane, and Orla lowered herself onto it without a word of thanks, because none was owed.

"There," said Orla. "That's a dram's worth of strapping paid off at about a farthing a night. You'll be clear of me by Christmas."

"It's nine nights."

"Then I've been overcharged." The stick went down between her boots. "Sit. Tell me what you saw today that you'd not have seen from inside it."

It was the only work there was, and Kiva had found on the very first afternoon that it was harder than she had expected.

She had sat on the bench by the Low Lane door from two until four with her leg out, while the third team ran the litter down the brick for Saturday. Jude had put Pru at the front, where Kiva usually went, and for the first three carries Kiva had watched Pru, because that was her place, and had seen nothing whatever except her own mistakes being made by somebody shorter. It was only in the fourth that she had looked away, to see where the drain cover was, and had found that Noa Bexley had been standing for a whole minute with nobody near him and his blade arm hanging.

"Nobody was looking at Noa," she said. "Not us, and not Jude's dummy side either. Everybody on that floor was watching whoever had the litter. He could have walked the length of the lane."

"Did he?"

"No. He was watching the litter too."

"Mm." Orla's crooked face did not change, but she moved the stick a little, which was as good. "You've stood in that lane six weeks and you'd have sworn you knew where all four of them were. So would I, at your age. A fighter in a lane sees the thing she's fighting. It takes a seat by the door to see the lane." She looked down the two rows of posts into the dark. "Write that one in the right-hand column and leave the others empty. You'll want it again, and it'll not be in a lane when you do."

Kiva wrote it. She wrote it under Monday, on a page that had nothing above it but the date, and it looked very small there.

There was another thing from the afternoon, and she did not offer it. Orla had asked what she had seen, and this was only something she had said.

---

It had been at four, when the others were putting the litter away.

Noa had come and sat down beside her on the bench with a folded sheet in one hand and his pencil in the other, and he had not said anything at once. He looked as he generally looked by Monday, as if he had been up for two nights with a column of figures and had enjoyed it.

"It's the fourth," he said. "The Chair's order says your consent in writing, every month. Last month's ran out on Saturday. I didn't like to bring it to your kitchen with the team there."

"What have I to sign?"

"The same as before. Every figure in your book the same day. You and your sponsor see the sheet first. I stop if you say stop." He unfolded it on his knee. He had written it out fair, in the small square hand he kept for things that were going to an office. "I've put one more line. You can strike it if you like. *Nothing to any chartered party, or to any house, without her leave.* A man from the Halden technical office came into the mast room on Saturday. He asked for my notes. He was very pleasant about it."

Kiva looked at him.

"I said they were your numbers." His ears had gone pink, which they did when he had done something he was proud of and did not want it mentioned. "He says the tape's open to him on the first of next month whatever I say, and that's true; I looked it up. So I've twenty-six days. I'd like to spend them finding out what's on it before he does." He turned the pencil over. "There's one thing on it that isn't even yours. It's the keeper's. Your aunt reconciles registers for the Works, doesn't she?"

"Yes."

"Then she'd know. What's a T-0?"

The field book was lying shut on Kiva's good knee. There were two loose sheets inside the back cover, one of them three days old when she got it and the other two hundred and thirty-four years, and three copied lines beside them that she had made herself in a records office seven weeks ago. She could feel the edge of the fold through the canvas.

"It's a mark in the Transient Register," she said. "Every district keeps one. If the lamps flicker and the Works can find a cause, they write the cause. If they can't find one, they write T-0. Transient, nought. No cause."

"That's all?"

"That's what it means."

It was perfectly true, and it had come out of her as smoothly as a lid going on.

"Oh," said Noa. He was disappointed, and too polite to show much of it. "I'd hoped it was a class of fault. Something with a table. Then old Dunmore only meant that his lamps blinked and he couldn't say why." He wrote it down all the same, in the notebook, on the page she was not supposed to have seen the heading of. "Still. Every lamp on a dead ring. I'd like to know how a lamp blinks with nothing in it. I'll ask at Hanley Street; they'll have the book the keeper copies into."

"They will," said Kiva.

She signed the sheet. She did not strike the new line, and she read it twice before she put her name under it, as she had learned to do at a kitchen table. *Without her leave.* He had written that for her, against a pleasant man who funded papers, and had been pink about it.

Then he had gone to wind the tape, and she had sat on the bench with the book on her knee.

*They aren't the team's,* she had told herself on Saturday night, with feet on the stair. That had been a reason. She had been rather satisfied with it. It was Monday now, and Noa was not the team, and he had just asked her to her face, and she had given him the dictionary.

She had known what she would say before he reached the end of the question. She remembered knowing. It was the same feeling as a foot coming down on a step that was exactly where she had expected it, and she had heard of that feeling lately, across a kitchen table, from somebody who had been on the other end of it twice.

*Not having your answer ready would be a start.*

In the yard, under the lamp, she turned back a page and looked at the right-hand column. There was room in it. She did not write anything there, because she did not yet know what the true entry was, and she had been told on good authority that a guess did not belong on that page.

"You've gone quiet," said Orla.

"I'm thinking about what I've not got an answer to."

"Good. Do it sitting down." The stick turned in the old hands. "You've a week of that left, and I've found that most people can't bear two days."

---

Healer Aske had Kiva's left hand flat on a towel on Wednesday morning, and a hat pin in his own.

"Eyes shut," he said. "Say when."

She shut them. The Wren House infirmary smelled of salt and boiled linen, and somewhere down the passage a trolley went by on a squeaking wheel. She felt the pin on her little finger at once, and on the next, and said so. Then there was a long time when she felt nothing at all, and then something that might have been a pin or might have been the memory of one.

"When," she said. "I think."

"You think." The cold thumb moved. "And now?"

"When. No. A moment ago."

"About two breaths ago, by my reckoning." He put the pin back in his lapel. "Open. Well, they've done sulking. I said a week, and they've taken it to the day. The first reports late and the second reports later, and neither of them will tell you the truth about a cord. That's where you were on the twenty-eighth, to a hair. You're not to mistake it for getting better."

"I shan't."

"You will, the first morning they don't trouble you. Everybody does." He wrote two lines on her card, and blotted them. "The knee stays strapped until I've seen it on the thirteenth. No lanes, no floors, nothing lit. That's not altered."

"May I go on a tram for something that isn't a yard?"

"Where to?"

"The Compact Library. I'd be sitting down."

Aske considered her over the card for some moments, as a person does who has been asked a harmless question by somebody with a history.

"In a chair," he said. "In a building. With a roof on it, I'm told, and a very fine one. I've never known a library to do anybody's knee a mischief, though I had a student once who fell asleep in one and was locked in till Monday." He handed her the card. "You may sit in it as long as you please. If I hear that you've sat in it on one leg, with anything open, I shall send your sponsor another account, and this time I'll charge for the stamp."

She met Greta Stroud on the stair.

It was the main stair, wide and shallow, and Stroud was coming down it very slowly on the wall side, with a boy in Graywater blue a step below her and both her hands held out a little before her as someone carries a tray. They were wrapped in dry linen now, and the wrappings were smaller. Her coat was buttoned to the neck, and done up wrong by one.

She stopped when she saw who it was. So did Kiva, since there was no way round on that stair that would not have been a kind of running.

"Shelf," said Stroud.

"Miss Stroud."

"They tell me a team sent a dram up here with my name on what it was for. At par, off its own book, four signatures. The sister showed me the line." The grey eyes were level. "First-years."

"Yes."

"My company pays for my easing. I'd have had every sitting of it without you."

"I know. The grader told me, and I told the team."

"Then what was it for?"

Kiva opened her mouth.

She had it. It was a perfectly decent answer, and she had given it at a kitchen table on Saturday night in front of three people: *we called the floor dark, and I'd like it to be ours.* It stood in her mouth as round and ready as a coin.

She shut her mouth again, and Stroud waited, with her hands held out in front of her.

"I don't know yet," said Kiva. "I thought I did. I'd something I could say. I'm not certain it's why." Her face was hot. "I'll tell you when I am, if you want it then."

Stroud looked at her for some time.

"Mm," she said. "That's the first thing out of you I've half liked. The other was very nicely put, I've no doubt." She began to go down again, a step at a time, with the boy backing before her. "Your girl goes out on Mace's line on Sunday."

"She's her own."

"So you said, and I said good." A step. "Then I'll tell you a thing for nothing, since she'll not think to. That line's the summer's work and it's a fair one, and it leaves the night van a ward short until I can button my own coat. Sorn's been doing her sums all week. If you've a shift coming with that van, and I hear that you have, it'll be a thin one." She did not look back. "I'd not hurry me. I'd not dawdle either."

Kiva stood on the stair until the two of them had reached the bottom.

Then she went down herself, holding the rail in her right hand. She had come for a healer's leave and had been given it. She had not expected to be told, by the person with the most right to say it, exactly what a dark floor went on costing a week after everybody had gone home, and she noticed that Stroud had not once raised her voice to do it.

---

The Compact Library had a very fine roof.

Everybody said so, and it was the first thing a stranger was shown, a long sweep of green copper over the top floor with a lantern of glass at the middle of it. Kiva had been under that roof perhaps a dozen times in her life, at a long table in the reading room, looking up tolerances for Mara. She had never once been below the ground floor, and she had not known until Thursday afternoon that there were three flights of stairs under it.

They were iron stairs, and they rang. She took them one at a time, with the strapped knee leading and the field book under her arm, and at the foot of the third she came out into a long low room where the shelves stood closer together than the posts in the New Lane.

It was cold down there, and very quiet, with a dry smell of old paste. There were induction lamps on the ends of the rows, turned low, and they hummed. Each row had a card in a brass holder. *Lane manuals, superseded. Works tables, superseded. Charters, lapsed.* She went along them slowly, reading.

At the far end of the room, under the one lamp that had been turned up, a woman sat at a table with her back to the stair.

She was small and upright and a great deal older than Mara. She wore two pairs of spectacles, one on her nose and one pushed up into grey hair that had been pinned by somebody in a hurry, and she was eating something out of a paper twist with her left hand while her right went on writing. She did not turn round.

"The reading room is two floors up," she said. "The stair you came down is the one you want. People find it easier going the other way."

"I'm not lost," said Kiva. "At least, I don't think I am. I was told there was a reader in the superseded stacks who could read a record hand that went out two hundred years ago. I was told that if anybody asked her nicely, she might."

The pen stopped.

"Were you?" The woman turned in her chair, and looked at Kiva over the lower pair of spectacles, from her boots to her hair. Her eyes were very bright. "By whom?"

"A clerk at the Hanley Street records office. He told my aunt, and she told me."

"The careful one. With the cough." She set down the paper twist. "He sends me somebody about once in three years, and he always tells them the same thing, and not one of them has ever known how it's done. They stand there. Some of them bring flowers. One man brought a ham." She folded her hands on the table. "Well? I'm told it's to be done nicely. I've twenty minutes before my tea, and I'm perfectly willing to be asked."

Kiva had practiced it on the tram. She found that she could not remember a word.

"Please," she said.

There was a silence.

"Is that all of it?"

"I'd some more. It's gone." She shifted the book. "I've a copy of a margin and I can't read it and I've wanted to for seven weeks. I'd be very grateful. I've no ham."

The woman laughed. It was a short, dry, astonished sound, like a book being dropped, and it seemed to surprise her as much as anybody.

"That'll do," she said. "It's a good deal nicer than the ham. Sit down, child, before that leg decides for you. I'm Lettice Arkwright, and you're to call me Reader, because everybody does, and it saves them wondering about Lettice. What have you brought me?"

Kiva sat. She took the clerk's sheet from inside the back cover and laid it on the table, and turned it to face the other way.

The Reader did not touch it at first. She pushed the upper spectacles down over the lower ones and bent until her nose was a hand's width from the paper, and for some time she only looked, moving her head a little from side to side, as Mara did over a drum.

"He's drawn it," she said. "Bless the man. He couldn't read a stroke of it, so he's drawn every stroke, and he's even kept the places where the pen rested. That's how I know that I can trust it. A clerk who thought he understood it would have tidied." Her finger came down beside the three plain lines. "This is the entry. *Night. Every lamp, every district. No fault found.* Anybody could give you that."

"Yes."

"And this is somebody who didn't care to leave it there." The finger moved to the row of bent pins. "It's the late record hand. The Works taught it to its night clerks until about 2480, so that a man could take down a fault as fast as it was shouted to him. It was never meant for the public, which is why the public can't read it. I should think there are four people living who can, and two of them are wrong about the vowels." She drew the lamp nearer. "I'll give it to you as it stands. I don't improve."

She read it aloud, slowly, with her finger under each mark.

"*Not for the column, by the Warden's order. A sister of the Even Hand was at the wicket before it was light. Sent down by her Master to ask whether the city's lamps had gone in the night. She did not know why she was sent. Told, yes, all. The Warden says a question is not a fault and is not to be entered. Entered here. Nobody had told them. R.*"

Kiva did not move.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere over their heads, a long way up, a chair scraped on a floor.

"Again," she said. "Please."

The Reader read it again, in exactly the same voice, and did not look up until she had done.

"*Nobody had told them,*" said Kiva.

"That's his reason. He's put it last, where a night clerk puts it, and he's underscored it, which I've not seen that hand trouble to do above twice." The Reader sat back. "R. is the clerk. I can't give you more of him than that; the Works paid its night men by the initial. He was told to leave something out of the city's book, by somebody who had the right to tell him. So he left it out of the column and wrote it at the side in a hand that his Warden very likely couldn't read. I've a good deal of sympathy with R."

"Did they ever say why they'd asked? The Even Hand."

"If they did, he didn't hear of it, or he'd have written it. That sort always does." She took off the upper spectacles and polished them on her sleeve. "Now. You came down three flights on one leg for this, and you've gone the color of the paper. I don't ask why; nobody in this room is obliged to say why. But I'll tell you what a reader would ask next, since you're new to it. She'd ask who the Master was."

"Do you know?"

"I know who held the hill house in 2446. So would anybody who'd been a child in Eastbank; they put her on the flood-wall in bronze." The bright eyes rested on Kiva's face. "Hesper Annick. Glory in 2431, and a ward over that wall for nine days. She's the Order's saint, though they'd dislike the word. And I've eleven boxes of her papers on the shelf behind your left shoulder."

---

Kiva turned round on her chair, carefully, and looked.

It was a shelf like the rest. The boxes on it were grey and square and tied with tape, and each had a number on its end. There were ten of them in a row. The eleventh stood a little apart, on the shelf below, and there was a red card in its holder.

"Why are they down here?" she said. "If she's as famous as that."

"Because they're in dispute, and the Library puts what's in dispute where it'll be kept and not handled. That's what the room's for. You'll have been taught that superseded means wrong." The Reader got up, with a hand on the table, and went along the nearest row. "It doesn't. It means that somebody decided a thing needn't be said any longer. Sometimes they were right. Here. I'll show you a small one, and then you'll know what you're sitting among."

She came back with two books, one thick and blue and nearly new, and one thin and brown with its spine gone.

"The *Founders' History* of your own school. I take it that it is your school; you've the walk of somebody who's been thrown about by the Crooked Line. This is the ninth edition, which they hand to first-years. This is the fourth." She opened them side by side, at pages she did not have to look for. "The same sentence. Read me the new one."

Kiva read it. "*The nine-ringed plate carried by the first teachers is preserved by the School.*"

"Is it? I've asked to see it for thirty years. I'm told that it's being conserved." She turned the brown book round. "And the old one."

The print was small and uneven. *The plate of nine rings went out with the Line and is not the School's to show. It is released to one who carries it. We have not been told why and have not thought it ours to ask.*

Kiva read that once.

She was aware of the cord at her collar as she had not been for a week. It lay where it always lay, and the thing on the end of it was cold, and she kept both her hands flat on the table and did not let either of them go up.

"They don't agree," she said.

"They do not. And the ninth agrees with itself from cover to cover, which is how you can tell that somebody's been through it with a comb." The Reader shut both books. "Nobody lied, I dare say. A committee found a sentence that made the School look as if it had mislaid something, and wrote a kinder one, and the next committee copied the kind one because it was the one in front of them. That's how a record loses a thing. It's hardly ever a fire. It's a person with good manners, copying."

She put the two books down, one on the other, and sat.

"Which brings me to the red card, and I'm obliged to tell you all of this before you ask me for anything, so you'll oblige me by listening to the end." Her voice had altered. It was drier, and a little slower, as a voice is when it is reading out terms. "Ten of those boxes are open to any reader. They're Ward patterns, and they're above my head and very likely above yours. The eleventh is her private papers. The Order says a Halden heir stole the lot and gave it here to look generous, and wants that box back. The Halden endowment says it was a gift, lawfully made, and wants it kept. They went to law about it, and in 2611 they settled, and I've administered the settlement for thirty-one years. It's four lines long. I know it better than my own address."

She counted them on her fingers.

"One. The box stays on that shelf and belongs to nobody until they agree, which they never will. Two. Any reader may ask for it, in the room upstairs, under my eye. No copying, not a word, not on a cuff. Three. A reader under eighteen wants a guardian's hand beside her own." Her eyes went to Kiva's face and stayed there. "And four. Every request is entered in a book, with the reader's name, and that book is open. I send notice to both parties the same day. They've fourteen days in which to say nothing, and each of them may send one person to sit in the room while the box is read. They may not speak to the reader. They may look as much as they please."

Kiva sat still.

"So if I ask, the Order's told."

"And the endowment. By name. *K. Rowan asks to read Annick, Personal.* I shall write it in my best hand and send it up the hill and across the river, and I've no leave to do otherwise, and I shouldn't if I had. That's the whole reason the box is still here to be asked for." She laid her hand flat on the two histories. "I'm not telling you not to. I'm telling you what it costs, before, because I've seen people told after."

"Might I see the book? The one that's open."

The Reader drew it out from under the lamp.

It was not a large book. It had a black cloth cover and a ribbon, and when she opened it at the ribbon there were not a dozen lines on the page. Most of them were the same. *Endowment's inspection, annual,* in a clerk's hand, with a date each spring. *A sister of the Order. Sat one hour. Box not opened,* each autumn, in the Reader's.

"She comes on the same day every year," said the Reader. "She isn't let open it without notice to the other side, any more than you are, and she's never once given notice. She sits beside it. I bring her a chair."

There were three lines on the two pages that were different from the rest.

One was thirty years old and in a faded ink, a scholar's name with a college after it. One was last spring's, *Halden technical office, by leave of the endowment; ten boxes, patterns; Personal not asked for.*

The third was sixteen years old.

*O. Dane, teacher, the Crooked Line. Annick, Personal. Read in the room, one afternoon. 2664.*

Kiva read it, and read it again. It did not alter. It was Orla's own hand, the square upright capitals that came back on Mondays in the margin of the field book, and she would have known it on a wall.

*It isn't much,* Orla had said, on Sunday, with the stick across her knees. *You shall have what I've got.* And she had given her a bearer's book at the Home Line hall, and a favor twenty-five years old, and a man who had found another hand in a ledger. She had given all of that, plainly and in order, and Kiva had sat on the spare crate and thought that she was being told everything.

"You know that name," said the Reader.

"She's my sponsor."

"Ah." It was a very small sound. "Then I've shown you something I'd no notion I was showing, and I'm sorry for the manner of it, though not for the book. It's open. That's what it's for." She shut it gently on its ribbon. "Shall I write you in?"

"Not today."

"No. I'd not either, with that face." The Reader folded the clerk's sheet along its old crease and held it out. "Take your margin. It's a good one, and it's yours, and nobody need be told that you've read it but the two of us and a man with a cough. When you've a guardian's hand and you know your own mind, I'm here until six, every day but Sunday. I shan't have gone anywhere. I never do."

Mara had the brass weight on the trunk book and a pencil behind her ear, and she listened to all of it without once looking down.

Kiva told it standing, with her back to the stove. She gave her the margin first, word for word, since it was the part that had been Mara's errand to begin with, and she watched her aunt's left hand come to rest on the table as it had rested on the canvas-backed book outside a healer's cart.

"*Nobody had told them,*" said Mara. "A sister at the wicket before light, and she didn't know what she'd been sent for." She was quiet. "I've read that register line forty times, Kiva. I'd have sworn there was nothing in it but lamps."

"There wasn't. He was told to leave it out."

"By a Warden of the Works. Two hundred and thirty-four years ago, and I could tell you what the man's voice sounded like." Mara took the pencil from behind her ear and laid it down. "Go on. There's more. You've not sat."

So Kiva told her about the eleventh box, and the four lines of the settlement, and who would be sent a notice in a reader's best hand. Then she told her whose name was in the black book, and in what year.

Mara did not say anything for rather a long time.

"Sixteen years," she said.

"She told me on Sunday that I should have what she'd got. She said it was little enough."

"And she gave you the Home Line's book and left out the Library's." Mara's face was perfectly still. It was the stillness she kept for a cell that was reading wrong, before she had decided whether the fault was in the cell or the meter. "I'm not going to tell you what that means. I can think of two things it might mean, and one of them I'd not forgive, and I've known that woman for two months. You've known her for four years."

"I thought I had."

"Then ask her. To her face, tomorrow, before you've made it into anything." Mara looked at the stove, and at the clerk's sheet, and at her niece. "You'll want my hand for that box."

"I'd meant to go by myself first. I did go by myself."

"So you did, and you came back with more than I'd have got." There was something at the corner of her mouth that was nearly a smile. "I'll sign. I'm saying so before you ask, so that you needn't. But I'll say one thing about the cost, since she was honest enough to name it." She tapped the black cover of the keeper's branch book, which still lay at the end of the table where it had lain since Saturday. "A man in a black coat came to a boy in a mast room and asked very civilly for your numbers. He'll read that tape on the first whether we like it or not. If your name's going to be in front of that house, I'd as soon it were in an open book, in your own hand, with a date on it, as in the back of somebody's notebook. A request book is a book."

"A meter, and a shutdown path."

"Don't be clever. I haven't found the shutdown path yet." Mara got up, and moved the kettle onto the hot plate, and stood with her hand on its handle. "Speak to your sponsor. Then we'll go on Monday, the pair of us, and be written down."

# Chapter 26 — Kiva: However Produced

Kiva did not practice it on the tram.

She had practiced the Library, and it had deserted her at the foot of the stair, and she had come away with more than she had gone for. So on Friday night she carried the crate out of the shed at a walk, and set it down four paces short of the crooked post, and waited until Orla had got herself onto it and had the stick between her boots.

"Your name's in the Reader's book," she said. "At the Compact Library. *Annick, Personal. Read in the room, one afternoon.* It's sixteen years old."

Orla did not start. She sat as she always sat, with her bad leg out and her hands folded on the head of the stick, and her crooked face in the lamplight was as hard to read as a wall.

"So she showed you the book," she said. "Arkwright. I'd wondered whether she would. She's obliged to, if you ask."

"I asked."

"Then you were down those stairs with your 2446 before I'd finished telling you there was a hall and a favor." There was something in her voice that was not displeasure. "What did the margin say?"

Kiva told her. She had it by heart now, and she gave it as the Reader had given it, without improving.

Orla heard it out. When it was done she sat for a little while and looked at the cross on the ground.

"*She did not know why she was sent,*" she said. "No. She wouldn't have." The stick turned. "Sit down, Rowan. You're standing over me like a bill, and I'd as soon be asked this at my own height."

Kiva sat on the spare crate. The lamp hissed on its hook.

"I read that notebook in the autumn of 2664," said Orla. "I'd been three years without a candidate of my own. I was not let sponsor one. I'd gone down to those stacks to find out whether anybody in five hundred years had ever written a line about training a boy with two doors, because I'd had one, and I had put him in front of four thousand people, and I wanted very much to learn that somebody else had made the same mistake first. Nobody had. Or nobody had written it." Her eyes did not leave the cross. "I found a card in the wrong drawer with a famous name on it. "Until my Kindling."

"Until every lamp in this yard went out at eight minutes to three, and you lay on the ground just there with your eyes open." She nodded at a place two posts down. "I'd a card, and a dead woman's notebook, and a child looking at something a long way off. Three things. I'd no line to draw between them. "No."

*A Method of Stepping Cells in Bank. 2391.*

He understood about half of it at the first reading and nearly all at the third.

A bank was only a row of cells. Each kept its own time and drifted its own way, which was slow and safe, and the Works liked it so, because one cell could fail without taking its neighbors. But a row that kept one time could be driven as one engine. The method was so simple that he laughed aloud at the table. You salted every cell late, with one pinch of ash that had already been made to keep a beat, and the whole row fell in behind it and beat together, and one operator with one hand on one cell could govern four-and-twenty. Halden had built its yards that way for almost three hundred years. The refineries ran on it, and the lift yards, and the big tram sheds. A pump house did not. He thought of twelve needles on a load board, nodding together, on a branch that had never been stepped in its life.

*The Proprietor claims the Step, and all ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.*

"That clause used to be thought rather greedy."

"Mr. Bexley. We do like to know which of our foundations the young are reading; I hope you don't mind. Adlam. I ought to have said so at the Pit." He nodded at the case. "*However produced.* My own grandfather's grandfather sat on the board that renewed it, and he asked what the words were for. What else produces a stepped ash, he said, but a stepped bank? Noa's mouth was dry again. It seemed to do that in this man's company.

"There's a caution," he said. "Under the claim. In the small print." He put his finger on it. "*Not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under load.*"

"There is. It's quite sound. A row of cells that have each been tuned to their own business don't care to be taken by the collar." Mr. Adlam looked at him with what seemed to be real kindness. Stepped ash had only ever come out of cells. That was what the patent said, and the comb, and a grader of nineteen years. Now it was coming out of a rift. Either a reed was wrong, or something on the far side of a tear had been listening to the banks of Meridian for long enough to learn the beat.

He did not write that. It was a guess, and he was getting particular about guesses.

He wrote the four figures instead: the notch, the eleven seconds, the Low Lane's blank tape, the clause. They were hers. His own terms said *every figure in her book the same day,* and he had signed them, and so had she.

She had not kept her end of something. He did not see that it let him off his.

*Tomorrow,* he wrote. *After the meet. All of it. Then ask her straight, in front of whoever's there, so I can't make it easy for either of us.*

# Chapter 27 — Kiva: One Crossing

The first tram on Sunday left Coper Street at twenty minutes past five, and there was nobody on it but the driver and two girls and a kit bag.

Kiva carried the bag. It had been settled on the laundry step without discussion, by her picking it up; it was slate blue, with a white wave stitched on the flap, and it was a good deal heavier than Orla's crate. Anwen had let her take it. She sat by the window with her hands in her lap and her list in her coat, and for the first three stops she watched the street lamps going out one after another as the light came up behind them.

"Finch crosses that cord right four times in five," she said, at the fourth. "I had her do it twenty times after you'd gone up. Watch the fifth. She gets it wrong when she's talking, and she talks most when she's frightened."

"I'll watch."

"Don't correct her in the count. Call the check and let her find it. She'll be quicker next time if she's found it herself."

"You never told me that was what you were doing."

"No. You'd have minded." Anwen turned from the window. "The left pan's still slower than the right, when Kellan has it. He knows. He'll tell you that he's allowing for it, and he is, and it's still slower."

It went on like that along the whole of the Brickfields road. She gave the team to Kiva a piece at a time, the corners and the pans and which of Perrin's jokes meant that his ribs were hurting, and Kiva put each piece away without writing it, because she was holding a kit bag in both arms and because none of it wanted a column. Neither of them mentioned a gate. It rode between them on the slatted seat, and they both knew where it was.

The company hall was a long slate-blue front with its lamps still lit. There were six people on the pavement already, standing about with bags at their feet in the manner of people who have been told a quarter to six and have come at half past five, and at the top of the steps a broad grey man with a board under his arm was counting them without appearing to.

"That's Mace," said Anwen. "He'll not speak to you. He doesn't speak to anybody before seven."

They stopped at the foot of the steps. Kiva set the bag down on the flags.

"The culverts first," said Anwen. "A fortnight of nights. Then the Sag. We hold a lamp for the inspectors and stand reliefs on anything that opens. I'll be allowed a wall of my own by the fourth week if I don't flicker." She looked up at the blue front, and for a moment her face was not level in the least. It was seventeen, and it wanted this more than it could conveniently hide. "I've wanted to stand on those steps since I was eleven."

"I know. I've seen the poster on your door."

"Everybody's seen the poster on my door." She bent and took the strap. "Write me the counts."

"What?"

"Whatever you stand under while I'm gone. The shift. Where the mark was, and why, and what it sealed at, and who called. Send it up by the hall; Mace's runner comes out to the line twice a week." The strap went over her shoulder. "I'd like to read what I wasn't at. I'm not asking for a letter. I'm asking for the counts."

"With the *why* column?"

"Particularly that." Anwen settled the bag. "And if you find that you've had an answer ready, put that in too. I'll know anyway. I'd as soon see it in your hand."

She went up the steps. She did not look back from the top, and Kiva had not thought that she would. The broad man glanced at her, and made a mark on his board, and she took her place at the end of the row with her back to the road.

Kiva stood a little longer on the pavement than there was any need to.

She had no answer ready. She had looked for one all the way from Coper Street and had found the place quite bare, and it had not felt like a virtue. It had felt like standing in a doorway with nothing to carry. Then she turned, and went to wait for the tram going the other way.

Hollis Vane had carried the tracing in his slate case for eleven days, and on Monday morning he took it down three flights of iron stairs.

He had shown it first to the two oldest hands on the Line. One had said that it was a laundry mark and the other had held it upside down. "It's the same hand," she said, mostly to herself.

"The same as what?"

"As a great deal that I've read. It's the Works' late record hand; your people borrowed it for a generation because it was quick." She did not explain further, and he had the impression of a door being shut with great courtesy. "Where does this stand in its book?"

"The back. There's a part headed in the old print. *Directions to the Unplaced.* Any officer who gives a field direction to somebody the schools haven't classified writes it there, with his reason. "One before me. In a book that's been copied forward since before the Compact had a charter."

Reason: the sluice wheel wanted a ward to clear its race and a stance to turn it, and there was no reaching it twice. Result: turned. Eleven streets dry.*"

Vane had stopped breathing through his nose.

"*Cost: her left hand, without feeling to the wrist, that night and after. Not returned by midsummer. She says the crossing took more than both together. I believe her. I would not direct it twice in one flood, nor to anyone I had not watched miss. E.K., watch.*" The finger moved down a line. It was very quiet under the lamp.

He had stood on a cinder floor with a slate, and a girl forty paces off had told him in a carrying voice that she had missed fourteen of fourteen. He had weighed that for about as long as it took to draw a breath. He had thought, at the time, that he was weighing it rather well.

*Nor to anyone I had not watched miss.*

A man on a levee two hundred and nine years ago had set that down as a rule, having learned it in one night. Vane had been handed the same rule by luck, by a candidate who happened to be honest, and he had not known that it was one.

"A crossing," he said. "They'd a word for it."

"They'd a word, and a heading for the page, and a column for what it cost." The woman took off the upper spectacles. "Your present ledger asks for the reason and the result, I should think. Somebody tidied the third away. It's nearly always the third."

It did. He had written *Hurt: Rowan, collapse after. Cade, ribs, again.* He had written nothing about a hand, because he had not known that there was a hand to write about, and nobody at the Wren House cart had thought it was a foreman's business.

"What became of her? Wold."

"That's every word in your ledger. If the Line kept a roll for the 2470s, I've never been shown it."

"It did. It was recopied in the 2550s. Names and years." He heard how that sounded. "No remarks."

"No. There wouldn't be." She folded the tracing along its crease and gave it back to him, and with it a half sheet on which she had written the whole thing out in a clear round hand as she read. "That's for whoever it concerns. I don't ask who. I'll say only that it's the second time in a week I've been asked to read that hand by somebody who went white at the end of it, and I've a notion that the two of you ought to be introduced."

He met them on the stair.

He was going up and they were coming down, the aunt first, whom he knew from a berm on the Sag Line with a mast drum under her hand, and the girl behind her, one step at a time on a strapped knee. There was not room to pass. He backed down to the landing and took off his hat.

"Miss Rowan. And Miss Rowan."

"Officer," said the aunt. She did not ask what a Home Line watch officer was doing in a library basement, and he saw her decide not to.

He looked at the girl. He looked, since he could not now help it, at her left hand on the rail, and at how the last two fingers held and the first two only rested.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "Your sponsor's yard, if she'll have me. There's a thing I've had read. It's about you only as far as it's about anybody who's ever done what you did, and I'd sooner you heard it from the page than from me."

"Is it bad?"

"It's two hundred years old. I don't know yet what it is." He put his hat on. "I know that I wrote four lines about you on the thirtieth and thought them complete. I'll be mending that."

The Circuit's travelling mast came into the Old Yard on Tuesday evening, in three pieces, and Perrin supervised.

He could do nothing else. He stood in the lane with his arms held a little away from his strapped sides, like a man in a barrel, and told Kellan and Noa where to put their feet. There was a brass head as big as a bucket, and a tripod of jointed legs taller than he was, and a drum in a felt-lined case that Noa would let nobody else touch. On the lid of the case hung a lead seal with the Placement Chair's stamp in it.

"A foot to your left," said Perrin. "No. The other left. You've a post behind you, Renn; it's been there five hundred years and it has the right of way."

"It leans."

"It's leaning away from you. Take the hint."

They stood it at last on the east side of the lane, six paces from the scraped line and well clear of the cross. Noa broke the seal in front of all of them and read out the number on it, and wrote the number down, and threaded a tape with his tongue between his teeth.

"One pen," he said. "Set to her and nobody else. I copy every tape for the Chair the next morning. That's her letter; it's what makes the instrument sanctioned." He hesitated. "It's also what makes the tape hers to show. Not to chartered parties in thirty days; that's for events. But she's a hearing coming with Dray's objection, and she's told Dray that she'll hear it on evidence."

"So anything I do in front of that drum, Selka Dray may read," said Kiva.

"If the Chair thinks it bears. Yes."

"It was Anwen's condition," said Kellan. "A sanctioned mast, every go. I thought at the time that it was for your safety."

"It was. It's for everybody's." Kiva looked at the brass head, which had begun to tick. She had asked a library for a private box the day before and had been told, very fairly, that there was no such thing. "A meter's a meter. It doesn't choose who looks."

Orla had watched all of this from her crate without a word. Hollis Vane stood behind her with his hat in his hand. He had come down the lane at eight exactly, and had taken off the hat to Orla and nodded to the rest, and since then he had waited, as a man waits who has learned on tear watches how much of the work is standing still.

"Now, Hollis," said Orla.

He did not make a speech of it. He told them what the ledger was and where the page stood in it, in about four sentences. Then he took a half sheet from his slate case and read out what was written there, in a flat, careful, outdoor voice, from the levee to the initials.

Nobody spoke while he read. The mast ticked. At *her left hand, without feeling to the wrist,* Kiva felt Kellan's eyes come round to her, and then Perrin's, and she kept her own on the half sheet.

*She says the crossing took more than both together.*

Kiva had written that. She had written it nine nights ago on that crate, with a yellow lamp over her, in words that were hardly different. *The work goes across. It costs more than both doors.* She had thought that she was the first person alive to find it out, and that had been a lonely thing to know, and rather a grand one. Now a watch officer was reading it to her out of the year 2471.

The woman had had a name. It had been written down by a man who believed her.

"*Entered on the Line's roll as catcher, autumn,*" said Vane. "*Hand as before.*" He lowered the sheet. "That's the end. There's no roll for those years that keeps a remark. I don't know how long she lived or whether it ever came back."

"A catcher," said Kiva.

"So it says."

"With one hand that couldn't feel a cord."

"I noticed that as well." His weathered face did not change. "I wrote on my own slate, at the Pit, *Hurt: Rowan, collapse after,* and I copied it into the ledger next morning as it stood. I never asked what your hand was doing. E.K. would have known to look. I've amended the entry. It reads now, *Cost: two fingers of the left hand, eased within the hour, returned to their earlier state and no better.* If that's wrong in any word, tell me, and I'll have it right before I go."

"It's right."

"Then it'll stand under my name." He put the sheet away. "Dane. That's what I had. The rest's yours."

Orla got up.

She did it slowly, with the stick, and she stood in the lane between the mast and the crooked post and looked at each of them in turn, the two who had carried and the one who could not and the one who was going to jump.

"I've run this yard on a stick and two eyes," she said. "I'd no rule for this, because nobody had left me one. Somebody's left me one now. It's two hundred years late and I'm going to use every word of it." She held up her left hand, and folded down a finger. "A step first, every night, off her own ration. A curb's height and a pace. If it isn't clean, there's no go, and nobody argues."

A second finger.

"One go in a night. Never two. *Not twice in one flood.* I don't know why he wrote that, and I'd sooner keep his rule than learn his reason."

A third.

"Before she leaves the line she tells me whether the bottom thread is cold. Aloud. If she says *one,* she sits down." A fourth. "In the air: if that Ember isn't shut clean at the top, she calls *off* and comes down as herself, on the right leg, rolling. That isn't a miss to be ashamed of. That's the only part of this I can teach, and I mean to see it done before I see the other."

"And after?" said Kiva.

"After, you show me that hand before you show me your face. Any white in those two fingers and we're done for the night. White past a slow sixty, and you see Aske before you see this yard again." The thumb came down on the rest. "Bexley says nothing till she's finished. That's Pryce's condition and it's a sound one. And there's to be one voice in this lane besides mine that may stop it."

"Anwen said I'm to call *off* for Seven," said Perrin. He looked rather surprised to hear himself. "While she's gone. Because I mind things."

"Then you call it for this as well. If you see what I don't, you say the word three times and I'll thank you afterward." Orla lowered her hand. "I'm told that there are two drams in a glass at the ash office with four names on them. I'd like the four to know what they're buying. It isn't six landings. It's six that I'd ask for twice. The misses will cost you next to nothing, and they're most of what there is to learn."

It was the thirteenth. She had not lit so much as a strut in a fortnight.

The step came first. She pushed off the line, a curb's height and a pace, and shut the Ember at the top, and set the still door under her foot before the ground. It was like stepping onto a mattress the size of the yard. Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder and went through her and into the earth, and she counted three and gave the ground its weight back and shut the door.

"Clean," said Orla. "Is the thread cold?"

Kiva laid two fingers of her right hand on her breastbone.

"Cold."

"Then you may have your go. To the cross. One low bound, as it was in the spring, and not an inch higher because there are people looking." The stick pointed. "Say it."

"Rowan, changing on three. Ember, to still."

It sounded very small. She had last said it across an arena to eleven hundred people. There were four here, and a brass head on three legs, and a post that leaned.

The mast ticked behind her. She had her back to it, on Orla's terms.

"*One.*"

The metal came off the cuff and ran down her sides and closed on her calves. She bent both knees, the good and the newly passed, and left the line.

It was four paces. It was nothing. She had crossed a brick sump on the same push with a Flask at her back.

And she could hear the pen.

She had not thought of that. On the shelf there had been a roar in the terraces and her own blood. Here there was a quiet yard, and behind her left shoulder a steel nib that had been scratching evenly and was now, as the struts took her weight, running up its paper with a small, thin, eager sound. She knew what it was drawing. She knew to the hundredth.

"*Two.*"

She shut the Ember at the top.

She shut it, and listened to hear whether the pen had heard.

It was no more than that. It was a quarter of a breath. But it was the same fault that she had spent a month unlearning with a tin and a stick, when she used to watch the metal run home along her arm to make certain it had arrived. The struts let go late. One of them clung for an instant, and she felt it cling. The metal was still running up her ribs when the top of the bound was already behind her and the cross was coming up out of the dirt.

She had half a breath in which to choose.

The still door was there. It was ready; she could feel it under her as she felt a pan under ash. If she opened it now it would open on a doorway that was not yet empty, and she knew precisely what that was called, and she had done it four times in this yard and once in a cart shed.

*Is the Ember shut clean at the top?*

*No.*

"*Off!*" said Kiva. "Off, off—"

She let go of the pan. She let the whole of it go, and came down as nothing but a girl of seventeen travelling a good deal too fast, and she took the ground on her right foot and did not try to keep it. She folded at the hip as she had been shown and went over her own shoulder, and the Old Yard came round once and hit her in the back.

She lay a pace short of the cross and half a pace to the side of it, looking up at the lamp.

Nothing hurt. That was the first thing. The left knee had not been asked to do anything. There was dirt in her collar and her right elbow was skinned, and the bottom thread on her breastbone was as cold as it had been on the line.

"Hand," said Orla's voice, above her.

Kiva held up her left hand. The old fingers turned it over in the lamplight, and pressed the two tips, and let go.

"Pink. Good." The crooked face came into view against the sky. "Now your face, since you're so anxious I should have it. You're angry."

"I was listening to the pen."

"I know you were. I watched your head go." Orla straightened, with a grunt. "And then you did the only thing in sixteen goes that I've set you and you've done. You asked yourself the question at the top, you got the wrong answer, and you came down on it. I've seen that ordered a great many times. I've seen it obeyed about twice."

"It's another miss."

"It's the first thing you've done in this yard that I'd have paid for, and it turns out that somebody has." She looked over her shoulder. "Mr. Bexley. She's finished."

Noa came out from behind the mast with the tape across both palms. He was trying very hard to keep his face from saying anything, and not succeeding.

"The step. Two hundredths; it always is. Then the bound, two again. Then there's a tremble at the top, about as long as a blink, and a hundredth goes on it. And then nothing. It's flat. You shut everything." He looked up. "Five. Five hundredths for the night, and three of them were the go."

"Three hundredths," said Kellan. He had his own book out, and he wrote it under a ruled line. "One dram and ninety-seven left. I'd call that cheap."

"I'd wanted the cross," said Kiva. She had sat up, and she was looking at it. It was a pace away, scratched in the dirt, and she had never yet come down on it in that yard. "I could feel the door. It was there."

"I'm sure it was," said Perrin. "I'd my mouth open. I'd got as far as the *o.*" He lowered himself onto the spare crate with great care. "I'd like you to know that I've never been so pleased to be beaten to a word in my life. I'm putting it on the list of things I don't mind."

She laughed, which she had not meant to do, and found that the anger had a crack in it.

She got up by herself. She brushed off what dirt would come, and went and stood on the cross for a moment in her ordinary boots, with nothing open, to see what the yard looked like from there. It looked much the same. The post leaned over her left shoulder. Under her shirt the disk lay against her breastbone, cold as a coin, and she noticed that she had not thought of it once between the line and the ground.

"Friday," said Orla. "Not tomorrow. Tomorrow you'll come and sit with your back to that drum for an hour while Bexley runs it empty, until you can't tell it from a clock." She was already turning toward the shed. "You'll want to write it. I'd put it short."

*Sanctioned go, the first. Old Yard, travelling mast. Called off at the top. Why: the pen. I listened for it, like the metal. The door was ready and the doorway wasn't.*

She thought. Then, in the column that asked what it had cost:

*0.03. An elbow. Nothing else.*

And beneath that, because she had been asked for it on a pavement at a quarter to six in the morning by somebody who would know anyway:

*I'd an answer ready for Orla before I'd stood up. She had hers first.*

# Chapter 28 — Kiva: The Cheap One

On Thursday she sat with her back to the drum for an hour, and by the end of it she could not have told the pen from the lamp.

It was the dullest hour she had ever spent in the Old Yard. Noa ran the tape empty, and Kiva sat on the spare crate six paces off with the field book shut, and Orla read a newspaper. At first every scratch of the nib went up her spine. Then it was only a sound, like the hiss of the wick, and somewhere about the fortieth minute she found that she was thinking of how a stiff new sheet might be got to lie flat, and had been for some time.

"That'll do," Orla had said. "You've stopped sitting up for it."

On Friday night she stood on the scraped line with the thread cold and the step behind her, and said the words to four people and a post.

"*One.*"

The struts closed. She left the line.

There was the nib, running up its paper. It ran, and it was a clock.

"*Two.*"

She shut the Ember at the top, and this time she did not wait to learn whether anybody had heard. The struts let go together. The metal went home along her ribs like water into a drain, and it arrived, and she had nothing.

She had forgotten how much nothing it was.

On the shelf there had been a wall coming, and copper under her, and no room to notice. Here she noticed. For as long as it takes to say a short word she was a girl in the air with no doors, and all the speed of the bound in her body, and she felt the ash go. It did not trickle. It went out of her all at once, a quarter of a dram, like the breath out of somebody who has been thumped between the shoulders, and it went nowhere that she could follow.

*That's the price,* she thought. *That's what he drew.*

She set the pan.

She set it under herself and ahead, wide and waiting, on a patch of dirt with two scratches in it.

"*Three.*"

Her right foot came down on the cross.

There was no jar. The yard took her as a great hand takes a small weight, and the bound went in at her heel and down, through the packed earth and whatever five hundred years of feet had made of it. The left knee never knew.

"Four," she said, and did not put the lid on.

Orla's ferrule came against her left shoulder. Orla leaned on it. She was a heavy woman and she leaned as she would have leaned on a door, and it went through Kiva like a rumor and into the ground.

"Five."

"Hold it."

"Six. Seven." There was a small bright thing rising in her chest. She knew what that was as well. She gave it a number. "Eight."

"Give it back," said Orla.

Kiva gave the ground its weight, slowly, as someone sets down a full pan, and shut the still door behind it with nothing left in the doorway.

Then she stood on two scratches in the dirt, in her ordinary boots, and shook.

"Hand."

She held it out. The fingers were pink. The first two were cold, and no colder than they had been at breakfast.

"Thread?"

"Cold. It never warmed."

"No. I was watching your collar." Orla took her stick back under her own weight. She did not smile. She looked at the cross, and at the boots on it, and at the line four paces off. "I've sat on that crate since the seventh of last month waiting to see a foot come down there. I'd begun to think the mark was badly placed."

"Is that one?"

"That's one." The crooked face was perfectly grave. "I saw a shelf from the far side of a pit, over a rail, and I've wondered every day since whether the place was being kind to you. It seems not. Now show me it wasn't the yard." She turned. "Mr. Bexley."

Noa read it with his thumbs on the tape. Two hundredths for the bound. Twenty-five in less than a fifth of a second, straight up, with the same small blot where the nib had jarred. Three for the stance.

"Thirty," he said. His voice cracked on it. "A dram and sixty-seven left."

"Sixty-seven," said Kellan, writing. He had gone rather pale. "I knew what it would cost. I proposed it. I hadn't watched it go."

"I'd like to say that I mind," said Perrin, from the other crate.

"We know."

"I'd like to say it anyway. That's near half a Kennel floor, gone in the time it takes me to sneeze." He was grinning from ear to ear. "And she came down like a cat on a cushion, and I held a capstan with my ribs for less. Write it down that I'm furious."

Kiva's hand had gone to her collar. She had not sent it. The disk was there under the cloth, round and plain, and it was as cold as the inside of a well.

It had been warm once. It had been warm for the first one, for the length of a road stair, and she had not asked it why. It did not seem that the second was to be told apart from a wet Tuesday.

She found that she had expected that. She let go of the cord, and it was not until she was writing up the columns that she noticed she had not been disappointed, and wondered a little at how much quieter a thing was when nobody was waiting on its opinion.

The third team went to Stonehand on Saturday and lost by two.

She was let play. "He does."

"It opens before, now. That's all it ever wanted. Somebody told me to arrive before I was there."

Monday's go was the best that she had ever made.

She knew it in the air. The new week's thimble was in her, and four-tenths over, and the step had been clean, and the thread was cold when she said so. She had slept. She had not listened for the pen since Thursday.

"*One.*"

She left the line as she might have left a curb.

It was the third time in six days that she had gone up over that strip of dirt, and something in her had begun to know the road. On Wednesday she had fought the top of it. On Friday she had gone through it like a diver through cold water, with her teeth shut. Tonight she came to the top, and the Ember was closing, and she found that there was no need to wait in the dark for it to finish.

"*Two.*"

She could feel exactly where the ground would be. She could feel the pan wanting to be set. And the metal was nearly home, all but the last of it, a thread along the ribs, and it seemed the most natural thing in the world to begin the one as the other ended. It was how one hand gives a cup to the other. She did not set anything down.

There was no nothing.

There was no girl in the air without a door. The breath was not knocked out of her. One thing ran into the next as smoothly as a seam, and the cross came up, and her foot came down on it.

"*Three.*"

It was soft. It was softer than Friday. She stood on the cross and the whole yard stood under her, and when Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder it went through her so easily that she could have laughed.

"Four. Five. Six."

"Hold."

"Give it back."

She gave it back, and shut the door, and this time she was not shaking. She stood with her hands at her sides, breathing as if she had walked up a flight of stairs.

She had known before they touched. She had known in the air, and had put it by, because the cross was coming and the cross was what she was there for.

"A little," she said. "It's nearly gone."

Orla did not move.

For the space of a breath, nobody understood him.

Then Kellan made a sound.

"She did," said Orla.

You open the second before the first is shut. Do it badly and they fight, and you get a line on your chest and a post in your face, and that's a jam, and nobody mistakes it for anything. "It didn't throw."

Kiva did not answer. She was remembering a drum under her left palm, and a needle that had gone on climbing after she took her hand away, and what had come up a chute to look for it.

"You'll not stop," said Perrin.

"No. But I'd have the facts."

On Wednesday she chose the dear one.

That was the whole of the work, and it was harder than landing. She stood on the line with the thread cold and the step clean behind her, and she knew now that there were two roads over that strip of dirt. "Rowan, changing on three. Ember, to still."

"*One.*"

Up.

It was there at the top, waiting for her, like a known step on a dark stair. The metal was closing. The pan wanted setting. It would have been so little to let the one begin.

"*Two.*"

The ash went. A quarter of a dram, all at once, out of her like a breath, and she was nobody at all, in the air, over a yard, and she let herself be it.

"*Three.*"

The cross. No jar. The whole of the Old Yard under one heel.

"Four." Orla's ferrule, and Orla's weight. "Five. Six. Seven. Eight."

"Give it back."

She gave it back, and shut the door.

"Thread?"

"Cold. All the way."

"Hand."

She held it out, and she knew before Orla took it. She could see it in the lamplight. The last two fingers were her own. The first two were the color of candle wax, to the second joint.

Kiva could not feel them. She watched them lying in the old hard palm as if they had been two things that somebody had put there.

At twenty, they were white. At thirty, the first of them had a faint flush along the nail. It came up slowly from the root, like tea into a lump of sugar, and at thirty-eight she felt something that was a long way off and belonged to her.

"There," said Orla, at forty-one. She let out a breath that she had been holding rather longer than the count. "Forty-one. That's inside the sixty, and I don't care for how far inside it wasn't." She let go. "Two. I'll say that first, so that you have it. That was a crossing, and it was the dear one, and you took it with the cheap one lying there in plain sight. I saw you choose. Two."

"And we're done for the night."

"We're done for the week. E.K. wrote *not twice in one flood* and I've begun to guess at his reason." She took up her stick. "You'll show that hand to Aske tomorrow and tell him the count. If he says there's to be no go until he's seen it after a rest, then there's none, and I'll not be argued with by a girl who's just had two fingers come back from wherever they went."

"Thirty-one," said Noa, very quietly, from the mast. "Two, and twenty-six, and three."

"A dram and a quarter," said Kellan.

He had done the sum before the tape was read. He turned his notebook round on his knee, so that all of them could see the column.

*0.03. 0.30. 0.11. 0.31. Spent, 0.75. In hand, 1.25.*

"Two counted," he said. "Four to show. Four at thirty-one is a dram and twenty-four. So there's ash for four, and a hundredth." He looked up. "There isn't a miss left in it. Not a drop. Not a stack. I said on the second that it came out exact. I'd no idea how exact."

"I mind it more now that it's working," said Perrin. "I thought that I ought to say."

Kiva was flexing her left hand. The fingers closed. They did it late, and they told her nothing whatever about the palm they closed on.

Somewhere in a ledger that had been copied forward since before the Compact had a charter, there was a woman whose hand had gone to the wrist and had not come back by midsummer. She had been put on the roll as a catcher in the autumn. Kiva had thought on Tuesday that it was a hard thing to read. She thought tonight that it might have been written as a comfort, by a man who had not known how else to say that there was still work for her.

She wrote the go up at the kitchen table, with Mara reading over the top of her own book.

Then she took a half sheet and copied both nights out fair, Monday's and Wednesday's: where the line was, and what the tape said, and who had called what. She put the *why* column in. She put in the cup, and the warm thread, and a thing that a Stonehand captain had told her in a passage and that she had filed on the wrong side of the page.

She addressed it to the summer line, care of the Graywater hall. It was not a letter. It had no beginning and it was not signed, and Anwen would know the hand.

# Chapter 29 — Kiva: The Night Van

The watch room at the Graywater hall had one wall that was all map, and one that was all sums.

They had been told six, and they were there at a quarter to, the four of them in a row on a bench with the sheet between their feet. It was a plain room. There were hooks for coats, and a stove, and a long table scarred by forty years of tin lids. On the map wall every sensing mast in the eastern districts had a small glass lamp beside its name, and every lamp was dark, and under them hung a brass bell with a cord that nobody touched.

Kellan was reading the other wall.

It was a painted board, ruled in columns, and somebody chalked it fresh each week. *District table. The season to date.* The companies went down the left side, and across the top ran five words that Kiva had last heard read out from a slate on a cinder floor, with one more added at the end.

*Closed. Lives. Works. Tinned. Remarks.*

"Saltmere's first," said Kellan, in the voice he kept for a sum that would not come out. "Twenty-seven calls, twenty-seven closed. Nobody lost, two hurt. Not an hour dark. Two thousand eight hundred and forty drams." His finger moved down. "Graywater, thirty-one calls. All closed. Five hurt. *Six hours dark, one branch.* Three thousand nine hundred and ten. One remark."

"That's the Pit," said Perrin. "Six hours is us."

"And third is Halden's First Company, with four thousand four hundred. They've tinned more than anybody on the wall. They've one call *held over* and two remarks, and they're third." Kellan turned round. "Graywater's eleven hundred drams ahead of Saltmere, and it stands behind. How is the table reckoned?"

"From the left."

The man who had spoken was sitting at the end of the long table, mending a cord. He was square and grey-headed and he had been there when they came in. Kiva had known him at once by his back. She had last seen it on a meadow, four paces from a headless Flask, not turning round.

"You read it as you'd read a line of print," he said. He did not look up from the cord. "Was it closed? If one company's closed them all and another hasn't, that's the end of it; you never get as far as the tin. Then lives. Then what was still working in the morning. You only come to the fourth column when the first three are level." He drew a knot tight with his teeth. "You can't buy a column with the one to its right. There's houses that keep trying."

"Then a clean yield—"

"Is the fourth thing we're for. I've been catching twenty-six years, and I'm the fourth thing we're for." He laid the cord down, finished. "Pike. You'll be Rowan. Sorn says you kneel like somebody who was taught by a tinsmith."

"By a Torch with a bad knee."

"Same thing." He looked at the bundle between their feet. "Is that wet?"

"It was trodden. An hour, in a trough."

"Who told you to?"

"A laundress."

"Then she's forgotten more about canvas than the Circuit ever knew," said Pike, and almost smiled, and the door opened.

Rhea Sorn was not as tall as the poster.

That was Kiva's first thought, and it was a stupid one. She had seen this woman on a door for six years, and across a gallery, and behind a grey column with a white line going up from her fist. She had never been in a room with her. The streak at the temple was real. The eyes were paler than they had been painted. It took eight times before Pike stopped wincing.

He did not say what was wrong. He stood in the hall yard under a hooded lamp, with his hands behind him, and each time the sheet was down he walked round it once and said, "Up," and they took it up.

"I've two fingers that don't tell me anything. I start in the hand, and then I have to hook the elbow to—"

Kiva looked at him.

She took the cord at the elbow from the first turn on the sixth throw, and the seventh, and the eighth. It was slower by a count. It felt like wearing somebody else's boots. Down the berm, where the big Ferren culvert came out under the dyke, there was a light.

She had been given that in her second week, and Kiva did not know enough to tell whether it was early.

"Somebody you know," said Rhea Sorn. She had come up beside Kiva without a sound.

"She was our Hold."

Anwen Pryce heard the second bell from the mouth of a culvert, at fourteen minutes past two.

It came across the flats from the east, very small and clear, three strokes and a pause and three again. She had learned the codes in her first week. Three and three was the weigh-house.

"Weighbridge," said somebody along the line.

"Not ours," said Mace. He did not turn his head. "Night van's. We're lamps. Pryce, you're dipping."

She was. She brought it up. It was the fifth hour she had held a light over a brick mouth for two men with a rod, and her arms had stopped being arms a long while ago and become two things that she balanced a lamp on. *A wall takes it out of your wrists,* Stroud had told her, with her own hands in linen. *A lamp takes it out of your patience. He'll leave you on a lamp till he's seen which you run out of first.*

The night van. She had seen it at ten, up on the berm, a long dark shape with its lamps hooded, and a row of people beside it against the sky. She had not been able to tell one from another. She had known all the same.

They would be on the benches now, with the sheet between their feet. Finch would be talking. Kellan would have a watch out. Perrin would be saying that he minded something, to keep from saying what he was afraid of.

There was a half sheet in her coat, under the list.

It had come up by Mace's runner on Thursday, with no beginning and no name at the foot. She had read it standing in a culvert in the rain. *Monday. Why: it was easy and I was pleased, and I didn't look at my feet.* And lower, under a ruled line, in the same square pencil: *A thing L. Brask said in a passage. I put it on the left. It belonged on the right.*

She had read that six times. It was not an apology. It had none of the roundness of one. It was only the counts, as she had asked for them, with the part left in that made the writer look a fool.

She was still angry. She had examined it that night as she would have examined a ward for a thin place, and it was all there, as sound as the day she had turned her list over on a kitchen table. Greta Stroud could hold a spoon now, and not a cup.

And she wanted very badly to be on that van.

It seemed that a person could have both of those at once and hold a lamp. Nobody had told her so. She had thought that when she finally stood on a Graywater line she would be one whole thing, with nothing left over.

She had learned a good many things in a fortnight that nobody had told her. A culvert at night has a tide in it, of air, and a lamp held against that draft wants the weight on the back foot. The oldest hand on the line ate a raw onion at midnight and could stand four hours on it. Mace watched feet, and never hands.

On Thursday he had stood behind her through the whole of a relief with his board under his arm. At the end of it he had said the first thing that he had ever said to her before seven in the morning.

*Tuesday. You'll take the end of the wall at the low arch, behind nobody. If it dips, I'll hear it.*

That was the second week, and not the fourth. She had not told anybody. There was nobody on the line that it would have been manners to tell, and the one person whom she would once have told before her own mother was on a bench in a van tonight. That person had been informed, across a fold of waxed canvas, that Anwen was not done with her.

She did not regret it. She found that she could want to tell somebody a thing and still not be done.

The bell had stopped. Whatever it was had been heard by the people whose business it was.

"There's the low arch, miss," said the inspector, from inside the brick. "A foot to your left, if you'd be so good."

She moved the light a foot to the left, and held it there, and it did not flicker.

There were railings, and front steps, and iron gratings set into the pavements over the cellars. At the bottom stood the old weigh-house with the mast on its roof. Beyond it, across the water, the Lantern lay along the far bank in a long low wash of steady white light, as it had lain every night for four hundred years.

On this side, every window was dark.

It was not an empty dark. She could feel that as she got down. There were people behind those windows, four or five hundred of them, in back rooms with their doors shut and their hands in their laps, and not one had so much as a candle. He said it quite quietly. There were five or six people at the gates, in leather aprons, standing well back in the shadow with their arms folded, and one of them was a short, wide man with a grey mustache who had lifted his hand about an inch when the van stopped and put it down again.

"Is he on nights?" said Kiva.

"He's always on nights. He says the cars are better company." Perrin had gone a curious color. "He's never seen me on a floor that wasn't behind glass."

Just inside the gates a woman sat on an upturned crate with a child asleep against her shoulder, and a man in his shirt stood over the pair of them with a blanket he had not thought to unfold. They had come out of a ground-floor window by the back. Somebody from the sheds had given them a mug of tea, and none of the three had touched it.

A lamp warden came up out of the dark with a dead lantern in his hand. He had the street's switch already pulled, he said, and the wardens rung. Two were out. The first had come with the bell, at fourteen minutes past, and gone in at a ground-floor window halfway down on the west side, and the family had got out the back and pulled their red handle as they went. The second had come at twenty-four past. It was sitting on the Hythe Building's step.

"Two that I know of. There's an old carter at the top of the Hythe on a breathing bellows; he's not been down those stairs in a year. The lamp warden rubbed his jaw. "They're both on the cores, ma'am. The cell boxes are only the reserve."

"Until a core trips. Then they're on a box the size of a biscuit tin." Rhea's eyes went down the dark fronts. "That's two more things behind us. And the thing itself?"

"Below the Pellow. Two-thirds down, plumb in the middle." He pointed with the dead lantern at a tall front on the east side, with a crack over its door that somebody had stapled long ago with iron. "It's kept its time so far, ma'am. One, and ten minutes, and one."

"So far," said Rhea.

She stood at the top of the street with her hands in her pockets and looked down it for about as long as it would have taken to count twenty. Kiva looked where she looked. The cobbles ran away downhill, grey in the shine from the far bank. Two-thirds of the way down, in the very middle of the roadway, there was a place where the dark was the wrong color. "Dark!" said Kiva. "One."

"Tidy," said Nairn. He had stood three paces off through all of it with his arms folded. "That's tidier than mine was at twenty. Get it on a pole."

Down the street, the blue stroke opened a little, like an eye, and a third husk stepped out of it onto the cobbles.

It was two minutes early.

"There," said Kellan. "That's ours. Same mark. It's fourteen paces from the cloth and the cloth's down."

Nairn had his back to them. He had turned to look up the hill, to where a woman with a white streak in her hair was standing in the roadway with one hand raised, flat, at the height of her shoulder. He began to say something. It began with *wait.*

It was down, and it was flat. The ground was the same ground. The air had not moved. Three minutes ago she had set a lure two paces below that hem and a Handful had come across the road to it like a tram on a rail, and they had sealed at forty-one.

"Cloth's down," she said. "Pru. Lure."

The tea tray came up, humming.

The third husk turned its head toward it.

And behind the husk, the blue opened as a mouth opens.

A fourth came through at a scramble, sideways, with a shine on its shoulders. And after it, before it was clear, something began to rise out of the gap that was not the size of a dog. It came up a knot at a time, as a rope is paid out of a hatch: a grey bulk, and another above it, and another, on legs as thick as lampposts, until its blunt top stood level with the second-floor sills and there was frost on it from crown to knee.

It stopped on a tea tray.

"*GATE!*"

That was Rhea. Kiva had never heard her shout. Up the hill four people were running, and their boots were loud on the stones.

"Seven, *dark!*"

"Pru!" said Kiva. "Dark, dark—"

The ward went out. Pru was good at that; it was the thing she was best at in the world. It was out before the second word.

But the Flask had already lifted a leg.

Kiva saw it come. It came over the south hem of her own sheet, a grey column against the shine from the far bank, quite slowly, with all the time there was. It was coming down on the place where the light had been. Pru was standing in that place with her hands still raised and her mouth open, and she was not narrating anything.

Something hit her from the side.

It was Nairn. He took her at the waist with his shoulder, running, and she went over like a ninepin and slid on her back across the wet canvas. He went down on one knee with the force of it.

The leg came down.

There was a sound. Kiva had heard a brake shoe let go once, in a tram yard, when she was small. It was like that, and a good deal shorter.

Then light broke across the street behind her, silver and enormous, and the top knot lifted off the place where Pru had been and went to it. The Flask took one step north. It took another. Its blunt head came to rest against the gate, eight paces up the hill, and it leaned there, and was still.

It was standing over them.

Kiva was on her knees at the north hem with six grey legs round her like the posts of a pier. The lowest knot hung an arm's length above her head, slack and heavy, turning a little. She could feel the cold coming off it. Kellan lay flat on the cobbles with both hands spread. Pru was on her back on the sheet, staring straight up.

Nairn was beside the south hem. He had got up onto his elbows. One of his legs was under him, and the other went away beneath a column of grey as thick as a barrel, and did not come out the far side.

He was not making any noise. His face was the color of the canvas.

"Nobody," he said, quite clearly, "light anything."

Nobody did.

The third husk trotted past them up the hill, to the wall. The fourth had not followed. Kiva heard its feet on the pavement below her, quick and light, going toward a door with a crack over it.

She knelt under a Flask with her hands flat on her own new sheet. Three drams, and four signatures. It had lain so well.

*Lure,* she had said. She had read the wind and the fall of the ground. She had not left one thing out that a catcher is taught to look for. It had not once crossed her mind to ask who else in that street might be able to see a light.

*Not one of you has told me what's behind you.*

Up the hill, behind the silver, a pair of boots was coming down at a walk. It was a heavy, careful walk, the walk of somebody who has been forbidden to run and has dropped whatever he was carrying.

"That's a remark," said Rhea Sorn's voice, from somewhere beyond the legs. It was perfectly level. "It goes on the table, and it's mine. Seven, stay as you are. Don't speak. I'm coming round."

# Chapter 30 — Kiva: What's Behind You

Rhea Sorn came round the legs of the Flask as a person comes round a parked wagon in a narrow lane, sideways, with her coat held in.

She looked at Nairn first. She looked for about a second, and then she crouched and put two fingers on the cobbles beside his hip, well clear of the grey.

"Below the knee?"

"Below. I felt it go." His voice was thin and careful. "It's standing on the weight. There's a lead weight in that hem and my shin's between the two."

"Then you'll keep still, and you'll not be brave." She stood. "Seven. Faces to me. Is anybody else under anything?"

"No," said Kiva.

"Then you may stand, slowly, and come out by the west side. Not Finch. Finch, crawl. Keep off your hands; there's wax on that sheet."

They came out. Kiva had not known how much she was shaking until she tried to walk. The Flask did not stir. Its blunt top lay against the silver eight paces up the hill, and beside it, small as a dog at a gate, the third husk stood with its head pressed to the wall.

Down the street there was a sound of iron.

It was a railing, being worked at. Kiva turned. A dozen paces below, on the east pavement, a grey shape with a shine on its shoulders had got its head and one forelimb through the area bars of the house with the cracked lintel, and was reaching for the window beneath the front step. There was a board across that window on the inside. Somebody had put it there in a hurry.

"That one's going in," said Rhea.

She said it to nobody. Then she turned her head a little, so that the four of them and the man on the ground could hear, and she spoke as Hollis Vane had spoken on a cinder floor, with the same flat care.

"There's a ground-floor front behind that board with people in it. I've no cloth that I can get at and no time to lay one. I'm going to cut it where it stands, on a pavement, over a cellar grating. The ash will go down the grating. It'll foul that building's core, and likely more." She was already moving. "A window before a cellar. That's my trade, and it goes on the slate under my name."

She did not run. She went down the east curb with long, even strides, and stopped a pace behind the thing at the railings, where its shine could not come round to her.

Her left foot came down. Her right came down.

# Chapter 31 — Kiva: Come Back Clean

They panned on the wharf, at a trestle out of the van, because it was the only ground in the Weighbridge with no cell inside thirty paces.

It was four o'clock and beginning to be grey. The bags stood in a row along the stone as they had come off the poles: three small and six large, and one by itself at the end, on the iron of the old bridge, with nothing near it. Pike had the comb in his hand. Kiva had not known that he carried one. It lived in a wash-leather roll in his coat, nine brass reeds on a bar, and he held it over each bag as he cut the cord, without hurry, as a baker holds his hand over a loaf.

The third reed sang every time. It was the clear thin note that had filled a grading bench at the Wren House, and it did not vary from the first husk to the sixth rung.

"Ward," said Pike. "Full. That's this spring's skin, all through; I've not had a plain string since the thaw." He bent his ear to the brass. "And there's your hair."

Under the song, the seventh reed was moving. It touched its neighbor and came away, so lightly that she saw it before she heard it.

"That's what ours had at the Pit," said Kiva.

"It's what they've all had. The Wren House passed yours First with that in it, and so would I." He moved on. "Pan. Not the end one."

They panned. Kellan had the left pan and was slow with it, and knew, and allowed for it. Pru cut cords. Kiva's left hand had come back as far as the wrist, in the manner of a guest who has been persuaded to stay and will not sit down, and she used the right and did not ask the other for anything. The tins filled, six drams to a tin, and each one clicked.

Pike went to the bag on the bridge last.

He did not cut the cord. He loosened the neck by the width of two fingers and held the comb above it.

The third reed sang. And the seventh knocked.

It did not tap. It rapped on the reed beside it, hard and even, like a knuckle on a door, and it went on rapping until he took the comb away. In the quiet afterward Kiva could hear the water against the wharf.

"Well," said Pike. He looked at the brass in his hand as if it had spoken out of turn. "Twenty-six years, and I've held that reed over a rift's leavings every working day of them. I couldn't have told you what note it made." He drew the neck shut again and knotted it himself. "That's a hundred and thirteen drams, near enough, and it goes to the cold room as it stands. Red tag. *Not for the ground.* On a catcher's word and a comb."

He had come down the street at a trot, a young man in Works oilskins with a tuner's satchel bumping on his hip and a handcart of empty cell cans behind him. Kiva knew him. "Brannock," said Rhea Sorn, from her mooring post. She had not got up. "You're the night tuner."

"It'll grade. I can feel it through the canvas; it'll hold a tune past a hundred. That's First. By what rule's it kept back?"

"By mine. I caught it."

"And by mine," said Rhea. "It's on my slate."

"Then I'd like the reason, because I've to write one, and *the catcher didn't care for it* won't do for my foreman."

Pike turned his square head and looked at Kiva.

She had not expected that. She had said her piece on the iron, inside a breath, and had thought it finished. It was a different thing to say it at a trestle, in daylight, to a licensed tuner with a satchel.

"It keeps time," she said. "All of it together. The reed knocks." She made herself go on. "There's a caution on Halden's patent. Whoever copied the third volume wrote *the installer* and thought that would do." She sniffed. "I've often wished that I knew. I've turned away a landlord's tins on that line myself, twice. I'd turn away a Torch's."

"I'd expect you to," said Rhea.

"So. There's a bag over there with a red tag, and the lad says you'll not give me that. Good. And there are these." She laid her small dry hand on the nearest row of tins. "He says there's a hair of something in them that nobody can weigh. Is it clean?"

Pike took rather a long time to answer.

"It's the cleanest I've caught this year," he said. "It was bagged inside the count, every rung. Nothing's printed. It'll grade First. And it has a thing in it that wasn't in any ash I caught for twenty-five years and has been in all of it since the thaw. I can hear it. I can't tell you what it does. I'll put my name to that tin, and I'll not tell you there's nothing to put a name to."

"That's an honest man's no," said Mrs. Cobb, "or an honest man's yes. I can't make out which." She turned on Kiva again. "You said the other was wrong. In front of a tuner. Is this one wrong?"

"Not as that is."

"I didn't ask you that."

"No." Kiva looked at the tins. She had sealed three of those bags, and Pike three. She knew which were hers by the knots. "I don't know that it's nothing. Ours had it at the Pit, and a dram of ours went to an infirmary for somebody's burned hands, and nobody's told me that it did harm. That's all I have." She stopped. Her answer had run out, and she let it. "If it were my kitchen, I'd take the second rung. I caught it. It lay stillest; there was no wind yet. I couldn't feel the hair in it at all until he held the reed over. I'd not put the sixth by a child's cupboard. My hand had gone and somebody else finished the cord."

"Which is the second?"

Kiva showed her. The tag was on the first tin of the row, in her own pencil. *Weighbridge wharf, the 24th. Graywater, T7 att. Rung two of a Flask, skinned. Sealed at 44. Catcher: K. Rowan.*

Mrs. Cobb read it, and then she took a stub of pencil from behind her ear and copied it into her book, at the ribbon, every word. She wrote slowly and she pressed hard.

"*Six boxes refilled, ground and first, front. Two-C topped. Ash: as tagged. Comb: W, full; I, a trace. On the catcher's word, and the comb's, and Warden Cobb's.*" She blotted it with her sleeve. "There. Now it's somebody's. If that cupboard misbehaves on Tuesday, I'll know whose door to knock at, and so will whoever has this book after me." She looked up at Brannock. "You may fill them. Slowly. I've had you in my cellar before, young man, and you left a hurry in my number four that I could feel for a month."

Brannock went red to the collar.

"I'll take my time, Mrs. Cobb."

"You'll take mine. I'll be standing over you." She shut the ledger, and the little chain clinked. "Bring the tins, somebody. Not you, strapped as you are. The tall boy with the watch."

Kellan took up a crate of six without a word. He had been standing at the end of the trestle since she began to read, quite still, with the left pan forgotten in his hand, and Kiva had seen his face when the old voice came to *stamped First, graded Third.*

"My mother grades at the Halden refinery," he said. It seemed to be pulled out of him. "Twenty-two years. She kept a jar, once, for eleven months."

"Did she?" said Mrs. Cobb. "Then she'll have known a man like mine. Mind the step going in. It's been cracked since before either of us."

Rhea Sorn wrote her slate last, and she read it aloud, since there were people on that wharf whose street it was.

The lamps had come on by then. The lamp warden had thrown his switch at twenty to five, and Weighbridge Street went up the hill in two rows of small steady lights with the daylight coming in behind them. Only the Pellow was dark. It stood halfway along with its cracked lintel, on its boxes, and somewhere behind those bricks, two floors up, was a cupboard with cold bottles in it and a light that came on when the door was opened. Kiva could not see it from the wharf. She had not expected to mind so much whether it was on.

"*Weighbridge Street,*" said Rhea. "*Tear, fourteen minutes past two. Closed, twenty to four, by the Home Line. Lives: none lost. Hurt: Nairn, blade, the left leg, both bones. One student bruised. One shed man's shoulder. Works: the Pellow's core, fouled by the shift officer's cut; a window before a cellar. Six boxes quenched, and filled again by five. No lamp failed that a person was depending on.*"

She turned the slate over.

"*Tinned: four hundred and eleven and eight-tenths, clean. One Handful printed at the gate; the officer's choice. One Handful lost down a grating; the officer's choice. A hundred and thirteen put by, stepped, not for the ground.*" A pause. "*Remarks: one. An attached team lit a lure inside a string's reading, unasked. The shift officer had put the question in the van and had not required an answer. The remark is the shift's.*"

She put the slate in her coat.

"Seven," she said. "That's a whole Flask, stood under a foreman. It isn't without remark. So you're attached still, and you'll be attached to me. I'd not have you take it for a punishment. It's a description." The pale eyes went along the four of them. "The second husk was fifty-five. I've seen it done in thirty by people I wouldn't have behind me. Pike will tell you what that's worth."

"It's worth being asked back," said Pike.

It was the longest speech of praise that Kiva had ever heard, and it had seven words in it.

The others had gone up the hill by the time Rhea spoke to her again. Perrin had been taken off by his father, who had him by the elbow and was telling him something about brake shoes. Pru was asleep sitting up against a mooring post, with her mouth open. Kiva was coiling cord, with her right hand, because it wanted doing.

"You called the lure," said Rhea. She had come to stand at the edge of the wharf, looking at the water. "And you called the bag. Same mouth, same night, about fifty minutes apart."

"Yes."

"I'd have you keep both. Most people keep one. They keep the bag, and tell it for twenty years." She did not turn round. "I'll give you one thing, and then I'll have given you all that I've got that's any use to somebody with your trouble. I've one door. I don't know yours. But Pike asked you *on what,* and you said that you couldn't weigh it. Go on saying that. Say it every time, to everybody, the Chair included."

"Why?"

"Because the day you stop, you'll be believed too easily. I have been, for twenty years. I say a thing on a floor and four wards do it." Her voice did not change. "That's how I came to tell Greta Stroud to slide a ward under a string. It was a fair notion. Nobody asked me what I could weigh."

Kiva said nothing. The cord went round her fist, and round.

"Hollis Vane has written to his watch for leave to show you a book," said Rhea. "He's slow about it; he's that sort. I'm not on that book's list. I knew the last name in it."

The cord stopped.

"Ilse Corran?"

"She pulled me out of a Ferren culvert by the collar when I was nineteen, with the water at the roof. She had one arm round me. Her other hand was shut on something at her own throat, and I've wondered for twenty-two years what she thought it was going to do." Rhea turned at last. She looked, quite openly, at the cord where it crossed Kiva's collarbone, and then at Kiva's face. "It didn't do anything. She did. I wrote two words on a card about you in the spring, and I've carried it since, and tonight I'm going to burn it. They were the wrong two words."

She went up the hill toward the van. She did not say what the words had been, and Kiva, who had her mouth open, found that it was not a question she had any right to an answer to.

She slept until four on Sunday afternoon, and woke with her left hand lying on the blanket like something that had been left there for her.

It came back by supper. It came back to where it had been, slow and late, and Mara watched her butter bread with it and said nothing. There was a note from the Home Line under the brass weight. *Thursday the 28th, four o'clock, the hall. The watch agrees that the bearer may read her own line. Your sponsor as far as the door. H.V.*

She wrote the night up at the table.

It took four pages. She wrote the lure under *What went wrong,* in six words, and beneath it, *Four of us looking at a cord and nobody at the gap.* She wrote *Nairn, leg, both bones, till the autumn* under *Who was hurt,* and sat for some time over what it had cost. A Handful printed. A Handful down a grating. A core. Six boxes. A remark on a painted wall in a watch room, in the last column, where a company could not buy it back with all the tin on the table.

*Second go:* Asked who else reads it. Put eyes on the gap, because she asked. Fifty-five.

*Why:* I read the ground. I'd never been taught to read the street. She asked us in the van what was behind us, and I had a good answer to a different question.

Kellan had left a half sheet under the door at noon, in his small exact hand.

*T7, attached, Weighbridge. By our own kill and catch: 22.6 and 22.2. Yield 44.8, to grade. If it grades as Pike's comb says: civic, half, 22.4, paid on the ground. Casualty reserve, 5.6. Schools, 8.4. Team, 8.4. That makes our line 116.8. Finch is paid off the Circuit's reserve card and not our book; I asked. The 8.4 is credited when it's graded and can't be entered until four have signed. One of the four is on the Sag.*

And below, after a space:

*I went to the refinery yard on Thursday, to watch. I've not said what I saw. I'll say it on Wednesday, at your table, with the team book open.*

She copied the counts for Anwen. Forty-one, and why it had felt so fine. Fifty-five. Forty-four, forty-six, fifty-two, and whose hand had finished the last. She put in three words that she had said over a flat sheet at about twenty-eight minutes to three, and she did not explain them. *Cloth's down. Lure.* Anwen would know what kind of sentence that was. She had been on the other side of two.

The small room off the reading room had a fire in it on Monday, though it was nearly summer.

There was a round table, and four chairs, and a window that looked onto a wall. The Reader had the grey box before her with its tape still tied. She had the black book open at the ribbon, and a stick of wax, and a candle that was the only flame Kiva had ever seen in the Compact Library.

"First things first," she said. "Miss Rowan."

Mara took the packet from inside her coat.

It was a single sheet folded in three and sealed with the shop's own brown wax, with the Works' reconciling stamp pressed across the fold, as she sealed a drum that was going to a hearing. She had done it on Sunday night at the kitchen table, and Kiva had watched her do it. Mara had not read the page over first. She had said that she knew what was on it.

"I'll say before these witnesses what I said at home," said Mara. "I've not read what's in that box, and I'm not going to. I shall sit beside her, as the settlement asks. I'll not look at the page. My own is shut in that packet, and I mean to be able to swear that I've never seen what it's being held against."

"That's very proper," said the Reader. "I wish that I met it oftener."

She dripped her own wax across Mara's, black over brown, and pressed a seal into it. Then she took the steel pen and wrote across the join, so that half of each figure lay on either side.

"*Eight minutes to two. Monday the 25th. Received shut. L. Arkwright.*" She showed it to everybody in the room in turn. "It goes into the press with the settlement, and it comes out to your hand or a magistrate's."

There were two other people in the room.

One sat by the window with his hat on his knee. He was about forty, and pleasant, and there was a narrow gold cord at his cuff. He had risen when they came in, and bowed to Mara, and had not spoken, since he was not let.

The other sat by the fire.

She was a woman of sixty or so, in grey, with her hands folded in her lap, and her hair cut short as a boy's. The Reader had brought her a particular chair. She had thanked the Reader for it with her eyes. She had looked at Kiva once, when Kiva came through the door on a knee that was still strapped, and since then she had looked at the box.

"You know the rule," said the Reader. "They may not speak to you. You may not copy. You may read for as long as you please, and stop when you please, and nobody in this room may ask you what you made of it." She untied the tape. "The notebook is the thin one with the green cover. I've put a slip at the place that the card describes. I'm obliged to tell you that it's there. I'm not obliged to tell you to begin with it."

Kiva sat down, and Mara sat beside her and turned her chair a little toward the window.

There were letters in the box, and a few sheets of drawings. They were all of hands: a hand flat, a hand cupped, two hands held a little apart as if round a bowl. She lifted them out one at a time. Under them lay a notebook with a cracked green cover, no bigger than the field book.

She opened it at the beginning. She did not go to the slip.

It was a large, quick hand that slanted hard to the right, the hand of somebody who had a great deal to set down and grudged the time. The pages were about Ward. They were diagrams, and lists, and short impatient notes. *Not the wrist. The wrist is where it ends. Begin at the feet.* She read six pages without understanding more than one line in three, and it steadied her. This was a woman who had worked. She had been wrong about a wall in the wet autumn and had crossed it out.

Then she turned to the slip.

*Fever again. Fourth time with this dream, so I will write it. Somebody should.*

Kiva's hands were flat on the table on either side of the book. She kept them there.

*They come from every road at once.*

"One," said Kiva, without a sound.

She had not meant to. It came as it came on a floor, when something in her chest stood up. *I do not know them. I know what they are, because I know what I am, and they are that. Each one carries a different light.*

*From every road,* she had told Mara, with a wet cloth on her chest. *The lights were all different.* She had been thirteen and lying in the dirt of a yard. She had been seventeen in a narrow bed. She had been standing on an iron shelf with a warm disk in her fist.

"Two. Three."

The Ember had stirred. She felt it at the cuff, a small quickening, like a dog lifting its head. She gave it a number and it lay down.

*When they get close enough, their light touches mine and theirs, and none of us can tell any longer where one stops.*

*Mine.* That was different. Kiva read the word twice. This woman had been among them. She had been one of the lights, walking in. Kiva had only ever been high up, and apart, looking down at roads.

"Four. Five."

*Above us the sky is not torn. I want to be very clear. I have seen rifts all my life and this is not a rift. The sky is not there.*

*And looking up,* she had told Mara, at ten o'clock at night, with a cloth on her chest. *No sky.*

The hand door came open a crack, behind her breastbone, cool and close. She shut it. "Six." The still door was already under her, wide, as it was under a falling weight, and she shut that. "Seven. Eight." There was a thread of warmth along the bottom line. It was the first time that she had ever had to call the meter over a page.

*As if someone had taken the roof off a house in the night and the house woke and found itself under the stars. And what is above comes down and in. Not crushing. Full.*

*Full.*

She had used that word. She had used it to Mara the first time, at a kitchen table, with her arm in a sling, because there had been no other. It was not a word that anybody would choose. It was the only one that fitted, and a woman had reached for it in a house in the hills two hundred and thirty years ago, with a fever on her, and found the same.

"Nine," said Kiva's lips. "Ten. Eleven."

*Like standing under a wave that does not break. I have spent my whole life holding back pressure, and this is not a pressure I could hold back. It is too large. It is paying attention.*

She had never said that last. She had not had it to say. She read it, and something that had been shapeless in her for four years took a shape, and she did not like the shape, and she knew that it was right.

*Every lamp in Meridian answers at once. Some go out. Some go white. One turns a color I have no word for.*

Below her, every lamp had answered. She had never seen what they did. She had only known that they did it.

There were no notes. She looked for them. This woman had not heard the lights, each on its own pitch, none in tune; she had not lost count at four; she had not heard them come together into one. And there was nothing small beneath it, at the end, that ticked.

*I do not know if we gathered to hold it shut, or to break it open, or to take it, or only to stand there together when it came. I wake before I find out. Every time.*

"Thirty," said Kiva. "Thirty-one."

So did she. Every time.

She had come down three flights of iron stairs for this, and signed a book, and let two houses be told her name. Somewhere under all the care she had taken, she had thought that the dead woman would know. A Glory master, who had held a flood-wall for nine days. There would be a line that said what it was for.

*I do not know if it is a memory, or a warning, or only what a fever makes out of a Glory master's fears. I have told no one in the house. They would make it into doctrine by spring.*

The woman in grey sat six feet away with her hands folded. Kiva did not look up.

*The sky unroofed. That is the only way I can say it.*

That was the end of the entry. The next page was a diagram of a wrist.

She went back. She looked at the head of the leaf, and the foot, and the page before, for a date. There was none. *Fever again.* It might have been the night that a clerk called R. had seen a sister at his wicket before it was light. It might have been a year after. The margin said that a Master had sent somebody down to ask. The notebook did not say that she had sent anybody anywhere. The two papers lay two hundred and thirty-four years deep, a mile apart, and each pointed at the other, and neither one touched it.

*The sky unroofed.*

It had a name. She had carried it for four years without one, like a stone in a pocket, and now a stranger had told her what it was called and nothing else about it.

She read the entry through once more, from *Fever again* to the last word, because she would not be let take a line of it out of the room. She counted the whole way. Then she shut the green cover, and laid the drawings of hands on top of it in the order in which she had found them, and put her own hands in her lap.

"I've finished," she said.

Her voice was steady. She had counted to two hundred and six.

Nobody moved for a moment. The fire ticked.

Then the man by the window got to his feet. He looked at the Reader, and not at Kiva.

"The endowment thanks the Keeper for her fire," he said pleasantly. "It notes, for the book, that this reader is the subject of a sanctioned tape which opens to chartered parties on the first. It asks the Library for nothing." He bowed again to Mara, and put on his hat at the door, and was gone.

The woman in grey had not stirred.

"Reader," she said.

It was a low voice, and rather hoarse, like a door that is not often opened.

"Sister," said the Reader. She had gone very still. "You've not spoken in my hearing in twenty years."

"No. Is it permitted to put a thing to the Keeper, in this room, for the book?"

"It is. Not to the reader."

"I know the rule. I have kept it longer than she has been alive." The grey eyes rested on the box. "Then the Order asks the Keeper to write that the reader counted. From the third line to the last, without stopping. We count so in the house, when a thing is asking to be let in."

Kiva's breath stopped.

"I shall write what I saw," said the Reader slowly. "I saw her lips move. I'll not write what they said."

"That will do. We know what they said." The woman rose. She was taller than she had looked. She laid her right hand flat on the lid of the grey box for a moment, as someone touches the shoulder of a person asleep, and took it away. "The Order has waited two hundred and thirty years to learn whether its Master was only ill. It asks leave to write to this reader's sponsor."

"You'll write to me," said Mara.

She had turned her chair round. Her face was as it had been in a hallway, with one hand going out to a red handle.

"She's seventeen. I'm her guardian, and that's my hand in the book beside hers. You may put your questions on paper and send them to Coper Street, and I'll answer the ones that I think you've a right to. You'll not come at her through a yard."

The woman in grey looked at Mara for some time.

"That is fair," she said. "We are not used to being told so. It is fair." She inclined her head, to Mara, and then, after the smallest pause, to Kiva, which the rule did not forbid. "We did not burn it. I should like that known. There were those in the house who wished to, in every generation, and it was taken from us before we had decided. I have sat beside that box each autumn for twenty years and have not known whether I was guarding it or waiting for it."

She went out. The door shut without a sound.

"Well," said the Reader, after a while. She took off the upper spectacles. "I have administered that settlement for thirty-one years, and in all that time those two have never been in a room together and both got what they came for." She began to tie the tape. "I'm not permitted to ask what you made of it, child. I'll say only that you may come back. As often as you like, on fourteen days' notice. It doesn't wear out."

"Thank you."

"Don't. I've just sent your name up a hill and across a river." She knotted the tape, and laid her hand on the box where the other hand had been. "You asked me nicely. I've been wondering all afternoon whether I did you a kindness."

Kiva told Mara on the tram that it was not all the same, and that it was enough the same.

"Don't tell me which parts," said Mara. She was looking out at the Brickfields road. "Not until I must know. One of us ought to be able to say that she hasn't been told."

"There's a name for it. She gave it a name."

"Then keep it. You've carried the thing four years without one; you can carry the name a little while without me." Mara's left hand came across and rested on Kiva's wrist, above the two fingers. "Did she know what it was for?"

"No."

"I'd thought that she wouldn't. I don't know why. A person who knew would have told somebody." A pause. "I'm sorry. You wanted her to."

"I did. I didn't know until she didn't."

She wrote four lines that night, with the book on the table where it would be read. *Read Annick, Personal, in the room. It is not all the same. It is enough the same. She woke before she found out. So do I.* Mara read them, and put the brass weight on the page, and said nothing whatever, and went to see to the stove.

"I supposed so. You've the look." The stick turned. "And?"

"You were right not to tell me. I'd have gone in looking for your parts."

"Which were mine?"

"I'm not going to say. You'd carry them."

Orla laughed. It was a short, hard bark, and it rang off the shed, and the mast, which had been ticking to itself in the dark with nobody to draw, seemed to check for an instant.

"There," she said. "Now there are two of us who've read it and won't tell the other what it says. I can't think of a worse arrangement, or one that I'd trust further." She settled the stick between her boots. "She counted, did you say? The grey one. She saw you."

"From the third line."

"Then they know what you are. Not what it means. They'll have that muddled; they always have." The crooked face was grave again. "There'll be a letter. And a man in a black coat reads a tape on Sunday. She looked at the cross, four paces off, with two sets of scratches in it now. "Sit down, Rowan. You've four to show me, and ash for four, and I'd as soon you did nothing clever before any of that arrives."

# Chapter 32 — Kiva: Held of the Line

Healer Aske turned her hand over on the towel on Tuesday and did not reach for his pin.

"I'm told that it went away," he said. "For forty-one counts in a yard on the twentieth, two fingers. For the better part of an hour on a wharf on Sunday morning, the whole of it, to the wrist. Your sponsor wrote me the first. A Graywater catcher wrote me the second, in pencil, on the back of a tin tag. I've never had a letter from a catcher before."

"Is it burned?"

"No. I've looked for that twice now and I'd tell you." The cold thumbs went along the two fingers and stopped at the knuckle. "It's a nerve that's being asked a question it can't answer quickly. Every time you do whatever it is that you do on that mast, something goes through here at a rate it was never built to carry. It doesn't scorch. It starves, and then it sulks, and each time it sulks a little longer." He let go. "I can ease it. I can't tell you how many more times it will come back to where it was. I'd be guessing, and I don't."

"Somebody's did not come back. Two hundred years ago. A whole hand."

"So your sponsor tells me. She sent me the line." He wrote on her card. "No go until Monday. After that, one in a week, and I see the hand the next morning, every time, before you're let have another. That's not a ban. It's a price, and I'm setting it where you can read it."

One in a week. She did the sum on the infirmary stair. Four to show, at one a week, was the best part of a month, and that was if nothing went wrong, and there was not a hundredth in the glass for anything to go wrong with.

The grading had been posted in the Measure Hall basement by the time she got there.

*T7 (att.), Weighbridge. Two Handfuls, plain. Yield 44.8. Grade First. Resonance: W, full. I, trace.* And on the standings, six lines from the top, where there had been a nine: *T7. Total yield, 116.8. Sixth of thirty-one. (att.)*

The brackets were still there. She found that she agreed with Kellan about them.

It was the board beside the standings that had the crowd.

Three papers had been pinned to it in a row since the morning, each with a seal, and people were reading them over one another's shoulders. Kiva read them from the back, between two heads.

*Claim. The Halden Works, proprietor, under the foundation patent of 2391. All ash exhibiting the Step, however produced. One bag, 113 drams, tagged at the Weighbridge wharf on the 24th and held at the Wren House cold room. The Works observes that this ash was kept from civic use on the word of a provisional candidate, unclassified, and a field comb, and asks on what paper.*

*Claim. The wardens of Weighbridge Street, by ground-first. The civic half of all ash tinned on the call of the 24th, the said bag not excepted. The wardens do not ask to use it. They ask that it not be carried off the street's account by anybody who was not there.*

*Notice. Wren House, reference shelf. Resonance I, full, from a field source: without precedent on the cards. Held for reference pending the Chair's direction. Halloran.*

And below the three, in a level hand that she knew:

*All parties, before the Placement Chair, Tuesday the 3rd, at nine. The catcher's book will be wanted. Y. Crest.*

"That's you," said somebody at her elbow.

It was the thin boy in Wren House green. He had no catch roll this time. He had a yellow card, folded small, and he was turning it over in his fingers.

"*A provisional candidate, unclassified.* I read it twice to be certain." He did not sound unkind. "We've our held tear tomorrow. The river flats, at first light, if it obliges. Sixth company, at last."

"I'm glad."

"I expect you are. I've stopped minding whether you are or not; it was wearing me out." He nodded at the card. "We were a blade short. Our Kill went home to Saltmere when the day was lost. So the Circuit's seated a reserve, off the list." He almost smiled. "Dray."

"*Selka* Dray?"

"She asked for it. She came and found me on Sunday. She said that she'd an objection before the Chair with a hearing on the second, and that she'd sooner stand a floor in the meantime than write another letter." He put the card away. "She told me to my face that she still thinks you oughtn't to be seated. Then she asked what my father's shop sold. He's opening at noon tomorrow whatever happens. He says he'll not shut it twice."

He went away up the stair. Kiva stood before three sealed papers that each had a bag in it, and her own name in the first, and a date.

Noa found her there.

He had the weigh-house tape in a roll under his arm, since the Chair had sent him for it, and he looked as if he had not slept since Saturday either.

"Nothing," he said, before she could ask. "I've been over the Weighbridge mast from the bell to the closing. There's a Flask on it, and five husks, and one blade getting longer. There isn't a notch. Whatever jumped sixteen pens at the Pit, it didn't happen on that wharf." He lowered his voice. "And I went back through your two on the travelling mast. Friday's, and the twentieth. Eleven seconds on from each cliff, and twelve, and all the way to a minute. There's nothing there either."

"So it isn't every crossing."

"Or it's only the first. Or it wants more than one pen to show, and a ring of lamps. I can think of five things that it might be and I can't choose between any two." He rubbed his eyes. "I'm not going to say which. I've a man in a black coat reading the same cliff on Sunday. I'd rather hand him a blank than a guess with my name on it."

"That's a hard thing to bring to a hearing."

"It's what there is." He shifted the roll. "I've stopped calling the page *probably nothing,* by the way. I've not thought what to put instead. I've left the top bare."

Kellan said it on Wednesday at the Rowan kitchen table, with the team book open and three chairs filled where there had been four.

He had brought his list. He laid it down beside the book and did not look at it.

"I went to the refinery yard last Thursday," he said. "I stood behind a rail for two hours. They've a contained string, a six-knot, in a lattice of their own. They've a chartered foreman every Tuesday and Thursday, and a ward line that's paid. Their Kill's a second-year called Pettit." He was speaking to the cover of the book. "He isn't better than I am. I timed him. He's four-tenths at the top, the same as me, and his feet are worse."

"But," said Perrin.

"But he's cut a rung a week since the thaw, and I've cut one in my life." Kellan looked up. "On Sunday morning I stood on a wharf with the corner of a sheet in my hand and watched one blade make seven cuts, each longer than the last, until she had to sit down. The second blade was in a van with his leg in a board. The third was me, and I wasn't allowed, and I ought not to have been. I've been over it every hour since. It isn't that I mind holding a corner. I found out that I don't."

"Fifty-five," said Kiva.

"Fifty-five. I'd hold that corner again tomorrow." His jaw was tight. "I want to be let. I want to be the second blade that a foreman can turn to. I can't get that off one night in a fortnight and a trough, and I can't get it by being excellent at a thing that I do alone at the top of a bound."

Nobody at the table spoke.

"So I've said yes. To the Halden license team, for the Regional. Not for the summer, and not for their yard after; I've told them that in writing." He drew a breath. "I stay on this book. I've asked the clerk; a house entry for the Regional isn't a transfer. If Sorn will take a blade on her van who's wearing somebody else's colors in the autumn, I stand the Flask we owe. If she'll not, I understand her."

"They've claimed the bag," said Kiva. She had meant to wait, and found that she had not been able to. "Your team's house. It's on the board. *However produced.*"

"I've read it. Twice. It has my mother's seal at the foot; every paper of theirs has." He did not look away. "I think that it's lawful. I think that it's wrong. I held one of the corners of that bag and I heard a shed foreman say that he'd not put a thimble of it in his own kitchen. I've told Mr. Adlam that I'll say what I saw on that wharf to anybody who asks me, whoever's paying my stipend."

"What did he say?"

"That they'd expect nothing else." Kellan's mouth twisted. "I'm aware that it's exactly what a pleasant man would say. I've written it down, with the date, and I've given a copy to my mother. She's the jar."

Perrin had been turning a spoon end over end. He put it down.

"I'd like it said that I mind," he said. "And I'd like it said that I'd have done the same, and that I'm going to the Hall's second team in the autumn myself, so I've a nerve." He blew out his cheeks. "A month ago I said there were four of us and I couldn't find two going the same way. Now there's one of us going two ways at once. I didn't know that was allowed. Is it in rule fourteen?"

"It's in nineteen," said Kellan gravely. "I looked."

"Of course you did."

Kiva had not said anything yet.

She had three things ready. She could feel each of them standing in her mouth, and they were all of them true, and one of them was even generous. She gave them numbers. They were for her, every one. Not one of them was a thing that Kellan had asked to be told.

"What do you want from us?" she said.

He was silent for a moment. He had plainly expected one of the three.

"Nothing," he said. "That's what's the matter with it. I've a list here of what I'd say if you asked me to stay, and you've not asked." He turned the list face down. "I'd like to have my name on that book when the eight and four-tenths is entered. And I'd like somebody to tell Anwen before a runner does."

"It can't be entered without her. Four signatures."

"I know. I've thought of that. So has the clerk; he says that a team book may go to a member on service, by a member's hand, and be signed where she stands." He looked at Kiva. "It ought not to be mine."

"No."

"I'll take it," said Perrin. "I'm the only one of us that she's not cross with."

"She's cross with you about the bean poles."

"That's an old crossness. It's nearly affection." He pulled the book toward him. "I'll go out on Saturday with the runner. I'll tell her about Halden, and about Finch walking a Flask down a hill, and I'll bring back her name and whatever she's put on her list about the lot of us."

There was a small sound from the end of the table.

Pru Finch had been let in at the beginning and had sat so quietly ever since that Kiva had forgotten her. She had a yellow card in both hands.

"I've to give this back to the Circuit on Friday," she said. "Or ask to keep it. Nobody's told me which." Her round face was very pink. "I stayed. I found that out. I'd like to do it again, with rather more warning, and I'd like it to be with you, if there's a you. I don't know who's meant to decide."

The three of them looked at one another across an open book.

"Four of us," said Kiva slowly. "And one's on the Sag. We'll put it on the page for Anwen. Her list said you, before any of ours did." She turned to Pru. "Keep the card. That much I can say by myself. It's a corner and a lamp, and you've done both."

"Not a wall."

"Not yet," said Kellan. He picked up his list, and folded it, and put it in his pocket without having read a line. "If you're wanting a blade for the Regional, and you'll be wanting one, I'd ask Talley. He's a second step and no team, and he's the only Ember in this school who's ever told me that I was wrong about something and been right. It isn't mine to fill. I'm only saying who I'd ask."

Mara came in then, from the shop, with a letter in her hand.

It was a grey envelope, and the paper was thick. There was no crest on it. It had been addressed in a small, plain, old-fashioned script to *M. Rowan, guardian,* and it had come up from the hill house by a boy.

"Six questions," she said. "Civil ones. I shall answer two." She put it under the brass weight, with the Works' notice and the Home Line's note, where everything in that kitchen went that wanted thinking about. "Go on with your sum. I only came for the kettle."

Orla Dane had sat on a great many benches in her life, and she thought that this was the hardest.

It stood in the passage of the Home Line hall, against a whitewashed wall, opposite a door. It was a plain door. There was no plate on it. She had walked past it perhaps four hundred times in twenty years as a field officer, with mud to the knee and a slate at her belt, and had not once been asked through.

*Your sponsor as far as the door.* Hollis had been as good as his word. He had come out for the girl himself at four, with his hat off, and had held the door for her, and had looked at Orla over her head with an expression that said a number of things and apologized for none of them. Then it had shut.

That was a quarter of an hour ago.

She did not mind. She had examined that carefully, as she would have examined a candidate's stance, pushing at it from several sides. She had expected to mind a good deal. She had saved a favor for twenty-five years to get this near that room, and had spent it, and here she sat with her stick between her boots like a parent outside a school.

It was only that the wrong person had been asking, all that time.

She had wanted to read the book because Ilse was in it. That was the whole of her reason, and it had never been enough to open a door, and she saw now that it ought not to have been. The girl had a better one. She was in it herself.

A boy went by with a bucket. A bell rang somewhere for the change of the levee watch.

There had been a visitor to the Old Yard, a fortnight ago, on a night when there was no go.

Mara Rowan had come down the lane at half past eight in her work apron, with her sleeves still rolled. She had not sat. She had stood beside the spare crate and said two sentences, in the voice she would have used to a meter.

*You kept a thing from my table. Don't do it twice.*

*I shan't,* Orla had said.

*Good. Then that's all. I've a re-sit in the ninth month and no room in me for a second grievance.* And she had looked at the cross, and at the brass head on its three legs, and at the crooked post, for about as long as it takes to read a drum. *She came down on that mark on Friday. She told me at the table. She was a good deal more pleased about the one that she called off.*

Then she had gone home. Orla had sat on for an hour afterward and found that she had been let off nothing and forgiven nothing, and that she could work with it.

There was a paper in her coat now, too. It had come from the Chair that morning, in answer to Monday's book. *Noted: one stack, a third of a second, landed, called a miss by the candidate's reader. I shall want the tape, the reader, and the sponsor on the 3rd. Y.C.* It was not a reprimand. Ysra Crest did not write reprimands. She wrote appointments.

Orla had a fair notion of what that Tuesday was going to hold. A bag that three parties wanted. A tape with a cliff on it. A girl who had read a dead woman's fever in front of two houses and had been seen to count. A boy with a strip of paper, who would be asked by a Placement Chair whether a Torch could tell a crossing from a stack with a stick, and who would have to say no.

She would have to say it too. She found that she did not mind that either.

*Two,* she thought. *Four to show. And the hand goes away a little longer each time.*

She had read E.K.'s line until she had it by heart. *Nor to anyone I had not watched miss.* She had watched this one miss sixteen times. She had watched her call *off* in the air with a door ready under her. It was not the landings that Orla would put her name to, when the day came. It was that.

The door across the passage stayed shut.

Orla Dane settled her stick, and her bad leg, and waited, as she had learned to wait on a tear watch, without leaning on the time.

The room was smaller than Kiva had imagined, and there was nothing in it but a table and a window.

The window looked out over the Ferren levee. The table was oak, and old, and on it lay a flat box of black wood with a brass hasp. Hollis Vane lifted the lid and turned it back, so that she could see the inside.

A sheet of paper had been pasted there, and renewed, and pasted over. It was a list. It was not long. *Officer of the watch, Sag Line. Officer of the watch, Ferren. Officer of the watch, river flats.* There were seven such lines, each an office and never a name. Under the seven, a little apart, in the same faded print:

*And the bearer.*

"You've been on that since you were thirteen," said Vane. "Nobody told you. The appointment was sealed, and the one person whose business it was to tell you had been dead three years when the seal was cut." He did not excuse it. "Your sponsor's known of this book for thirty years and couldn't be shown it. You could have walked in any morning and asked. I'd like to say that we're a careful service. We're a service that copies what's in front of it."

He lifted the book out and laid it on the oak.

It was bound in canvas, like a field book, and the corners were soft. He opened it at the first leaf and stood back.

It was in print, the old uneven print of the fourth *Founders' History,* and it was short.

*One plate, dark, of nine rings, on a cord. Held of the Line. Released to a bearer.*

*The bearer is named by the bearer before, and by nobody else. The bearer writes why. The watch witnesses, and does not choose.*

*It is not a rank. It is not a reward. We do not know what it is for.*

*Write down what it does.*

And below, in a smaller letter:

*How it came to the Line is in the first book. The first book is not here. This is copied from the second, which did not say where the first had gone.*

Kiva read it twice. Then she put up her right hand, without hurry, and drew the cord out of her collar, and laid the disk on the table beside the page.

It was the first time that she had ever taken it out in front of a stranger on purpose. It lay on the oak, dark and smooth, the width of her palm. There was still a thread of grey Kennel ash in three of the grooves.

Vane looked at it. He did not touch it, and he did not ask to.

"That's it," he said quietly. "I've stood four watches in this room and I'd never seen it. Corran kept it in her shirt." He cleared his throat. "Turn over. It's ruled like one of ours."

It was. There were five columns, and she knew the shape of them before she read the headings, as she would have known the shape of her own kitchen in the dark.

*Bearer. Received, and from whom. Why. What it does. Released, and to whom.*

There were a great many names.

She did not try to keep them. She let her eye go down the leaves as Vane turned them. The hands changed every thirty years or so; the ink went from brown to black. Some of the reasons were a line long and some a paragraph. One said only, *She asked.* One, in a hand like a row of bent pins, she could not read at all.

"Nor can I," said Vane. "It's the same script as the other. I've to ask seven people's leave before I may trace a line out of this book. I've asked."

It was the fourth column that she could not stop looking at.

*Nothing.*

*Nothing.*

*Cold.*

*Nothing, in twenty-two years.*

It went down the page like a drip. Leaf after leaf, bearer after bearer, each of them had been told on the first page to write down what it did, and each had carried it for half a lifetime and come back with the same word. Once, halfway through, a narrow upright hand had written, *Warm? In a fever. It may have been me.* And beside it, in the margin, in different ink: *Not entered as fact. Watch.*

"Five hundred years," said Kiva.

"Of people who were asked a question and were honest enough to write that they'd no answer." Vane's voice was dry. "I thought when I first read it that it was the dullest book in the hall. I've come round."

He turned one more leaf, and took his hand away.

*Corran, I. Received 2643, from M. Gill, at his death.*

Under *Why,* in a square old-fashioned hand that must have been Gill's: *She went into a culvert after a child and came out counting who was still in it.*

Under *What it does,* there were three entries, years apart, in a big loose scrawl that Kiva had seen on birthday cards. *Nothing.* And then: *Nothing. I talk to it. It's a poor listener.* And last, in ink that was hardly faded:

*Nothing. I've stopped asking it. I.C.*

Kiva's eyes stung. She had thought that she had found that out for herself, on a stair at the Kennel, with a tin under her arm.

*Released: to K. Rowan, by sealed appointment, 2669. To be delivered on her Kindling day and not before. Witnessed by the watch, which did not choose.*

And under *Why.*

*Ione Rowan's girl. I sat an hour with her on a stair after the Tallow fire. She is six. She asked me one thing the whole time, which was whether anybody had written down what her mother did. I said that the Works would. She said, "Will they put why?"*

*I have looked for a better reason than that for a month and have not found one. I do not know that it wants one. I.C.*

Kiva read it standing, with her hands flat on an oak table.

She did not remember saying it. She remembered the stair. She remembered a tall woman's coat, and that it had smelled of rain, and that nobody had made her talk. She had been six, and her mother had pulled a quench by hand, and she had wanted to know whether it was going to be in a book.

It was not a prophecy. There was nothing in it about doors. A woman who had carried a cold thing for twenty-six years had needed to give it to somebody, and had chosen a child who asked the only question that Ilse Corran had ever thought worth asking. She had written down that she had no better reason, and had signed it.

There was one line more, beneath, in a clerk's hand.

*Guardian (M. Rowan, aunt) was asked, and said no. Appointment sealed regardless; it is the bearer's right. The Line owes that household an answer and has not got one. Watch.*

"She said no," said Kiva.

"She did. And we went round her, by our own rule, and wrote down that we had." Vane's face was set. "That's eleven years old and nobody's been to Coper Street. I'll come myself. Not to defend it. To say that it's there."

"She'll give you tea and not forgive you."

"I'd expect both."

The last line on the leaf was her own.

*Rowan, K. Received 2676, by courier, sealed, from I. Corran (d. 2673).*

There was nothing after it. The *why* was above. The fourth column was empty, a clean white space the width of three fingers, under five hundred years of one word.

"I'm obliged to tell you two things before you pick up that pen," said Vane. "The Reader at the Library has a way of doing it; I've borrowed her manner." He turned back to the first leaf and put his finger at the foot. There was a line there that she had not read, added in a later ink. "*The watch may recall the plate from any bearer, on a vote of the seven, for cause written.* It's been done once. A man sold it, in 2590, for forty drams. They had it back in a week. That's what *held of the Line* means. It's yours to carry. It was never yours to keep from us, or ours to keep from you."

"And the second?"

"Anything written in that fourth column that isn't *nothing,* I read aloud to the watch at its next sitting. That's Sunday." He met her eye. "There's a great deal coming at you on Sunday. I'd not add to it without saying so. You may leave that space white and walk out, and I'll enter that the bearer read her line. Nobody could fault it."

Kiva looked at the white space.

She thought of a page at a kitchen table, with one word crossed out. She thought of a request book that was open because that was what it was for. She thought of a clerk called R., who had been told to leave something out of a column, and had put it at the side in a hand that his Warden could not read, and of how long it had taken anybody to come down three flights of stairs and ask.

She thought of an old woman on a wharf, pressing hard with a stub of pencil. *Now it's somebody's.*

"Is there a rule about how much I may put?"

"Only what it did."

She took the pen.

Her left hand held the page flat, the last two fingers doing the work. She wrote slowly, in the square pencil hand turned to ink, and she did not let herself hurry the dull parts.

*Warm, once. Wednesday the 29th of last month, at the Pit, a little before half past ten in the forenoon. First felt after I had shut the still door, at the end of a crossing, Ember to still. I do not know when it began. It lasted from the south shelf to the top of the road stair. Cold by the keeper's gate.*

*Cold since. Cold through two crossings more, on a sanctioned mast, the 15th and the 20th.*

*I did not ask it anything. It did not do anything. K.R.*

She blotted it. It looked very long, under all that *nothing.* It looked like somebody making a great deal of a small matter.

"You've used his word," said Vane. He had read it upside down, as a watch officer reads everything. "*Crossing.*"

"It was there first. I only found it."

"Mm." He took the pen from her, and under her initials he wrote, small: *Entered in the bearer's hand, in the presence of the watch officer, Sag Line, who did not prompt. H.V.* "There. Now it's a fact, and seven people hear it on Sunday, and I can't tell you what they'll do with it. I can tell you that I shall say I gave a field direction to that hand and would again."

"May I copy my own line?"

"The bearer may copy her own, and the line that gave it to her, and show them where she likes. Not another's."

She copied them into the field book. She copied Ilse's reason last, and when she came to the six-year-old's question she put it in the right-hand column, by itself, because there was nowhere else in any book of hers that it could go.

Then she picked the disk up off the table. It was cold. She put the cord over her head, and tucked it in, and it lay against her breastbone where it had lain for four years.

It weighed what it had always weighed. She had rather expected that it would feel different, now that she knew whose it was.

Orla got up from the bench when the door opened. It took her a moment, and she did not hide that it did.

Kiva did not say anything. She held out the field book, open, turned the right way round.

Orla read it standing in the whitewashed passage, with her stick hooked over her arm. She read Kiva's own line first, and the long entry under it, and her eyebrows went up a little at one word. Then she came to the square beneath.

*She said, "Will they put why?"*

The crooked face did something that Kiva had never seen it do. It did not last. Orla shut her mouth hard, and looked for a while at the wall.

"That's Ilse," she said. Her voice was rough. "That's her to the life. She never could find a grand reason for anything. She'd sooner have a true small one and be laughed at." She gave the book back. "I'd wondered for four years what was in that seal. I'd made up a dozen things. Not one of them was as plain as that, or as hard to argue with."

"It says that they don't know what it's for. On the first page. In print."

"I'd guessed that much."

"And that it's held of the Line. They can call it back, on a vote."

"Can they." Orla took her stick in her hand. "Then there's one more office with an opinion. We've the Chair on Tuesday, and a house with a patent, and an Order with a letter, and now a watch." She began to walk, slowly, toward the daylight at the end of the passage. "You wrote in it."

"He told me what it would cost first."

"I don't doubt it. He's become very scrupulous since somebody read him a ledger." She glanced sideways. "You've your answer ready as to why you did it, I suppose."

Kiva opened her mouth.

She had. It was about open books, and a clerk called R., and it was rather good. She shut her mouth, and walked four paces, and found something behind it that was smaller.

"I didn't want to be the next *nothing,*" she said. "Not when it wasn't true."

"Hm," said Orla. "That'll do."

They came out onto the levee. It was a grey, mild evening, with the water low in the Ferren and the first lamps showing in the streets beyond. Somewhere over there was a pump house that had never dropped a stroke, and a street with a cracked lintel in it, and a yard with a brass head on three legs that had not been asked to tick since the twentieth.

"What was she like?" said Kiva.

It was a plain question. She had never put it to anybody. She had a coat that smelled of rain, and a laugh in a kitchen, and a line in a book.

Orla did not answer at once. She stood with both hands on her stick, looking down the levee to the place where it bent.

"Loud," she said. "She was the loudest officer on the Line, and she never raised her voice at a tear in her life. She laughed in other people's kitchens. She fell asleep on watch once, standing up, and kept her count." A pause. "She took me down my first culvert. I've told people for twenty-five years that I took Hollis down his. I never said who took me."

"You never told me that."

"You never asked. You've only lately begun asking things you've not already answered." It was said without any edge at all. "Come along. There's a tram at the half hour, and your aunt will want that page on her table tonight, and I'd give a good deal to see her face at the line about the tea."

They went down the levee steps together, the one on a stick and the other on a strapped knee, at about the same pace.

Kiva had the book under her arm. In it were four old lamps and a clerk's margin; a dead woman's name for a thing that she had not understood either; a tag copied into a tenement's ledger; and a child's question, in ink, with a witness who had not chosen. On Sunday a watch would hear what the disk had done, and a man in a black coat would put a glass on a cliff. On Tuesday there would be a room.

She had two, and four to show, and ash for four.

It was not hers. It had been put into her hands by a tired woman who could find no better reason, and written down, and she found, going down the steps, that she would sooner carry that than any of the things that she had made up.
