# Chapter 41 — Kiva: Not the Copper

There was a boy on the Coper Street step when the tram let them down, and he had a slate-blue card in his fist.

He was one of Graywater's runners. He had plainly been told to wait for a girl with a strapped knee, and he looked at Kiva's knee before he looked at her face.

"From the Torch," he said. "I'm to see you read it."

It was three words long. *Yard. Now. Van.*

The bells were still going. There were three of them, and they did not keep together. Mast five rang its two and two from over the Sag. The weigh-house rang three and three. The third came up off the river on a code that Kiva did not know, and each of them was a little out with the other two, so that the whole sky over the east bank sounded like a clockmaker's shop at noon.

"That's an attached team turned out," said Mara. She had the trunk book under her arm still. "Go on. I'll not be at home in any case. Rusk will want a reader at mast five within the hour, and I'm the only one he has who'll go up a mast at dusk without being paid double."

"You said it wasn't mine."

"It isn't. A bell is. You're on a company's card." Mara looked at her hand, and then did not say what she had been going to. "Have you your book?"

"In my coat."

"Then put what you see on the left and what you think on the right, and don't mix them. I shall be doing the same thing a mile off, and I'd like one of us to be tidy."

Kiva went with the boy.

The Graywater watch room had three lamps lit on its map wall, and nobody in it was pretending to be calm.

They were calm all the same. That was the difference between a company and a school. Rhea Sorn stood in front of the wall with her coat on and her hands in its pockets, and read the three small lights as a carter reads a street.

"The weir. The weigh-house. Mast five." She did not turn round. "Three bells inside a minute, on three masts that are a mile apart. I've been twenty-three years in this room and I've seen two together, once, in a flood. Imrie, you've the second van and the weigh-house; it's a street, so it's yours. The Home Line has mast five. I'll take the weir." The pale eyes came round at last and went along the wall bench. "Seven."

They were all there. Anwen had come from the laundry with flour on her cuff. Perrin had come at a run and was pretending that he had not. Kellan stood at the end in his own coat, and Jude Talley beside him, and Pru had her yellow card out already, held in both hands.

"You're attached. You'll ride with me, and you'll do what you're entered for and nothing else. Rowan."

"Recovery," said Kiva. "Dark hands. And I'm barred any go until the first; Aske has it on a card." She held up the left. "Two don't report. Finch has the cord from the first turn. That's arranged."

"I know that it is. I was in the Pit." Rhea took her hands out of her pockets. "Say it anyway. Say it every time. Pike."

"Two sheets on the van," said the square catcher. "And I've a word to say to that first-year about her third turn, on the way."

It was dusk when they came down the bank into the weir meadow.

Kiva had sat on that bank in the spring with sixty other candidates and watched Graywater take a Flask to pieces. It looked smaller without one. There was the long grass, grey in the last of the light, and the weir beyond it making its one continual noise, and the mast on its three legs with the bell under it still swinging.

There was nothing else.

Four wards in blue brought a gate up at the head of the meadow all the same. It stood there, silver and patient, lighting the seed-heads. Rhea walked the ground behind it with her hands in her pockets. She walked the whole of it, and she looked behind her twice.

"What's there?" she said.

"Nothing," said Anwen. "The grass isn't lying. There's no cold. I've stood at the tape of a tear all day, and the back of your neck knows before the rest of you." She frowned. "Mine doesn't know anything."

"Cade."

"A weir. A mast. A bell that somebody ought to stop." Perrin considered. "And a cow, on the far bank, who's as surprised as I am."

"Renn."

"There's no light in it," said Kellan slowly. "A tear's blue from the side. I've walked round that mast and it's only an evening."

Rhea did not ask Kiva. She looked at her, which was a different thing.

Kiva had been standing on something. She had known it through her boot for a minute or more, without attending to it, as one knows that a tram is coming before one hears it. She knelt down in the grass.

It was a square of iron, a yard across, sunk level with the turf. She had written a line about that plate in the spring, with a question mark after it. *Stopped on an iron plate in the grass, 2–3 counts, on its way to the wall.*

She laid her right palm on it, and beside that the heel of her left.

It came up slow and even and many. It was not strong. It was the hum of the tram sheds heard from the far end of a street. It was the thing that she had felt in a doorpost on the Brickfields road at four o'clock that afternoon, with her aunt beside her counting needles.

"It's keeping time," she said. "This is. There's water under it."

"That's the sluice feeder," said Pike. He had come up behind her with a roll of cord. "It runs under the meadow to the Ferren main."

"Then the main's keeping time." She heard how that sounded, and took her hands off the iron. "I can't weigh it. It's what comes through a plate. I'd not have anybody stand a wall down on my saying so."

"Nobody's standing one down. You were asked what's there." Rhea had her slate out. She wrote on it, briefly, and it was not in the fourth column. "You've an aunt reading drums for the Municipal Works. Tell her. Tonight, in those words, with the *can't weigh it* left in."

A man was coming down the bank with a lantern. He came at a walk, as he came everywhere, and he had his hat on.

"Nothing at five," said Hollis Vane. "I've had a closing crew stand round an empty field for an hour like mourners at the wrong grave. Imrie's sent from the weigh-house. Nothing there but a carter who thinks that we owe him a night's sleep." He stopped beside the gate and looked at four wards burning ash for the benefit of a meadow. "Stand it down, Sorn, if you're content. I've the reason, such as it is. It came from her aunt, up a ladder."

"Go on."

"A mast rings when its drum climbs past a mark. The mark's set on the grid. Mrs. Rowan says that the three drums didn't climb. She says the mark came down to meet them, for about as long as it takes to breathe in, and went back." He said it flatly, like a casualty list. "All three inside the same minute. She says it'll do so again at the top of some other breath, tonight or Thursday, and she can't tell me which. She says she can stop the bells by having the marks moved."

"And then they'll not ring when there is one," said Rhea.

"She said that as well. She said it first."

Nobody spoke. The four wards let their wall go out, one after another, and the meadow went dark as a room does when a lamp is carried from it.

"Then a bell's no use to me until further word," said Rhea. "I'll have eyes on every mast in my district by morning. People, sitting, with a drum in front of them and a boy to run." She put the slate away. "That's forty hands I haven't got."

"You've a Circuit full of students who can read a tape," said Kellan. He said it to the grass. "There's one of them reads it better than anybody in Meridian. I'd begin with him."

"Bexley."

"He'd do it for nothing. He'd very likely pay you."

Rhea almost smiled. It did not reach her mouth.

"Write me his name on the back of that card, Renn, and the names of nine more who won't fall asleep." She turned to go up the bank. "Finch. Pike has a word to say to you about a cord."

He said it in the van, by the light of the shuttered lamp, the whole way back along the Brickfields road. Pru crossed a waxed cord on her knee four-and-thirty times with the square grey catcher watching her hands, and got it right on thirty.

"Four," said Pike. "That's four that'd be a lost bag. Do you know which four?"

"The ones where I was thinking about it."

"That's the first sensible thing anybody's said tonight." He took the cord from her and laid it across her palm again. "Then you'll do it until you're not."

Mara went to drum twenty-two on Tuesday evening, since it was twenty-two's turn, and she took Kiva to carry the books.

"The records rate doesn't run to a clerk," she said. "You'll stand on the pavement. You'll hold the lamp to the page when I ask. You're not to come over a sill, and if you feel anything through a wall you're to say so in the same words that you used to Sorn."

"I told you those."

"You did. I've had them in my head all day, like a tune."

A drum house was not much to look at. Kiva had walked past a hundred of them without seeing one. It was a brick hut no bigger than a coal store, set back from the road behind an iron rail, with a slate roof and a green door and a number on the lintel. Inside was one drum, taller than a man, that fed three or four streets. There was a needle under glass on its face.

Twenty-two stood on the Kiln loop. The copper left the Brickfields road at the gasworks corner, after the twenty-first drum, and went east in a long bend round the old brick kilns and the rows of small houses that had been built for the kiln hands. It came back to the road a mile farther on. Mara had explained that on the tram, with her finger on the plan.

"There," she said, from inside the door. "Come to the sill and look. That's a drum minding its own business."

The needle stood. It trembled a little, as a needle does, on a time of its own.

"It's due," said Kiva.

"It's due tonight, if the thing keeps the time that it's kept. Nineteen on the Wednesday, twenty on the Friday, twenty-one on Sunday." Mara had her left palm on the drum's iron side. "I can't feel that it's so much as thinking of it."

They waited. It got dark. A lamplighter went along the row on his lift-board, a hand's height off the paving, touching each lamp awake. Mara read the needle every quarter of an hour and wrote the figure, and Kiva held the lamp, and nothing whatever happened.

At twenty minutes to nine somebody came round the bend of the loop on a Works bicycle, very fast, ringing the bell.

"*Mrs. Rowan!*" It was a boy in an apprentice's apron. He had no breath. "Mr. Rusk says—you're to come—it's gone in. Not here. It's twenty-five."

"Twenty-five," said Mara.

"On Stopgate. The foot of the hill. The warden there had her hand on the door for her eight o'clock and saw it nodding." He leaned on the handlebars. "It's gone past three. It's skipped the whole loop. Mr. Rusk says it's got faster. He says, if it can do three in a night—"

"I can do that sum for myself, thank you." Mara had already shut the green door and turned the key. "Give me the plan, Kiva. No. Hold it. Walk."

It was twelve minutes to the foot of Stopgate, going quickly.

Kiva knew the street. Everybody at the Crooked Line knew it. It was the long hill that climbed from the Brickfields road to the bottom wall of the school, straight as a rule, with a tram line up the middle and tall narrow houses on both sides. She had gone up it every morning for six months. She had never once wondered why it was called that.

There was a small crowd at the rail of the twenty-fifth drum house. A woman with a warden's handbell stood at the open door with her arms folded.

Mara went in. Kiva stopped at the rail.

She could see the needle from there. It went up, and it came down. It did it slowly, and with a horrible ease, like something that had been doing it for years.

"When?" said Mara's voice.

"I can't say when. I looked at eight. It was nodding at eight."

"And at seven?"

"I don't look at seven."

Kiva was not listening any longer. She had turned round.

It was behind her. She had felt it as she came along the pavement, in the soles of her feet, and had taken it for the tram. There was no tram. The line up the middle of Stopgate lay empty under the lamps, wet and shining, and the hum was not in the rails.

It was in the crown of the road. She walked out to it.

There was a grating there, between the rails, a yard long, of the old thick iron. She had stepped over it every morning of the summer. She knelt beside it, on the wet setts, and put her right palm flat on the bars.

Slow, and even, and many.

And there was a sound under it. It was a long way down. It was water, and it was not running as water runs in a drain. It drew in, with a soft hollow noise, like breath taken through the teeth. Then it let go.

In. And out.

"Mara," she said. She did not shout it. "It's here. It isn't at the wall. It's in the road."

Her aunt came out to the rail.

"Say it properly."

"There's a grating. There's water under it, and the water's keeping time. I can hear it. Four in, and four out." She took her hand away. "I can't weigh it. I can't. But you could look. It's only a hole with water in it."

Mara stood quite still for a moment. Then she took the lamp out of Kiva's hand, and came through the rail, and knelt down on the setts in her work skirt, and lowered the lamp on its chain between the bars as far as the chain would go.

It lit a round brick throat, green with slime, and at the bottom of it a black shine.

The shine rose. It came up the brick by the width of two fingers, and left a wet mark, and went down again.

Mara watched it do that six times. Her lips moved.

"That's the Ferren main," she said. She said it very quietly, to the hole. "It's the storm drain for the whole of this side. It runs from the top of this hill to the sump under the pump house, and three pumps lift what's in it over the levee." She did not look up. "Three pumps, on a bank of four-and-twenty. I've stood in that house for nine days and listened to them all take their stroke together. I thought that it was rather restful."

"It isn't in the copper," said Kiva.

"No. I said so yesterday, and I thought I was saying that I didn't know where it was." Mara drew the lamp up, hand over hand. "Give me the plan. Hold the corner down; it's wet."

She spread it on the setts, under the lamp, and Kiva held it with the heel of her left hand.

"There's the copper. Twenty-one at the gasworks. Then it goes off round the kilns, twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four, and comes back in here, at the foot of the hill. Twenty-five." Her finger went back to the gasworks corner. It did not follow the red line. It went straight up the road, along a thin blue one that Kiva had taken for the edge of the paper. "And there's the main. It doesn't go round the kilns. Why should it? Water won't climb a bend to oblige a brickmaker. It comes straight down Stopgate and straight along the road."

"So twenty-five's the next one. On the drain."

"On the drain, twenty-five is the next after twenty-one. It hasn't skipped anything. It's kept its two days to the hour." Mara sat back on her heels. "And the three on the loop aren't due at all. They never were."

The warden had come to the rail to see what the Works was doing on its knees in the road. The apprentice had come with her.

"Then it hasn't got faster," said the boy.

"It hasn't got faster. It's got nearer." Mara's finger moved up the blue line. There were five numbers on it between the foot of the hill and the top. "Six-and-twenty. Seven. Eight. Nine. And the core house. I said eighteen days yesterday, to my niece, in a doorway." She took her hand off the paper. "It's ten. If it keeps its time."

Nobody said anything. In the hole between them the water drew in, and let go.

"Is that a reason?" said Kiva.

"It's one drum." Mara began to fold the plan. Her voice was level, and Kiva knew that levelness; it was the voice in which she read a figure that she did not like. "The first three were on the copper and the drain alike, so they tell me nothing. I've a single drum out of its turn, and a drain underneath it, and a girl who says that she can't weigh what she felt. That's a result. A man could drive a cart through what it isn't."

"What would make it a reason?"

"A second one. Called beforehand, in writing, by somebody who stood to look a fool." She got up, and brushed the wet from her knees, and it did no good. "I shall have to go and be that person in front of Rusk. I'd hoped for a quiet autumn."

They had the Chair's hearing room again on Wednesday morning, and this time there were more than forty.

The Municipal Works had the left of the long table. Examiner Rusk sat in the middle of it, broad and tidy, with linen guards over his cuffs and the trunk plan pinned out before him under four brass weights. He had three of his own people. He had also Mr. Adlam, who sat a little apart from them in his black coat, as a guest sits who has been asked for one thing only.

The wardens of Stopgate had the right. There were eleven of them. The School had sent its bursar, a thin worried man in a gown, and the keeper of its core house, who was a short woman named Ansty with a ring of keys at her belt and did not look worried at all.

Rhea Sorn and Hollis Vane sat together at the far end, which Kiva had not seen before.

She was not at the table. She was on the wall bench, behind Mara, with the books on her knee. She was there to hand up a volume when it was asked for, and she had been told so.

"I'll say what I am not," said Ysra Crest. "I am not the Works, and I'm not the Line. I've a Circuit, and a school of that Circuit stands at the head of a hill with seven hundred students in it. Mr. Rusk has been good enough to ask for this room. I've lent it. It's his."

Rusk stood up.

He did it heavily, with his scarred thumbs on the edge of the table.

"Then I'll be short, since I've been wrong once this week already. On Monday I had a thing walking my trunk at a drum in two days, and nine drums to spare. Last night it was at the foot of Stopgate with five." He looked along the wardens. "There are two accounts of how it came there. I'll give you mine, and then I'll have my reader give you hers, since she'll not thank me for softening it."

"Yours first," said Ysra.

"It goes from ash to ash. That's the only way that anybody has ever seen a cell take a beat, in three hundred years of the trade. Mr. Adlam will bear me out; it's his house's patent." Adlam inclined his head. "A bank's put into step by salting it with ash that already keeps one. So I say that something is carrying from drum to drum, along my trunk, by a road I can't see, and that last night it found a short way. If I'm right, it wants ash to walk on. I can take the ash away."

He put a thumb on the plan.

"Six-and-twenty, and seven-and-twenty. I quench the pair of them tonight and I leave them dry for a week. Two dead drums, halfway up the hill. A firebreak. Whatever it is can come up to the edge of that and look across."

"And the streets on those two?" said a warden.

"On their boxes. Six hundred households, for a week, in the tenth month. A box is three days. So I'll want cans, and the casualty reserve has undertaken to find them." Rusk glanced up the table. "It'll be asking the Circuit's teams to lend at par, I'm told. I'd not have chosen to come to students with my cap out."

"We'll speak of that," said Ysra. "Mrs. Rowan."

Mara did not stand. She laid her left hand flat on the table, as she laid it on a drum.

"It didn't find a short way. It went to the next drum that the Ferren main runs under. I've the plan. I've the hour for each of the four. And the water in that main is rising and falling by two fingers, at the foot of Stopgate, on a count of four and four. That's the pumps' stroke. I measured it against a mark on the brick, with a lamp, last night, and again at six this morning with Mr. Rusk beside me."

"I saw it," said Rusk. "I don't dispute that it moves."

"Then here's what I think, and I'll say plainly that I only think it. Three pumps that used to take their stroke each in its own time now take it together. So the whole main draws and lets go like a lung. Every drum house on that line has had the same slow push come up through its floor for eleven days." Her voice did not alter. "I don't say that water can step a cell. Nobody's ever seen it. I say that the drums have fallen in exactly in the order that the water reaches them, and not in the order of the copper, and that I've one drum to prove it by."

"One," said Rusk. He said it without unkindness.

"One. If you quench two drums and I'm wrong, you've stopped it, and I'll come and say so in this room. If you quench two and I'm right, it won't know that they're dry. It'll go underneath."

"Mr. Adlam," said Ysra. "You've a patent."

"I've a patent in which no drop of water is mentioned from the first leaf to the last, ma'am." He had his hat on his knee, and he turned it. "I should be astonished if a storm drain could do what the Works spent forty years learning to do with a pinch of salt. I've been astonished five times since the spring. I've stopped regarding it as evidence." He looked along the table at Mara, quite pleasantly. "If the lady will put a date to it, I shall be very glad to be astonished a sixth."

"I'll put a date to it. I'd like it sealed, by this office, before anybody's quenched anything. I've seen what becomes of a thing that's remembered afterward."

She had written it already. She had done it at the kitchen table at one in the morning, on a half sheet, and Kiva had watched her blot it. She passed it up.

Ysra read it aloud before she folded it.

"*If it is the drain: drum twenty-eight, on Monday the third of next month, with two dry drums between. If it is the copper or the ash: nothing, and I am wrong. M. Rowan, Grade Two.*" The Chair reached for her wax. "That's five days. You'll both be very tired of one another."

"We were that by Thursday last," said Rusk. "I'll quench tonight. I'd be a fool not to. It's cheap, and it mends, and if she's wrong I've saved a school." He sat down. "And if she's right I've put six hundred families on boxes for nothing, and I shall have that to carry. I'd sooner carry it than not try."

It was a fair thing to say. Kiva could feel how fair it was, as she had once felt the fairness of an offer for a bag.

She had a question. She had had it since Monday, in a doorway, and it was not hers to ask in that room. She leaned forward and said it into the back of her aunt's collar.

Mara's head turned a quarter of an inch.

"My clerk wants to know what the shutdown is," she said. "It's a good question, and I ought to have asked it myself. You've a firebreak, Mr. Rusk. That's a fence. If the thing comes through the fence and reaches the head of that hill, what do you pull?"

"The core scrams," said the keeper, Ansty. It was the first thing that she had said. "On its own weight. I've a red lever with a pin in it, and I've pulled it twice in a drill and never in earnest. The rods fall. There's nothing in my house that needs ash to make it safe."

"So the School goes dark and nobody's hurt."

"The School goes onto boxes. I'd have the first-years sent home, all the same. I don't like them near anything that I haven't a lever for."

"Nor do I," said Rusk. "And it isn't the core that keeps me awake. It's the pumps. I've a bank of four-and-twenty that was built so that any one could fail alone. Now none of them can do anything alone. If one's fouled, I think they all cook. Then there's no stroke at all, in a wet autumn, and the Hythe has water in its cellars in half an hour." He rubbed his eyes with a thumb. "I can't quench that bank and fill it fresh. I'd want four hours. I've been given the rainfall for the month."

"There's a third thing," said Vane.

He had not moved. He had his hat on the table in front of him.

"A bank fell into step on the seventeenth, and in the same minute four husks stood a hold over a tear in the Pit. I don't say that the one made the other. I've a catcher who's taught me to be particular." He did not look at the wall bench. "I say that I had three bells on Monday and no tear under any of them. I say that every tear this spring has opened by water. The culverts. The flats. The weir. The Sag. I've a main drain breathing under a hill with a school at the top, and I don't know what a tear does when it finds the ground already keeping time."

"Nor do I," said Rhea Sorn. "I'd as soon not learn it with seven hundred children watching."

"Then I'll say what's mine," said Ysra, "and it's small." She drew a sheet toward her. "The School sends its first-years home from Friday. The rest stay; it's ten days, and I'll not empty a hill on one drum. Officer Vane will draw me a plan to clear that school and that street in the dark, by litter where it's wanted, and it will be walked on Saturday night by everybody who'd have to do it. Every four that holds a second step turns out under its officer. I want to know before I need it whether the thing can be done."

"I've never drilled a street," said the bursar faintly.

"No. You'll find that it's very like a lane, with more people in the way."

Four of them signed the book that evening, and there were six at the Rowan kitchen table, and a seventh who would not sit.

Jude had come for his terms. He stood by the dresser, as he had on Sunday. Pru had come since she had been told to, and had been given the stool.

Kellan had the page. He had ruled it himself.

"*In hand: held, 5.62. Credited from the Pit, 18.0. Total, 23.62.*" He looked up. "The reserve's asking every four on the roll to lend at par, for the boxes on the two dry drums. It's a loan. It comes back when the drums are filled. They've put no figure on it."

"Then we put one," said Perrin. "Four drams."

They looked at him.

"I've been on a box. In the spring, when the Pellow's core went, my aunt on Weighbridge Street had the baby's milk on a biscuit tin till noon. It isn't a hardship. It's only that you lie awake and listen for it to stop." He had gone red. "I'd like it known that I'm the one who said *no* to six-tenths in the seventh month. So when I say four, it's four."

"It's noted," said Anwen. "Four. I'll not argue with a man who's found his brake." She wrote it. "Call reserve. Half a dram a fighter, on the card, for as long as the bells are doubtful. That's five."

"Five?" said Jude.

"You, if you'll stand. By name, on the line, with a head's share of anything that's tinned. Those were your three things. I've not forgotten the third; she sets the mark for the blade she's got."

"There are two blades," said Jude. He did not look at Kellan.

"There are," said Kellan. "I've thought about that. Talley's the Kill. He was asked, and he stood the Regional, and I went and wore somebody else's jacket. I'm on this book as a blade who's told where he's wanted." His ears had gone white. "I'd sooner say it than have it settled round me."

Jude scratched his jaw.

The Hall's plate lay on the Long Terrace at Stonehand on Thursday afternoon, and Perrin Cade walked round it twice before he would look at it.

It was not much to see. It was a slab of grey iron the size of a door, with two brass rings let into it, and a figure cut in the stone of the terrace beside it. There had been one figure for thirty-one years. Since the spring there had been three more, each newer and sharper than the last. *340. 350. 355. 362.*

"I was withdrawn," he said. "On the fourth of the fifth month. I'd my hands in linen. I sat in the front row and watched three people I mess with beat a thing that had stood since my father was courting." He rolled his shoulders. "I've had six months to mind that."

"What do you want it to be?" said Kiva.

She had his coat. She had been given it, without a word, as a thing that wanted two arms and no fingers.

"I want it to be thirty counts. That's the Hall's second team. That's a place of my own, in my own colors, with nobody's brackets on it." He grinned, rather whitely. "I've been told not to want the other."

There were a dozen people on the terrace. The Hall's master was there, with his chisel in his apron. Lowen Brask stood at the rail with his notebook shut. Noa had come, with a pencil, and stood where he would be in nobody's way.

A healer strapped Perrin's wrists. The plate was loaded, slab by slab, by two third-years with a hoist, and a clerk called each weight as it went on.

"Three hundred and forty."

Perrin stood over it. He set his feet. The oak of the terrace hummed.

He lifted.

He did it as he had carried a girl across the Pit, without hurry and with his whole attention. It came up to his thighs, and his chest, and then he turned his hands under it and it went over his head, and stood there on his two locked arms like a roof.

"One," said the clerk. "Two."

Kiva counted with him. She could not help it. She watched the big red face go still, and stiller, and she saw that he was not straining. He was listening to something, as a man listens to his own ribs.

"Twenty-nine. Thirty. *Hold made.*"

He did not put it down.

"Cade," said the master.

"I'd like forty on," said Perrin. His voice came out squeezed, and perfectly polite. "If it's allowed. I'm up here now."

The master looked at the healer. The healer looked at Perrin's wrists, and his color, and nodded once. The third-years ran the hoist. It was done with the plate in the air, a slab on each side, as a man is handed two more parcels.

"Three hundred and eighty."

Something happened to the terrace. Kiva felt it in her boots. A still door that had been open went a great deal wider, quietly, like a gate swung back against a wall.

"One," said the clerk. He had lost his clerk's voice. "Two."

It was the longest thirty that she had ever counted. At twenty his arms began to shake. At twenty-five the shaking stopped, which was worse. He had his eyes shut.

"Thirty!"

He gave it to the ground. He did not drop it. He let it down through himself and into the oak, by its corners, and the Long Terrace boomed once from end to end.

Then he sat down on the plate and put his head between his knees.

Nobody cheered at first. The master had taken the chisel out of his apron and was looking at it.

"That's eighteen kilos over the best that this Hall ever saw," he said. "By a first-year. With two ribs that were in pieces at midsummer." He knelt, stiffly, at the edge of the stone. "I've cut that line four times since the thaw. I cut it once in thirty-one years before that. I said it was a strong year. I said it in the spring and I said it in the summer. I'm getting tired of hearing myself."

He began to cut.

Noa was writing. Kiva saw what he wrote, since he had turned the page a little toward her. It was his own page, the one with the heading that he had found on a tape's margin. He put the figure under four others, and the date, and nothing else.

"That's five," he said, under his breath. "On one plate. In one year." He put the pencil behind his ear. "I've a column of those now. Hold. Draw. A plate. And I've another, for tears, and skins, and a thing that stood a square. They both go up. They've both gone up faster since the fifth month than they did before it."

"Is that a reason?"

"No. It's two columns." He looked at her. "Adlam says that if I'd a third, from somewhere that wasn't Meridian, somebody would have to read it."

Perrin had lifted his head.

"I'd like it known," he said hoarsely, "that I don't mind. Not any of it. I've looked." He wiped his face on his forearm. "Is that my coat? Give it here. I'm going to go and tell my dad, and he's going to cry on a tram."

Mara had forty corks in a string bag, and she went up Stopgate with them like a woman delivering eggs.

It was a clear cold morning. The hill was quieter than it ought to have been. Six hundred households on the middle stretch were on their boxes, and one could tell which houses by the windows, which had the dim brownish look of lamps turned down to save a can. A cart from the casualty reserve stood at the rail of the twenty-sixth drum house with its tailboard down.

"Hold that," said Mara.

It was the slate. Kiva held it against her chest with her left forearm and chalked with her right.

They did it the same way at each grating. Mara knelt in the road between the tram rails, and lowered a cork on a measured cord until it floated, and tied the cord off to the bars. Then she let down the lamp and scratched a line on the wet brick with a nail on a stick, at the place to which the water rose, and another at the place to which it fell.

"Twenty-five. Rise and fall, two fingers and a half."

Kiva chalked it.

"It was two on Tuesday."

"So it was. Write the Tuesday figure beside it, in a ring, and don't join them with anything. I'll join them when I've a third."

They went on up. At twenty-six, under a dry drum, the water rose and fell by two fingers. At twenty-seven it was one and a half. A tram went by them, clanging, and the motorman leaned out to look at a Grade Two operator on her knees in his road.

"For forty gratings?"

"It's got a figure now. I'm obliged to it."

Twenty-nine was near the top, where the houses grew taller and had brass on their doors. Half a finger. Above that there was only the last grating, at the very head of the street, and beyond it the wall.

"That's one," she said.

Lowen Brask had the head of it. He did not trot. He came through the gap at his long, even walk, with the dummy lying in the poles as though it were asleep, and he stopped.

He stopped dead, two paces short of the post, and stood there.

"There's something here," he said, to the dark. "I can hear the wall's come nearer. Wace, a pace left. Slowly." They went round. "Rowan. Is that yours to tell?"

"It's a post. It's out of its row. It leans."

"I know which it is. I've fought in this yard." He was already past. "I'd not thought of it with a litter. Somebody ought to say so at the gap."

"I did say posts."

"You said posts both sides. That's true of all of them." His voice came back along the lane, quite without malice. "It's the one that isn't like the others that wants saying."

The fourth litter hit it.

It was two Wren House second-years with a real person, an old man from number fifty-eight who had declined to be a dummy and insisted on being carried in his own chair. He was not hurt. The chair's leg was. He made a great deal of noise about it, and a bird in a cage on his knee made more.

"Two," said Kiva. She had her book out, on her forearm, and she was writing in it by the edge of Pru's shutter. "Forty counts, and then about ninety. That's two in four, and the two that didn't were people who knew."

"You could stand at it," said Pru. "With the lamp."

"He said nothing lit."

"Then you could just stand at it. And say."

It was what she wanted to do. It was exactly what she had done at the Pit, and on a street, and in the lane on her first morning. She would plant herself where the trouble was and call it for everybody, in a carrying voice, until the last litter was through. She had her mouth open to say *I'll take it.*

*Tell me what goes wrong. Not mend it.*

She shut her mouth.

It was not that she could not do it. It was that if she stood at that post, then the post was hers, and on the night that mattered she would be somewhere else. She would be four paces off with a hand gone, as she had told a kitchen table on Wednesday, and there would be a post in the road and nobody at it.

"No," she said slowly. "It's not a thing for one person to stand at. It's a thing for whoever's carrying to say."

The fifth litter was Perrin's.

She heard him before she saw him. He was telling the dummy that it was being rescued.

"Perrin! Stop at the gap!"

He stopped. He had Hendry Moss of his own Hall behind him, a boy with a neck as thick as his head, and they stood there in the dark with the poles on their shoulders.

"There's a post," said Kiva. "The last of the south row. It's a pace out, into the lane, on your left as you go in. I'm not going to stand at it. I want you to call it. Aloud, before you're through the gap. *Post, left. Keep right.* And then I want Moss to say it back."

"Post, left. Keep right," said Perrin obediently.

"Post left, keep right," said Moss.

"And when you're past it, you say it over your shoulder. To whoever's behind. Whether you can see them or not."

"What if there's nobody behind?"

"Then you've told a wall, and it's cost you nothing."

Perrin thought about that.

"It's a lane call," he said. "It's *Yours* and *Mine.* It's only that it's for a post." He hitched the poles. "I'd like it known I think that's rather good. *Post, left! Keep right!*"

They went through. They did not touch it. She heard him bawl it backward down the lane, in the voice that he had been saving since the spring, and a moment later she heard somebody at the black gate take it up who had not been told to. *Post left. Keep right.*

By the ninth litter it was coming up the hill ahead of the bearers. By the fourteenth a warden with a handbell was calling it at the wicket, and had no idea why.

She wrote that down. *Told one team. The call went on by itself.*

Then the bell rang.

It was the School's own, on the core house roof, and it rang right over their heads, two and two and two, as loud as a dray on cobbles. Every foot in the yard stopped.

It lasted perhaps three seconds. There was a litter halted in the gateway, and a warden with her bell held up and her mouth open, and forty people on a dark hill all turning their faces the same way.

"*It's the mark!*" That was Noa. His thin cracked voice came down off the roof. "*It's the mark, not the drum! I've got the needle! Walk! Go on walking!*"

"*Walk!*" said Vane, from somewhere below. "*You heard the roof! That's what you've a roof for!*"

They walked.

It was done by twenty past eleven. Vane read the time off his watch at the black gate, with the wardens round him and the fours sitting on the cobbles.

"An hour and twenty minutes. For sixty-two houses, fourteen litters and a school, with nothing chasing us." He did not sound displeased. He did not sound pleased. "I'd want it in forty. We'll walk it again on Tuesday. Now. What went wrong? Bottom of the hill first."

They told him. A warden had rung evens before odds. The tape had come off the curb at number thirty. Twenty-Eight's ward had heard a join in Mace's gate and had said so, and had been right.

"The yard," said Vane. "Rowan."

"A post," said Kiva. She stood up. "The last of the south row in the old lane. It's a pace out of line, and it leans into the road at hip height. Two litters in the first four struck it. Forty counts and ninety. The two that missed it were people who'd known it for years, so it wasn't that they saw it." She read it off the page. "Brask stopped short of it and felt his way. Then I had one team call it at the gap and call it back over the shoulder, and after that nobody struck it. I didn't stand at it. I thought that I shouldn't be there to."

"A post," said Vane.

"Yes, sir."

"In the middle of my casualty road." He turned his head. "Bursar. What is it doing there?"

The thin man in the gown had been sitting on a trunk. He got up.

"It is retained," he said, "for ceremonial continuity. That's the manual's phrase. It's the oldest thing in the School. It's said to be older than the School."

"Is it holding anything up?"

"I shouldn't think so."

"Then it can be ceremonial somewhere that I'm not carrying an old man in a chair. I'll have it out by Tuesday. I've a man with a saw."

"No," said Orla Dane.

She had been in the yard the whole night, on her crate, by the first shed, where she had sat for one-and-twenty years. Nobody had asked her for anything. She had her stick between her boots, and she did not raise her voice.

"You'll not put a saw to that, Hollis. Not on a bursar's word, nor on mine."

"Give me a reason."

"I haven't one." The crooked face was grave. "That's what I've to say, and I've said it to every first-year who ever asked. There used to be one. It was printed. It was taken out of the manual seventy-seven years ago by a committee, and something civil was put in its place, and nobody now living at this School has read what was there before." She shifted the stick. "I've sat under that post since before you had a slate. I've wondered what it was for most nights. I've never once thought that the answer was *nothing.*"

"That's not a reason. That's a feeling about one."

"It is. I'm not a girl; I know the difference." Orla looked along the fours until she found the one she wanted. "You've a ledger in your hall that's older than this School. You found a hand in the back of it that had been tidied away, and you put it back, and I thought the better of you. I'm asking you to look once more before you cut. If there's nothing, I'll hold the lamp for your man myself."

Vane was silent.

He was a watch officer. He had stood on the cinders of the Pit and read a column aloud that somebody had ruled out of a book. Kiva saw him weigh the one thing against the other, a post and a litter, and she saw that he did not enjoy it.

"Tuesday," he said. "Noon. It's in the road, Dane, and a girl of seventeen has just shown me how to get forty people round it with their voices, which I'll grant you is more than the saw would have taught them. That buys it three days. Bring me why it's there, or it comes out." He put his watch away. "And I'll look in my own. I'd be a poor sort of man to ask it of your shelf and not of mine."

"Bexley," said Orla.

Noa had come down off the roof. He had a roll of tape under his arm and his pencil behind his ear, and he was already looking at the post as he looked at a notch.

"The superseded stacks," he said. "Three flights down. I know the stair. The Reader shut at six." He swallowed. "She opens at eight on a Monday. I shall be on the step."

It rained a little before dawn, and Mara was on the hill by seven on Sunday to see what the rain had done to her corks.

It had done nothing. A wet drain breathed exactly as a dry one had. She read them from the bottom, with Kiva chalking, and the slate filled slowly with pairs of figures, a Friday and a Sunday, each in its ring.

"Twenty-five. Two and a half, and two and a half. Six-and-twenty, two, and two. Seven, one and a half, and a little over." She knelt at the next. She was a long time. "Eight-and-twenty. It was a finger and a bit. It's a finger and a half."

"It's growing."

"It's growing at the top of the hill and standing still at the bottom. Like a tide making up a creek." Mara sat back on her heels in the road. "Tomorrow's the third. If that drum does what I wrote down, I shall be believed, and I find that I've no stomach for it."

They went on. Nine-and-twenty was a full finger. The last grating, under the wall, was more than a hair now. One could have put a rule to it.

Ansty let them in at the wicket. The yard was empty and wet, and the trunks were gone.

Mara went straight to the gully by the cart shed. She let the cork down, and drew it up, and turned it over in her fingers.

"Wet," she said. "From the rain. There's an inch of rainwater in the bottom of that, lying as flat as a plate." She did not get up. "It isn't moving. Not a hair. I've a main that breathes a rule's width on one side of that gate, and ten paces inside it I've a puddle."

"Then it isn't under here."

"It's drawn under here." She had the plan out. She laid it on the wet cobbles and put her knee on a corner of it. "There. A blue line, from the gate, north, across this yard, past the core house, and on up the hill. It's as straight as if somebody had done it with a ruler."

Kiva looked at the line. It was very neat. It had a small note beside it, in a clerk's round hand.

"*Recopied from the worn sheet, 2553,*" she read.

"So it was done with a ruler." Mara's voice was quite level. "A clerk had a sheet that was coming to pieces, and he copied it fair, and where the old line wandered he made it sensible. I've done that reconciling for eleven years. I know that hand. It's the hand of a man who thinks that he's being helpful."

She shut her eyes for a moment.

"Somebody's tidied my drain," she said.

Kiva had her palm on the cobbles already.

There was nothing. There was wet stone, and under it the dead flat silence of ground with no hollow in it. She moved a pace toward the gate, and knelt again. Nothing. A pace west. Nothing.

She worked along the inside of the bottom wall. She did it on her knees, in the wet, with her right palm and the heel of her left, as a person feels along a shelf in the dark for a thing that has rolled. Mara watched her and did not say a word.

At the gap in the low wall she felt it.

It was very small. It was the least thing that she had ever called a feeling. It came up through the packed dirt on the far side of the gap, slow and even, and it was not coming from the north.

She went through on her knees. She followed it. It was like following the warmth of a pipe along a wall. It ran west from the gate, close under the brick, and it grew a little as she went, and then, eight paces into the Old Yard, it stopped being ahead of her.

It was underneath. It was directly underneath, and stronger than anywhere, and beyond that place it went off at a slant, along the lane, toward the sheds.

She took her hands off the ground and looked up.

There was a cross in the dirt in front of her knees, scratched over and over. There was a black post beside it, split, with two iron bands.

"It's here," she said. Her voice came out oddly. "It doesn't go north. It comes in under the gate and turns. It runs along under the wall to here, and then it goes off down the lane, on the south side, about where the posts are." She sat back. "I can't weigh it. It's what comes through dirt. You'd have to dig."

"I can't dig up a school on a palm."

"No."

"I can't dig it up on a cork, either, since I've no hole to put a cork down." Mara had come to the gap. She stood looking at the lane, and the two rows of posts, and the one at the end that was a pace out of its line. "There'll be a sheet older than that plan. There always is. Somebody drew the worn one before it was worn."

"Noa's at the Library tomorrow at eight. About the post."

"Then he may ask about a drain while he's there. It's the same stair."

She went back to her gully, and her puddle, to write down what she had not found.

Kiva stayed where she was.

She had come down on that cross again and again since the summer. She had stood on it in the still door, with the whole of the Old Yard under one heel, and felt the ground take her. It had never boomed. It had never been anything but ground. Whatever was down there was a long way down, and bricked over, and it had been there for longer than anybody had kept the reason.

She looked at the post.

It leaned to the left. She had always thought of it as leaning away from its row, like a tooth gone bad. A child with a plait had gone round it on Friday by a little sideways step, without looking, and so had every student who had ever used that yard.

It struck her now, for the first time, that it might not be leaning away from anything. It might be leaning toward.

# Chapter 43 — Kiva: A Drain in a Box

The Halden Works lent its license yard on Sunday afternoon, and Foreman Birch made it very plain whose yard it was.

"My slate," he said, at the rail. "My ward line. My string, that I've had in that cage since the eighth month, and I know its temper. The Municipal Works has asked for a floor that keeps step, and there's only the one in Meridian with a rift on it. It's got it. Nobody lights so much as a pipe on that brick until I've said."

It was a bigger floor than the Kennel's. It was paved in blue brick, with a lattice house at the north end and a paid ward line in black standing easy along the wall. Kiva had not been inside the refinery gate before. She had counted the rivets on the way under it, since somebody had once told her the number, and had lost count at two hundred.

What Mara had built stood down the middle of the yard.

It was a trough. It was twelve paces long, knee high, made of new planks and lined with lead, and it had a foot of water in it. At the south end stood a brewer's hand pump with its handle off, and beside the pump, on a bench, six cells in a row with their needles under glass.

All six needles were nodding. The pump drew, and let go, as they did. Down the whole length of the trough the water rose a finger on the lead, and fell.

"There's your main," said Mara. "And there's your pump house. Mr. Adlam's people stepped that bank for me at eight this morning, with a pinch of their own salt, by the book. It took them four minutes. They were very civil about it."

"And that?" said Kiva.

At the north end of the trough, twelve paces from the bench, there was a stool. The stool stood with its feet in the water. On it sat a single cell with a needle of its own.

"That's a drum on Stopgate. One plain cell, filled at eight, on its own time. There isn't a wire to it. There's nothing between that cell and the bench but twelve paces of water going up and down." Mara looked at it with dislike. "At eight it stood. At ten it had a tremble. I'd like you to come to the rail and tell me what it's doing now. Not past the rail."

Kiva went to the rail.

The needle on the stool went up, and came down. It did it with the other six.

"It's in," she said.

"It went in at ten minutes to twelve. Mrs. Renn had her glass on it."

There was a woman at the bench whom Kiva had never met and knew at once. She was narrow and upright, with a grader's seal on a cord round her neck, and she had Kellan's eyes. In front of her stood a brass instrument like a clock with no hands.

"It didn't go all at once," said Kellan's mother. She did not look up from the glass. "It leaned for an hour. Then it went. I'd say that a cell is a patient sort of thing, and it's been pushed from underneath, quite gently, a great many times."

"Then it's the water," said Rusk.

He stood behind the bench with his thumbs in his waistband. He looked like a man who has slept in a chair.

"It's the water in a box," said Mara. "I'll not say more than that until tomorrow night, and nor should you. I've a paper under seal." She took a half sheet from her apron. "But I'll say what we've all been assuming, since Thursday, as though it were written in the patent. I've put it down so that it can be wrong in front of everybody."

She read it out.

"*That the water moves because the pumps move it. Stop the pumps and the main lies still. Then a bank may be quenched and filled fresh, and it will keep the time that it is given.*"

"That's the plan," said Rusk. "That's the whole of the plan, if the fence fails. I stop my three pumps for as long as the sump will bear, and I put every hand in the Works on that bank at once. I'd want half an hour."

"It's a good plan. I'd have drawn it myself." Mara put the half sheet down on the bench, under the brass weight that she had brought from her own counter. "I'd like to see it work once in a box before I trust a district to it. And Officer Vane wants to know what a string makes of a floor like this one. Mr. Birch has been kind enough to say that we may ask it."

"I said that you might ask," said Birch. "I didn't say it'd answer politely."

There were a good many people along the rail. The Placement Chair had come, since there were students on a floor with a rift at one end of it, and she stood by the gate with her hands folded. Hollis Vane was there with his slate. Rhea Sorn had brought Pike, and Greta Stroud, and Ruddock with his bag, and nobody else.

The fours were along the east wall, dark. Old Four had come, and Twenty-Eight with its reserve blade, and Halden's own three in their black and gold. Seven stood at the end.

"Gate," said Birch.

Four of his paid wards walked out onto the blue brick. They brought a wall up twelve paces west of the trough, as the Circuit's book had required since the spring, silver and square.

"That's the brightest thing on my floor. It's lit, and it's worked, and it's nearer than the bench. Any string I ever let out of that cage has gone to a gate like a dog to its dinner." He raised his arm. "Lattice. Give me the door."

The cage at the north end opened with a rattle.

Kiva had seen a good many strings by now. This one was small. It had six knots and not eight, and it stood no higher than a tram's window, on four legs. It had frost on it from crown to knee.

It came out onto the brick and stopped. Its blunt top turned, slowly, a quarter of a circle, as a weathercock turns.

It turned past the gate.

It did not pause on it. Four people were burning ash to make the brightest thing in the yard, and the grey head went by them as a man's eyes go by a stranger's in a street. It came round to the south, and stopped, pointing down twelve paces of lead-lined trough at a bench.

"That's not right," said Birch.

The string walked. It walked to the north end of the trough and stood there, astride the stool, with one pair of legs on either side of the water. The cell on the stool went on nodding between its knees.

Then the shine on it moved. It ran down off the shoulders, as Kiva had seen it run once before, in the Pit, in the spring. Two white cords went down from the lowest knot, as thick as a wrist, and into the water.

"Sorn," said Vane.

"I see it." Rhea had not moved from the rail. "It did that on the bus, in the fifth month. I said then that it had found something better than a gate. I didn't know what the something was."

"It's found the beat," said Mara. Her voice was very quiet. "It doesn't want the bright thing. It wants the thing that keeps time."

"Then I'll have a rung off it while it's standing still to be cut," said Birch. "That's what my paper says a string in my yard is for. Renn."

Kellan Renn had come through the refinery gate that afternoon in his own coat, and for the first time in his life he had not counted the rivets.

He had been thinking about his feet.

Birch had not answered his letter. Birch did not write. He had been standing at the rail when the students came in, with his slate, and he had looked at each of them as a grader looks at a tin. When he came to Kellan he had looked down.

*They've not got worse,* he had said. *Tuesdays and Thursdays. Bring your own cuff.*

That was the whole of it. It was a place, and nothing was owed for it, and there was no jacket on a peg.

"Top rung," said Birch now. "Yarrow has the cloth. He kneels where you put him, and you ask him. I'm told that's how it's done by people who mean to bag anything."

"Yes, sir."

He walked out onto the blue brick, dark.

He disliked it at once. It was not the string. He had cut that string's top rung nine times since the seventh month, and he knew how it stood. It was the floor. There was something coming up through his boot soles, slow and even, like the tread of a great many people going past on the other side of a wall. His feet wanted to fall in with it.

He had the best feet in the School. He had been told so since he was fourteen. It was a new thing to find that they could be asked to do something by a floor.

*Count against it,* he thought. He had watched a girl do that on the cinders of the Pit, with a hold's corner planted under her heel. He had not understood what she was at until a blade with a broken leg had explained it to a cart.

He counted in threes. It was the first number that came. His feet steadied.

Yarrow was laying the cloth. He was a still-door, long and incurious, and he threw a sheet as a man lays a table that he has laid before. He put it on the east side of the trough, hard up against the planks, under the string's top knot.

"That's close to the water," said Kellan.

"It's where it'll fall."

"It's a pace from a thing that's got two cords down in it."

"I've sixty counts," said Yarrow, patiently. "Ash doesn't run. It lies. It lies where it lands and leans to the nearest lamp, and I've put myself between it and the bench. What else is there?"

It was a fair answer. It was what the book said. Kellan looked at the trough, and at the white cords going down into it, and could not find the place in the book where it said anything else.

He looked along the east wall instead. He did it without quite meaning to. There was a catcher there who was particular about ground.

She had her eyes on the cloth. She was frowning. But she was not his catcher today, and she had not been asked, and he saw her shut her mouth on whatever it was.

"Renn," said Birch. "While we're young."

He went up the back of it dark, on his struts, in two bounds.

"Now?"

"Not yet," said Yarrow. "It's swaying. Now."

He lit.

It was four-tenths. He had it to a hair. The edge went through the top knot level and came out the other side, and he was dark again before his feet had found the brick. He had never made a cleaner one in that yard.

Sixty drams fell like sand out of a split sack.

"*Dark! One!*"

It did not lie.

He saw it from three paces. The heap hit the canvas and spread, as a heap does, and then the near edge of it began to move. It did not drift. It ran. It went across the waxed cloth toward the trough in a grey tongue, as flour runs to the low side of a tilted board, and over the hem, and up the planks.

It was climbing the cord.

"*Corners!*" Yarrow had the hem in both fists. His voice had gone up. "Corners, *now,* it's going—"

Pettit and Hesketh hauled. The neck came in. It closed on what was left, and Yarrow's cord went round it three times as fast as Kellan had ever seen a cord go.

"Sealed," said Yarrow. "Twenty—" He stopped. He weighed the bag in his two hands, as a catcher will. "That's not thirty. That's not anything like thirty."

There was a grey scum on the water.

It lay all down the trough, from the stool to the pump, moving with the rise and fall. Half a rung of open ash had gone into a foot of water inside ten counts.

"The bench," said Kellan's mother.

She said it quite quietly. He turned his head.

All six needles had jumped. He saw them do it, together, like six hands flung up. They stood for a moment against their upper pins, quivering. Then they lay down. They did not come down one after another, as cells die in a bank. They went as one, and the glass over the third cell cracked across with a small flat sound.

The pump stopped.

"That's fouled," said Rusk. His voice was thick. "That's one pinch of open ash in the water, and six cells cooked inside a breath. All six. There wasn't one of them that could say no to the others."

"There were six," said Mara. "I've four-and-twenty."

Nobody answered her.

Kellan stood on the blue brick with his blade dark on its cuff. He had cut a rung as well as he knew how, on the word of a catcher who had done what the book said, and it had killed a bank.

*I asked,* he thought. *I asked him and he told me, and it was a good answer.*

He had learned to ask. It had cost him a season. It had not until this moment crossed his mind that asking the catcher was no use at all when the catcher did not know either.

She had not been asked. She had said it aloud all the same, since it was the thing that was there.

"We can see that the pump's dead," said Birch.

"The water isn't."

They looked.

The brewer's pump stood with its plunger down. The six cells on the bench lay cold. Nothing in that yard was drawing on the trough, and all down its twelve paces the grey water rose a finger on the lead, and fell.

In. And out.

"You don't put a ward on it," said Greta Stroud.

They tried iron. It was a fair thing to try.

The fitter had a boat-hook. He stood well back, on the west side, and reached across the trough with the full length of it, and got the hook under the nearer cord where it left the water.

Frost ran up the shaft like a fuse.

He had let go already. He was not a fool. The boat-hook hung in the air for a moment, white from end to end, with nobody holding it. Then the cord drew up, a hand, and the hook went with it and was laid down in the water as gently as a spoon.

"That's my left," he said. "I'd like it noted that I'm right-handed."

"It's noted," said Perrin, with feeling, from the wall.

Nobody laughed much.

Kiva had been watching the cord.

She knew that rise and fall. She had had it in a tray between her palms, in the Pit, for one slack count, light as a clothesline. She had not held it. That was the whole point of what she had done. She had put a pan under it at the instant that it stopped pulling, and had been somewhere else before it began again.

"It's got a slack," she said. "Four counts hauling and four easing. A ward for one tap, on the slack, and folded before the haul comes back. It doesn't take a ward that isn't there any longer."

"That's a catcher's notion," said Pike.

"It's what I did. I only did it once."

"I know what you did. I was behind three posts with three cloths." He was not looking at her. He was looking along the east wall, at a small round first-year with a yellow card. "It wants the best fold in the yard. That isn't yours."

"No," said Kiva. "It's hers."

Pru Finch looked behind her, to see who was meant.

"Me?"

"You fold from the far edge. You don't let go. You've always got the last of it in your palms." Kiva heard that she was talking quickly, and slowed. "I learned it from you on a pail. I do it with two fingers stuck out like a fork. You'd do it properly."

"I can't go anywhere with it. I haven't an Ember. I can't put it down."

"Nobody's asking you to go anywhere. I'd like to know whether it can be lifted off the water at all. For a count. By a ward that isn't taken."

Pru looked at Rhea Sorn.

"Your card says a corner and a lamp," said Rhea. "And the cord. I underlined the cord on Friday." The pale eyes rested on her. "It doesn't say this. So it's yours to refuse, and I'll think none the worse. Stroud will tell you what it costs if it's late."

"Six weeks," said Stroud. "And a spoon held for you."

Pru swallowed. She put her card away in her tunic, and buttoned the pocket.

"I'd want it called," she said. "The slack. I can't watch the cord and my hands."

"I'll call it," said Kiva.

It was out before she had thought. Of course she would call it. It was a hem under load, with a count in it, and she was the catcher. She had called the count on every sheet that Seven had thrown since the fifth month.

She went and knelt at the rail of the trough, a long pace from the planks. She laid her hands on the blue brick, the right palm and the heel of the left.

It came up through the brick, slow and even. She could feel the haul in it. She could feel the easing.

Pru knelt on the east side, three paces from the string's near leg, with a ward of Birch's standing over her like a porch. She put up her round hands. The silver stood between them, no bigger than a tea tray, and she bent it into the bowl of a spoon.

"*Out,*" said Kiva. "Two. Three. Four. *In*—"

She knew as she said it.

It was late. It was not much late. It was the width of the difference between two hands, of which one told her the truth and the other told her that there was a floor. She had taken the middle of them, as she had for four years, and the middle was wrong by half a count.

Pru slid the pan under.

The cord was not slack. It was just beginning to draw. It came up into the bowl of that small bright ward with the whole haul of three knots behind it, and Kiva saw the silver go grey at the rim, like a window breathed on.

"*Fold!*" That was Pike, and Stroud, and Anwen, all at once.

Pru folded.

She did it faster than Kiva had ever seen anything done with a ward. Far edge. Near edge. A penny. Shut. The cord fell back into the water with a slap.

She sat down on the brick with her hands held up in front of her face.

They were white. They were white to the first joint, all eight fingertips and the two thumbs, as though she had dipped them in milk.

"They've gone to sleep," she said, in a high surprised voice. "They've all gone to sleep. I'm narrating. I can't feel my card. I had my card."

Ruddock was over the rail already, with a towel. Anwen was a pace behind him.

Kiva had not moved. She was still kneeling, with her hands on the brick.

"Who called that?" said Pike.

"I did."

"I know that you did. I want the rest of it."

She made herself say it to the whole yard, in a carrying voice, as she would have called a mark.

"I called the *in* half a count late. I had it through two hands and I took the middle. One of them can't hear." Her mouth was dry. "I told a yard on Friday night that I couldn't take a count any longer by myself. I said that somebody else would have to have it. Then I knelt down here and took it, since it's what I've always done. That's mine. It's her hands."

"It's the tips," said Ruddock, over his shoulder. "It's a nip. She folded inside it. Ten minutes, and she'll be able to pick her nose."

"I don't," said Pru faintly.

Kiva stood up. Her knees were not quite steady.

"Brask," she said. "You called the Pit's. Would you call this?"

Lowen Brask had been standing with Old Four, with his notebook shut. He came along the wall without hurry.

"I'd call it," he said. "I'd sooner you'd asked me five minutes ago. I was standing there with it in my mouth." He looked at her, quite kindly. "You said it on Friday in the Graywater yard, I'm told. "I know."

"I know you know. I'm only saying it as it was said to me." He turned to the trough. "Finch. When you're ready. Not before."

It was twelve minutes before the color came back. Pru would not be hurried, and nobody hurried her. Then she knelt down again in the same place, with her jaw set, and put up her hands.

"*Out,*" said Brask. His voice was as even as a clock. "Two. Three. Four. *In.*"

The pan went under.

For one count the white cord lay in a small silver spoon, a hand's breadth above the water, light as a clothesline.

"*Two.* Fold."

It was gone. The cord dropped.

And down the whole length of the trough, the water missed a beat.

Kiva saw it. Everybody saw it. The grey scum rose a finger on the lead, and fell, and rose, and then for the space of one slow breath it only lay there and rocked.

On the stool, between the string's knees, the single needle checked. It stood for a count. Then the water drew again, and it went on.

"There," said Kellan's mother, from her glass. "It fell out. For as long as that arm was off the water, that cell had nobody to follow. It came back in as soon as it was asked."

Nobody in the yard looked at Kiva.

It was a strange thing to be at the middle of. Forty people had all at once found something to study in the brick, or the lattice, or their own boots.

"I'll say it, since it's on my board," said Ysra Crest, from the gate. Her level voice carried the whole floor. "*It is not to be asked of her.* I wrote that under a result, with my name. I meant it. Nobody in this yard will ask."

"Nor will it be offered," said Rhea Sorn. "Not on my card."

"She's attached to me. That's my hand on her entry and my name against whatever becomes of her. "I've named this one to myself. It's too dear. I'll not have a catcher of mine go up in the dark over a hole in the road and come down with half a hand, for a plan that had a wrong sentence in it an hour ago. Find me another way."

"I'd be glad to," said Mara. "What is it?"

"Plank the holes. Every one. Give it no water to put an arm in. Then it's a string on a street, and I take it down a rung at a time on a gate, as I've taken two hundred."

"And if it finds a hole that we didn't?"

"Then I'll cut it where it stands and bag what I can."

"With its arm down my drain. You watched what half a rung did to six cells."

"I watched."

They looked at one another. They were not angry. Kiva saw that, and it frightened her more than anger would have. They were two women who were each right about a thing, and the things did not fit.

She had an answer.

It was a good one. It was about a foreman with nobody south of a string, and a hand that was already spent, and what a catcher was for. It would have sounded very well on that brick.

She looked at Pru's fingers. They were pink again, and Pru was holding them up to the light one at a time, as though she had been given them for a present.

*I'd an answer ten minutes ago,* thought Kiva. *It was to kneel down and call it.*

She put the good one where she had put the others.

"Sorn's right that it isn't hers to have offered," she said. "I've nothing to say. I'd like to know how you get that one back in its cage."

Birch grunted.

The fitter pulled it.

The water went out across the blue brick in a grey sheet and found the yard's drain. The trough emptied in a slow minute. The wet line went down the lead, and down, and there was a foot of air where the water had been.

The two white cords hung in it.

They hung for perhaps ten counts, reaching, with nothing to reach. Then they simply let go. They came apart from the bottom, softly, into loose grey ash that pattered on the lead like rain.

The shine went back up onto the string's shoulders. Its blunt head lifted. It turned, a quarter of a circle, and found a wall of silver twelve paces to the west, and walked to it and leaned.

The man with the saw turned out to be a perfectly good shovel, and by nine on Tuesday he had the cross dug up.

Rhea Sorn sat in the Graywater watch room on Tuesday night with the map wall at her back, and ruled a line on a slate.

She had read that slate from the left for twenty-three years. *Closed. Lives. Works. Tinned. Remarks.* It was the whole of her trade in five words. One could not buy a column with the one to its right. She had told a boy so in the sixth month, and he had written it down as though it were news.

There was nothing to the left of *Closed.* There never had been. A call began when a bell rang, and what one did about it went into the first column, and what it had cost went into the others afterward.

*I cut, and I count after, and I'm thought very honest for it.*

She had said that to a girl with her hand in a cloth. She had been rather pleased with it at the time, as one is pleased with a thing that is true and costs nothing.

"You've been looking at that for a quarter of an hour," said Pike.

He was mending a hem by the stove. He did it every Tuesday, whether a hem wanted it or not.

Rhea did not answer.

It was a hard thing to hear from a man who had knelt at her hems for the best part of her time on the van. It was harder that he had said it while threading a needle.

A catcher, with eight fingers that reported. A healer's card that said he would name the price to a foreman's face. "Me."

"*It's the drum! It's the drum, it isn't the mark! It's climbing! It's the drum!*"

It was not a stroke standing in the air, like the gap at the edge of a door. It was lying down. It was a seam, a hand wide, in the surface of the street, as though somebody had drawn a knife along a tablecloth and the color of the table were showing through.

The planks over the nearest grating had gone white. She saw frost run along them, and round the sandbags, like spilled milk.

Then, the whole length of that seam, things began to come up.

They did not come one above another. She had seen a string climb out of a hatch a knot at a time. These rose together. Eight grey shoulders came up through the blue, side by side, eight paces apart, each on a pair of legs as thick as lampposts, and between each and the next there ran a white cord at the height of a man's chest.

It was a string. It was a string laid on its side.

There was not a top to it. Every one of the eight had a blunt head of its own, and every head was turning, slowly, a quarter of a circle, as a weathercock turns.

Below her, on the hill, eleven handbells went off like a dropped tray.

Ten of them made forty paces of silver, cart-wide each, along the east curb. Six were the night van's. Four were students. Anwen had the uphill end, with Tibb on her left hand and the night on her right.

*If it dips, I'll hear it.*

She had learned an end in a culvert, standing still. She had not learned one that moved. A wall that walked had to be let go at the back and brought up at the front, a holder at a time, each coming up inside the next as a relief comes. It was Graywater's oldest trick. It was only that nobody did it for a hundred paces up a hill.

"Fifty-two's dark," said a warden's voice, behind her. "Fifty-four. Dark. Fifty-six, there's nobody in. Dark."

"Step," said Anwen. "Tibb. I'm coming off. Now."

She let go of nothing. That was the craft of it. She leaned on Tibb's wall until hers held no weight at all, and then it was not there, and she had gone three paces up the wet pavement and brought it up again, new, at the top.

Six paces to her right, across the gutter, a frost wall stood twice her height. She could feel the cold of it on her cheek. Above it the cord ran white from one grey shoulder to the next, drawing up and letting down.

She did not look at it. She looked at the curb.

"Fifty-eight," said the warden. Her voice changed. "Fifty-eight's lit. Mr. Garland. *Mr. Garland!* Your handle!"

There was a light behind the fanlight. Anwen saw it at the edge of her eye, small and yellow.

There was a head turning, too. It was the ninth, the new one, eight paces up the hill from where the wall ended. It came round east and stopped.

"That's on us," said Tibb. He had stopped humming. "Pryce. That's yours. It's past the end."

"I know where it is."

It was eight paces beyond her. There was nobody to bring a wall up inside. There was only the pavement, and a tall house with brass on the door, and a grey thing in the road that had found the one lamp on the hill.

The cord swung.

It did not come fast. The ninth knot leaned, as the Flask had leaned at the Pit, and the white line between it and the next below bellied out toward the east curb like a rope with a weight hung on it. The frost came with it. It crossed the gutter.

"*Step!*" said Anwen. "All of it! Eight paces! I'm going long!"

She went.

She let go of Tibb's wall without waiting to feel it take, which was a thing that she had been told for four weeks never to do. She ran eight paces up the wet stone and stood in front of a green door with her back to the brass, and put up her hands.

The wall came. It was hers alone, a cart wide, with nothing on its left and nothing on its right.

The frost met it.

It did not strike. It leaned. It was the weight of a string's knot, sixty drams with legs under it, and it came onto her two hands as a full tin comes onto a shelf. She had held that weight before, with three beside her, for forty minutes. She had never held it with nobody.

*It'll bear weight.* She had said that about a different thing. She found that it was true of this one.

"I've an end with no middle," she said. She said it in a carrying voice, level, as one reports a tin. "Fifty-eight. I'm holding. I want a neighbor on my left and I want that handle pulled. In that order."

"Coming up," said Tibb. Then, sharply: "Stiles. You're short. You're a hand short on the outside, and she's got nothing to lean on if you join like that."

"So I am," said Stiles.

She felt them come. It was like hearing a cart turn into the street. Tibb's wall came up inside her left edge, and beyond his, Stiles's, snug this time, and the weight on her two hands became a weight on six. She did not dip. She knew that she had not, though there was nobody to tell her.

The green door opened behind her.

"You're being rescued," said a voice that she had known since it was a first-year's with purple hands. "It's on a count. There's a lady holding your porch up, so we're to be quick."

"I'll not leave Admiral," said an old man.

"He's coming. He's got his own handle. Brask, the box."

There was a sound from inside the house, a flat iron *clack.* The light in the fanlight went out.

Anwen felt the weight go off her hands as one feels a hand lifted from a shoulder. The ninth head turned away. It went round west, and north, and stopped pointing at nothing, and the cord drew back across the gutter to the crown of the road.

"Dark," said the warden. "Fifty-eight. And about time."

They came out past her. Perrin had the chair in his arms, with an old man in it and a cage on the old man's knee. Lowen Brask walked behind with a hand on the back rail. A bird was saying something disagreeable.

"I'd like it known," said Perrin, as he went by, without turning his head, "that I mind every one of these. That's Pryce. She's held a string on her own outside a front door, and I've had my back to it. Again."

She did not laugh. She found afterward that she had wanted to.

Mace was behind her. She had not heard him come.

He was looking at her boots. He looked at them for some time. Then he looked along the wall, at the place eight paces down the hill where she had let go of a join without waiting for it.

"You came off Tibb before he had you," he said.

"Yes."

"I've a card in a drawer that says you never do that."

"I know what it says."

She had the answer. It was not a tidy one.

"Hm," said Mace.

"Step," he said. "It's not done. There's an eleventh coming up, and the sixties are lit."

The lid was half across.

The arm went down beside it. It went down through the gap that was left, a foot wide, into eight feet of brick shaft, and she heard it meet the water.

It was a small sound. It was the sound of a rope's end dropped into a well.

In. And out.

"It's in the drain," said the man in the apron. He had let go of the ring. "It's got its arm down my slot."

Kiva put her right palm flat against the post behind her.

She could feel it. She did not need the ground. It came up through the old wood, slow and even and many, as it had come up through a doorpost on the Brickfields road. It was stronger than it had been. It was a great deal stronger.

"Vane," she said.

He was in the gap. He had seen it go down.

"Say it."

"It's pumping. The main. It's the same as the trough." She took her hand off the wood. "If my aunt stops her pumps now, the water won't lie still. She can't fill. And if Sorn cuts, it runs down that arm into the drain, and it's under the pump house in an hour."

"It can't come down. There's a thing in the slot."

Vane did not write that. He stood and looked at a white cord going down into the ground at the foot of a post.

Vane read it aloud. He read everything aloud.

"*Pump house, eighteen minutes to twelve. I have nineteen hands of twenty-four and the rest coming. Pumps stopped two minutes, on trial. The water is still moving. It moved less a quarter of an hour ago. Something is in it. Pumps running again. I cannot fill over this. Tell me when it is out. M. Rowan.*"

He folded the slip.

"*Tell me when it is out,*" he said.

The yard was very quiet. There were perhaps twenty people in it now. The fitters stood at their bar. Lowen Brask had come to the gap, and Rhea Sorn behind him, with her coat open and her hands out of her pockets. Orla was on her crate by the first shed, where she had been since the bell. Somebody was setting up a tripod under the shed lamp. It was Noa. He had come down off the roof with the travelling mast on his back, and nobody had told him to.

Kiva looked at the cord.

It lay in the angle of the post, where it left the ground, drawing up and letting down. It had a slack in it. Four counts of haul, and four of ease. She knew what it weighed on the slack. She had held one like it in a tray between her palms.

She knew what was four paces from it. She had not looked yet. She would have to put a door down and find out.

Nobody in the yard looked at her.

She saw them not do it. She saw a watch officer study his slate, and a Torch study the wall. She saw the Placement Chair fold her hands. They had every one of them heard it said on a blue brick floor, and they were keeping to it as people keep to the edge of a pavement.

*It is not to be asked of her.*

It was the kindest thing that anybody had ever written about her. She understood, standing there with her back to a post, that it had a second half which nobody had been able to write. If she was not to be asked, then nobody could say it for her. Not her sponsor, and not her officer, and not an aunt two miles away with nineteen hands.

It would have to be said by the one person in the yard who would rather have been told.

Her mouth was dry. She had an answer, and for once it was not standing ready. She had to go and fetch it, a piece at a time.

"I'd like to say what it costs," said Kiva.

"*Out,*" said Selka Dray.

Her voice was not strong. It was exact. It came across five paces of dark from a chair against a post, and it did not hurry.

"Two. Three. Four. *In.* Two. Three—"

Kiva put up her hands.

They came up low, at her right hip, over the foot of dark beside the iron. Two fingers stood clear of the rest, straight and stiff. She bent the ward into the bowl of a spoon, and she bent it the wrong way round, with the curve to the east and the open side north.

It was cold. She could feel the cold through the silver.

"Hand, to Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on three, and on five."

The arm drew up. The lid shivered under her boots.

It was a hair early. It had been promised so.

"*One,*" said Kiva, and slid the pan under.

For four counts it lay there. It was the whole arm of a thing that stood ten abreast down a hill, and it lay in a tray between her palms, light as a clothesline, humming.

"*Two.*"

"*Out,*" said Dray.

They hauled.

It was not three posts. It was ten. The cord came up out of the dark like a hawser taking a ship, and found a spoon under it, and the whole of that haul went round the curve of her ward and into her right side.

It did not throw her east.

It turned. She felt it turn, a quarter round, as water turns in the bowl of a ladle, and it struck her in the ribs and the hip and pointed her out across the lane.

And it had a count in it. It was slow and even and many, and it was stronger than anything that she had ever had her hands on, and for the length of one tap it nearly had her.

"*Three.*"

She folded. Far edge. Near edge. A penny, in her palms. Shut.

Nobody.

She was nobody, going sideways over the packed dirt of the Old Yard with a hill's worth of haul in her spine. The ash went out of her like a breath. And the count went with it. It was left on the far side of a shut door, as mud is left on a step, exactly as it had been once before.

The struts caught her. Low, and long, and north. She was over the lane. Her left hand was cold to the wrist already, and she shut the Ember at the top and did not listen for it. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs and arrive.

Nobody, again.

It came out of a place that had been emptied in the Pit, and had only half filled. She had thought that she knew what that was like. There was a moment in the air when it seemed to her quite reasonable that there should be nothing to pay it with. Something paid. It went out through her left arm. She felt it pass the wrist, and go on, and she felt it pass the elbow.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the pan. Wide. By its corners.

"*Five!*"

Her right foot came down on a cross in the dirt, and the yard took her.

Then the corner came.

It came in at her heel like a tide coming up a slip. It was ten things leaning back on a line, and they went on leaning. It went down through her and into the clay and spread there, and the whole of the old ground under the wall took the weight of it, and held.

There was a white cord running down to the dirt a foot from her right boot. It was as taut as a stay. It ran away from her, east and a little south, over a low wall, to a black gateway, and from it a wall of frost was already dropping, thin as breath.

"*Planted!*" said Kiva. "*Six!*"

"*PLANTED!*" It was a boy's voice, from the lodge door, and it would have carried to the Sag. "*Planted, planted, send it!*"

"*Bar!*" That was Brask.

She did not look. She was counting.

She counted in fives. *Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.* The thing had fours. It bit on the first of each, as a girl in a chair had said that it would, and she went past it, a beat on, every time. It was like walking down a moving stair.

"Wace," she said. It came out between two numbers. "The plank. Your end."

*Boom.*

A still door opened on her left, a yard off, and the lane rang with it. It was a great deal wider than hers. Wace had been a post before.

*Boom.* Another, beyond him. Hendry Moss stood there with his thick neck sunk between his shoulders, and between the two of them something hummed, like a plank laid from trestle to trestle.

"Mine," said Wace.

She leaned it.

She had never done that. She had watched it done to her, on a floor that boomed, by a boy who pushed one man and moved another. She let the corner go over, a little at a time, from her own heel to the end of that plank, as one lets a full tin down onto a shelf.

It went. She felt it go off her. She felt two still-doors take it and sink a finger's breadth into the dirt.

"It's got a count," she said. "Don't keep it. Go in fives. I'll call them. *One. Two. Three. Four. Five.*"

"One," said Wace, through his teeth. "Two."

She shut the door.

She shut it on an empty doorway. She was standing on a cross in the Old Yard with nothing open, and a cord a foot from her boot that was not hers any longer.

She did not sit down. She had meant to. She had two people beside her now, and then three; a long incurious boy in black and gold had walked out of the dark without a word and set his feet at Moss's shoulder, and the plank had become a thing with three ends.

"Yarrow," he said. "Halden. I'm told it goes round."

"*One,*" said Kiva. "*Two. Three. Four. Five.*"

Five paces off, a girl in a chair was calling fours.

Perrin Cade had the end of an ash pole in both hands, and he had never wanted so much to say that he could not.

It was not heavy. That was the insult of it. He had held three hundred and eighty kilos over his head for thirty counts on Thursday, and a man had cut the figure in stone. This was a bar as thick as his wrist, stuck through a post, and it would not go round.

"It's not weight," said Brask. He was at the other end, with his feet set. "It's a screw. You can't lift a screw. You lean, and you wait for it."

"I've leaned."

"You've pushed. Open the door, Cade. Wide. And don't push at all."

Perrin opened it.

The dirt of the Old Yard hummed under him. It was a narrow place to stand. There was thirty feet of brick a yard behind his heels, and in front of him, three paces off, a wall of frost had come down across the lane like a sheet let fall from a line. He could see through it. *You don't look up.*

He looked at the bar.

"On my word," said Brask. "*Give.*"

He let his weight go into the pole. He did not shove. He stood there, wide, and let the screw have the whole of him, as he had let the Long Terrace have a plate.

Something groaned under the ground.

"*Take.*"

The bar moved a hand.

"*Give.* It's coming. Don't chase it. *Take.*"

His ribs were telling him something. They had been strapped from the armpits to the hips for a spring and a summer, and they had a good memory.

*That's not pain,* he thought. *That's only where pain was.*

"Eight," said the man in the apron, behind him. He was counting on his fingers like a child. "That's eight. One more, and it's in the groove."

"*Give.*"

It stopped.

It stopped as it had stopped for four fitters since Tuesday. The pole stood across the lane, and the two of them stood at its ends with everything they had in the ground, and nothing moved.

It did not take.

Through the frost he could hear two counts going. There was a girl calling fours, thin and exact. There was another calling fives, and her voice was not steady, and she had two people answering her now, and then three.

She had planted it and given it away. He had heard her do that. She had stood on that lid and named every part that was somebody else's, and his was this.

"Brask," he said. "I'd like it known that I mind."

"What?"

"All of it. Everything since the fifth month. I've been saying that I don't, since Thursday, and it was a lie; I was only pleased." He shut his eyes. "I mind that I had my back to the Pit. "Then mind it into the bar," said Lowen Brask, "and stop telling me."

He did.

It was not strength. He had no more of that. It was only that he stopped keeping any of himself back for afterward. The still door went down through the dirt, and the clay, and the old brick of the wall's footing, and the whole of the bottom of the School came up under his boots.

The screw shrieked.

The bar went round. It went a quarter, all at once, and the two of them walked after it, stumbling, and under the ground an iron plate as broad as a door went down into its groove and struck bottom with a sound like a struck tank.

Through the frost, a girl was still calling fives.

Mara Rowan had never liked a room with twenty-four of anything in it. It was too many to keep in one's head and too few to stop trying.

She had them, all the same. She had got the last of her hands at six minutes to twelve, off a tram, in a body: five students in Wren House green who had stood a Bench in the ninth month and had been waiting at the Brickfields shed with the carts. There was an operator to every cell that she could find one for. There was a student at the rest, with a red handle under one hand and a fresh can at his feet.

Rusk had cell one. He had taken his coat off.

"I've not pulled a handle since I was made examiner," he said. "I find that I remember where it is."

The three pumps stood at the far end, turning. Old Lumb, the keeper, sat on an upturned bucket beside the sump hatch with a watch on his knee and a notched stick down the hole.

"Five-and-twenty minutes," he said. "From the stroke that they stop. I've told you that four times. It rained Sunday. At five-and-twenty the sump's at its sill, and then it's the Hythe's cellars, and I'll have a hundred people at my door in the morning with wet coal."

"I heard you the first time, Mr. Lumb."

"I'm only saying."

She had a chalk mark on the sump wall. It was a hand above the water. The water came up to it, and fell.

It had come up to it for nineteen days.

There was a sounder on the keeper's desk, a small brass arm on a spring. A Works clerk sat over it with his pencil. Beside him stood a narrow woman with a grader's seal on a cord and a brass glass like a clock with no hands; and behind her, with his hat in his hand, a pleasant man in a black coat who had asked very civilly whether he might watch.

The sounder chattered.

"*Planted,*" said the clerk.

Mara did not allow herself to think about what that word had cost, or whom. She had told a girl in a yard that she could only manage it from two miles off.

"Mr. Lumb. Stop your pumps."

He threw three levers, one after another. The slow breathing at the end of the room faltered, and ran down, and ceased.

It was very quiet. She had not heard that room quiet in her life.

"Handles!" said Mara. "Hands on. On my word, and not on your neighbor's. *Quench!*"

Twenty-four red handles came over in one flat sound, like a shutter.

All down the long wall the needles stopped nodding. They did not go one after another. They went as they had lived for nineteen days, together, and lay against their pins.

"Dead," said Rusk. "That's a bank that can't be cooked. Whatever comes down that drain now, there's nothing here for it to foul."

"There's the water," said Mara.

She went to the sump hatch. She knelt, in her good skirt, and held the lamp down.

It came up to the mark. It fell. It came up again, a little short.

"It's still moving."

"It's less," said Lumb. He had his stick in. "A finger. It was two."

"It's being let go of. Something's had hold of it, and it's been taken off, and the water's only rocking with what was in it." She did not take her eyes from the chalk. "I want it flat. I'll not fill over a rock."

"Three minutes," said Lumb. "Of five-and-twenty."

The sounder went again.

"*Down,*" said the clerk.

That was a gate. That was an iron plate in a groove at the head of a hill, and a core house on the dry side of it. She had drawn that in ink on Tuesday and had not believed in it until this breath.

She watched the water.

It rose toward the mark, and did not reach it. It sank. It rose less. It was like watching a cup that somebody had set down too hard. She counted a slow twenty, and at the end of the twenty the black surface lay in the lamplight as flat as a slate, with her own face in it.

"It's lying," she said. Her voice was not quite her own. "Send that. One word. *Still.*"

The clerk's hand rattled on the key.

"Five minutes," said Lumb.

It was a long way to send a word, and a long way back. She knelt at the hatch and watched her own face in a drain. She had no notion what was being done on a hill by a School. She had arranged it so. She had told herself that it was so that she could do her work.

The sounder spoke.

"*Back,*" said the clerk. And then, since it went on: "*Hem down. Ten cut. It is out. Fill. H.V.*"

"*Fill!*" said Mara.

She was on her feet. She did not remember getting up.

"Draw your dead cans! Fresh home! Every one's had its pinch; I salted them myself. Count your sixty aloud, each of you, and I don't care that it sounds like a schoolroom. Then tune. And *not* to the lamp tune. Every one of you has a card under your handle with a number on it. That's your count. There aren't two alike in this room."

They drew. They filled. The long room filled with voices counting sixty, all out of time with one another, and it was the most beautiful noise that she had ever heard in a pump house.

She went down the row.

She had known that she would have to. Twenty-four fresh cells stood side by side on one wall, and a cell was a patient thing, and it leaned to its neighbor. Somebody had to stand between each and the next, for a slow ten, while its tune took hold, and feel which way it was leaning, and tell the tuner.

She laid her left palm on the first drum.

"That's yours, Mr. Rusk. Three. It's holding three. Good."

The second. "Five. It's leaning down toward three. Bring it up. More. There."

It was like standing in a doorway between two rooms of people who each wanted her to come in. She held her hand on the iron and counted against both. She had watched a girl do that on the cinders of a pit, in fives, and had not understood until now what it had been taking out of her.

"Seven. Good. Four. Nine."

"That one's going back," said the narrow woman at the glass. She had not raised her voice. "Eleventh from the door. It's fallen in with the tenth. I have it on the dial."

"I felt it. Quench eleven. Fill it again." Mara did not stop. "There's a second can under your stool, boy. That's what it's for."

"Eleven minutes," said Lumb.

At the fourteenth cell she noticed that her fingertips were white.

At the eighteenth she could not feel the iron. She could feel that there was a drum. She had a girl at home who used those words.

*The count of a thing. Somebody else has that.* Rusk had told her that it was all over the Works. He had thought it rather funny.

"Mr. Rusk," she said. "You'll take the odd ones from here. Your thumbs are as good as my palm; I've said otherwise for eleven years out of pride. I'll take the even."

He came without a word.

"Lumb. The first pump. It's on cells one to eight, and no others. Start it."

One pump drew, and let go, in the quiet. It had a slow, private, limping sound. It was a three.

"Fourteen minutes," said the keeper. "That's one. I want three."

The second took its eight at nineteen minutes, on a seven. The third would not. The eleventh cell had fallen in a second time, and a third, and it was a white-faced Wren House student who said at last, in a very small voice, that he thought the can was cut.

It was. It was civic stock, and somebody had put soot in it.

"Twenty-five," said Lumb. "That's my sill. There's water going over it. I can hear it."

"I can hear it too. Give me a can off the spare shelf. Not that one. The one with the wax unbroken." She had her left hand on the drum. She could not feel it. She watched the needle instead, as she had taught a child to do. "Sixty. Tune it to eleven. Hold it. *Hold.*"

It held.

"Start your pump, Mr. Lumb."

The third pump drew. It was a five.

Three pumps took their strokes in the long brick room, each in its own time, like three people walking who do not know one another. It was six-and-twenty minutes by an old man's watch. Along the left wall four-and-twenty needles trembled, and stood, and not one of them nodded to the next.

"Seven cellars," said Lumb. He had his ear to the hatch. "Perhaps eight. A hand of water in each. I've known the Hythe have worse from a blocked gutter." He looked at her. "You're holding that arm oddly, missus."

She was. She had it against her chest, with the right hand over it.

It was white to the wrist. It did not hurt. It lay there like a thing that she was minding for somebody.

"That's starved," said the woman with the glass. She had come round the desk. She took the hand, quite briskly, and turned it to the lamp. "I've seen it on graders who hold a reed too long over a loud tin. It's not burned. It wants ward-trace, plain, inside the hour, or it'll sulk for a month." She frowned. "There's not a pinch of plain ward-trace in Meridian. It's all had the hair in it since the thaw."

"Then it will have to sulk," said Mara.

She was looking at the needles.

"I said to a watch officer that everything in my house had a meter and a way to shut it down," she said. "I've been nineteen days finding the second half." She was very tired. "I should like somebody to tell me what it cost."

Nobody in the pump house could.

"A dram and two-tenths," said Aske. "I'll not take more than I can use. The rest goes back under wax."

They heard it as they went down the hill to the van. It came up Stopgate behind them in a cracked voice that Kiva had last heard on the cinders of the Pit.

"*Closed! Ten to one, and it's shut!*"

She did not turn round. There was a tin to be carried.

They went along the Brickfields road in the Graywater van, with the rings whining and Ruddock at the tiller.

The Ferren pump house had every window lit.

Kiva stopped on the sill, out of habit. Then she remembered that she had no certificate that mattered tonight, and that there was nothing in that room which could be harmed by a girl with no doors, and she went in.

It sounded wrong. That was the first thing. She had stood in that doorway ten days before and listened to three pumps breathe as one. Now there were three sounds. One was slow, and one was quick, and one limped, and they went in and out of one another like three people on a stair.

Along the left wall stood four-and-twenty needles under glass. Every one of them trembled a little, on a time of its own.

Mara was sitting at the keeper's desk.

She had her left hand in her lap, with the right laid over it. She looked up, and saw who had come in, and her eyes went at once to an arm in a blanket.

"So that's what it cost," she said.

"Aske thinks it'll come back. He doesn't know."

"To the elbow."

"A hand past it."

Mara did not say anything. She looked at it for as long as she might have looked at a drum.

"I said in this room an hour ago that I should like somebody to tell me," she said at last. "I find that I'd sooner have gone on not knowing until morning. That's not to my credit." She moved her right hand. "You'd better see mine. I'm told that we're to be a pair."

"I did. I hated it. He was perfectly competent, which was worse."

"Plain," said the narrow woman. "I'd forgotten what that sounded like. It's like a room with the window shut."

It was not much to watch. Aske shook a pinch into a shallow glass. "There," he said, after a slow two hundred. "There's the first of it."

A flush had come at the root of the thumb. It crept, as damp comes up a wall.

He gave her the hand back.

"I've got the bit."

"So you have." Something happened at the corner of her mouth. "Sit down before you fall. I've a thing to show you. I found it on Tuesday and I'd no room in my head for it."

It was a book. Old Lumb fetched it, from a press behind the pumps that held three centuries of them, bound in calf gone black. He carried it as a man carries a thing that he has been asked for at last.

"The keeper's book for 2604," he said. "The missus has had the lot out since she came. She says she's looking for how the bank's behaved. Nobody's asked me for a book in forty years."

"I went looking for a night when all four-and-twenty did something together," said Mara. "I thought there might have been one. There's a line in your own book, in the left column, that you've carried since the spring. *T-0.* Four of them. One's 2604."

She opened the calf at a straw.

It was a keeper's hand, round and careful, in ink gone brown. Kiva read it by the desk lamp.

*The 11th of the third month. Keeper took ill in the evening; a fever, three nights. Entered here because the needles did it too.*

*Dreamt that the roof was off the house and there was nothing over it. Not dark. Nothing. And all four-and-twenty stood up together, and were looking. I do not know at what. I woke and they were as usual.*

*The levee lamps went out that night and came on again of themselves. No fault found. Not grid. I have put that in the fault column and this in remarks, as I am told that a fever is not a fault.*

Kiva read it twice.

She was aware of her own breathing. There were three pumps going in the room, and none of them kept time with it.

"*The roof was off,*" she said.

"You know it."

"I read a page in the Library, in the sixth month. I'm not to copy it. I'm allowed to have read it." She kept her eyes on the brown ink. "There's a phrase. *The sky unroofed.* That was a Master of an Order, in a house on a hill, a hundred and sixty years before this. She'd no notes. She said that it was paying attention." Her mouth was dry. "*Were looking.*"

"And you."

"I'm not on a page that you can open. You sealed yours."

"So I did. I'm rather glad of it tonight." Mara turned the book a little, so that the lamp fell square. "Then here's what I have, and it isn't much. "And every lamp."

"And every lamp. On a night that the Works has in its fault column." She shut the calf, gently. "It doesn't tell me what it is. It doesn't tell me that it's coming, or when, or whether it's a thing that happens or only a thing that people dream when a fever's on them and the needles jump. I'd not build a shed on it."

"What does it tell you?"

"That it isn't you." Mara looked at her. "I've thought since you were thirteen that you'd been singled out for something, and I've hated whatever did the singling. It's older than you. It's wider. "There'll be others," said Lumb.

They looked at him. He was not a man who had expected to be looked at.

"Keepers' books. There's one in every pump house on the coast, and they all keep a remarks column, and nobody reads it. If there was a night in 2604, somebody at Saltmere had a temperature as well." He rubbed his nose. "I'm only saying."

"That's the second sensible thing that you've said tonight, Mr. Lumb," said Mara. "The first was five-and-twenty minutes."

There was a man standing by the wall of needles. He had been there the whole time, with his hat in his hand, and he had taken care to be in nobody's road.

"I should like to add one line," said Mr. Adlam. "Under my own name, as before. I was watching that bank when the word *back* came through." He nodded at the row. "T-0," said Kiva.

# Chapter 48 — Kiva: Why It Is Crooked

There were more than seven in the room on Thursday, and that had not happened in the Ferren's time.

The Reader has asked to see it every spring for thirty years. I've told her every spring that it was being conserved. So had the bursar before me."

"Thirty-one," said the Reader.

"Thirty-one. I beg your pardon." He untied the tape. "I was shown a post last week. It had been retained for ceremonial continuity since 2603, and it turned out to be the handle of a gate. I thought that I had better go and look at what else we retain."

He lifted the lid.

There was a bed of yellowed wool. In it lay a disk the size of a penny, of a dull white, with nine rings cut into its face.

It was plaster.

Kiva did not need to be told. One could see the grain of it, and a chip at the edge where it had been dropped. It was a cast.

"There's a label," said the bursar. His voice was not steady. "On the underside of the lid. I've read it eleven times."

He read it a twelfth.

"*Cast of the plate of nine rings that was carried by the first teachers. Taken by leave of the bearer, for the School's case, that students may know its look. The plate went out again with the Home Line the same day, on its cord. It is not the School's and never was. We do not show the plate. We show this.*" He put the lid down. "Somebody stopped reading that label, and somebody else wrote a ninth edition. I don't think that either of them meant a lie. I think that they tidied."

Nobody at the table spoke. It was a word that had been used a good deal lately.

"I've a request," said the Reader.

She had risen. She was not much taller standing.

"I can't lay a hand on the Line's plate. I've read the watch's ruling of the first of the seventh month; I had it copied. No hand but the bearer's. I ask whether the bearer may lay it beside that cast, in her own hand, for as long as it takes an old woman to count to nine."

"That wants a vote," said the Ferren. "To show it."

Seven hands were flat already. They had not been lifted.

"Then that's voted. Miss Rowan. If you're willing. It's yours to refuse; nobody in here chose you."

Kiva drew the cord out from her collar.

She had worn it for four years. She had shown it to a steward once, with a paper, and to a watch officer, and had never laid it on a table in a lit room for anybody to look at. It came over her head, and she held it in her right palm, and set the back of that hand down on the oak beside the box.

It lay there, dark, with its nine grooves.

The Reader bent over it. She had both pairs of spectacles on. She did not touch.

"Nine," she said. "And nine. The fifth ring's wider than the rest; so is the cast's. There's a flaw across the third, at about four o'clock, like a hair. Look at the plaster, child. No. You look. I've seen what I came for."

Kiva looked. There was a raised thread across the third ring of the cast, where the plaster had gone into a scratch.

There was a scratch on the disk. She had known it since she was thirteen. She had thought that she had made it herself, on a stair.

"It's the same," said Noa.

He had come off the bench without being asked, and stood a pace behind the Reader, with his hands shut behind his back so that nobody should think he meant to reach.

"I drew that. In the sixth month. I drew the nine rings off the plate in the fourth edition of the Founders' History, with the fifth one wider, and I wrote beside it, *Is hers the same?*" His narrow face had gone red. "I never asked to see. It wasn't mine to ask. Then the watch said that it wasn't anybody's. I've had it on a page ever since, with a question mark."

"You may take the question mark off," said the Reader. She straightened, a hand in the small of her back. "I'll say it, since I'm the only one in this room whose trade it is to say that one old thing is another. That is the plate of nine rings that the first teachers carried. It was never preserved by anybody. It was *released.* It has gone from hand to hand by appointment, with a watch to witness and not to choose, for as long as there has been a Line, and it has come down that road to a catcher of seventeen who was named by a woman who liked a question that she'd asked." She took off the upper spectacles. "I have asked the School for it for thirty-one years. I have been looking at a girl's collar since the sixth month."

"The tenth edition will say so," said the bursar. "Out of the fourth. Word for word. I've the printer on Monday."

"*It is released to one who carries it,*" said Orla, from the bench. Her voice was rough. "*We have not been told why and have not thought it ours to ask.* I learned that off a first-year who'd been to a library. I was twenty years on the Line and nobody'd told me."

"What's it for?" said Kiva.

She had not meant to ask. She had asked it of a levee catcher's line, and a watch officer, and an empty kitchen. It was out before she had thought that this was a room where somebody might know.

The Reader looked at her quite kindly.

"I can tell you whose it's been. I can tell you that it's the one in the picture. That's the whole of what I've given you, and you had most of it already." She folded the spectacles. "The first book's lost. I've looked for that as well."

"I asked a thing in the summer," said the Ferren. "I'd had four-and-thirty years of *what does it do.* I said that I'd begun to wonder what else was happening when it did." She was looking at the dark disk on the girl's palm. "It's had two bearers put *warm* in my book, and each put it once, after a first crossing, and each put *cold* for every crossing since. That's all that I know. I'm seventy-one in the spring. I've decided that I can bear to leave a question in a book with nothing under it."

Kiva closed her hand.

It was cold. It had been cold on a table in front of twelve people, as it had been cold in the Old Yard at midnight with an arm of a string under her heel. She put the cord over her head.

She had been told what it was. It did not feel any different. She had rather thought that it would not.

Rhea Sorn sent a boy with a card that evening. It was four words long. *Yard. Six. Bring Finch.*

There was no van out. There was only the hall yard, and the hooded lamp, and a tall woman sitting on an upturned tub with her elbows on her knees. Pike stood behind her with his hands at his back.

"I told you on the cinders that I wasn't offering you anything," said Rhea. "I wasn't. You were seventeen, with a hand in a cloth, and your team had my name on its entry." She did not get up. "You're seventeen still. The brackets came off this morning. So I've nothing on you any longer but what I choose to put, and I've thought for a week about how little that ought to be."

She held out a card. It was slate blue, and it had a good deal more than twelve words on it.

Kiva took it in her right hand, and read it under the lamp.

*Rowan, K. Catcher's second, to Pike. The night van, two nights in the month, from the first of the new year, when her healer's card is up. Dark hands. No door on my floor that is not on her certificate. Finch with her, for the cord, if Finch will. If T7 is called on a night of mine, she is T7's and I go short. Until the last of the third month. Then we look again, and either of us may say no. No seat is promised. R.S.*

It was a small thing. It was an edge of a wall and a lamp, written out long.

"That's all of it," said Rhea. "I've put the ends on it on purpose. I burned a card once with two words on it about you. One of the two was *mine.* I'd had no business writing it, and I knew it the same night." The pale eyes came up. "This one's got *either of us* on it. I'd like you to notice that I can learn."

"What's a catcher's second?"

"She kneels at my other hem," said Pike. "She sets nothing. She calls nothing. She watches a man who's been thought a great hand for six-and-twenty years, and she tells him afterward what he did that he didn't say aloud." He nearly smiled. "I'm told that there's a deal of it. I've a first-year to thank for the notion."

"And Pru?"

"Ask her. I did, an hour ago, at her mother's door."

Pru was standing a pace behind Kiva's shoulder with her hands in her sleeves.

"I said that I'd have to think," she said. "Then I said yes so quickly that I bit my tongue. Then I said that I'd only come if I was asked by you as well, since I'm not your cord. I'm a catcher with a sheet. I've got a red tag in a cold room." She went scarlet. "That came out bigger than I meant."

"It came out the right size," said Kiva.

She looked at the card again.

She had wanted a thing like this since she sat on the Measure Hall steps in the spring and watched a boy with steady eyes shake hands under three striped awnings. She had wanted it on ordinary terms. She had thought that when it came she would know what to say.

*What's behind it?* That was Anwen's question. She found that she had it ready, and that it was a better thing to have ready than an answer.

"Two things," she said. "Before I say. I'd like the *before* column read out, each night, on the slate, as you read it on the fifth. Whatever's in it. To all of us, not to me."

"That's on the slate already. It's ruled. I'd look a fool rubbing it out."

"And I can't say yes for the four. If Seven wants me those nights for a thing that isn't a call, a floor, or a lane before Saltmere, they've the first word. I'd have to ask them. I don't know what they'll say."

Rhea looked at her for some time.

"I had a speech for this," she said. "In case you said yes at once. It was about not being grateful." She got up off the tub, stiffly. "I shan't want it. Take the card home. Ask your four. Bring it back signed or bring it back torn, and tell me which on the first of the month." She put her hands in her pockets. "I'll say one thing, since I'm told that I'm allowed a piece of advice a season. You'll hate being a second. You'll kneel at a hem and see every single thing that you'd do differently, and you're not to open your mouth until the bag's sealed."

"I've been a corner. For Pru."

"I heard. I heard that you were corrected eleven times."

"Fourteen, by the end."

"Good," said Rhea Sorn. "That's about the right number."

"*Saturday the 15th of the tenth month. Call reserve, 2.5: spent, 0.70. Pryce, forty-one hundredths, on a wall. Cade, fourteen, on a bar. Talley, nine. Renn, six. Rowan, none. Orla Dane had moved her crate.

It was not far. It was a pace to the north, out of the line of the south row. A watch officer had pointed out, quite civilly, that she had been sitting on a casualty road for one-and-twenty years, and she had not been able to find an answer to it.

It was Sunday evening. The yard was empty. She had the lamp on its hook.

There was a bar on the bottom wall now. It hung on two iron hooks that a fitter had let into the brick, at the height of a man's shoulder, an ash pole as thick as a wrist. Under it stood the post, black and split and banded. At the post's foot, where a cross had been scratched for her by a girl's heel all summer, lay a square of iron with a ring in it.

*You're no help,* she had said to that post. She had said it for years.

She took the card out of her coat again.

It had come on Friday, by the coast boat. It was a plain card, of the cheap sort that pump-fitters used for their accounts, and it had four lines on it in a round, rather childish hand that she would have known in the dark.

*Your letter was four lines. So is this.*

*I am a pump-fitter. Our harbour bank went into step on the 17th, at twenty past two. I knew what it was before anybody told me. I knew it with the hand, the bad one.*

*Tell the one who writes everything down that I should like to read it.*

*J.H.*

She had not written to be answered. She had said so, to a Placement Chair, across a desk, and had been rather proud of the sentence.

She found that it had been a lie of the tidying kind. She had wanted this for twenty years. She had only arranged matters so that she should not have to know it.

He had not said that he forgave her. She examined the card for that, as a wall-holder goes over a wall for a thin place, and it was not there. He had said that he fitted pumps. He had said that the hand was bad. And he had asked for something, plainly, of a girl whom he had never seen, since she had a thing that he wanted.

*I did not know to teach you that.*

It seemed that somebody had taught him to ask.

There were feet in the lane. There were a good many, and they were small.

She put the card away.

They had sent the juniors. There were fourteen of them, in their grey smocks, in a huddle by the first shed, and with them a bursar's clerk holding a proof sheet as though it might go off.

Kiva had come by the side gate with Anwen. She had not known that there would be children.

"The tenth edition," said Orla. She did not get up. "They've set the lane. Seven columns. They've put the seventh back, in the same type as the other six, and I'm told that it cost the printer a night's sleep to make it fit." She took the sheet from the clerk and held it out. "The bursar wants to know whether anything should be added. He asked me. I said that I wasn't the one who'd found it out twice."

Kiva took it in her right hand. Anwen, without being asked, held the lamp.

It was all there. *The lane with the crooked posts. At Meridian, under the south wall, on the line of the hill drain.* It had the nine posts, and the sluice, and the bar. *Call the turn before the eighth, aloud, and say who has the bar.* It had a collarbone, and a cellar in the Hythe.

And at the right, in print, with two initials that nobody in Meridian could put a face to:

*Why: because the road isn't straight. S.V.*

She had thought about that line a good deal in ten days. She had thought that when she understood it she would have something to say.

She did. It was about a plan that had been tidied by a helpful clerk, and a post that had been aimed and called crooked, and a girl who had gone east in the Pit and had nearly gone east again. It was about there being room on a bar for two. It had a shine on it that one could have shaved by.

She did not write any of it.

"There's a pencil in my coat," she said. "The left pocket. I can't get it out with this hand; the pocket's got a button."

"I know which pocket," said Anwen. She got it out.

Kiva laid the proof on the lid of Orla's crate. She held it flat with the heel of her left hand, and the last two fingers. The first two lay on the paper, and told her that there was paper.

She did not touch the last column. It had been right for five hundred years. It had only wanted the other six.

She wrote in the fifth, where it said *Went wrong,* under the collarbone.

*2680. The mark was called east, since east had served once before. East was the drain. The step was tried first, and it said so.*

And in the sixth, under *Second try:*

*Called north, aloud, before. Said back by two. Three others had the corner after six. Two had the bar. One had the count, from a chair. K.R.*

She read it over. It did not look like wisdom. It looked like a meter reading.

"You've not put what it cost," said Orla.

"It's in Vane's ledger. And Aske's card. And the green book." She gave the pencil back to Anwen. "I'd be putting it in a fourth place so as to have it where students could see."

"That's a reason not to."

"It's the reason I've got."

Orla looked at the two lines for some time. Her crooked face did something complicated, and settled.

"I asked you in the spring to show me six," she said. "I was rather pleased with it. I'd no notion what I was asking for." She folded the proof along its crease and gave it to the clerk. "That goes as it is. Tell the bursar that there's a hand in the fifth column that isn't a founder's, and that if he tidies it, I shall come up his stair."

The clerk fled.

The juniors had been edging down the lane. They had got as far as the fourth post. The sandy one with the plait was in front, and she had her eyes on the bar.

"Miss Dane says that we're to run it," she said. "Dark. From the sheds. She says that it's in the manual again." She looked at Kiva. "You said that you didn't know why it was crooked. I asked you. You said that you'd wondered for four years."

"I had."

"Do you know now?"

It was the plainest question that Kiva had been asked since a watch officer had held up a slate.

She could have told her. It would have taken about a minute. The child would have nodded, and gone round the post by her little sideways step, exactly as before, with a sentence in her head in place of a blank.

*Then they should go and find out,* somebody had once said, of students who laughed. Kiva had never heard it. She had only been walking toward it since she was thirteen.

"I know what it's for," she said. "I'd sooner you told me. Run it. From the sheds, in the dark. There are two things to say aloud before you reach the eighth post, and I'll give you the first. *Turn.* And which way."

"What's the second?"

"Who has the bar."

"*I* haven't got the bar."

"No. So you'll have to say who has. Before you get there. That's the whole of it."

The child thought about that. She looked along the fourteen of them.

"There's got to be two," she said slowly. "It's too long for one. Somebody'd have to be at the other end." Her face cleared. "Bettony. You're tall. You're the bar with me. Say it back."

"I'm the bar with you," said a long thin girl, in a voice of doom.

They went up to the sheds in a body, arguing about it.

Kiva walked back along the lane to the first shed, and stood under the lamp, where Orla had sat for twenty-one years. Anwen came with her. Neither of them stood at the post.

"You could have told her," said Anwen.

"I had it ready."

"I know. I watched you put it in your pocket." She was not smiling. She had the look that she wore when a wall had been gone over and had no thin place in it. She put the pencil back in the left pocket, and did up the button, without comment, as one does up a child's. "I told Mace on Thursday. About coming off a join. He'd it on his board already. He'd written, *Said so as she went.* He's left my name where it was."

"I knew that he would."

"You didn't. You hoped. They aren't the same."

"No."

The lamp hissed. At the far end of the lane, somebody small was counting three, aloud, for courage.

Kiva looked down at her left wrist. The cuff had come undone again. A button wanted an edge found, and a hole, and one thread told from two.

"Anwen," she said. "My cuff."

She said it before she had tried. It was not hard to say. That was what surprised her. She had hated it for a fortnight, and Pike had promised her a year.

"Give it here," said Anwen.

They came.

They came out of the dark at the sheds' end in a pounding, uneven line, fourteen pairs of boots on packed dirt, with nothing lit. They went by the lamp like a flock of starlings. A small, breathless, sandy voice went up ahead of them.

"*Turn! Left! Bar's me and Bettony!*"

"*Bar's me!*" That was the voice of doom. "*Say it back!*"

"*Turn left! Bar's them!*" said twelve others, more or less together.

Not one of them struck the post.

Two of them reached up to the wall. Kiva heard the ash pole come off its hooks, and a great deal of advice. She heard it go home in the socket. She did not hear it turn. They had been told that a gate was not to be shut on a dry night, and for once in the history of that School a junior had believed a thing because she had been given the reason with it.

"That's the first time that lane's been run to the bar since 2390," said Orla. She had her palm flat on the dirt beside her crate, as a woman feels for a tram. "I've sat here one-and-twenty years. I never thought to hear it."

Kiva did not answer.

She was looking over the bottom wall. It was a clear night, with a frost coming. Beyond thirty feet of brick the long hill went down, straight as a rule, with a lamp at every twentieth pace, to the Brickfields road and the river and the dark.

There was a card in her coat from a company, with *either of us* on the front of it and four names on the back. There was a line on a board about a Measure at Saltmere, in the third month. There was a pump-fitter on the south coast who knew a thing by his bad hand, and wanted to read what she had written down. Somewhere beyond all of them, a keeper whom she had never heard of had a book with a remarks column.

It was two hundred miles to the coast. She had seen the map on the wall of the watch room. The road went round the Sag, and doubled at a ferry, and followed a river that had never in its life gone where a clerk would have drawn it.

Down the lane the juniors had found that the bar would lift two of them off the ground if four more sat on the other end.

"Your cuff's done," said Anwen. "You may have your arm back."

She looked once at the post. It stood a pace out from its row, black against the brick, with a bar through it and six children hanging from the bar. It leaned to the left, as it always had.

It was not listening for anything. She could see that now. It had only ever been pointing at the place where the road turned, for as long as there had been somebody to say so aloud.
