# Chapter 33 — Kiva: Cause Written

Hollis Vane had read a great many things aloud in his life, most of them from a slate, in the open, to people with mud on them.

He had never read to the watch.

Seven of them sat round the oak table on Sunday afternoon, with the window at their backs and the black box open in the middle. In that room nobody had a name. It was an old courtesy and a useful one. A person was the Ferren, or the Weir, or the Lantern, for as long as the door was shut, and whatever was said was said by a stretch of water and a wall. Vane was the Sag. He had been the Sag since the spring, and he had not once been asked to open his mouth.

"Fourth column," said the Ferren.

She was the oldest of them, a spare woman of seventy with a river pilot's squint, and she had sat four-and-thirty years in that chair. She had her hands folded on the table like somebody waiting for the usual thing.

Vane turned the canvas book to the last leaf that had ink on it. He read the new line as he would have read a casualty list, flat and whole, without leaning on any word.

"*Warm, once. Wednesday the 29th of last month, at the Pit, a little before half past ten in the forenoon.*"

Nobody moved. He read to the end of it. He read the two crossings on a sanctioned mast, and *Cold since,* and the last line, which he had thought about a good deal on the levee road. *I did not ask it anything. It did not do anything. K.R.* Then he read his own small hand beneath it, since that was on the leaf too.

The room was quiet for some time. A barge went down the Ferren behind them with its engine thudding.

"Did you prompt?" said the Ferren.

"No. I told her what it would cost to write, before she took up the pen. Then I held my tongue."

"Did she ask you what the others had put?"

"She'd read what the others had put. Five hundred years of it. It's the bearer's right." He kept his eyes on the leaf. "She asked me one thing. She asked if there was a rule about how much she might write. I said, only what it did."

"Hm," said the Ferren, and looked at the page for a while longer. "Well. I've heard *nothing* read in this room every spring since I was six-and-thirty. I'd begun to think it was the plate's name."

"I've a motion," said the River Flats.

He was a broad, careful man, the youngest but Vane, and he had come with a paper folded in his cuff. He laid it on the oak.

"Recall. For cause written, as the first leaf has it. I'll give the cause, and I'll ask the Sag to hear it without taking it as said against him." He spoke slowly. "The bearer is seventeen. She's a student, provisional, on a sponsor's line, and the sponsor reported in her own weekly book that she cut an account to fit. The bearer's on a Circuit tape that opened to chartered parties this morning; there was a Halden man at the Pit's mast room before the keeper had his boots on. There's a house claiming a bag she tagged, and an Order writing to her aunt. And the plate has now done something. I don't say she's unfit. I say that the plate has never in five centuries been carried by anybody with so many people looking at her, and I'd have it lie in that box until the season's read. Then she may have it back, and welcome."

It was a fair motion. Vane had expected it, and had expected to dislike it more than he did.

"The Sag may answer," said the Ferren.

"I gave that hand a field direction," said Vane. "I wrote the reason, and I'd give it again. That's all I've got that's mine to say." He turned back the leaves to the first, and laid his finger on the print. "The rest is here. *Released to a bearer.* It doesn't say released to a bearer nobody's looking at. And it says *write down what it does.* She's the first in this book who's had anything to write, and she wrote it with a watch officer standing over her and seven of us to hear it. If we take the plate off her for that, the next one who feels it warm will have a very good reason to put *nothing.*"

"That's an argument for what's convenient to the book," said the River Flats.

"It is. I thought the book was what we were for."

The Weir laughed, shortly, and turned it into a cough.

They voted as the watch had always voted, with a hand laid flat on the oak for *aye* and a fist for *no.* The River Flats laid his hand down. So, after a moment, did the Lantern, who was a cautious woman and looked sorry about it. Five fists.

"Not recalled," said the Ferren. "Two for it, and I'd have the two written. A bearer ought to know how near a thing came." She unfolded her hands at last. "Now. We've not chosen her and we shan't begin. But a plate that does something is not the plate we've been sitting over, and I'll not have every clerk in the Compact deciding for himself what it is. Sag. Write."

He wrote, in the back of the book, where the watch's own lines went. She gave it to him a sentence at a time.

*Heard. Not recalled. The plate is held of the Line and borne by K. Rowan. It is not equipment. It is not an instrument, and it is not evidence. No hand but the bearer's is to be laid on it, to test it or weigh it or show it, without a vote of the seven.*

*The bearer will write in the fourth column within the week after any crossing, before a watch officer, whatever she has to put.*

"She'll be writing *cold* four times this month," said Vane.

"Then she'll be in excellent company, and she'll learn what the rest of them learned, which is to come and say so." The Ferren nodded at a letter that lay beside the box, on thick cream paper, with a school's crest. "That goes with it. The Crooked Line's bursar has seen fit to ask whether our plate and the one in his ninth edition are the same, and if so whether he may have the loan of it for the anniversary case. I'd be obliged if the Sag would answer him out of the fourth edition, word for word, and add nothing."

"*It is released to one who carries it,*" said the Weir.

"That'll do nicely."

There was one thing left, and Vane had been waiting for it.

"I asked leave to trace a line," he said. "In the fourth column, about the middle of the book. It's in the old night hand, and none of us can read it. There's a woman at the Compact Library who can."

"Whose line?"

"The bearer was a Thirza Bly. Received 2466. Her first column's in a fair hand. Her fourth isn't."

"She didn't mean it read, then."

"Or she meant it kept, and not noticed. I can't tell which until I know what it says." He looked round the table. "The first leaf tells every bearer to write down what it does. One of them did, in a hand her own watch couldn't read, and we've copied it forward for two hundred years like a stain on the paper. I don't think the watch can sit over a book it hasn't read."

Seven hands went flat on the oak. Even the River Flats did not hesitate.

"Trace it," said the Ferren. "And then go to Coper Street. That clerk's line has stood eleven years. *The Line owes that household an answer and has not got one.* We've still not got one. You may at least go and say so with your hat off."

He came on Sunday evening, at seven, and he did take his hat off.

Kiva saw him from the shop window, coming down Coper Street past the bakery with a slate case under his arm, and she had time to say "He's here" before the bell went. Mara did not get up at once. She finished the line that she was reconciling. Then she wiped her pen, and laid it in the tray, and went to let a Home Line watch officer into her kitchen.

She gave him tea. She gave it to him in the good cup, which was worse, and sat down opposite with her hands on the table and the page of the bearer's book in front of her. Kiva had brought it home on Thursday. It had been under the brass weight for three days.

"I've read it," said Mara. "*Guardian was asked, and said no. Appointment sealed regardless.* I've read that part rather often."

"I've come to say it's there," said Vane. "Not to defend it. Ilse Corran had the right by the first leaf, and she used it. The watch witnessed and did not choose. You weren't told that there was a rule that could go round you, and nobody came in eleven years to tell you that it had." He had not touched the tea. "I was on the river flats in 2669, with a slate of my own. I'd like to say I'd have come. I don't know that I should."

"No. I don't suppose you would." Mara's voice was perfectly even. "I told her no on that step out there, with my sister three weeks buried. I said the child had lost enough already, and I'd not have her given a thing nobody could explain. And she nodded, and said she understood me, and went away and did it." Her left hand moved on the page. "I've been angry with a dead woman for eleven years for not listening. It turns out that she listened. She wrote it down that I'd said it. I find I'm no less angry, and I can't any longer think her careless, and I don't thank you for that."

"I'd not expect it."

"Good." She turned the cup's handle a quarter toward him, which meant that he might drink. "Then I'll ask what I'd have asked her, if I'd known there was anything to ask. Every device in this house has a meter and a way to shut it down. What's that plate's?"

Vane was silent a moment.

"The meter's the book," he said. "It's all the meter there's ever been. As to shutting it down, the watch may call it in, on a vote, for cause written. That was moved this afternoon."

Kiva's hand went to her collar before she could stop it.

"It failed," said Vane. "Five to two. I'm bidden to tell you the two, so that you know how near it came." He took the half sheet from his case and read them the lines, in his outdoor voice, from *Heard* to *whatever she has to put.* "If the day comes that it carries, I come to this door myself, in daylight, with seven initials on a paper, and I put the paper in your hand before I ask for anything. That's not on the first leaf. I've added it under my own name, and the Ferren let it stand."

"*Not equipment. Not evidence,*" said Mara slowly. She had taken the half sheet and was reading it for herself, with her forefinger. "That's not for our comfort. That's so a house can't have it on a bench."

"It's so that nobody can. The School asked for it on Friday, for a glass case."

"Did they?" Something happened at the corner of Mara's mouth. "And I thought I'd been ill-used." She laid the paper under the weight, on top of the Order's grey letter and the Works' notice. "You may tell your watch that the household has had its answer, and that the answer's *we don't know either.* I'd sooner have had that eleven years ago. I'll take it now. I'm not forgiving anybody tonight; I haven't the time."

"She said you'd give me tea and not forgive me."

"Did she?" Mara looked at her niece. "Then one of us has been paying attention."

He stayed twenty minutes. He told Kiva what the watch asked of her, and she said that she would be writing *cold* four times, and he said that he had told them so. He told her that he had leave to take one old line to the Library, and he did not tell her whose.

When he had gone, Kiva washed the good cup.

"Perrin came while you were at the bakery," she said. "He's back from the Sag. He left the book."

It was on the dresser, the team book, with its green cloth cover. She had opened it on the landing, standing up, before she had her coat off. There were four names under the entry now, and the fourth was in brown ink, in an upright hand that she had known since she was nine. There was nothing else. Anwen had signed, and dated it, and had written no word beside her name.

"He says she's had the end of a wall since Tuesday," said Kiva. "Behind nobody. He says she didn't dip. She told him that he could tell me that, and that it wasn't a letter."

"And Kellan?"

"She said, *Good.* Perrin says she meant it, and that it took her about a minute to." Kiva dried the cup, and found that she was doing it with her right hand and holding it against her chest with the left, as she did most things now. "She read the counts twice. She didn't send anything."

"Then nothing's what she had to send." Mara took the cup from her and put it on its hook. "You'd a deal sooner she'd written and been cross. So should I. It isn't ours to order."

Selka Dray had brought tapes.

That was the first thing Kiva saw on Monday morning, in the Placement Chair's office, at eight. She had expected the pressed tunic and the red head held up. She had not expected three strips of paper pinned to a board, fair copies, in a clerk's hand, with the Chair's stamp at the foot of each.

"I asked for them on Friday," said Dray. She spoke to the long plain face behind the desk, as she had in the spring, and not once to the chair where Kiva sat. "I was told an objector might read what bore on her objection. They're from the travelling mast in the Old Yard. The fifteenth, the eighteenth and the twentieth."

"I know what they are, Miss Dray," said Ysra Crest. "I had them sent."

"Then you'll have seen the middle one."

She laid her finger on it. Kiva did not need to look. A step of two hundredths, and then a hump, round-topped, six hundredths high and a third of a second across.

"That's two doors open together," said Dray. "On a sanctioned instrument. She landed it. It was soft. It cost a third of what the others cost. Her reader says her own sponsor felt nothing wrong in it, and I believe him, because he's written that the sponsor would have signed." Her voice was quite steady. "In the spring I said she'd fouled a cell with her hand off the drum, and didn't know she had. I was told that was one day and one cell. Here's a second. She did the thing that ended Hart's hand at the Regional, and it felt like nothing, and nobody in that yard could tell but a first-year with a strip of paper."

Orla was sitting at the back, by the door, with her stick. She did not stir.

"I'm not saying take her off the roll," said Dray. "I said that in the spring, and I've stopped. I've stood on a shelf with her." It was the nearest that she had yet come to looking round. "I'm saying that the Regional has a Bench with live cells on it, and a floor with a rift at one end. I'm saying she can do that soft thing without knowing, and her sponsor can't see it, and the only thing in Meridian that can is a pen. And there isn't a pen on her in a lane."

Ysra turned her eyes on Kiva.

"Miss Rowan."

Kiva had an answer.

She had made it on the tram. It was about a called miss, and a reader who had stopped it, and a sponsor who had reported it in her own book on the Monday. It was all true. It would have sounded very well in that office.

"She's right," said Kiva.

Dray's head came round at last.

"I didn't know I'd done it. I thought it was only that I was pleased. I'd have gone home thinking it was the best I'd ever made." She kept her hands in her lap, the right over the left. "I've done two since that were the dear kind, and one of them I chose with the cheap one in plain sight. I can tell them apart now, in the air. I couldn't prove that to you. I can't weigh it. So I'd ask for what she's asking for. Put a pen on me. Every floor."

There was a short silence.

"That is not, I think, what you came in here expecting to say," said Ysra.

"No, ma'am."

"No." The Chair looked at her a moment longer, and then drew a sheet toward her and wrote on it, without haste, and read it out as she wrote.

"The objection is upheld in one part. *Rowan, K., provisional, shall open no second door on any Circuit floor save by a crossing declared to a count, and read from a sanctioned mast by a reader of this office, at the time. A stack so read is an incident.*" The pen went on. "*She does not stand at the Bench. On any floor with a contained rift she is seated in the recovery role only, as her certificate has it.* I've an appointment tomorrow with her sponsor and her reader which will settle who holds the pen. The seat stands, Miss Dray. I told you in the spring that I'd have no roster left if I unseated on unease. I have rather less unease before me this morning, and a good deal more paper, and you brought the paper."

Dray picked up her board.

"That's what I asked for in the spring," she said. "There wasn't a mast then."

"There was not. I'm aware of who paid for one."

They went down the Measure Hall stair at the same time, since there was only the one. Dray went first, quickly, with the board under her arm. At the turn she stopped, and spoke to the wall.

"We got our second step. Twenty-Eight. On the river flats, on the twenty-seventh. Fifty-three drams in the bag at forty-eight."

"I heard you had the seat."

"I lit after the cloth." It came out stiffly, like something that had been practiced and was not enjoyed. "Marten said *not yet* twice. I'd have had it dead on the first, and it would have gone in the river. I stood there with my blade out like a lamppost until he said. I've never been so angry in my life." Her jaw worked. "It held a hundred and four counts on the test tune."

"That's First."

"I know what it is." She went down three more steps. "I don't want you off the floor any longer, Rowan. I want you where I can see the needle. It isn't the same thing as being pleased you're there, and I'd not have you think so."

"I don't."

"Good," said Selka Dray, and went on down.

Noa told her about the Halden man that evening, in the lane, while he was threading the tape.

He had been at the Pit's mast room at eight on Sunday. Mr. Adlam had come at nine, with a glass and a pair of dividers in a morocco case, and had wished him good morning as if they had arranged it.

"He measured the cliff," said Noa. "Three times. He measured from the cliff to the notch, and he got twelve, and he said, *Eleven or twelve; you've the right of it, Mr. Bexley, and I shall say so tomorrow in front of whoever asks.* Then he asked the keeper for the wiring plan of the lamp ring." He pulled the tape through with his teeth. "He didn't ask me one thing about you. I'd rather he had. I've nothing to refuse him now."

"What did he write?"

"Two lines. I couldn't see them. He writes very small." Noa set the drum turning, and listened to it. "He's asked the Chair for leave to seat a recorder of the Works' own at the Regional, beside the Circuit's mast. It's a thing they use in the refinery yards. It doesn't draw what a fighter spends. It shows whether anything on a floor is keeping step with anything else."

Kellan had come in at the gate behind them. He had heard the last of it, and stood still.

"A step glass," he said. "My mother has one on her bench. It's for finding the cell in a bank that's fallen out." He looked as if he were adding a column. "They'd not carry that across the river to look at a lane."

"No," said Noa. "I thought that as well."

It was a quarter to eight, and the thread was cold, and the step behind her was clean. Kiva had a week's ration in her, and thirty-one hundredths out of a glass with four names on it, and there were ninety-four left when that was gone. She was standing with her toes on the scraped line when the others turned their heads.

Somebody was coming down the lane. It was a tall, upright figure in a grey coat, walking without hurry, and behind it came a clerk with a folding stool.

"Sit down, Ysra," said Orla, from her crate. She did not get up. "You'll find the spare one's got a nail in it."

"Thank you. I've brought my own." The Placement Chair had the stool set three paces from the mast, and sat on it, and folded her hands. "I've read four of these on paper. I thought that I should see one before I ruled on what paper can do. You'll go on exactly as you would have gone. I'm not here."

"You're as much here as that post," said Orla. "Mr. Cade. You've the word still."

"I've the word," said Perrin. He had been turned off the spare crate and was standing by the shed with his arms out from his ribs. He looked at the Chair of the Placement Board, and then at Kiva, and went rather red. "I'd like it known I'm not nervous."

"It's known," said Kiva.

She was. It sat under her breastbone like a swallowed stone. The woman on the stool had put a hand on a master stop in the spring and not pulled it. She could end a season with four lines on a sheet, and had written three of them that morning.

*That's the pen again,* she thought. *It's only a larger one.*

"One thing," said Ysra. "Before she goes. Orla, you'll oblige me by writing on your slate, when she's landed, what you think it was. A crossing, or the other. Before the boy reads. I shall do the same."

Orla looked at her for a long moment.

"That's fair," she said. "I'd have asked it in your place."

"Rowan, changing on three," said Kiva. "Ember, to still."

"*One.*"

She left the line.

It was the fifth time over that strip of dirt, and her body knew the road now, both roads, the dear and the cheap. The cheap one was there at the top, as it always was, like a known step on a dark stair. She shut the Ember. She waited for the last thread of metal to run home along her ribs and to arrive.

"*Two.*"

Nothing. A girl in the air with no doors. The ash went out of her all at once, like a breath, a quarter of a dram, and a woman on a folding stool was watching it go.

She set the pan.

"*Three.*"

The cross. No jar. The whole of the Old Yard came up under one heel and took her.

"Four." The ferrule, and the weight behind it. "Five. Six. Seven. Eight."

"Give it back."

She gave the ground its weight, and shut the still door with nothing left in the doorway. Then she turned, and held out her left hand before she showed anybody her face.

"Slate," said Ysra.

Two pencils scratched. Orla wrote one word and turned her slate face down on her knee. The Chair took rather longer.

"Mr. Bexley."

"Two hundredths," said Noa. His voice was not quite steady. "Then twenty-six, straight up. It's a cliff. Then three for the stance. Thirty-one."

Orla turned her slate. *Crossing.*

Ysra turned hers. She had written three words, in the level hand that Kiva knew from the foot of a notice board. *I cannot tell.*

"Nor could I have, a fortnight ago," said Orla. "I wrote that because I watched her wait at the top. I'd not swear to it against the tape."

"No. Nor shall you be asked to." The Chair put the slate away in her coat. She did not look displeased, or pleased. She was looking at Kiva's hand.

It lay in Orla's two palms under the lamp. The first two fingers were the color of candle wax, to the second joint, and Orla had begun to count.

Nobody spoke. Kiva watched them as she had watched them on the twentieth, two things that somebody had put down in an old hard hand. At thirty they were white. At forty-one, where they had come back before, they were white. A flush crept along the first nail at fifty, as slowly as damp comes up a wall.

"Fifty-eight," said Orla, and let her breath go. "That's inside the sixty. By two."

"It was forty-one last time," said Kiva.

"I can count, Rowan." Orla did not let go of the hand at once. "That's three. I'll say it, since it's so. Three, and three to show."

"Is that entered anywhere that I read?" said Ysra. "The hand."

"In my book," said Kiva. "And on Healer Aske's card. He sees it tomorrow, before your room."

"Then from tonight it will be on mine as well. I've had a boy lose one at the Regional. I was a ward-holder on that Bench. I held the wall that the two others were dragged behind." She rose, and the clerk folded the stool. "Nine o'clock, Orla. Bring the slate."

She went back up the lane as she had come, without hurry.

"Ninety-four," said Kellan, into the quiet. He had it written already. "Three more is ninety-three. There's still the hundredth." He shut the notebook. "I didn't know she'd been on that Bench."

"No," said Orla. "She doesn't tell it. She was twenty years on a Wren House ward line before she ever sat behind a desk, and she spent ten of them not speaking to me." She took up her stick. "I thought for a long while that it was because she blamed me. It was a good deal later that I learned she'd been asked to sign his entry, and had."

Healer Aske had the pin out at seven on Tuesday, and the towel, and a card with a new column ruled on it.

"Eyes shut. Say when."

She said it for the little finger and the next. Then she waited. She waited through what felt like a slow walk across a room.

"When."

"Three breaths. It was two, a month ago." The cold thumb moved. "And here?"

She did not feel the pin at all. She felt, some time after, that something had pressed.

"That isn't the pin you're feeling. That's my thumb." He put the pin in his lapel. "What was the count last night?"

"Fifty-eight."

"And the time before that?"

"Forty-one."

He wrote the two figures in the new column, one under the other, and sat looking at them. He was not a man who drew lines through things.

"What do you reckon the next one is?" he said.

"More."

"So do I. And I'd be guessing, and so would you, and we've both said it." He blotted the card. "They've come back. They've come back to where they were, and I'd not say a hair past it. You may have your go on Monday. I want that count brought to me, written, whatever it is."

"If I rested it," said Kiva. "A long while. Would it fill?"

"Rest is the only physic I've ever trusted." He handed her the card. "I can tell you what a week buys, because I've watched it buy less each time. I couldn't tell you what a month buys. Nobody's ever given me one to look at." He glanced at the clock. "You're wanted across the square at nine, I'm told, with half the district. Go on. Don't let them keep you standing."

She went out by the infirmary stair, with the card in her right hand.

It was a clear morning, and already warm. Across the square the doors of the Measure Hall stood open, and people were going up the steps by ones and twos. She saw a black coat with gold at the cuff. She saw a very small woman in a man's overcoat, with something under her arm on a chain, being helped up by a wide man with a grey mustache who had plainly never been inside the building in his life.

There was a bag in a cold room with a hundred and thirteen drams in it. It had her pencil on the tag.

She put the card in her pocket, and crossed the square.

# Chapter 34 — Kiva: On What Paper

The Placement Chair's hearing room had been built for quarrels between schools, and it had benches for forty.

There were not forty. There were perhaps five-and-twenty, and they had sorted themselves along the two sides of the long table as water sorts itself in a trough. On the left, by the windows, sat a pleasant man in a black coat with a clerk at his elbow and a flat leather case before him. On the right sat the wardens of Weighbridge Street. There were three of them. The lamp warden had put on a collar. Warden Cobb had not put on anything that she did not wear every day, and the ledger lay on the table in front of her at the end of its little brass chain.

Perrin's father sat behind her, on the wall bench, with his cap on his knee. He had not been asked. He had walked her up the steps and had not seen his way to leaving.

"That the bag go where such ash has gone for three hundred years, into a bank built to carry it. Perrin's father shifted on the wall bench, and Kiva heard his cap fall off his knee.

It was a fair offer. She could feel how fair it was. Everybody was to be paid, and the red tag was to become a matter of bookkeeping, and the only thing that would have happened on a wharf at four in the morning was that a bag had been a little delayed.

"Not for an instant, ma'am."

"The reference shelf wants a dram," said Halloran. She did not get up either. "One. I've nineteen years of cards, and every one that speaks on the seventh reed came out of a swept bank. This didn't. I can't card what I can't keep. I've no opinion on who owns the rest and I'd thank everybody not to ask me for one."

"Catcher."

Pike opened his book. He read the tag as it stood, in his flat voice, the wharf and the date and the rung, and at the end of it, *Red. Not for the ground. On a catcher's word and a comb.*

"Whose word?"

"Hers first." He nodded down the room. "She had the north hem. She told me before I'd finished saying *sealed.* I put my hand on it and felt nothing; I never have. Then I held the reed over it, and the reed knocked."

"Miss Rowan."

Kiva stood up. Her knee let her, though it was still strapped.

"What did you weigh?"

"Nothing, ma'am. I can't weigh it." She had been told to go on saying that, by somebody who had cause to know, and she found that it came more easily in a full room than it had on a wharf. "I'd my hand on the hem. It came up through the canvas, all of it together, like the hum off the tram sheds. I haven't an instrument for that. I'd not have asked anybody to hold a bag back on it."

"What did you ask?"

"I said it was keeping time. He held the reed."

"So that your word was a reason to look, and the comb was the looking."

"Yes."

"And the caution? You gave a tuner a caution from a patent, I'm told, across a trestle, at four in the morning."

"A friend had read it to me. *Not to be introduced to an unstepped bank under load.* I didn't know what six boxes on one landing were. I thought they might be a bank." Her face was hot. "I still don't know. I said so then."

There was the smallest pause.

"The Works would expect nothing else of Mr. Renn," said Adlam pleasantly. "It said so to him a week ago."

Kellan sat. His hands were shut on his knees. Kiva did not look at him, since it was the only thing that she could do for him.

"*One. The bag lies where it lies, in the Wren House cold room, under three seals: the Works', the wardens' of Weighbridge Street, and this office's. It is opened with all three present or not at all.*

"*Two. It is entered as made in Weighbridge Street on the 24th, off a call answered by the Graywater Company, catchers Pike and K. Rowan. Whoever comes to own it owns it with that written on it.*

"*Three. The street is owed its ground-first half, and has waited nine days. The casualty reserve will advance fifty-six and a half drams of plain stock to the wardens' account today. Whoever is found to own the bag will repay the reserve.*"

"Plain," said Mrs. Cobb.

"Combed in front of you, Warden, by anybody you care to name."

"I'll name her." The old woman pointed with her chin at Halloran, who looked resigned.

"*Four. One dram is drawn for the reference shelf and one for the Halden technical office, under the three seals, and no more. A thing that nobody may test is a thing nobody will ever agree about.*" Ysra turned the sheet. "*Five. The Works' claim under its patent is entered. It is not ruled on. The Works may take it to the Compact's court, where it belongs, and I expect that it will.*"

"It will, ma'am," said Adlam.

"I'd think the less of it otherwise. Now the part that is mine." She took a fresh sheet. "*Six. A catcher of any step, on any Circuit floor or any shift a Circuit team is attached to, may tag a bag red on grounds stated. A bag so tagged is combed before it is panned, and the tag goes on the slate under the catcher's name. A catcher's word is sufficient to withhold. It is not sufficient to prove. It may stop a bag. It may not name one.*"

Pike's square head came up a little.

"*Seven. Until the court has spoken, no ash that speaks on the seventh reed above a trace is to be issued, bought, burned or settled on any floor of this Circuit. Every dram that goes through the gate at the Regional Measure will be combed there, the houses' with the rest.*" She looked up. "Its yard has. I can't answer for what comes out of the Kiln, ma'am. Nor, I think, can you."

"No," said Ysra. "I can't. It's why I've written the sixth."

She let them go at ten, all but four. The wardens went down the stair in a body, with the lamp warden carrying a paper that had a figure on it. Mrs. Cobb stopped in front of Kiva on her way to the door.

"Nine mornings," she said. "I've been up to Two-C every one. The light comes on when the door's opened. It's not so much as winked." She looked Kiva up and down, as she had on the wharf. "I'd have known whose door to knock at. I'm rather pleased not to have had to."

She took Perrin's father's arm and went out.

Noa stood up. The roll of tape slid off his knees and he caught it.

Kellan Renn had never liked the refinery gate.

It was iron, and thirty feet high, and it had the Works' device on it in gilt, a hand with a flame standing in the palm. He had walked under it with his mother's dinner every school holiday since he was eight. When he was nine, he had waited outside it for four hours on the day that they nearly took her stamp away, and he had counted the rivets. There were six hundred and twelve.

He counted them again on Tuesday afternoon, out of habit, while the porter found his name. They had it on a list now. *Renn, K. License team. The yard, Tues. and Thurs.*

The grading room was at the top of the old still house, under a skylight, with no lamp in it and no cell within thirty paces. His mother was alone at the long bench. She had a tray of pinches in front of her, and the comb, and the brass seal on its cord, and she did not look round until she had finished the cup that she was on.

"You've been in Crest's room," she said. "Your collar's wrong. You pull at it when you've stood up in front of people."

"I said what the foreman said. About his kitchen."

"I thought that you might." She held the comb over the next cup. Nothing spoke but the third reed, a thin clear note, and under it the hair that was in everything now. "Was it true?"

"I was four paces from him."

"Then you'd no choice in the matter, and I don't know why you've come up three flights to tell me." She wrote a figure. "Sit down, Kellan. You've your father's way of standing about."

He sat on the stool at the bench end. On the shelf over her head stood the jars, forty or fifty of them, each with a batch number and a date in her narrow hand. He knew which one was the eleven months. She had never moved it.

"Your seal's at the foot of their claim," he said.

"My seal's at the foot of the reading. I held a reed over a pinch that a Wren House woman brought across the river in a cold tin, with two wardens watching her, and it knocked. I wrote *Step, full,* and I sealed that. I'd seal it again." She laid the comb down. "I didn't seal *whose.* I've never sealed whose in two-and-twenty years. That's written by people on the second floor, and they put it over my seal because my seal is what makes them believed."

"Doesn't that trouble you?"

"It troubled me a great deal when I was your age." She looked at him at last. She had his eyes, or he had hers, and she was not smiling. "Then one morning a man came up those stairs with a tin that was stamped First, and it was soot. He told me to put my seal under it, and I wouldn't. They were eleven months finding out which of us was lying. I learned that if I only ever put my name to what the reed said, there'd be one thing in this building nobody could make me unsay. It's a narrow sort of safety, and I've never found another."

"I told Adlam I'd say what I saw to anybody who asked."

"And what did he say?"

"That they'd expect nothing else. He's said it twice now. Once to me, and once in front of the Chair."

"Then he's cleverer than the man with the tin." She took up the next cup. "It doesn't make him your friend. It makes him a person who's understood that he can't use you for anything but cutting. I should cut very well for them, if I were you, and be exactly as much trouble as you were this morning, and no more."

He went down to the yard at four.

It was a bigger floor than the Kennel's, paved in blue brick, with a lattice house of its own at one end and a ward line in black standing easy along the wall. They were paid. One could tell it by how they stood. There was a string in the lattice already, a six-knot, grey and patient, and a square man with a foreman's slate was reading out rungs.

The boy called Pettit was at the rail, lacing his cuff. He was a second-year, fair and heavy in the leg, and he nodded as one nods to somebody who has been given half one's dinner.

"They've given you my rung," he said. "The top. I'm to carry."

"I didn't ask for it."

"I know. Birch decided on your feet. He told me so to my face; he's that sort." Pettit pulled the lace tight. "I'd like to mind it more than I do. I watched you light after the foot on Thursday. I've been lighting on the way up since the thaw, and nobody ever told me it was wrong, since the tin always came in."

"It isn't wrong. It only prints, if your catcher's close."

"Ours kneels at eight paces. That's the yard's rule." Pettit looked at him curiously. "How near does yours kneel?"

Kellan thought of a north hem, and a voice that said *not yet,* and a bound that he had once spent dark because of it.

"Near enough to be asked," he said.

There was a jacket on the peg with his name chalked over it. It was black, and good cloth, with a narrow gold cord at the cuff. He put it on. It fitted him across the shoulders as nothing he owned had ever fitted, and he stood for a moment with his arms a little out, like Cade in his strapping, and disliked how much he liked it.

Then the foreman said his name, and he went to cut.

The second letter came up from the hill house on Wednesday, in the same thick grey paper, by the same boy.

Mara read it standing at the dresser. Then she brought it to the table and laid it down between them, and for once she did not put the weight on it.

"I answered two of their six," she said. "Whether you'd had it more than once, and whether you'd been ill each time. I said yes, and yes. I didn't think they'd a right to the rest." She sat. "This is what they've sent back. It isn't a question. You'd better have it in their words."

It was written in the small, plain, old-fashioned script, and it was not signed. The Order, it seemed, did not sign.

*The house thanks the guardian for two true answers and does not press her for the four.*

*It offers this. The reader may come up the hill as a guest of the house for a year. She would live under our rule, which is silence at table and one door. Every other is kept shut, and we know how to keep them shut; it is the only thing that we have studied. We ask nothing of her in return, and we should teach her nothing that she did not ask for. It is how the house has always kept a thing from being let in by accident.*

*One of us sat near her in a room. She saw the reader's left hand upon the table and knew it. Our old wall-holders carry theirs so. Such a hand is not burned. It is spent, and it fills with a long rest and with nothing else that we have found. Some of ours have come back whole in a year. Not all.*

*We do not say that she ought to come. We say that the door is there, and that it would be shut behind her gently.*

Kiva read it twice.

It was a kind letter. That was what she had not been ready for. She had expected to be wanted for something, as the Works wanted a bag, and here was a house on a hill that had looked at two fingers lying on a table and had offered her the one thing that Healer Aske had said he trusted.

"A year," she said.

"They've had a good deal of practice at years."

"I asked Aske yesterday. Whether rest would fill it. He said that it was the only physic he believed in, and that he couldn't tell me what a month would buy, since nobody had ever given him one."

"So they're not lying to you."

"No."

Mara waited. She had her left hand flat on the table, and her right in her lap, where she generally kept it.

Kiva looked at her own. The first two fingers lay beside the others, a little straighter, a little apart. She could see them perfectly well. They were slow to tell her that the table was there.

She did not have an answer ready. She noticed that, and it frightened her rather more than having one would have done.

"If I went," she said slowly, "I'd come down in a year with ten fingers and one door. I'd not be on a roll. There'd be three counted on a mast and three never shown, and Orla's name on a line with nothing under it. And the next time a foreman had nobody south of a string, there'd be nobody." She stopped. "That's the good reason. It's a very good one. I can hear how good it is."

"And the other?"

"I want it." It came out small. "I want the six. I want to find out what the third one does. I've wanted to know what I am since I was thirteen, and I'm nearer than I've ever been, and I'd be going up a hill to be kept from it by kind people." She made herself look up. "I think I'd be choosing to keep the fingers so as not to have to find out. I don't know that that's right. I know it's what's true."

Mara did not say anything for some time.

Then she turned her right hand over on the table, palm up, where the lamp could reach it. Kiva had seen the scar ten thousand times. It ran white and shining from the heel of the hand to the roots of the fingers, and in the middle of it there was a place the size of a coin that had felt nothing since a cell on Linden Row.

"Nobody offered me a year," said Mara. "I'd have been told to rest it if there'd been a soul to tell me, and I'd a shop, and a girl of fourteen in the house who had just been told there wasn't a column for her. So I went on, and I taught the other hand. I've never known whether it would have come back." She closed the fingers. "I'm not going to tell you that I'd have refused. I don't know. I'm telling you that I know what it is to go on with a piece of yourself not answering, and that it can be done, and that I'd not wish it on you for all the doors in Meridian."

"That isn't advice."

"No. I've none. You'd not have asked me for it a year ago; you'd have told me what you'd decided, very clearly, with reasons." She took up the letter and folded it along its crease. "I'll write that the reader thanks the house and keeps its door in mind. I'll not write *no.* I'd leave a thing like that on the latch."

She put it under the brass weight after all.

Kiva wrote that night, in the right-hand column, under the date. *The Order offered a year's rest and one door. Aske thinks rest would help and can't say how much. I'm not going. Why: I want the six and the third door more than I want the fingers sure. I may be wrong. Nobody told me to.*

She read it over. It did not look brave. It looked like a meter reading.

The Great Floor was the largest room that Kiva had ever stood in.

She had sat in it. Everybody in Meridian had sat in it, on some holiday or other, up in the banked oak tiers that held four thousand, to watch the Regional's lanes. She had never before come in by the fighters' door at the north end and walked out onto the boards with nobody in the seats. It was Monday the ninth, and her week was up.

It was an oval, sixty paces long. The floor was not boards. It was oak blocks set on end, like the paving of a quiet street, black with two hundred years of wax, and when she stepped on it something answered from below.

"It's hollow," she said.

"It's sprung," said the Chair's clerk, who had the keys. "There's an undercroft. It was a cistern before the Compact had the building."

A brass stud the size of a penny was let into the blocks near the middle, and four paces from it somebody had laid a strip of white tape. On the rim above the south tier stood the Circuit's own mast, a great dark thing with four-and-twenty pens, and Noa was up there already, a small figure bending over the one that had been set to her.

Ysra Crest sat in the front row, by herself. Orla had been given a chair on the floor.

"The step first," said Orla. "As ever."

Kiva stood on the tape. She pushed off, a curb's height and a pace, and shut the Ember at the top, and set the still door under her foot before the wood.

It boomed.

The whole floor boomed, like a hand laid on a drum. The stance went down through the blocks and found a room underneath where the ground ought to have been, and for half a count she had her weight on nothing. Then it caught, somewhere lower, with a lurch. Orla's ferrule came against her shoulder and she went back a full step.

"That's not clean," said Orla.

"No."

"Then there's no go. That's the first rule, and I didn't make it for yards only." She turned her head. "I'm sorry, Ysra. You've come down for nothing."

"I've come down to see a rule kept in front of me when it was inconvenient," said the Chair. "I shouldn't call that nothing. When?"

"Tomorrow. If the step's clean."

Kiva stayed when they had gone. The clerk wanted his dinner, and said so, and she asked him whether he would show her the undercroft first.

It was down a stone stair behind the north tier. He held a lantern up for her, grudgingly, and she stood at the bottom and looked along a low brick vault that ran the whole length of the hall. It was dry, and it smelled of dust. Square brick piers stood in rows down the dark, each as thick as a tram's wheel, with oak beams across their heads.

"How far apart?"

"Four paces. Each way."

"Which one's under the stud?"

He did not know. They paced it out together, from the foot of the stair, with the lantern. The stud was not over a pier. It was in the very middle of four, with two paces of nothing under it in every direction but a beam.

*What's behind you,* Rhea Sorn had asked, in a van. Kiva had learned that one in a street, at a cost that was not hers. It had not crossed her mind until this afternoon that a floor might have a behind as well, and that it might be underneath.

"Could the mark be moved a pace?" she said. "Over that one."

"Moved?" The clerk was shocked. "That stud's been there since the Compact was signed. They put the litter on it. Nobody moves the floor for a fighter, miss. Not at the Regional."

"No," said Kiva. "I didn't think so."

She walked the blocks for an hour in her ordinary boots, with nothing open. She stood on the stud and rocked. She learned where the beams ran by the sound, and where the four piers stood round her, a pace and a half off at each corner, like the weights in the hem of a sheet.

That was what did it. She had been setting the still door as she set a pan, flat and under her, and waiting for the ground to come up and meet it. There was no ground. There were four corners. A sheet did not lie on its middle.

On Tuesday evening Kellan was not there. It was the first go that he had missed. Tuesday was a refinery yard now, and Perrin had the tally, in a notebook of Kellan's, with strict instructions.

The step was clean. She set it wide, out to the four piers and down them, and the floor hummed under her and held.

"Rowan, changing on three. Ember, to still."

Her voice went up into four thousand empty seats and came back a little late.

"*One.*"

Up. The Ember shut. She waited for it, at the top, for as long as it takes to say a short word, and the ash went out of her like a breath.

"*Two.*"

Nobody. A girl in the air over a cistern.

She set the pan by its corners.

"*Three.*"

Her right foot came down on a brass stud, and the Great Floor rang once, deep, like a struck tank. The bound went in at her heel and out four ways, along the beams and down the brick. She felt each pier take its share. Then it was quiet, and she was standing on it.

"Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight."

"Give it back."

She gave it back to four corners, and shut the door.

"A cliff," said Noa's voice, from very high up. It cracked. "Thirty-one. And there's nothing on the other pens. Not a hair. I've three-and-twenty of them idle and I've watched them for a slow thirty."

"So it wants more than a great many pens," said Kiva.

"Or it wants a ring of lamps that's dead. Or it was only ever the first." He leaned over the rail. "I still can't choose. I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Write it."

"Four," said Orla. She had the hand already. It was white, and she was counting, and she counted for a long time. The Chair came down out of the front row and stood where she could see. At sixty, Orla's mouth went thin. The first flush came at sixty-eight.

"Seventy-five," she said at last. "That's past my sixty. So it's Aske before the yard, by my own rule, and he'll have the count in writing."

"Sixty-three left in the glass," said Perrin. He had written it very large, and underlined it, and held the book up so that nobody should doubt him. "Two more is sixty-two. And one hundredth. I'm told that I'm to say *and one hundredth* every time."

Ysra Crest was still looking at the hand.

"I'm content that it wasn't the yard," she said. "I'm rather less content with the rest." She put on her gloves. "Forty-one, fifty-eight, seventy-five. I can add, Orla."

"So can I," said Orla. "So can she. It's why she's the one who's to bring it to the healer."

# Chapter 35 — Kiva: Six

Healer Aske wrote *75* under the other two on Wednesday, and looked at the three of them for longer than he had looked at her hand.

"I don't draw lines through figures," he said. "I've known healers who do. They get very fond of the line." He put the cap on his pen. "You may have next Tuesday. It'll be worse. Bring me the count."

It was ninety-five.

She made the fifth in the Old Yard on Tuesday the seventeenth, on packed dirt that she could have found in her sleep, and there was nothing new in it but who was missing. Kellan was at the refinery. Perrin had the notebook. The step was clean and the thread was cold, and she went up and shut the Ember and waited, and was nobody, and paid, and came down on the cross with the whole yard under her heel. Noa read a cliff. Orla said, "Five," and took the hand.

They stood under the lamp for a slow ninety-five while two fingers came back from wherever it was that they went. Nobody made a joke. Perrin wrote *thirty-two* in the notebook and did not hold it up.

"One go," he said quietly. "And a hundredth."

She brought Aske the count on Wednesday morning. He wrote it in the column and said nothing whatever, and passed her for the twenty-fourth.

It was a strange month to be seventeen in. The whole of her work was one bound in seven days. Round the edges of it, everybody that she knew was going somewhere. Kellan cut a rung on Tuesdays and Thursdays in a black jacket. Perrin's Hall had taken the top row of strapping off him and let him carry a sandbag up a stair. The third team finished its card on the fourteenth with a win at home, eight to seven, and Jude Talley shook everybody's hand afterward like a man closing a shop.

She went to find him on Thursday, in the Low Lane, when the others had gone.

He was sitting on his upturned bucket with the litter poles across his knees, rubbing oil into them. He did not have to. They were the school's poles, and he would not be carrying them in the autumn.

"I've come to ask you something," said Kiva. "I'd sooner you heard all of it before you answer."

"That sounds like Pryce."

"It is, rather." She sat on the bench under her peg. "Seven's entered for the Regional. Sixth by yield, attached. Kellan's cutting for a Halden four that week; he's on our book still, and it isn't a transfer, and he's the one who said to ask you. So I'm not going behind him. We've no blade. You've a second step and no team." She kept her hands still. "I'm asking whether you'd stand our Kill. I don't know what you'd want for it. I thought I'd ask that as well, and not guess."

Jude went on with the oil for a moment.

"Has he told you why he said me?"

"He said you're the only Ember in the school who ever told him he was wrong and was right."

"I told him his feet were in love with themselves. He was fourteen." The chipped tooth showed. "He went away and fixed it in a week, and I've not been able to touch him since. It's the worst advice I ever gave." He laid the pole down. "I'll tell you what I'd want. It's three things, and you won't like the third."

"Go on."

"My name on the yield line. Not *reserve.* I've had a yellow card in my tunic for two years, and I cut a rung at the Pit as somebody's turned ankle. I'd like to be written down as a person." He held up a second finger. "A head's share of whatever Seven tins that day. I'm not on your book and I don't ask to be. But I'm a third-year with no team, and if I'm to go before a draft board in the spring I'd like to have earned a dram somewhere that wasn't a favor."

"That's fair. It wants four signatures. I can't promise them. I can ask."

"I know you can't. You've got better at knowing that." He held up the third. "And I'm not Renn. I want that understood by whoever sets my mark. He's four-tenths at the top. I'm nearer a second, and I come down heavy. If you put the cloth where you'd put it for him, I'll print it or I'll miss it. You'll have to set the mark for the blade you've got."

"A second's what Rhea Sorn takes on a sixth rung."

"She's cutting through a paving stone. I'm only slow." He grinned, briefly. "Who's your wall?"

"I don't know yet."

"Pryce is back on Sunday. The whole school knows it; her mother's had the bunting out."

"I haven't written her name on the sheet," said Kiva. "I've left the line bare. It isn't mine to fill in."

Jude looked at her for some time, with his head a little on one side, as he had looked at her on her first day, across a quench box that somebody had left at her peg.

"No," he said. "It isn't. I'd not have thought you knew it." He picked up the pole again. "Tell your four I've said yes, on the three things. And tell Renn that if I meet him in a lane I shall go for his feet."

Anwen Pryce held the end of a wall for the last time on Saturday night, at the low arch, with the Sag behind her and nobody in front.

It was not a great wall. It ran from her two hands eleven paces north to a ward-holder called Tibb, who had held walls for nine years and hummed while he did it. Beyond Tibb there were four more. It had stood since six. Nothing had come at it, and nothing was expected to. Mace stood walls on a quiet culvert as other men oiled a hinge.

She had learned, in four weeks at the end of it, what an end was.

In the middle of a line a wall was held on both sides. One leaned a little on the neighbor to the left and a little on the neighbor to the right, and the weight went along like a plank laid on trestles. At the end there was a neighbor on one side and the night on the other. Whatever came, came there first. Nobody had told her that the hard part was not the weight. It was that there was nobody to tell her whether she had dipped.

*If it dips, I'll hear it,* Mace had said. He had not said that he would tell her.

At ten the relief came down the bank. It was a big, good-natured man called Stiles, six years on the line, who had relieved her every night for a week. He brought his wall up inside hers as it was always done. She leaned on it, and waited to feel it take the weight, so that she could let go of nothing.

It did not take it. It was there, silver and broad, and it was a hand's breadth short of her own.

She had about a breath in which to decide. He was six years her senior. Every hand on that line would have let go and trusted him. She had been told by Greta Stroud, in a hall kitchen, with her own hands in linen, what it sounded like when a join went wrong, and she had no wish to learn it from the inside.

"Short," said Anwen. "Stiles. You're a hand short on the outside. I'm still holding."

There was a pause in the dark.

"So I am," said Stiles, mildly surprised. The silver moved. She felt it come up under her own, snug, like a second shelf beneath a first. "Now?"

"Now," she said, and leaned, and let go, and there was no sound at all.

Mace was behind her. She had not heard him come. He had the board under his arm, and he was looking, as he always looked, at her boots.

"Six years," he said, "and you told him."

"He was short."

"He was. He's short one night in five, at that end; his shoulder's been wrong since the weir. I've waited a week to see whether you'd say so or be polite." He wrote on the board. "Most are polite. Then there's a join, and somebody's in linen."

She did not know what to say to that. She said nothing, which she had found to be what he liked.

"Stroud's back on Monday," said Mace. "So I've no seat for you, and I'll not pretend to one. I've a card. It says *P., end of wall, low arch, four weeks. No dip. Told a six-year man he was short.* It goes in a drawer that I open in the spring, when I'm asked who I'd have." He tucked the board away. "Come and see me when the Regional's been read. Not before. I'd as soon see what you do with a floor that isn't mine."

He went up the bank.

She walked back to the hall along the top of the berm, by herself, with her arms aching. There were three half sheets in her coat now, under the list. She knew them by heart, and she took them out anyway at the hall steps, under the lamp.

The first was the cheap one, and a thing that Lowen Brask had said in a passage. The second was the Weighbridge. *Cloth's down. Lure.* She had read those three words in a culvert with the rain coming in sideways, and had been so angry that she had nearly dipped. It was not the anger of somebody who had been wronged. It was the anger of somebody who has said a thing twice, plainly, and then watches it happen to a stranger's leg.

The third had come on Thursday.

*Mon. the 2nd. Dray's objection, before the Chair. She had the tape of the stack. I'd an answer made on the tram. Did not give it. Said: she's right. Why: she was.*

And lower, after a ruled line:

*Wed. A letter from the hill. A year's rest and one door, and the fingers might fill. Am not going. Why: I want the six and the third more than I want the fingers sure. May be wrong.*

Anwen had read a great many reasons in that square pencil hand. She had been reading them since they were nine, on the backs of laundry tickets. They had always had a kind of shine on them. They were the reasons of somebody who had worked out beforehand what a sensible person would think, and had then thought it.

There was no shine on *I want.* It was not even a very good reason. It was only what was so, put down by somebody who had stopped tidying.

*That's the first one,* she thought. *I've waited eight years for that, and it's come by a runner, on a half sheet.*

She was still not done. She examined that under the lamp, as carefully as she would have gone over a wall for a thin place, and it was there. It had altered, though. It had been a fist, six weeks ago. Now it was more like a hand held out flat, palm down, to see whether a thing would bear weight.

Seven would want a wall. She knew it as she knew the route of the tram. There was a Regional in the autumn, and Finch had a card that said *lamp,* and there would be a kitchen table and a green book and three faces turned toward the stair when she came up it.

She would not go up it.

She folded the half sheets. She would go home tomorrow, and unpack, and hang her blue on the back of her own door, and wait. If she was wanted, she would be asked, properly, as Mace asked for a wall, with the reason and the ground and what was behind it. And if her name was already written on a sheet when she got there, she would know that six weeks had taught one of them something and the other nothing, and she would take her card to the Wren House and stand somebody else's Bench.

It was a hard thing to decide about a person one had folded sheets with since the age of nine. She found, going up the hall steps, that she could hold it without flickering.

Kiva did not go round on Sunday until four.

She had seen the tram come. She had been at the shop window, not by accident, and had watched a girl in a brown coat get down at the Coper Street stop with a slate-blue bag and be seized on the laundry step by a mother with flour to the elbows. There was bunting. Jude had not exaggerated.

She gave them the afternoon. Then she went through the passage to the drying yard, and knocked on the gatepost, which she had never done in her life.

Anwen was hanging shirts. She had her sleeves rolled, and her arms were browner than they had been, and thinner. She looked round at the knock, and Kiva saw her notice it.

"You've never knocked on that," said Anwen.

"No."

"Come in, then. Mind the basket."

Kiva came in as far as the pump. She had not practiced. She had found, on the way through the passage, that there was a speech trying to assemble itself, and she had counted it down as she would have counted a door.

"I've something to ask you," she said. "It's for Seven, and it's a thing a team asks. It isn't a thing a friend's owed. I'd like to say all of it, and then you needn't answer today."

Anwen put a peg in her mouth, and took it out again.

"Go on."

"There's the Regional. Friday and Saturday, the sixteenth and seventeenth of the ninth month. Four boards. We're entered sixth, under Sorn's hand, attached. Jude Talley's said yes to the blade, on three conditions that want four signatures, and yours is one. Perrin carries if his Hall's healer passes him to the shoulder. I catch. I'm barred the Bench by the Chair, as of the second." She drew a breath. "We've no Hold. Finch has a card that says a corner and a lamp. I've not written anybody on that line. I'm asking whether you'd stand it."

"And what's behind it?"

It was the question. Kiva had hoped that it would be, and had been afraid of it.

"A Bench with live cells, that I can't go near, so it'd be you and Perrin and Jude, and you'd be leading it. Lanes, against people who've read my tapes. A floor with a rift at one end, and Vane or somebody like him downing one of us in the middle of it. And me, with a go a week and a hand that's taking longer each time." She made herself finish. "I've an answer if you say no. It isn't a good one. Finch stands as a lamp, and we give up the Bench and fight the lanes a wall short, and we come about twelfth."

"Why are you telling me the bad answer?"

"So you'll know I've got one. So it isn't—" She stopped. "I didn't want to ask in a way that only had one end."

Anwen pegged a shirt. She did it slowly, and squared the shoulders of it on the line.

"Mace asks like that," she said. "He tells you what the ground is. Then he tells you what he'll do if you say no, and it's always something he could bear." She took up another. "Who taught you?"

"You did. On the laundry step, with my bag in your arms. I didn't know it was a lesson until about a week ago."

"It wasn't. I was only cross." There was something at the corner of her mouth. "I'll tell you on Wednesday. At your table, with the book open, in front of the others. I'd like to see one first."

"See what?"

"A crossing. I put my name to two drams on the second of last month, on three conditions. I've had the counts. I've never once watched the thing I paid for." She looked at Kiva directly. "Tuesday, isn't it? The sixth. I'll stand at the gate. I shan't say anything. I'd not be allowed to, by my own condition."

"It's the last. There's ash for it and a hundredth."

"I know. Perrin's told the whole street." Anwen bent for the basket. "Go home, Kiva. You asked properly. I'm not going to spoil it by answering quickly."

There were thirty-two hundredths in the glass on Tuesday night, and Perrin had worked out what that meant and could not stop saying it.

"A crossing's thirty-one. That leaves one. An *off* is three; I've looked it up, it's the bound and a tremble. Thirty-two less three is twenty-nine, and twenty-nine isn't thirty-one." He was sitting on the spare crate with the notebook on his knee. "So if she calls it tonight, we've two counted goes that'll never be six, and I shall have to go and ask four people for two hundredths of a dram. I'd like it known that I'd rather lift the plate."

"It's known," said Orla. "Now be quiet. You've the word, and you'll need your breath for it."

The lane was very full of people who were being quiet.

Noa was at the mast. The Chair's clerk had come down, with the stool and without the Chair, to see the sixth read. And at the yard gate, just inside, with her back against the post and her hands in the pockets of her brown coat, stood Anwen.

Kiva had the step behind her. It had been clean. She laid two fingers of her right hand on her breastbone.

"Cold."

"Then you may have it."

She stood on the scraped line. Four paces off, under the leaning post, there was a cross in the dirt with five sets of scratches in it.

She was aware of the glass. It was a mile away, in a locked press in the Measure Hall basement, and she could feel it as she could feel a pan at her elbow in the dark. There was one in it. There was not a miss.

"Rowan, changing on three. Ember, to still."

"*One.*"

The struts closed, and she went up.

The cheap road was at the top. She had expected it. She had not expected it to have grown.

It had never been dangerous on a Tuesday before. It had been a known step on a dark stair, to be walked past. Tonight it had a figure on it. *Eleven.* She could do it in eleven hundredths. She knew exactly how, as a hand knows how to pass a cup, and it would land like a cat on a cushion, and there would be twenty-one left in a glass with four names on it. Nobody would have to be asked for anything. A girl at a gate would see a foot come down on a cross, soft and sure, on the one night that she had come to look.

It was the first time that the stack had ever come to her dressed as thrift.

*That's an answer,* thought Kiva. *It's standing ready. It's got a reason and everything.*

"*Two.*"

She shut the Ember.

She waited for it. She waited while the last thread of metal ran home along her ribs, and because there was no miss in the glass she waited to be certain that it had arrived, a hair longer than she had ever waited. It was not long. It was long enough for the top of the bound to go by.

Then she was nobody, falling.

The ash went. A quarter of a dram, all at once, like a breath, and she was already coming down, with the cross rising faster than it had ever risen and nothing under her.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the pan. She threw it under herself as a catcher throws a sheet at a heap that has already begun to lift, wide and ahead and a little too late.

"*Three!*"

Her right foot hit the cross.

It was not soft. It jarred her from the heel to the hip, a hard, honest knock, and for the quarter of a count that mattered she did not know whether the yard had taken her or only struck her. Then the ground was there. It was under the whole of her, as wide as the lane. The left knee had felt nothing.

"Four," she said, through her teeth.

The ferrule came. Orla leaned as she would have leaned on a stuck door. It went through, and down.

"Five. Six. Seven. Eight."

"Give it back."

She gave it back. She shut the door on an empty doorway, and turned round, and held out her left hand, and only then let herself breathe.

"Mr. Bexley," said Orla. She had the hand, and had not yet looked at it. "Before I say a word."

Noa had the tape against the lamp. He was a long time over it.

"Two," he said. "Then it's flat. It's flat for nearly two-fifths of a second; that's the longest gap I've ever drawn for her. Then it goes straight up. Twenty-six. A cliff." His voice wobbled. "Then three. Thirty-one."

"One hundredth," said Perrin, in a voice of awe. "There's one hundredth in that glass. I'm having it framed."

"Six," said Orla.

She said it quietly. She stood in the lane, with a girl's hand in both her own, and looked at the cross.

"That was the worst of the six. You came down on it like a sack. And I'd ask for that one before I asked for the other five, because you had every reason in that glass to be quick, and I watched you be slow." Her crooked face was grave. "I told four people in this lane that they weren't buying six landings. They were buying six I'd ask for twice. They have them."

Then she looked down, and began to count.

Kiva could see for herself. The two fingers were white to the knuckle now, not to the second joint, and the white had a bluish look at the nails that she had not seen before. They lay in the old palm like two things made of tallow.

Sixty went by. Ninety. She heard the gate creak; somebody had taken a step forward and stopped.

"A hundred and thirty," said Orla. Her voice was rough. "There. There's the first of it." She did not let go. "I've had my six. I'm bound to say what I've bought with them, and I'll say it in front of the clerk."

She took a folded paper from her coat. It was Kiva's certificate. It had three lines on it in the square capitals, and room beneath.

She wrote standing, against the mast's leg, and read it out as she wrote.

"*Added, the 24th. One crossing, Ember to still, low, to a mark. Declared to a count. On a sanctioned mast, read by the Chair's reader. One in seven days, the left hand seen by a healer the next morning. Not within three paces of open ash or any charged cell.*" The pencil went on. "*Not certified: a stack of any kind. Any third door. Anything I have not seen six times.*"

She signed it.

"Now hear what that isn't," she said. "It isn't mastery. She can do one thing, one way, at a walk, on a week's rest, and she's hurt after it. It doesn't say why a quarter of a dram goes out of her with nothing open; none of us knows, and Bexley's stopped guessing. It doesn't make the soft one right because the soft one's cheap. And it doesn't say what that hand will be in a year." She folded the paper. "It says it wasn't luck. That's the whole of it. I've waited since the spring to be able to write that, and I find it's a smaller thing than I'd pictured, and I'd not give it back."

The clerk had it all down. He closed his book and went away up the lane with his stool, and the yard let out its breath.

Kiva had her hand again. The fingers closed late. She looked at the cross, with six sets of scratches in it.

"And three?" she said.

"I wondered how long you'd be." Orla lowered herself onto her crate. "Sit. All of you. Pryce, you may come away from that post; you've seen what you came to see. I'm going to say this once, slowly, before anybody in this lane uses a word they don't know the meaning of."

Anwen came down the lane. She did not sit. She stood behind Perrin's crate, and her eyes went to Kiva's left hand and stayed there.

"Three doors can be had three ways," said Orla. She held up a finger. "All open at once, and fighting, with nobody choosing. That's what she did in the Cellhouse with her palm on a drum. I'd call that a competition. It costs you a line on your chest, and whatever's near takes the print of it, and it calls things." A second finger. "All open at once and agreeing. That's a stack. It's soft and it's cheap and it feels like being good at something. For as long as it lasts you're three things with no wall between them, and anything in reach can walk in. I put a boy on a Bench in that condition." A third. "Or one after another. Each shut before the next is opened, with nothing of yours open in between. That's a sequence. It has two walls in it, where a crossing has one. So it has two quarter-drams, and two of whatever it is that goes through that hand."

"Nobody's done it," said Noa.

"Nobody's written that they did. I've come to think those aren't the same." She looked at Kiva. "You've an order in your head. I can see it sitting there."

Kiva had. It had come to her on a wharf, some time after four in the morning, kneeling at a hem with one hand, and she had not known what it was until tonight.

"Hand, Ember, still," she said. "It's the trade. A ward takes a thing as it falls. That's the catch. The bound takes it somewhere else; that's the carry. And the stance sets it down." She heard how tidy it sounded, and went on more slowly. "I don't know that it works. I've never passed anything out of a ward. Mine splash."

"They do. That's the first thing that'll go wrong." Orla did not smile. "I'll not have it tried on a notion. Write me a page. The order. Where every limb is at each of the three. What it costs, to the hundredth. Who may stop it, and at which moments. And every way you can think of that it ends early, with what you do when it does." She pointed the stick. "Then take your page to the four who'd be paying. I've no glass. You've a hundredth."

Kiva read the page aloud on Wednesday, at the kitchen table, to five people and a brass weight.

She had written it three times. It was one side of a sheet. *Order: hand, Ember, still. Catch, carry, plant.* She read out where her feet would be on the line, and that the ward would be raised at the left shoulder for one tap and no more. She read the price.

"A ward for a tap is two hundredths. Then the gap. Then the bound, two. Then the gap again. Then three for the stance." She did not look up. "Fifty-nine. Call it six-tenths of a dram, each time it's done whole."

"Six-tenths," said Perrin.

"Who may stop it: Orla. Perrin, with the word. Me, in two places, after the ward and at the top. And the thread, before I've left the line." She turned the sheet. "How it ends early. The ward splashes: I stand still and take the shove as myself. The Ember isn't clean: I call it, and roll. The thread warms in the air: I land, and say so, and it's a miss whatever it looked like. Any white before: no go." She laid it down. "That's the page. I'm not asking for anything tonight. I'm reading you what it costs so that you can say no to it."

Nobody spoke at once. Mara had gone down to the shop and shut the door, which was her way of saying that it was not her book.

"I'll go first, since I've least right," said Kellan. He had come in his own coat. Kiva had noticed that. "I'm cutting for another four in the autumn, in the same lanes. I'll sign whatever three of you agree to, and I'll not vote on a ration for a thing I may have to stand against. And I don't want to be told the count for it. I know her two-door count. I can't unknow that. I'd as soon find out the rest on a floor, like anybody else." He turned to Kiva. "I'll take no head's share of what Seven tins that week, either. I shan't be on your cloth."

"That's handsome," said Perrin.

"It's only arithmetic."

"Then I'll go," said Anwen.

She had the green book in front of her. She had read the whole of it since Sunday, every entry, and Jude Talley's three conditions on a loose slip.

"The Hold line. I'll stand it." She said it to the book, and then lifted her head and said the rest to Kiva. "On terms. I'm asked on the floor every time, as you asked me in the yard, and never taken as read. I call the gate. And I'm not done. I've looked. But it'll bear weight, and I'd sooner stand on it than wait until it's handsome."

Kiva found that she could not say anything. She nodded.

"As to the six-tenths," said Anwen. "I saw a hand last night. I counted with her; I got a hundred and twenty-eight. I'll not vote ash for two of those in one go until I've seen what a miss does to it. That isn't no. It's *not yet,* and I know whose word that is."

"And I'm no," said Perrin.

He had gone red. He had his big hands flat on the table.

"I mean it properly. I've said *I mind* about everything since the spring, and voted yes, and it's got to be a sort of joke. This isn't that." He drew a breath as far as the strapping would let him. "That eight and four-tenths came out of Weighbridge Street. My dad had his shoulder under a leg for it. There's a child's cupboard that ran on a biscuit tin till noon. And we're to put near on two drams of it up a mast, for one of us, again, for a thing nobody's ever seen, when Talley's never once cut on our cloth and I haven't lifted anything heavier than Gerald since the twenty-ninth." His voice shook a little. "I voted for the six. I'd do it again; I've seen them. But I can't find the yes for this one, and I'm not going to pretend I looked harder than I did."

It needed four. Everybody at the table knew it.

Kiva had the speech. It was a good one. It was about a foreman with nobody south of a string, and it would very likely have turned him, since he was Perrin and hated to be the one in the way.

She put it where she had put the one for the laundry yard.

"What would you put in its place?" she said.

He blinked.

"I thought you'd fight me."

"I've got the fight. It's in my pocket with Kellan's list." She pushed the pencil across. "It's your street. What ought it to buy?"

He took the pencil as if it might be hot. Then he wrote, in his round careless hand, and they went through it line by line, and it took an hour.

*Entered, Wednesday the 25th. In hand: 8.4 credited, 1.6 held, 0.01 from the practice glass. Total 10.01.*

*Kennel floors, three, for the four who stand the Regional: 2.1.*

*Regional reserve, half a dram a fighter: 2.0.*

*Returned at par to the wardens' account, Weighbridge Street, for the Pellow's boxes: 1.0.*

*Held: 4.91.*

*Talley, J., entered as Kill for the Regional Measure, by name, with a head's share of that week's yield. Renn, K., takes none.*

*Three-door, K. Rowan: not carried.*

Four of them signed it. Kellan signed last, without reading the top half, and blotted his name.

"The medical's still sealed," said Anwen. "Two drams, ward-trace. It isn't training ash. I'd like that written, in case anybody's tempted in the eighth month."

"Write it," said Kiva.

When they had gone she sat on at the table with her own book. She had a ration, half a dram on Mondays in a glass thimble, and a step every night that took two hundredths of it. She did the sum. A ward and one gap and a bound came to thirty. She could afford the first half of the thing out of her own week, once, and nothing else.

*Three-door line, not carried,* she wrote. *Why: it's the street's ash, and Perrin's right that it is. I had a speech. Didn't give it.*

She looked at that. Then, underneath:

*So: the first half. Hand to Ember. Off my own ration. One a week. Find out if a ward can be passed at all, before I ask anybody for anything.*

# Chapter 36 — Kiva: Catch, Carry, Plant

Perrin hung Gerald from the shed hoist on the first Tuesday of the new month, and was very particular about the knot.

"He's a hundredweight," he said. "I've had him on the Hall's scale. From there," and he chalked a mark on the shed door, at the height of his own knee, "he comes in about as hard as Keld's shoulder. From there he's Moss. I'd not go higher than Moss on the first night. He's had a hard life, but he's got no judgment."

The sandbag turned slowly on its rope. Somebody at the Hall had inked a face on it since the spring.

The rope ran over the hoist beam and back to a cleat by the door. Let go, the bag swung down across the head of the lane and came through the scraped line from the left and a little behind, at the height of a shoulder. Kiva had stood on that line for twenty minutes while Perrin adjusted it, and had been struck twice, lightly, as herself.

"The step first," said Orla.

The step was clean. The thread was cold. She had a week's ration in her and nobody's ash but her own, and she said so aloud, for the mast, since Noa copied every tape to the Chair.

"Hand, to Ember. One crossing. It isn't on my paper. It doesn't count toward anything."

"It does not," said Orla. "Say what you're going to do with your body."

"Feet on the line, square to the cross. Left shoulder to the bag. Ward up at the shoulder, curved, for one tap. I shut it. Then the struts, and one low bound to the mark, and I come down on the Ember, as I'd come down from any bound."

"And if the ward isn't shut?"

"I stand still and take him as myself."

"Mr. Cade."

"On three," said Perrin. He had the rope in both hands. "And I'd like it noted that I've got the word *and* the bag. If I call *off,* I can't call him back. He's committed."

There were two people by the yard gate who had not been there the week before. Anwen stood with her back to the post, as she had on the twenty-fourth. Beside her, on an upturned pail, sat Pru Finch, who had been told that there would be a sandbag and had come to see it.

"*One,*" said Kiva.

Her hands came up. The ward stood between them, tray-sized, faintly silver, and she bent it, as she had bent it a hundred times in that yard, into something like the bowl of a spoon.

"*Two.*"

Perrin let go of the rope.

She heard Gerald come. She did not look. The ward was at her left shoulder, and the bag walked into it with a hundredweight of sand behind an inked grin, and the ward rang like a struck pan.

It held. That was the first surprise. It did not stop the bag. It turned it, as the curve of a spoon turns water, and the whole of that shove came round her shoulder and into her back and pointed her down the lane.

*Now shut it.*

She shut it. She brought her hands together and let the silver go, and reached for the cuff.

"*Three.*"

The metal ran down her sides. The struts closed on her calves—

And the bottom thread on her breastbone went hot, like a wire laid across a stove.

"*One!*" It came out of her before she knew what she was saying. "Off, off, off—"

She let go of everything. The struts sprang back. Gerald's shove was still in her, and with nothing to take it she simply went, three stumbling steps down the lane with her arms out, and sat down hard in the dirt a pace short of the cross.

The bag swung back, and forward, and Perrin caught it against his chest.

"Hand," said Orla.

It was pink. Kiva held it up, and it was pink, and it was the other thing that was wrong. There was a line of heat from her breastbone to her collarbone, sharp and familiar. She had not felt it since the spring.

"That was a jam," she said. Her voice shook. "I shut the ward. I *shut* it."

"Mr. Bexley."

"Two for the ward," said Noa. He was frowning at the tape. "Then there's a hump. Nine hundredths. It's the mouse up the curtain again. That's the Low Lane's eighth try to the hair." He looked up. "There wasn't a gap. The first one never went down to nothing. There's a little shelf on it, about so high, all the way to where the second starts."

"A shelf."

"As though there was a bit of it left on."

Kiva looked at her hands.

She had brought them together. She had felt the ward go, in her palms and her wrists and the last two fingers of each hand. She had not felt it go in the first two fingers of the left. She had not felt anything there at all. They had told her that the ward was shut exactly as they told her that a cord was crossed, which was to say that they had reported nothing, late.

"There was a rim," she said slowly. "At the edge. Where these two were. I couldn't feel that it was still there."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then that's a thing I couldn't have taught you," said Orla, "and I'm glad you found it with a sandbag. Eleven hundredths, and a line you'll feel till Friday." She turned her head toward the gate. "Miss Finch. You've been sitting on that pail like somebody with her hand up. Out with it."

Pru went scarlet.

"It's only that she doesn't put it out," she said. "I didn't like to say. She lets go of it. Everybody does; they hold a ward, and then they stop holding it, and it sort of falls off them. Mine used to. It's why they splash."

"And what do you do?"

"I finish it." Pru got off the pail. She came down the lane a little way, and held up her round hands, and a ward the size of a tea tray stood between them, humming. "I don't let go at all. I fold it. From the far edge, in toward me, like a cloth. That side first, then that, and when it's as small as a penny I shut my hands on the penny. So I always know where the last of it is. It's in my palms." The ward went out between one word and the next. "It's the only thing I'm any good at. Sorn said so. She said it was why my name was on her card."

Kiva was still sitting in the dirt. She looked up at a first-year who had run from a spaniel.

"Will you show me?" she said. "Slowly. I'd need to do it without those two."

"Without—" Pru looked at the hand. "Oh. Then you'd fold toward the others. Toward your little finger. And you'd keep those two right out of it, straight, so there's nothing for a rim to sit on."

"Passengers," said Kiva.

It was a catcher's word. A square man had said it to her in a hall yard, with a groove worn across his palm. *I took the cord the way I could hold it on my worst day.* She had learned that for a cord, and it had not once occurred to her that a ward had a grip as well.

They stayed until ten. Pru folded and Kiva folded after her, forty times, with two fingers held out stiff. It felt like wearing somebody else's hands. By the end of it a ward would go out of her as a cloth goes into a drawer, and the last of it was in her palms, where she could feel it.

Anwen had not said a word all evening. At the gate, going out, she stopped.

"The hand was pink," she said.

"It was a jam. There wasn't a gap."

"I know. I'm saying that I looked." She went on up the lane with Pru, who was already telling her about the spaniel.

---

The next Tuesday the bag came in at Keld's height, and Kiva folded the ward from the far edge to her palms with two fingers standing clear of it.

"*Three.*"

She knew that it was shut. She had it in her hands like a coin.

For the length of a short word she was nobody. That was not new. What was new was that she was nobody going sideways, with a sandbag's shove in the bones of her back and nothing whatever to tell it what to do. The ash went out of her all at once, a quarter of a dram, like a breath.

Then the struts. They closed on her calves, and they did not push. They had nothing to push with; she had not bent a knee. They only caught what was already there, as a gutter catches rain, and turned a stumble into a bound.

It was low and long and a little crooked. She shut the Ember at the top out of habit, and opened it again on the way down, and landed on the cross on her struts, as she would have landed from any Stride. The knee held.

"Hand," said Orla, before she had straightened.

It was white. Both fingers, to the knuckle, and the nails blue.

"Mr. Bexley, while I count."

"Two. Then flat. Flat to the line." Noa's voice had gone up. "Then a cliff. Twenty-six. Then two for the bound. Thirty." He swallowed. "That's a crossing. Hand to Ember. Nothing dropped."

"So a ward can be passed," said Kiva. She found that she was shaking. "It went into the bound. I never pushed off."

"I saw your knees. They were straight as rails." Orla did not look up from the hand. "Be quiet, now. I'm counting, and I don't like where I've got to."

She got to two hundred and forty. It was four minutes by Noa's watch before the first finger flushed. Anwen had come away from the gatepost at ninety. She stood in the lane with her arms folded hard across her chest until it was over.

Healer Aske saw it on Wednesday morning, and wrote the figure down, and laid the pen across the card.

"A ward is made in the hands," he said. "So whatever it is that goes, goes through there twice as hard, and I ought to have guessed as much. Tell me what the whole of this thing is. All three."

She told him. He heard it out with his eyes on the window.

"Two of those in one go," he said. "Then that's a fortnight's worth, and I'll have a fortnight after it with nothing. Every time. A single one, a week, as before. And no white on the day, not a trace, or you go home." He wrote it on the card in full. "I'm setting it where you can read it. I can't make you mind it. I've noticed that you do, which is why I go on troubling."

She took the card to the drying yard that evening, since there was a thing that she was bound to tell them.

"Aske's ruled," she said. "A fortnight after any go with two gaps. So it's fewer than I wrote on the page. Between now and the Regional there's room for three, and that's if nothing's white."

Perrin was sitting on the mounting block with a pencil behind his ear. He had been very quiet for a fortnight.

"I've got a thing," he said. "I've been at it since the first."

He took a slip from his pocket. It was a receipt, in a hard black pencil that pressed through the paper. *Recd. of T7, one dram, plain, combed. For the boxes. E. Cobb, Warden.*

"She sent that up by my dad. He says she wrote it standing on the Pellow's step and made him wait." He turned it over. "So the street's had its dram. And you've gone at that bag twice out of your own thimble and sat down in the dirt, and you never once asked me to change my mind. I kept waiting for you to. I'd got my answer ready and everything."

"Had you?"

"It was very good. It had my dad in it." He scratched his jaw. "I can't use it now. So here's the other. A dram and eight-tenths out of the held, for three whole goes, and the misses out of the same. And I keep the word. If I call *off* on it two nights running, it's done till the season's read, and nobody argues with me, not Dane either." He looked round. "I'm not saying I've stopped minding. I'm saying I'd sooner be the one with his hand on the brake than the one who wouldn't let the cart out of the yard."

"That's a better line than mine," said Kiva.

"I know. I'm keeping it."

"I'll put a condition," said Anwen. She had the green book open already. "The *why* goes in before each one. Not after. What it's for that night, and what it's likely to cost her, written and read out on the line. There was a man on a levee who had a column for that and somebody tidied it away."

"E.K.," said Kiva.

"If I'm paying for a hand, I'll have it priced first."

Kellan signed on Thursday, in the passage outside the grading room, with the top half of the page folded under. Held stood at three and eleven hundredths.

---

The first whole one was on the fifteenth, and she called it off herself.

She had the ward folded. She had the first gap behind her, and the struts had caught, and she was in the air with a thing done that nobody had written down in five hundred years. She was so astonished to be there that she listened for it. It was a quarter of a breath. A strut clung.

"*Off!*"

She came down on the right leg, rolling, and the Old Yard hit her in the back. Thirty-one hundredths, and ten minutes of white. It was the oldest fault she had, and it was almost a comfort to meet it.

The second was on the twenty-second, and it was worse, because it landed.

It landed beautifully. The ward folded, and the gap came, and the bound; and at the top the cheap road was waiting, as it would always wait, and she was tired, and the hard part was done. The metal was nearly home. The pan wanted setting. She felt one thing begin as the other ended, as smoothly as a seam.

She came down on the cross like a cat on a cushion. Orla's ferrule went through her and into the ground.

"One," said Kiva.

She said it standing there, with the stance still open and the stick against her shoulder.

"That was the cheap one. At the second. I stacked it." She gave the ground its weight and shut the door. "The thread warmed at the top. I felt it and I went on. It's a miss."

Nobody moved. On the spare crate Perrin had his mouth open, and no word in it.

"Mr. Bexley," said Orla, very quietly.

"Two. A cliff. Two." Noa turned the tape to the lamp. "And then a hump. Six, with a round top. Then three. Thirty-nine." He looked at Kiva with something like wonder. "You said it before I'd found the place."

"I looked at my feet," said Kiva.

"So you did." Orla took her stick back. Her crooked face was doing something complicated. "On the eighteenth of the sixth month you made that same fault and went home thinking it the best of your life, and a boy had to tell you. Tonight you told the boy. I've been wondering since the spring what I was teaching you. I believe that may be it." She held out her palm. "Hand."

It was twenty minutes. The fingers were dull until the next morning.

There was a dram and ten hundredths left of Perrin's line, and one Tuesday before Aske's fortnight ran into the Regional.

---

Anwen read the *why* on the twenty-ninth, from the green book, standing on the scraped line beside her.

"*Tuesday the 29th. Three-door, whole, the third try. For: to find out whether it can be done at all, once, before a floor. Likely cost: both fingers, some hours. Possibly the hand. Fifty-nine hundredths.*" She shut the book. "That's what's written. Is it still what's so?"

"Yes."

"Then I've nothing to say. I'll be at the gate."

The step was clean. The thread was cold, and there was no white.

"From Keld," said Perrin, at the rope. He was pale. "On three."

"Hand, to Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on three, and on five."

"*One.*"

Her hands came up. Two fingers stood clear.

"*Two.*"

The rope ran. She did not look.

Gerald walked into the ward, and it rang, and the curve of it took his whole weight round her shoulder and aimed her at the post.

"*Three.*"

She folded it. Far edge. Near edge. A penny, in her palms. Shut.

Nobody. Sideways, in the dark behind her own breastbone, with a hundredweight of sand in her spine. The ash went, like a breath.

The struts caught it. She was up, low and long, and the metal had hardly closed before she was shutting it again, at the top, and this time she did not listen. She waited. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs and arrive. It was the longest thing that she knew how to do, and she had now to do it with one gap already paid and her left hand gone cold to the wrist.

"*Four.*"

Nobody, again.

It was different the second time. She had not known that it would be. The first gap had been a girl with a shove in her. The second was a girl with the same shove and nothing else left, like a purse turned out twice. The ash went out of somewhere that had already been emptied. For an instant she thought that there would not be enough of her to set anything.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the pan. Wide, ahead, by its corners.

"*Five.*"

Her right foot came down on the cross.

There was no jar. The yard took her. And then, a moment after, something else arrived that was not her. It came in at her heel, slow and heavy and a little stupid. It went down through the packed dirt and spread there, under the crooked post, four paces from where it had struck.

It was Gerald. It was the whole of a sandbag's swing, set down in the ground like a full pan on a table.

"Six," she said. Her voice was a thread. "Seven. Eight. Nine."

"Hold." Orla had not touched her with the stick. She had her palm flat on the dirt beside the cross, like a woman feeling for a tram. "I've got him. I can feel him under my hand. Go on."

"Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen."

"Give it back."

She gave it back, and shut the door on an empty doorway, and stood.

Behind her the bag was swinging to and fro against Perrin's chest, light as a lantern, with nothing left in it.

"Mr. Bexley," said Orla.

"Two." He could hardly get it out. "A cliff. Twenty-six. Two. A cliff. Twenty-six. Three." He put the tape down on the drum case with both hands. "Fifty-nine. There are two walls in it. You could cut yourself on either one."

"One," said Orla. "One, of three." She got up off her knees with the stick, and it took her some time. "I'll say what I saw, and no more. A ward took a blow. A bound carried it four paces. A stance put it in the ground, and I had it under my palm for a slow eight. Nothing was open between." She breathed. "I've taught for one-and-twenty years, and I was the best part of twenty on the Line before that. I never saw it, and I never met anybody who had."

Kiva had not held out her hand. She could not find it.

It was there; she could see it, at the end of her arm. It was the color of a candle from the wrist down. Orla took it in both of her own and did not begin to count.

"There's a thing more," said Noa. His voice was odd. "It's on her pen. After the second cliff, when she's shut everything and it's flat. Eleven seconds on, there's a notch. A hair." He looked from one of them to another. "I've one pen. If she so much as twitched, that's all it is. I can't tell her from the mast with one pen."

"I didn't twitch," said Kiva. "I don't think. I couldn't swear."

"The tram lamp winked," said Perrin. "The one at the foot of the lane. I thought it did. I was looking at her, not at it."

"Then it isn't entered as fact," said Orla. "Write it at the side, Bexley, in whatever you call the hand that you use for doubts. Rowan. Is that thing at your neck warm?"

Kiva put her right hand to her collar.

"Cold."

"I thought it would be. Sit down before you fall."

---

Orla Dane sat on in the yard after they had gone, as she had sat on after most things that mattered.

The mast was packed. The bag hung from the hoist with its inked face to the shed. Cade had walked the girl home with her arm across his shoulders; he was passed for it, to the shoulder, and had mentioned the fact several times. Her hand had not come back. It had not so much as begun.

*One.*

She had said, in the spring, *Show me six,* and had been rather pleased with the sound of it. It was a good rule. She had not foreseen that it would meet a healer's arithmetic. A go a fortnight, and each one emptying a hand for longer than the last. Six of those was a year. It was very likely more hand than the girl had.

So there would be no six. Not of this. There would be one, on a tape with two cliffs in it, and a Regional in seventeen days.

She turned that over, and looked at what was underneath.

It was a Regional. It had not escaped her. She had last brought a candidate with more than one door to that floor twenty years ago, certain of him, with a sum worked out to the last figure and a reason for every step. She had explained each drill to him beforehand. He had never once surprised her, and she had taken that for trust.

*What's different?*

She made herself answer it as she would have made Rowan answer, with no shine on it.

The girl was not at the Bench. That was Dray's doing, and Crest's, and Orla had been glad of it in that office and ashamed of how glad. There was a pen on her, held by a boy who had twice told a Torch that she was wrong. There was a word that a Stonehand lad with cracked ribs could say three times. There was a page with the cost written before the go, by a ward-holder who had not finished being angry and voted anyway.

And the girl had stood on a cross a week ago, with a stick against her shoulder, and said *one.*

Jessamy had never said *one.* She had not known that it wanted teaching.

It was not safety. She had stopped expecting that there would be a morning when it was. It was a meter and a shutdown path and a book, as a woman in a work apron had told her months ago, standing beside the spare crate, in two sentences.

There was a letter. She had written it in the seventh month, in the square capitals, and had sent it care of a pump-fitter's yard on the south coast that a Saltmere officer had once named to her. It had four lines in it. *There is another. She writes everything down. I did not know to teach you that. O.D.* It had not been answered. She had not written it to be answered.

*What do I say, if I'm asked?*

She would be asked. She had sat on enough floors to know it. There was a foreman at every board, and a string at one end of the last of them, and in a bad spring nothing kept its time. Somebody with a slate would turn to the rail and say, *Sponsor. What's she passed for?*

"Recovery," said Orla aloud, to the post. "Dark hands. One crossing, Ember to still, to a mark, on a week's rest."

And the other?

*I have seen it once. I have seen it missed three times, and the last miss she called before her reader did. It costs six-tenths and a hand. It is not on her paper and I'll not put it there. Cade can stop it. So can I. So can she.*

That was all of it. It was not much to say across a floor. It had the merit that every word had happened.

She would not spend the week of the sixth on another, nor the thirteenth. Aske's card forbade the one. The other she would forbid herself. A hand bought a yard or it bought a floor, and she was done pretending that she might have both.

She took up her stick. The post leaned over her in the dark, split and black, with its two iron bands, as it had leaned over every teacher who had ever sat there and wondered what it was for.

"You're no help," said Orla, and went in.

---

The hand came back on Thursday, at about noon, forty hours after it had gone.

It came back to less. Kiva knew that before Aske told her. The first finger found the pin after four breaths. The second did not find the pin at all. It found that something had pushed.

"That's not the same nerve reporting late," said Aske. "That's a different one reporting something else. I'd not say it's finished coming back. I'd not say it hasn't." He wrote. "Nothing till the thirteenth. You know that already."

She went from the infirmary to the Home Line hall, since it was within the week.

She had been eight times now. She knew which stair creaked. The Weir had witnessed her twice, and the Lantern once, and Vane the rest, and each time she had taken the steel pen and written one word in the fourth column, under her own long entry, with the date. *Cold. Cold. Cold.* It went down the page like a drip. She had rather come to like it.

Vane had the book open when she came in. There was a half sheet beside it, in a clear round hand that she knew.

"I've had this since the second week of the seventh month," he said. "The watch wanted it read to all seven before it was read to you. They sat on Sunday." He turned the book back, forty leaves or so, to a place that he had marked with a straw. "Bly, Thirza. Received 2466. You'll not be able to read her fourth column. Nor could we."

It was a single line of bent pins, in brown ink.

He read her the half sheet.

"*Warm, once, after the first crossing. I have crossed nine times since. Cold. I put this in the night hand, that it may be kept and not made much of. T.B.*"

Kiva read the round hand for herself, twice.

"She crossed," she said. "She was a bearer, and she crossed."

"So she says. She doesn't say from which door to which, or how many she had. There's nothing in her *why* but that she was a levee catcher and the bearer before her liked how she kept a count." He laid the straw back in the fold. "Somebody on this Line knew a word for it in 2471. I've wondered where they had it from."

"It was warm for the first. And never again."

"In thirty-five years."

She took up the pen. She wrote *Cold,* and the date, and under it, because the first leaf asked it of her, *After three doors in sequence, once, on a sanctioned mast. Cold.* Vane witnessed it.

She had thought, on the tram, that she would mind. She had carried the thing for four years waiting for it to say something, and it had said one word, once, and a dead levee catcher had just told her that it would not say another. She found, going down the stair that creaked, that she minded about as much as one minds a clock for not striking between the hours.

---

Mara sat her practical on the seventh of the ninth month, in the examiners' room at the Works, with her left entered as her reading hand.

She came home at four with a paper in her bag. She did not say anything. She went down the hall to the dead panel, and took off the tag that had hung there since the spring, and read it once. *QUENCHED 13TH. NOT REFILLED PENDING REVIEW.* Then she fetched a can and her tools, and Kiva stood at the kitchen door, well back, and watched her aunt fill a cell and tune it, slowly, with the wrong hand, which was now the right one.

The panel lit. It was a small, steady, yellow light. It had been dark for the best part of four months.

"He said the tune was a hair slow," said Mara. "He said he'd rather have it slow from somebody who knew which hand she was using. He's a dreadful old man." She wiped her fingers on her apron. "You're still not to touch it."

"I know."

"I know you know. I only like saying a thing that I'm licensed to say."

They had the three Kennel floors in those weeks. Jude cut on Seven's cloth for the first time, and came down heavy, and took a full second at the top. Kiva set his mark a pace short of where she would have set it for Kellan, and they sealed at forty-seven, and forty-five, and forty-four. Perrin was let carry Gerald out of a tank on his shoulder. He told Gerald beforehand.

Kellan came to the shop on the Wednesday before the Regional. He would not come up.

"I'll say it on the step," he said. He was in his own coat. "The draw's posted. If you get past Brask, and I get past a Saltmere four, it's us in the afternoon. I'm going to use what I know."

"I'd supposed you would."

"I want it said, all the same. I know your count. I know you declare a change on *three* and make it on *three,* because the rule says you must. I know there's a moment at the top when there's nothing of you open and you can't so much as lean." He was looking at the doorpost. "I told you to shut it there. I'm the one who knows it's there."

"It's the only place to hit me."

"Yes." He let out a breath. "I'll not go near Cade's ribs. That isn't a number; that's a hurt, and I've told Birch so, and he said it was my lane. The rest I'm going to take. I'd not think much of either of us if I didn't."

"Kellan."

He looked at her.

"Go for the gap," said Kiva. "I'd sooner lose to you than be let off by you. I've been let off. It's worse."

Something eased in his face. It was not a smile.

"I'm told Talley's coming for my feet," he said.

"He says they're in love with themselves."

"They were. It was the best advice I ever had." He went down the step. "Friday, then."

On Thursday night Jude chalked it on the Low Lane's brick, for the five of them, as two boxes and a great many arrows.

"Sixteen fours. Twelve by yield and four houses. Four boards." He tapped the first box. "Friday, the Great Floor. The Lane from nine, knock-out, three carries, Circuit rules, and rule nine for anybody who changes, which is to say for her. The Bench at five, when the lanes are down to their last two. Pryce leads that; Rowan sits in the tiers and doesn't so much as look at a cell." The second box. "Saturday, the Pit. The Carry in the forenoon. And the best four on the three boards make the first company for the Call."

"What's the Call?" said Pru.

"It's new. It's what they've made of the Weighbridge." Jude rubbed his jaw with a chalky thumb. "A string out of the Kiln, and husks let out behind it, and a row of lit boxes along the east wall for a street, and dummies to be fetched. A rung and a husk and a casualty to each four. It's read in five columns, off a foreman's slate, from the left."

"Closed, lives, works, tinned, remarks," said Kiva.

"And every dram's combed at the gate. And Sorn's signed our entry." He took a folded card from his tunic. It was slate blue. "Twelve words. *T7, att., under my hand. Rowan: recovery. Finch: reserve, a lamp. R.S.*"

"She's still not underlined me," said Pru. "I've gone down in the world."

Kiva had a paper of her own for the gate. It had seven sets of initials on it, and it said that a thing on a cord was not equipment.

She had a card from a healer that said *one, in the week, if the lane wants it. Then nothing.* She had a certificate with four lines on it and a fifth that began *Not certified.* She had ten fingers, of which eight reported, and a reader with a flag.

It did not feel like being ready. It felt like a sheet laid down with every corner called.

# Chapter 37 — Kiva: Take a Corner

Grader Halloran combed every dram that went through the fighters' door on Friday, and she did it as if she had been asked to count the hairs on a dog.

There was a trestle across the passage. There were sixty-four glass thimbles on it in rows, each with a name, and she held the brass over them one at a time with her head a little on one side. The third reed sang over every one. The seventh touched its neighbor and came away.

"Plain," she said. "A trace. Rowan, K." She pushed the thimble an inch toward its owner. "I've said that sixty-one times this morning, and I shall say it three more, and then I'm going to sit down."

"Is it all a trace?" said Kiva.

"Every pinch in the Compact's store has been a trace since the thaw. If I held back a trace there'd be nothing to light the hall with." Halloran took up the next. "I'm to hold back what knocks. Nothing's knocked. Go on through; you're stopping the passage."

There was a steward beyond her with a list. He looked at Kiva's cuff, and her strapped knee, and then at the cord at her collar.

"What's on that?"

She had the paper ready. He read it twice, down to the seven sets of initials, and then turned his list over to see whether there was anything on the back.

"*Not equipment,*" he said. "I've a column for equipment. I've a column for what isn't allowed. I've nowhere to put a thing that's neither." He scratched his ear with the pencil. "Who's that at the foot?"

"Home Line. The watch."

"Then it's theirs to be wrong about." He wrote something very small in the margin, and let her by.

She came out of the passage into the noise.

She had walked that floor twice with nobody in the tiers. It was a different room with four thousand people in it. The sound of them came down off the banked oak like weather, and the black blocks hummed under her boots. Somebody up in the west tier said her name, not loudly, and somebody else said, *That one. The one with the doors.* She kept her eyes on the brass stud.

"Don't look up," said Anwen, at her shoulder. "It's only a hall. It's got a floor and four corners, and you've been under it with a lantern."

"I'm not looking up."

"You're looking at where you'd look up from."

High on the rim, above the south tier, the Circuit's mast stood against the windows with all its four-and-twenty pens set. There was a small figure at the rail beside it. He had two flags stuck in his belt, a white and a red. A step along from him sat a man in a black coat with a brass instrument on a stand, like a clock with no hands, and the two of them had their heads together over something.

"That's Noa," said Perrin. "With his Halden man. They look like a pair of old women at a window."

"He says they compare," said Kiva. "He says the man's never once asked him for anything he shouldn't."

"That's what'd worry me."

Jude came across from the referee's table with the draw in his fist.

"Old Four," he said. "At ten, on the long axis. We've the north line." He looked round the four of them, and at Pru on the reserves' bench with her card. "I've watched Brask captain a lane three times this year, and he's never once done the same thing. So I've no plan. I've got a habit. Nobody runs. Everything's said aloud. If you see a thing, you say it in a carrying voice, and I'd sooner hear it wrong than not hear it."

"That's my habit," said Kiva.

"I know. I stole it in the spring." He showed the chipped tooth. "Rowan. What do you declare?"

She had a card from a healer in her tunic. *One, in the week, if the lane wants it.*

"Still," she said. "No change. I'll keep the other until I know it's wanted."

---

The Regional's litter was called the Alderman, and he weighed as much as a small piano.

He lay on the brass stud in the middle of the Great Floor, an oak-and-leather dummy with a plate on his chest that had been polished by two hundred years of hands. Thirty paces to the north was a white line that was Seven's. Thirty paces to the south was Old Four's. There were no posts, and no porch, and no sill. There was only a great deal of black oak, which boomed.

That was the first thing that the floor did to a lane. Kiva had learned it by herself with a lantern, and now she heard four thousand people learn it at once. The referee was the grey-braided woman from the Terrace. She dropped her flag, and Kiva opened the still door wide to its four corners, and the Great Floor rang under her like a struck tank.

It rang again, twice, from the south. *Boom. Boom.* Two more still doors had come open, one after the other, as deliberate as a man knocking.

"There's two of them," said Perrin. "Brask. And the catcher. Wace. I never knew he had a door."

Old Four did not come for the litter at a run. They walked to it. Lowen Brask reached the Alderman's left pole and stopped a pace beyond it, square and quiet, with his hands open. Wace took the right. Behind them the small stern ward called Edda raised a wall, a real one, silver and as wide as a cart, and leaned it forward until its two ends rested on the two men's shoulders.

Gower, their blade, stepped inside it and picked up the dummy's feet.

"That's a box," said Jude. "They've built a box. Cade, with me. Lean on it."

They went in. Perrin opened his own door with a boom, and put his shoulder to Brask as he would have put it to a capstan bar. Jude came round the right with his dark blade low, looking for Wace's ribs.

Kiva saw what happened, and did not at once understand it.

Perrin pushed. He was a strong still-door, the strongest first-year his Hall had turned out in thirty years, and he pushed with all of it. Brask did not push back. He gave a little, like a man shifting his weight on a tram, and the wall on his shoulder hummed. Four paces away Wace's boots settled half an inch deeper on the oak. The floor under *Wace* boomed.

The push had gone in at one man and come out at the other.

"It's a plank," said Kiva. She had seen that hum before. She had seen it leaned from post to post in the New Lane by a first-year with her eyes shut. "Jude! It's Finch's plank, with a man at each end! Whatever you put on one, the other's got!"

"Then where—"

He did not finish. Wace's open hand came round the end of the wall and landed flat between his shoulder blades.

"*Down!* Seven!"

Jude knelt. The box began to walk.

It did not hurry. It went south a pace at a time, Brask and Wace stepping together on a word, with the wall riding on their shoulders and Gower inside it carrying an alderman by the ankles. Perrin leaned on it and was carried backward. Anwen brought her own ward up against Edda's, edge to edge, and for a moment the two walls rang on one another like plates in a rack; then the box simply stepped round her.

Kiva got in front of it. She planted, and Brask walked up to her, and stopped, and looked at her quite pleasantly over a foot of air.

"Morning," he said. "You're in the way of a wall. It's not the same as being in the way of me."

The wall slid off his shoulder. It went past her on the right, with Wace under the other end of it and the Alderman behind, and she was standing in an empty lane holding up nothing.

The litter went over the south line at two minutes and forty.

"Four, nought," said Perrin, in the interval. He was grey, and his ribs were heaving. "I'd like to say I mind. I haven't the wind. I pushed that man with everything I own and it came out of the other one's boots."

"It's a lid crew," said Jude. "It's eight on a bar, with two."

"It's a relief," said Anwen.

She had not sat. She was standing with her arms folded, looking down the floor at four people in Stonehand grey and Wren House green who were passing a water dipper.

"That's how Mace's line stands a wall. You don't hold your own weight. You lean it on the next, and they lean theirs on you, and it goes along. If you can hear the join, it's a bad one. I couldn't hear theirs." Her voice was level and exact. "He's had a summer on a Home Line hold. He's come back and made one out of four students and a dummy."

"Then how's it beaten?" said Jude.

"I don't know how a hold's beaten. I've only ever stood in one." She frowned. "I know where it's worst. The end. In the middle you've a neighbor each side to tell you you've dipped. At the end there's a neighbor one side and nothing the other. I had four weeks of it."

Kiva looked down the floor.

A plank with a man at each end. Push one, and the other had it. So nobody could be pushed. But a man who was holding up his end of a plank was not doing anything else. He could not turn. He could not lift a hand, or the plank came down.

"Then it's not the wall," she said. "It's the post. If one of them can't move, the plank can't walk. And if he's holding the plank, he can't mind his own back."

"Which post?"

"Brask. He's the one who calls the step." She had said it quietly. She said it again in a carrying voice, as Jude had asked, as she would have called a mark across a street. "*We take a corner! Brask's! I stop him where he stands! Jude, you'll have a count of two on his back, and you come from behind the wall!*"

Several hundred people heard it. On the reserves' benches at the west side, a girl with cropped red hair had a notebook open on her knee, and beside her a thin boy in Wren House green was watching the floor with his chin on his fist.

"That's very loud," said Perrin.

"It's meant to be. I'd sooner he knew what I was doing than that you didn't."

"He'll change it."

"Then we'll see what he changes it to."

---

He changed it, and it cost them two points before they had drawn breath.

The flag dropped. The floor boomed, three times. The box formed on the stud as it had before, Brask left, Wace right, the wall across their shoulders. Perrin and Jude went in together against the middle of the wall, to feel for the thin place that Anwen had promised them, and leaned.

"*Give!*" said Lowen Brask.

Edda's wall went out.

It went out between one count and the next, clean, with nothing left hanging. Kiva knew that trick. Seven had played it on Stonehand in the spring, and Brask had lain on his back on the New Lane's brick and learned the other half of rule nine. He had not forgotten.

Perrin and Jude had the whole of their weight on something that was no longer there. They went through it and down, one on his hands and the other on his face. A dropped wall throws whoever is leaning on it. Wace's palm was on Perrin's back, and Gower's dark blade was flat on Jude's shoulder, before either had got a knee under him.

"*Down! Down!* Seven, two!"

The wall came up again. Kiva had not moved.

That was the thing that Brask had not changed. She was standing a pace in front of him, in the still door, with her heel over a pier that she had found with a lantern. The box had been built round a plank, and the plank had only just been put back on his shoulder.

"*Mine!*" she said. "Brask! Anwen, his wall, the west end!"

She let her weight go into the floor.

It was not a push. She had nothing to push with. She only stood there, wide, and let the oak have her, and when Lowen Brask took his first step south with a wall on his shoulder he walked into something that was fastened to four brick piers.

*Boom.*

He stopped. For the first time that morning his face changed. He leaned, and she felt him, the whole careful weight of a third-year who had taught the junior lanes, and it was real. It reached the pier. It went down the brick and stayed there.

"Hm," said Brask.

He could not go round her. He had a wall on his shoulder. He could not put it down; Wace was under the other end, already stepping, and Gower was inside with a dummy's feet in his hands. The plank walked a pace at one end and stood still at the other, and Edda's wall stretched between them, thinner and thinner, like dough.

Anwen laid her own ward against it, edge on, exactly where it was thinnest. She did not strike. She leaned.

The wall parted.

Jude Talley had been on one knee for ten counts. He got up at eleven. He came through the place where a wall had been, behind Brask, who could not turn, and laid a dark blade across the square grey back as a man lays a rule across a plank.

"*Down!* Old Four!"

Brask knelt. He did it without hurry, and he looked up at Kiva as he went, and he was very nearly smiling.

"That's a corner," he said.

For ten counts Old Four had no box. It had a ward with a hole in it, and a blade holding a dummy by the ankles, and one still-door who had been a post and had never been asked to be anything else. Perrin picked up the Alderman by the head. He had been told by a healer what he might lift and how high, and he held it at his waist and walked. Wace's open hand met Kiva's stop and got no farther. Gower let go of the feet to reach his blade, and that was a mistake, and he knew it at once.

The Alderman went over the north line at three minutes and ten.

Four, to six.

It was not over. Brask did not build a box in the third carry; he had seen it taken, and he was not a man to be taken the same way twice. He came loose and fast with Gower hunting ahead of him. Twice in the first minute Kiva went to plant and found a square quiet shape already standing where she had meant to stand.

*Arrive before you're there.* It was his own trade. She had been taught it behind his back, and he had come to collect.

His palm found her forearm, and then her shoulder. Two halves made a down. She knelt on the booming oak with her hands on her knees and counted ten, and felt four thousand people watching a girl with three doors be put down by a boy with one.

It did not matter. She found, kneeling there, that it truly did not. Jude had gone for Gower's feet, and had them, and Gower was kneeling too. Anwen had a plank across the lane with nothing behind it but nerve, and Perrin was walking a small piano home behind it a pace at a time, on the words *Yours* and *Mine.*

The Alderman went over the north line with nine seconds on the clock.

The grey braid went up.

"Team Seven, eight. Old Four, seven."

The tiers made a noise like a wave coming up a shingle beach. Perrin sat down on the Alderman.

Lowen Brask came across the floor while they were still getting their breath. He had his notebook in his hand, and he did not open it.

"You called that from thirty paces," he said. "*Take a corner.* I heard it, and I thought, good, she's told me, I'll drop the wall on them. And I did, and it was worth two, and then you took the corner anyway." He shook his head. "I'd stood it all wrong. I'd put my best man on the end. You're supposed to put him in the middle."

"Anwen said the end's where it's worst."

"She's stood one. I can tell." He nodded to Anwen, who nodded back, as one hand on a line acknowledges another. "I'll say one thing, since I shan't be here to take it off you this afternoon. That worked on four of us and a dummy. Don't go away thinking you've learned how to break a hold."

"What have I learned?"

"How to break mine." He scratched his jaw. "A real one isn't a plank on two men. I stood a summer inside them. Take a ward-holder off a Home Line square and there isn't a gap. There's the other three, closer. It shuts on you like a gate." He put the notebook in his pocket. "I only say it because you said it so loud. Half this hall thinks that's the answer now."

He went back to his four.

Kiva watched him go. On the reserves' benches the red-haired girl had shut her book.

She wrote it down in the interval, on her knee. She put it on the right, where things went that she did not yet understand the use of, and Jude called her for the draw before she had blotted it.

---

Halden's license four came out at three in black and gold, and they did not build anything.

They did not need to. They had two blades. Kellan Renn stood on the left with his slab dark on its steel cuff, as still as she had ever seen him, and on the right was the heavy, fair second-year called Pettit, who bounced a little on his toes. Behind them a tall ward named Hesketh had a wall up already. Their catcher, Yarrow, was a still-door, and looked bored.

"Rowan," said the referee. "Your door."

It was what the lane wanted. She had known it since the draw. Two blades against one, and Jude a full second at the top; Seven could not win that by standing. The stop had to go to them. It had to be put down in front of Pettit, on the far side of a wall, where no still-door could walk.

There was a card in her tunic.

"Ember," she said. "One change, called to a count. Ember to still."

The white flag went up. High on the rim a small figure took the red one out of his belt and held it ready in his other hand.

The first carry was a scramble, and Halden took it three to two. She kept the Ember and did not change. She used the bar as a gate, as she had learned to, and Kellan did not come near her once. He went for Jude. He went for Jude's feet, with a courtesy that was almost insulting, and had him down inside a minute.

"He's saving it," said Anwen, in the interval. "He's not looked at you."

"He told me he would. On our step."

"Then he will." She took Kiva's left hand, quite matter-of-factly, and turned it to the light. It was pink. "I'm told I call the gate. If you go, where do you want the wall?"

"Between me and Hesketh. So she can't lean on me when I'm down."

"And at the top?"

"There isn't anything you can put at the top. I'm nobody there."

"I know that," said Anwen. "I'm asking whether you do."

The second carry was four minutes old by the clock over the north door when Pettit got the Alderman by the head and began to run.

He had twenty paces of clear oak and a wall beside him. Jude was on one knee. Perrin was the wrong side of Yarrow. It was exactly the moment, and it had been built for her, and she could see that it had been built.

"*Rowan, changing on three! Ember, to still!*"

"*One.*"

The struts closed. She left the oak.

It was a low bound, over the corner of Hesketh's wall, to a place four paces ahead of a running boy. She had made it seven times on a mast. She could feel the pier that she meant to come down on.

"*Two.*"

She shut the Ember at the top, and waited for it.

There was somebody beside her.

He had gone up when she went up. He had been watching her knees, and he was a Fire, with the best feet in the school, and he had left the floor a quarter of a count after she did and arrived at the top a quarter of a count before. He was dark. He had been dark the whole way. He was turning in the air beside her as easily as a man turns on a stair.

She was nobody. That was the whole of the trouble. For the length of a short word there was nothing of hers open, and she could not have warded a feather.

The flat of a dark blade lay down along her ribs. It was not a blow. It was hardly a touch. It was as gentle as a hand laid on a shoulder in a crowd.

"*Down!* Seven!"

The ash went out of her. It went as it always went, a quarter of a dram, like a breath, with a referee's voice in the middle of it.

And there was no pan.

She had been touched in the one place where she could not afford to notice anything, and she had noticed. The floor was coming up, and the still door was there under her, ready, and the doorway was not empty; it had a boy's blade in it, and a shout, and four thousand faces.

*Is it clean?*

*No.*

"*Off!*" said Kiva. "Off, off—"

She came down as herself. She took the oak on her right foot and folded, and went over her shoulder, and the Great Floor did not boom. It only hit her.

She lay on her back and looked at the lantern of the roof, a long way up.

There was a white flag at the rail of the south tier. He was holding it as high as his arm would go.

"*Reader?*" That was the referee.

"*A crossing begun!*" Noa's voice came down thin and cracked across the whole hall. "*Declared, and made on the count! Not landed! There's a cliff and nothing after it! It isn't a stack!*"

"Then it's a down, and no incident. Ten, Miss Rowan."

She knelt for ten. Pettit had put the Alderman over the south line by the time she reached six.

The third carry she fought on the still door and nothing else, and they took it three to one, which was not enough. Jude got Kellan's forearm, for a half, and looked as pleased as if it had been a down.

"Halden license, eight. Team Seven, six."

---

Kellan came over while the tiers were still shouting.

He had his cuff unlaced. There was sweat in his hair, and the black jacket was open at the neck, and he did not look like somebody who had won a quarter-final at the Regional Measure.

"I'd say I was sorry," he said. "I'm not, and you'd know."

"It was the right place."

"It was the only place. I've thought about it for a fortnight. I worked out how late I could leave the floor and still be there first." He was looking at her left hand. She had it against her chest, with the other over it. "Show me."

She showed him.

The two fingers were white to the knuckle, and blue at the nails. They had been white for some minutes.

"That didn't land," said Kellan slowly. "There wasn't a stance. Why is it—"

"It isn't the landing that does it. It's the gap. I had the gap."

He stood quite still.

"How often are you let?"

"One in a week."

"And that was—"

"Yes."

She watched it reach him. He had a column for most things. He had not had one for this. He had found the one moment in which she could not be touched and had touched her in it, fairly, as he had said that he would; and she had paid a quarter of a dram and a week's worth of hand, and he had taken a point for it in a game.

"I didn't know it was the week's," he said. "Nobody on a floor ought to know that. I'd not have—" He stopped. "No. I would have. That's the worst of it. I'd have done exactly the same and felt sick first."

"I'd have thought the less of you."

"So you said." His jaw was tight. "You called it off. I heard you. I'd laid a blade on you at the top of a bound in front of the whole Compact, and you asked yourself the question and came down on your shoulder."

"It's the only part I'm certified to teach."

He nearly laughed. It did not get as far as his mouth.

"I've a semi-final at four," he said. "And Twenty-Eight at six, if we're both let. Dray's been reading my feet all day." He laced the cuff, badly. "If there's a thing I can do tomorrow that isn't a point, tell me. I'll not ask what it's for."

He went back to the black and gold. A square man with a foreman's slate was waiting for him there, and said something, and clapped him on the arm.

Orla counted in the passage under the north tier, with her back to the wall and the hand in both of hers. She stopped at three hundred.

"I'm not going on," she said. "I can see what it's doing. Sit there until it's done it."

It was nearly two hours.

Kiva spent them in the west tier, in a seat on the aisle, with her left hand in her lap like a thing that she was minding for somebody. Orla sat beside her with the stick between her boots. Below them the lanes went down to their last two. Then the stewards cleared the oak, and wheeled out six trestles, and on each trestle they set a bank of six cells.

"That's the Bench," said Kiva.

"That's the Bench." Orla's voice had no expression in it at all. "I've not sat in this hall for it since the autumn of '60. He stood at the third from the north door. They've not moved the trestles in twenty years."

Ysra Crest had come down onto the floor. She stood at a brass pedestal by the north door, upright, in her grey coat, with one hand resting on a lever.

It was a fault to be found. Each bank had a hurry planted in one cell of the six. A four had to find which, and quench that one and no other, and tune it clean, and bring the bank up again, and the load board over the door showed every needle to the whole hall.

Seven went third. Kiva watched her own team walk out without her.

Anwen had the drum. She had been trained for it since she was thirteen, at the Wren House, and she went down the row with her palm flat on each cell in turn as her mother might have gone down a line of shirts feeling for the damp one. She stopped at the fifth. She said something. Perrin put the quench box down at her feet, and Jude called the needle from the board in a carrying voice, a tenth at a time.

"She's told them what she's doing," said Orla. "Before she's done it. Listen."

Kiva could not hear the words. She could hear that there were words. Anwen's hand came off the fifth cell, and went to its red handle, and pulled, and one needle on the great board dropped to nothing while the other five stood exactly where they were.

It took her four minutes and a little over. It was the third-best time on the day.

"That's a wall-holder," said Orla. "That's somebody who's been told the end of a line is the worst place, and has gone and stood on it." She was silent a moment. "I'd like to have had her on that floor in '60. I'd like to have had anybody who said a thing aloud before they did it."

"You've got her now."

"I haven't. You have. And you've only got her because you knocked on a gatepost." The stick turned in the old hands. "What's the card say?"

Kiva did not need to take it out.

"*One, in the week, if the lane wants it. Then nothing.*"

"And the lane wanted it, and you spent it, and a boy in a black coat had the good of it." Orla did not say it harshly. "I'd have declared the same. I'll put that in my own book, so that it's not only in yours. Tomorrow you kneel. You set a mark and you call a count and you open nothing whatever."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so that I've heard myself say it."

The standings went up on the great board at seven, in chalk, a line at a time.

Halden had the Lane. They had beaten Twenty-Eight in the final by two, and Selka Dray had got a blade on Kellan's forearm twice. Twenty-Eight had the Bench, on a thin boy from the Wren House who had found his hurry in under three minutes. On the two boards together, Seven stood sixth of sixteen.

The feeling had been back in her fingers since a little after five. It had come slowly, from the root, and it had come back to less. The first found the edge of the seat after she had been looking at it for some while. The second found that there was a seat.

She did not tell Orla that. Orla was watching the floor, where a steward was wheeling away the third trestle from the north door.

She wrote it in the book instead, where it would be read.

# Chapter 38 — Kiva: The Call

Healer Aske had his cart at the Pit's west gate by seven on Saturday, and he saw her hand on the tailboard, standing up, with the pin.

"When," said Kiva, for the first finger. It had taken five breaths.

She did not say it for the second. She waited until he took his thumb away.

"That's twice now it's told you about my thumb and not my pin," said Aske. "Yesterday's didn't even land, I'm told. A boy touched you in the air." He did not wait to have it confirmed. He took the card out of her fingers and wrote on it, large, across the bottom, and gave it back. "Read me that."

"*No go before the 23rd.*"

"And what's today?"

"The seventeenth."

"Then we understand one another." He put the pin in his lapel. "You're catching. I've no quarrel with catching. You've eight fingers that report, and you've a first-year who crosses a cord right four times in five, and I'm told that you've the sense to hand it to her. Kneel all you like. Open nothing." He looked at her over the card. "I can ease a hand that's starved. I've done it for you once. I can't ease one that's been spent to the bottom, because there's nothing left in it for the ash to speak to. I'd like that to be the last thing I say to you this week."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"*Yes,* she says." He turned away to his linen. "They always say yes at seven in the morning."

---

The Pit had been altered since the spring, and Kiva could have told whose hand had been in it.

She came down the keeper's stair with her kit on her shoulder and stood at the east rail, and looked. It was the same yellow oval, and the same grey cinders, seventy paces from the Kiln at the north to the sump at the south, and forty across. The race ran down the middle of it still, a line of iron gratings as straight as a ruled margin.

But there was a green iron box now on the wall above the sump, where the south link had stood naked for a hundred years. A rod ran from it along the foot of the east wall to a long handle beside the load board. A man could open the branch from the floor in the time that it took to say so. Nobody need cross anything.

"They've buried a feeder to the lattice house as well," said a voice beside her. "Round by the road, in a pipe. So the race can be dropped and the cage stays lit."

It was Mara. She had a seat in the east terrace, two rows up, and she had come down to the rail in her good coat.

"That was your margin," said Kiva.

"It was the fourth margin on that link in sixty years. Mine happened to arrive the week after a girl had stood on the lever." Mara's eyes went along the rod, joint by joint, as they would have gone along a drum. "They've done it properly. I could wish that they'd done it for a better reason than being frightened."

Along the foot of the east wall, under the reserves' rail, there was something else that was new. Twelve oak posts stood in a row, and on each post was a cell box no bigger than a biscuit tin, with a small lamp burning on its lid.

"What are those?"

I have watched her miss.*" He lowered it. "A man called E.K. had a column for that, and somebody tidied it away. I've put it back, and I've put it first. Do you take it?"

It was the plainest question that she had ever been asked.

She did not look at the square. She looked up, into the east terrace, two rows above the rail. Mara had stood. She had not come down. She had said that she would not, and she was keeping her word; she had her right hand pressed flat against the front of her good coat, the scarred one, as a person holds a thing that has been knocked.

*It can be done,* Mara had said. *I'd not wish it on you for all the doors in Meridian.*

"I'm frightened," said Kiva. "I'd like that written as well."

"It's written."

"Then yes."

It took less than a minute to set, and every word of it was said aloud.

"House four, off the floor."

That was a square man in black, with a foreman's slate of his own. He had come along the wall from the Halden end.

"Birch, chartered, the Works' license yard. It's struck. My paper puts no student of the Works under a Home Line direction, and I'll not pretend it does to be liked." He jerked his head. "Pettit. Yarrow. Rail. Hesketh, a gate's the Circuit's rule and no man's direction; you may stand one if you choose. Renn."

"No, sir," said Kellan.

He had stepped out of the line. His face was white, and perfectly composed.

"West side," said Rhea. "I'll be glad of you."

"Three posts," said Vane. "Three blades. Renn, the north-east. Talley, north-west. Gower, south-west. You come at them from outside, dark. They've their backs to you; the skin's turned in. You light after the foot, and you cut on one word, and the word's hers."

"What word?" said Jude.

"*Back,*" said Kiva. "When she's clear. I say *back,* and I give the corner to the ground and shut. If you cut then, there are four corners gone at once and nothing left to haul."

"It comes down like a sheet off a line," said Lowen. "I've seen ours dropped so, on a count. It's the only way it ever comes down kindly."

"Pike."

"Three cloths," said the square catcher. He had them under his arm already. "Behind three posts. I'll call *dark* for the lot, and I'll bag what I can reach. If two of them lie, they lie."

"Cade. You're the Carry."

"With Marten," said Perrin. "She's his. I'd not take her off him."

"Pryce."

"A gate," said Anwen. "For what's coming up. When that wall's gone it'll read, and I'd sooner it read us than a terrace." She was already looking for the ground. "Head of the sixth sheet. Fifteen paces south-west of that corner. Four wards: mine, Edda's, Hesketh's, and Twenty-Eight's. I call the step."

"It's a string. You've held a rung's worth."

"I've held the end of a wall for four weeks with nothing in front of me. I know what I'm asking for." She turned her head. "Kiva. The *why* was read. The price was read. I've nothing to add to it. Ask me."

It was a strange moment to be given that. Kiva found that her eyes had stung.

"Pryce," she said. "The gate. On *back.*"

"Mine," said Anwen, and went.

"Brask," said Vane. "You know that thing's breath."

"It hauls for four counts and eases for four. I've been watching the cord." Lowen had moved to where he could see the south-east corner. "I'll call the *in.* She goes on the *in.* So do the blades."

"Then go."

They went.

Kiva walked out across the cinders with Perrin on her left and a thin boy in Wren House green on her right. It was eighteen paces. She laid two fingers of her right hand on her breastbone as she walked.

"Cold," she said, to nobody. There was no white. She had looked.

Selka Dray was lying on her back with her red head on the cinders and her left leg gone under a white line as thick as a wrist. The wall rose from it. It was so thin that Kiva could see through it, the blue stroke, and four grey knots now, and a fifth beginning.

Dray's face was the color of lard. Her eyes came round.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"Don't make a speech."

"I haven't got one." Kiva stopped a pace from her hip, on the south-east side, and set her feet. "You're being rescued. That's Cade's line; he lends it. I take that off your leg, there, where it comes up. It throws me that way. I put it down four paces off and stand on it. When I say *planted,* they pull you. Not before."

"And if you miss?"

"Then I call *off,* and it comes back down where it was. And I'm no use to you, and I've lost the fingers for nothing."

"*Fingers?*"

"It's what it costs. He read it out."

"I couldn't hear. I've had a thing on my leg." Dray shut her eyes for a moment. "Eight to a breath. It bites on the first four. I've been counting. It's the only thing I can do." They opened again. "I objected to you twice."

"I know. I was in the room both times."

"I'd do it again. You oughtn't to be on a floor without a needle on you." A breath, held, through a bad four counts. "There's a needle on you. I looked. He's got his flag out. Get on with it."

Perrin had knelt at her head, dark, and got his hands under her arms. Marten had her right shoulder. Neither of them said a word.

"*Out,*" said Lowen Brask, from the wall. His voice was as even as a clock. "Two. Three. Four. *In.* Two. Three—"

Kiva put up her hands.

They came up low, at her left hip, a tray's width apart. Two fingers stood clear, straight and stiff, with nothing to do. She bent the ward into the bowl of a spoon and held it a hand's breadth above a girl's thigh, under the place where a white cord left the ground.

It was cold. She could feel the cold through the silver.

"Hand, to Ember, to still. Rowan, changing on three, and on five."

"*Out,*" said Lowen. "Two. Three. Four."

The cord came taut. Dray made a sound in her teeth.

"*In.*"

"*One,*" said Kiva, and slid the pan under.

For four counts the cord was slack. It lay in the curve of her ward, light as a clothesline, humming a little. She had it. She had the whole corner of a thing that nobody had ordered, lying in a tray between her palms.

"*Two.*"

"*Out,*" said Lowen.

They hauled.

Three grey shapes leaned back together, and the cord came up out of her ward like a hawser taking the weight of a barge. It did not push. It lifted. It came off a girl's leg and found a spoon under it, and the whole haul of three posts went round the curve and into Kiva's back, and flung her.

It was not Gerald. It was not anything like Gerald. Gerald had been a hundredweight of sand with no judgment. This had a count in it. She felt it for the length of one tap, slow and even and many, like the hum of the sheds coming down a street.

*It wants me to keep step.*

"*Three.*"

She folded. Far edge. Near edge. A penny, in her palms. Shut.

Nobody.

She was nobody, going sideways across the Pit with a thing in her spine that was heavier than she was. The ash went, like a breath. And the count went with it. She felt that, and it was the one thing all afternoon that she had not expected. The haul was still in her. The beat was not. It had been left on the other side of a shut door, as mud is left on a step.

*The wall between the rooms.*

The struts caught her. Low, and long. She was over open cinders with her left hand gone cold to the wrist, and she shut the Ember at the top and did not listen for it. She waited. She waited for the last thread to run home along her ribs and arrive.

"*Four.*"

Nobody, again.

It came out of a place that had already been emptied once that minute and once the day before. There was a moment, in the air, when she thought quite calmly that there was nothing left to pay it with. Something paid it. It went out through her left arm like water out of a cut hose. She felt it go past the wrist.

*Arrive before you're there.*

She set the pan. Wide. By its corners.

"*Five!*"

Her right foot came down on the cinders of the Pit, and the floor took her.

Then the corner arrived.

It came in at her heel, slow and enormous, and it did not stop coming. A sandbag had been one swing. This was three things leaning back on a line, and they went on leaning. It went down through her into the packed clay under the cinders and spread there, and she felt the whole oval of the old pit take it, as a wharf takes a mooring.

There was a white cord running down to the ground a foot from her right boot. It had nothing under it but cinders. It stood there, taut as a stay, fastened to the place where she was.

"*Planted!*" said Kiva. "*Six!*"

---

Perrin Cade had never in his life wanted so much to look at something and been so entirely forbidden.

*You don't look up,* a Torch had told three shed hands on the Weighbridge, and one of them had been his father. He had thought at the time that it was an easy thing to be told.

He had a girl under the arms. She was not heavy. She was a blade, and built like one, all cord and temper. The trouble was that one of her legs had been under something for the best part of five minutes and had come out the wrong shape.

"*Planted!*"

"That's us," said Perrin. "Marten. On three, and along the ground; we don't lift till she's ten paces off. One. Two."

"I can count for myself," said Selka Dray, through her teeth.

"I know. I'm only telling you. Three."

They pulled.

She came. There was nothing on her. Where the cord had been there was a stripe across her breeches as white as salt, and the cloth cracked when it bent. She made one noise, a short one, and then she shut her mouth on it so hard that he heard her teeth.

He went backward on his knees and then on his feet, bent double. Each step he waited for his ribs to tell him that they had begun again. They said nothing. He had been strapped from the armpits to the hips for the whole of a spring and a summer, and had held a corner and a pole and a sandbag. He had said *I can't* in front of a Torch, and his father had called him a good lad for it.

He was dragging a person. He was passed for it.

"Seven," said the girl on the ground. Her eyes were open, and fixed on something behind him and to his left. "She said six. That's seven. Eight."

"Don't count her. Count yourself."

"I'm not *hurt* enough to count myself. Nine."

He could hear it. That was the worst of having his back to it. He could hear Brask's voice going on, *out, two, three, four, in,* as steady as a pump, and under that he could hear something else, a girl's voice, quite quiet, counting in a different time altogether.

*Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. One. Two.*

She was counting in fives. Brask had it in fours. They did not fit, and they went on not fitting, and Perrin understood all at once that it was being done on purpose. It was what she did when a thing was trying to come in. He had watched her do it for six months and thought that it was nerves.

"Ten paces," said Marten. His voice was shaking. "That's ten."

"Then up. I've her shoulders. You take the good leg and leave the other to hang, and you say *sorry* afterward and not during." He got his arm under her. "To the shoulder. I'm told that I'm allowed."

He stood up with her.

It was the first weight that he had put above his own waist since the twenty-ninth of the fifth month. It went onto his shoulder, and down his back, and into his boots, and the cinders had it. Nothing gave. He stood for one count in the middle of the Pit, with eleven hundred people looking at him and a girl's red head against his ear, and he could have sung.

"*Clear!*" he shouted. He did not turn round. "*She's clear! Kiva! She's off it!*"

"You've a voice like a tram," said Selka Dray, faintly.

"I've been saving it."

He walked. The west gate was forty paces, and a bald man with a board was already running out from the cart. There was a long-legged fellow on a camp stool beside it, who had got up on his stick.

Behind Perrin, the two counts went on. Fours, and fives.

Then there was one word.

"*Back!*"

He did not look. He saw it all the same. He saw it on the terraces in front of him, on eleven hundred faces at once, three flashes laid across them like lightning on a wall: one blue-white and short, and two more a good deal longer. He heard something come down behind him. It did not crash. It sighed, the whole length of it, like a wet sheet let go from a line.

"*Dark!*" That was a square, flat voice. "*One.*"

And a moment after, from the south-west, a girl's voice that he had known since he was a first-year with purple hands. It was not loud. It was perfectly level.

"*Mine.*"

"Is that your Hold?" said the girl on his shoulder.

"That's Pryce."

"She's got a string on her."

"I expect she has." Perrin kept walking. His eyes had gone wet, which was the cinders. "I'd like it known that I mind. I've carried you forty paces with my back to the best thing I'm ever going to be near, and I'm putting it on the list."

---

Kiva had said *back,* and given the ground its weight, and shut the door.

She had done it on Lowen's *in.* That much she was certain of. She had felt the cord go slack at her heel, and had let the whole corner down into the clay as one sets down a full pan, and there had been nothing left in the doorway when she closed it.

After that she was not certain of very much.

There had been light. Kellan's she knew, a line as long as a breath, away to the north. There had been two more. Then the white cord beside her boot had simply lain down. It did not snap. It went loose from end to end, all four sides of it together, and the frost that hung from it came down after, slowly, folding as it fell.

It lay on the cinders in a ring, eight paces across, pale and thin. It looked like the mark that a tub leaves on a floor.

"*Dark! One.*"

Pike. Somewhere behind three heaps of ash. She was dark. She had nothing to be anything else with.

She was sitting down. She had not decided to. The cinders were under her, and Pru Finch had her by the shoulders from behind and was saying something very fast, of which Kiva caught only the word *narrating.*

In the middle of the ring, with nothing round it any longer, stood a string.

It had seven knots. The eighth was coming up between its legs, out of a thin blue stroke that was already narrower than it had been. There was frost on it from crown to knee. Its blunt top turned slowly, a quarter of a circle, as a weathercock turns.

It had every right to come to her. She was the nearest thing in the Pit that had been worked.

It went past her. It went to the south-west, where four people stood in a row at the head of the sixth sheet with a wall of silver between their hands, and a girl in the middle of them was calling the step.

"Step," said Anwen. "Step. Edda, you're a hand short on the outside. That's it. Step."

The Flask put its blunt head against her wall and leaned.

It was a thing that Graywater did with four Torches. Kiva saw the wall take it. She saw it dip, at the south end, by the width of a hand; and she saw Anwen feel that, without turning her head, and say one word, and saw it come level.

"You're not to watch that," said Pru. "You're to show me your hand. Dane's shouting it from the rail. *Hand, Rowan.* She's said it three times."

Kiva looked for her hand.

It was at the end of her arm. She lifted the arm, with her shoulder, and the hand came up with it. It was the color of a candle to a place two inches above the wrist. It did not feel like anything. It did not feel cold. It was only a thing that she could see.

"That's not pink," said Pru, in a small voice.

"No."

She put her right hand to her collar instead. That one still worked. The disk was where it had always been.

"Cold," said Kiva.

She had known that it would be. A levee catcher had told her so, in a hand that nobody was meant to read.

There was a white flag at the east rim. He had it up as high as his arm would reach, and he was holding the rail with his other hand as if the terrace had moved under him. Beside him a man in a black coat had got to his feet.

Then every lamp on the rim of the Pit showed a point of light, once, and went out.

It was the middle of the afternoon. The branch was dead; she had heard the handle go. There were forty of them, round the whole oval, and for the space of a blink each had a spark in it, like forty eyes opening and closing.

There was a noise in the terraces like wind in a wood.

"Keeper," said Vane's voice, quite steadily. "Did I see that?"

"You did." Dunmore was at the load board with both hands on the case. He sounded like a man who has been told the same joke twice. "They're dead. They've been dead since I dropped the race. That's the second time in my life I've seen a dead ring jump, and it was on this floor, with her on it." He spat. "T-0. I'll write it, since I'm paid to. I'd as soon somebody told me what I was writing."

Nobody told him.

For sixty counts nothing crossed the ring. Pike had seen to that, in six words, and Vane had put a line on his slate. The stuff lay open, and it was not ash, and nobody in Meridian had a rule for it.

At sixty-one, six people and a handcart came out from the lattice house.

Kiva knew the walk of the woman in front. She had last seen her on a bank on the Sag Line, weighing tins. The closing crew went in over the pale ring without looking at it, as a carter steps over a hose, and gathered round a blue stroke that was no wider now than a hand.

Whatever they did there, they did with their backs to the Pit. It was not student work. It had never been shown to her, and she did not ask.

"*Closed!*" called Bettan. Her cracked voice went up into the terraces. "Ten past three, and it's shut!"

---

There were two strings on the floor of the Pit, and the Regional Measure had been struck, and it was a call.

It was read as a call. Kiva saw that much from where she sat, with her back against the tinning table and her left arm lying across her knees. Nobody ran. Rhea Sorn took the old string down the west side, rung by rung, on her own gate, and a square man in Halden black took the new one, on Anwen's. They cut turn about. Each blade asked a catcher before it lit.

The top four rungs of the new string were student work, and Vane said so.

"You're second step, the lot of you. One rung, under a foreman, held and cut and bagged. I'm the foreman. I see no reason why it shouldn't be done because it's real." He looked along what was left of his first company. "Old Four. Twenty-Eight, with a reserve blade; you've a yellow card somewhere in that line. Seven. And Halden, if Halden's foreman will lend me his four for a rung under my slate and his own eye."

"He will," said Birch, without looking round. "That's a rung. That's on my paper."

So it was that Seven's rung was called at about half past three, and that Kiva did the only part of it which she had left.

She set the mark. She walked the seventh sheet with Pru's arm through hers, and read the ground and the air, and what was behind them, the whole circle round. She put her right palm on the canvas.

"Here. A pace north. Jude takes a full second; he'll want the room."

"And the cord?" said Pru.

"Yours. From the first turn. Not on a three. All of it." She had thought that it would be hard to say. "I'll call the count and I'll call the check. You've the hands."

"I cross it right four times in five."

"I know. Pryce told me. I'm to let you find the fifth yourself."

Jude went up the back of a string dark, and hung there, and asked.

"Not yet," said Kiva. "It's a foot short. Anwen. One more."

"Step," said Anwen.

"Now."

He lit for a full second, and came down heavy, and sixty drams fell like sand.

"Dark! One."

Perrin had a corner. Kellan had a corner; he had come without being asked, in a black jacket with the cord torn off one cuff, and knelt on the lead. Pru drew the neck in at her elbow, as she had been shown by a girl who was now holding a Flask's face, and at the third turn she stopped.

"It's wrong," she said. "It's under and then over. I've done the fifth one." Her round face was scarlet. "Wait. Don't tell me. Over. Then under, toward the catcher."

"Cord check," said Kiva.

"Crossed right."

"Sealed. Forty-nine."

It was not a good count. It was eight worse than a street at half past two in the morning, when everything had been fine. Nothing had happened.

Pike held the comb over the bags afterward, at the table, and she watched his face.

He did not need to tell her. She had laid her right palm on the neck of each as it came off the pole. Rhea Sorn's rungs had the hair in them, the bend in the grain that all of that year's ash had carried, and nothing else.

The other string's were loud.

Every one of them. It came up through the canvas into the heel of her good hand like the hum of the sheds, slow and even and many. It was the same count that she had left on the far side of a shut door.

"That one keeps time," she said. "And that. All of his. All four of ours. I can't weigh it. It's only what comes through a neck."

"No. You can't." Pike held the brass over Seven's bag. The third reed sang. The seventh rapped, hard, like a knuckle on a door. "But there's a rule now that says you may stop a bag on it, with your name. I heard it read. I thought at the time it was a clerk's notion." He took a red tag from his roll, and a pencil, and put them down in front of her right hand. "You'll have to write it yourself. I've never been a catcher of any step whose word was good for that. You are."

She wrote it. *Red. Not for the ground. Stepped. On a catcher's word and a comb. K. Rowan.* It took a long time, since she had nothing to hold the tag still with, and in the end Pike put one broad finger on the corner of it.

"There's a thing on my glass that the keeper ought to hear."

It was a pleasant voice. Mr. Adlam had come down from the rim. He had his hat in his hand, and he was not looking at the bags.

"Mr. Dunmore. The Works has a bank of four-and-twenty across the road that keeps a district dry. I had it on the step glass at eleven minutes past two, running as it ought, every cell on its own time." He spoke with great care. "It fell in at the instant that square stood up. All of it. I assumed that it would come out when you dropped the race. It did in the spring; I've read the trunk book."

"It's off the bus," said Dunmore. "There's a foot of air in that link."

"I know that there is. I watched you open it. It is still in step." Adlam turned his hat round by the brim. "It's beating as one, with nothing joined to it. I've a patent in a leather case that explains how a bank is put into step. I have looked through it twice this afternoon for the page on how one is taken out."

"And?"

"There isn't one. Nobody ever wanted it."

Kiva heard that. It went by her like something said in the next room.

She had her arm across her knees. Somebody had put a blanket round her. The Wren House cart had been brought in at the west gate, and Healer Aske had come across the cinders to her, since she had not come to him.

He did not take out the pin. He took the hand in both of his, and turned it over, and pressed the two fingertips, and then the palm, and then the wrist. He was a long time over it.

"Can you ease it?" said Pru.

"With what?" He did not say it unkindly. "Ward-trace ash eases a nerve that's starved. It gives it something to answer. This isn't starved. It's been turned out. There's nothing in there for the ash to speak to." He laid the hand back on her knee, quite gently, like something returned. "The wrist will come. I think the palm will come. I'll tell you about the rest when I've a week to tell you with."

"You said. At seven."

"I said it at seven and I said it at half past two. You'd an answer for me both times." He stood up. "It was the same answer. I notice that. Most people have a different one in the afternoon."

They had put a stretcher on the cart's tailboard. There was a red head on the pillow, and a leg in a board, and a thin boy sitting on the step below with his face in his hands.

"Ten," said Selka Dray.

Kiva turned her head.

"You held it ten. From *planted* to *back.* I was counting. Cade told me not to." The grey eyes were sunk, and exact. "It bit you every fourth, and you were going in fives. I couldn't make out what you were at. Then I could."

"It wanted me to keep step."

"I know what it wanted. I'd had it on my leg." Dray looked up at the yellow brick of the west wall. "I still think what I thought. You oughtn't to be seated without a needle. I'm writing to the Chair to say that it should stand for anybody like you, ever, and that it worked." Her jaw moved. "I'd be under a string if they'd unseated you in the spring. I can hold both. I've been practicing."

"So have I."

"Then don't thank me, and I'll not thank you. It would only be a speech."

Kiva looked at her left hand. It lay on the blanket, pale and neat, like something that she was minding for a stranger.

She had been told what it would cost. She had been told by a healer, across a cart, and by a watch officer, off a slate, in front of eleven hundred people. She had said that she was frightened, and that had been written. Nobody had gone round her. Nobody had cut anything to fit.

It was the first thing that had ever been done to her entirely in the open, and she had done it herself.

She found that it did not make the hand feel like anything. She had rather thought that it might.

# Chapter 40 — Kiva: Hand as Before

Hollis Vane read his slate at five, standing on the cinders, since there were people in that Pit whose floor it had been.

The terraces had not emptied. Eleven hundred people had been told twice by the stewards that the Regional Measure was struck and that they might go home, and perhaps a third of them had gone. The rest sat on in the low sun, quietly, like people at the end of a long service who are not sure that it is over.

The bags stood in two rows along the tinning table. One row had white tags. The other had red.

"*The Pit, Saturday the 17th,*" said Vane. He had his outdoor voice, flat and whole. "*The Call, opened at two. A tear on the open floor, outside the lattice, held by four husks, at twenty past. Struck, twenty-one past. Closed, ten past three, by the Home Line.*"

He turned the slate a little to the light.

"*Lives: none lost. Hurt: Dray, blade, the left leg above the knee, the bone, and a burn of cold. Rowan, catcher, the left hand; see the direction. Works: the race dropped an hour and ten minutes by the keeper. The Row quenched, twelve boxes, by the keeper; none fouled. The Ferren bank in step since twenty past two and not come out; that is the Works' to enter, and I have told them so.*"

There was a man in a black coat standing by the load board with his hat in his hand. He inclined his head.

"*Tinned, clean: five hundred and twelve drams. Put by, red, on catchers' words and a comb: five hundred and three and seven-tenths. And a ring of something that is not in the yield book, swept under the Chair's seal, which nobody here has a name for.*" He turned the slate over. "*Remarks: one. A blade cut a husk inside her step, on her catcher's word, before the foreman had read the floor. The foreman had not called a stand. The remark is the event's.*"

"That's not right," said a voice from the cart.

They had not yet taken Selka Dray away. She had refused to go until it was read.

"It was my cut. I'll have it under my name."

"You'll have the cut under your name, Miss Dray. It's in the kill line, with the others, and it was as clean as any I saw today." Vane did not look round. "The remark is for a thing done wrong. You did what a second-step blade is taught to do with a Handful that has its back to her. The floor was wrong. I'd a company on it and I hadn't read it. That's whose it is."

Dray said nothing. After a moment the thin boy on the cart's step put his hand on the edge of the stretcher, and she let him.

"Now the pot," said Vane. "It was struck. A struck floor's shared the Line's way, and I'll say what that is, since three of you have a house's paper and will want it said. It isn't shared by who was kneeling where. Everything clean goes in one, and it's divided by the standing rule." He read the figures off the back of his hand. "The civic half, two hundred and fifty-six. The casualty reserve, sixty-four. The schools, ninety-six. The teams, ninety-six. That's four-and-twenty to a team. Halden's four tinned more of it than anybody. They'll draw the same as Seven, who tinned a rung that's wearing a red tag."

"The Works does not object," said Mr. Adlam.

"I'd have been surprised," said Birch, beside him, rather sourly. "It was my four tinned it."

"And the red row?" That was Jude. He had Seven's own bag at his feet. *Stepped. On a catcher's word and a comb. K. Rowan.*

"The red row goes where the last red bag went. To a cold room, under three seals, with every catcher's name on it. There's a rule, as of the third of the seventh month. I was glad of it today." Vane hung the slate on his belt. "That's a response. It's on the district table by morning under the Home Line's name. I'd sooner it had been a Regional."

He came across to the table afterward.

Kiva was still sitting with her back to it. Aske had bound the hand in dry linen, loosely, more to keep it from being knocked than for any good that it would do, and it lay in her lap like a parcel.

"I've a ledger to write tonight," said Vane. "The back of it. I'll put the direction as I read it. Under *Result* I'll put that it was taken off her, and carried, and held for ten, and given back, and that the hold came down." He looked at the linen. "There's the third column. I filled it before. I'd like to fill it after as well, when your healer knows. E.K. did. He went back in the autumn and wrote two words."

"*Hand as before,*" said Kiva.

"I'll come and ask you, when it's time. I'll not put what I've only been told."

Rhea Sorn was sitting on an upturned tub at the end of the table.

She had cut five rungs since ten past three, each longer than the last, and she looked it. She had her elbows on her knees and her hands hanging. She did not get up.

"I told you on a wharf to go on saying that you couldn't weigh it," she said. "You said it again today. I heard you. *It's only what comes through a neck.* Then you wrote a tag on it."

"Pike put the pencil down."

"Pike's no fool." The pale eyes came up. "I'll tell you what I saw that I'd not seen. You stood on this floor and said what it would cost before you'd done it. You had a healer say it, and a foreman write it, and you said you were frightened. I've been twenty-three years at this. I have never once named the price first. I cut, and I count after, and I'm thought very honest for it."

Kiva did not know what to say to that.

"I'm not offering you anything," said Rhea. "You're seventeen, with a hand in a cloth. Seven's still mine. The board will want a Flask stood on my own van before it takes the brackets off, and it's right to. I only wanted it said by somebody who'd know." She looked at her own hands. "I burned that card."

"You said you would."

"I did it the same night. It's the only thing I did that week that I'd do again without thinking."

Lowen Brask came by with a canvas roll on his shoulder. He stopped, and nodded at the linen.

"Did it come easy?"

"No."

"Good." He nearly smiled. "Look at your feet anyway. I'm told Vane wants me on the Line's roll in the spring. A hold crew, for wages. I said I'd need to think, and then I went and stood by that ring for a quarter of an hour, and I found that I'd thought." He shifted the roll. "They'll not be standing holds for a while, I shouldn't wonder. Somebody's been watching how it's done."

"You said nobody showed them."

"I said I wasn't saying anybody did." He went on toward the west gate. "That's not the same. I had that off a catcher who's grown particular about guesses."

Noa came last, down the keeper's stair, with the tape rolled against his chest in both arms.

He did not unroll it. He sat down on the cinders in front of her, cross-legged, like a boy at a lesson.

"Two cliffs," he said. "Twenty-six, and twenty-six. They're a little under two seconds apart. There's nothing between them but two hundredths for the bound. I held the white up before you'd landed. I didn't need the glass."

"And after?"

"You know what's after. You saw the lamps." His narrow face was grey with tiredness. "It's on all sixteen. Every pen on the drum. A hair, at one instant, eleven seconds on from the second cliff. Fifteen of them were people standing dark against a wall or running with a casualty. One was you, sitting down." He swallowed. "Adlam saw it. He wasn't looking at his own glass. He was looking over my shoulder, and he said, *There,* before I did."

"What does he want?"

"Nothing. That's the thing about him." Noa rubbed his eyes. "He's written to the Chair. He showed me. It's four lines, and it's under his own name and not the Works'. It says that he was present and saw sixteen pens move together with nothing lit on the floor, and that he knows of no fault in a mast that would do it. He says that he'd like to be second, when I've something to print."

"Have you?"

"I've a page with no heading. I've had it since the sixth month." He looked at her. "You've been asked what it is all day. What do you call it?"

"I can't weigh it."

Noa took his pencil from behind his ear. He unrolled a hand's breadth of the tape's blank margin against his knee, and wrote three words on it, small and square.

*Not yet weighed.*

"That'll do for a heading," he said. "It's the first true one I've had."

There was no dream that night.

She had expected one. She had lain down at nine, in the narrow bed, with a basin on the chair and the hand propped on a folded towel, and Mara had sat in the doorway with the pencil and the single sheet, as the household rule required. They had both waited. The fever did not come. The lights did not come by any road.

At eleven Mara put the sheet away, with nothing on it but the date.

"*Nothing. No fever,*" she said. "I've written that. I'd as soon have it on the page that there was a night when you did the largest thing you've ever done and it had nothing to say about it."

"The plate's cold too."

"So I'd supposed. You'd have told me." Mara did not go. She sat with her back against the doorpost and her knees drawn up, which she had not done since Kiva was small. "I didn't come down."

"I saw."

"I got as far as standing. I'd my hand on the seat in front. I'd said that I wouldn't, and I thought, if I go to that rail now she'll look at me and not at the foreman, and it won't be hers any longer." Her voice was quite level. "I told you once that I wanted you safe without owning what you chose. I'd no notion how much it would cost to mean it. I'd like you to know that I minded. I shan't say it twice."

"I looked up. Before I said yes."

"I know you did. I'd my hand on my coat like a woman who's lost her purse." She got up, stiffly. "A man was saying something about the Ferren bank, by the load board, as they carried you past. I heard two words of it. I've the trunk books in the shop, and Rusk's leave to read them. I shall go across to that pump house on Monday and put my hand on cell nine."

"What did you hear?"

"*In step.* And *not come out.*" Mara turned down the lamp. "Go to sleep. It isn't yours. It's the Works', and for once I'm glad to be the one of us who's licensed."

The wrist came back on Sunday, toward evening, like a tide coming up a slip. The palm came on Monday morning, and the thumb with it, and the last two fingers. By the time she had her boots on she could feel the lace in them.

She could see the first two. She laced round them.

The board in the Measure Hall basement had the result on it by ten on Monday, in a clerk's hand, and there was a crowd.

*The Regional Measure, 2680. Read on three boards; the Call struck.*

*1. Halden License. 2. Old Four (att.). 3. Team Twenty-Eight. 4. T7 (att.).*

"Fourth," said Perrin. He read it with his lips. "Of sixteen. With brackets." He considered. "I've decided I don't mind the brackets. I'd got so used to them. It'd be like losing a tooth."

"There's a line under it," said Kellan.

There was. It was in a different hand, level and upright, and people were reading it over one another's shoulders as they had once read three sealed papers.

*Entered by the Chair, for the Circuit's record. On the floor of the Pit, on the 17th, under a recorded field direction, after the event was struck: three doors opened in sequence, one after another, nothing open between. Declared. Witnessed by this office and by eleven hundred others. Read on two instruments. The candidate is not thereby classified. She is not thereby certified. It is not to be asked of her. It is written here because it occurred. Y. Crest.*

Kiva read it once.

She was aware of the people round her. Nobody moved their kit away. A first-year whom she did not know looked at the linen on her hand, and then at her face, and then, rather quickly, at the board again.

"*Because it occurred,*" said Kellan, under his breath. "That's the best reason I ever saw anybody give for anything."

She was sent for at eleven.

Ysra Crest had three papers on her desk and her hands folded on them. Orla was in the chair by the door, with her stick. She had brought a paper of her own, and it lay on the corner of the desk, unsigned for by anybody yet.

"Sit," said Ysra. "You may put that hand where it's easiest. I've a number of things to say, and none of them is a ruling."

Kiva sat.

"In 2676 a clerk of this office wrote *Classification deferred* against a girl of thirteen, and under it, since something had to go there, *There isn't a column for this.* The sheet came to me, and I let it stand." The long plain face did not alter. "I have let it stand every spring for four years. I told myself that it was caution. Some of it was. A good deal of it was that I had once signed a boy's entry to a Bench because his sponsor was certain, and I had sooner have no column than a wrong one."

"I know about the Bench," said Kiva.

"I imagine that you do. Orla tells a story to its end, these days." Ysra unfolded her hands. "I still have no column. I'll not pretend otherwise to oblige anybody. I can't rank you. I don't know what your Hold is, or your Draw, with three things that take turns. I don't know what you are, and you are not to take a line on a notice board for my saying that I do."

"No, ma'am."

"But I've been using *no column* as a reason to write nothing, and that was wrong, and I've been shown that it was. By a catcher's tag, and a night clerk called R., and a watch that put *cost* back into a ledger." She laid her finger on the first of the three papers. "So I've opened a page. It isn't a class. It has a heading, which is *Witnessed,* and it has ruled lines, and what goes on them is what was done, where, on whose direction, what it cost, and who read the tape. It has one entry. I'll not have a second written on it by anybody who wasn't there."

"What does it change?"

"Your place? Nothing. You're provisional, Third Division, on a sponsor's line, with the conditions that you had on Friday. I've added none and I've taken none away." She almost smiled. "It changes what the next Chair finds in the drawer. I'm told that's what a record is for."

She touched the second paper.

"Miss Dray has written to me from an infirmary bed, in pencil. She asks that the mast condition stand for any candidate ever seated with more than one door. She says that it worked, and that she objected to you twice and would again. I've granted it. It's her rule now; I've put her name on it." The third paper. "And a Mr. Adlam has written, under his own hand, about sixteen pens. I've filed that beside Mr. Bexley's. I have not the least idea what to do with either."

"Nor has Noa."

"Then we are three." She turned her eyes to the door. "Orla. You've brought me something."

"My account," said Orla. She did not get up. "As her sponsor. What I'd seen and what I hadn't, what I said on that rail, and that I said I didn't know it would work. And what it cost her. If a hand's the price of a thing my candidate does, I sign for the hand. I didn't, the last time. A board sealed it for me and I let them."

"I was on that board."

"I know you were."

They looked at one another, the Chair and the Torch, across twenty years and a girl's bandaged hand. Kiva kept very still.

"I'll countersign it," said Ysra. "Not as Chair. As the ward-holder who put her name to his entry. It ought to have two of ours on it." She drew the paper toward her, and took up her pen, and stopped. "You wrote to him. I heard that you had."

"In the seventh month. Four lines."

"Has he answered?"

"No," said Orla. "I didn't write it to be answered. I wrote it so that it would have been written."

She went up the stair that creaked on Tuesday, since it was within the week.

It was the Ferren who witnessed her. Kiva had not met the old woman before. She sat at the oak table with her river pilot's squint, and watched a girl of seventeen take up a steel pen in her right hand and hold a page flat with the heel of her left.

*Cold. Saturday the 17th, at the Pit, about half past two in the afternoon. After three doors in sequence, under direction, with a hold's hem carried and planted for ten counts. Cold before, during and after.*

*I did not ask it anything. It did not do anything. K.R.*

"You've a tidy hand for somebody writing round two fingers," said the Ferren.

"It's the other hand. I only hold the paper with this one."

"So I see." The old eyes went down the column, past *Cold,* and *Cold,* and *Cold,* to the one long entry at the head of it, and back. "I sat four-and-thirty years in this chair hearing *nothing* read. I laid my fist down against recalling it in the summer, and I'd do so again. But I'll tell you what I told the Sag. A plate that does something is not the plate we've been sitting over." She blotted the line herself. "I'd begun to wonder whether we'd all been asking it the wrong thing. *What does it do.* It's on the first leaf, in print. I've never thought to ask what else was happening when it did."

Kiva looked at her.

"Don't mind me. I'm seventy, and I've no business having notions." The Ferren closed the canvas book. "Go down by the left of the stair. The third step's been loose since Corran's day. She used to jump it."

Healer Aske gave it the week that he had promised, and on Saturday he took the linen off for the last time.

He had the towel, and the pin. He had also a penny, and a cup of water from the kettle, and a scrap of the waxed cord that catchers used.

"Eyes shut."

She shut them.

She felt the pin on her little finger, and the third, and said so. She felt it on her thumb. She felt it, sharp and at once, in the middle of her palm, and at the heel of her hand, and on the wrist.

Then there was a long time when nothing happened.

"I've had the pin in the first finger for a slow ten," said Aske's voice. "I'm pressing rather hard. I'd be bleeding you if I pressed harder."

"Something's pushing."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

"And here?"

"The same. Less."

He put the penny between her two fingers and her thumb. She could feel it against the thumb. Against the fingers it was only that there was something there, as one knows through a boot that one is standing on a stone.

"Edge or flat?"

"I don't know."

"The cup. Is that hot?"

She had two fingers in water. She knew that from the sound.

"I can't tell."

"The cord. I'm laying it over them. Tell me if it's crossed."

She could not have told him that there was a cord. She opened her eyes. It lay across the two fingertips, over and then under, as plain as print, and she felt nothing of it whatever.

Aske took it away. He dried her fingers himself, one at a time, on the towel, and capped his pen before he had written anything.

"Then I'll tell you," he said. "I said that I should, when I'd a week to do it with. They've come back as far as they're coming. They'll bend. They'll grip. They'll tell you when something's pushing them and when it's stopped, which is a good deal more than nothing, and you're to use it. They'll not tell you edge from flat, or hot from cold, or one thread from two. That's gone."

"For good."

"I've never seen it come back from there. I looked for a case. I wrote to the Order's infirmarian, since they'd been civil enough to offer, and she was civil enough to answer." He folded the towel. "She says that their year's rest is for a hand that's hungry. It does nothing for one that's been emptied. She says that she's sorry, and that she thought I'd want it plainly."

"So the door on the hill—"

"Was for before. It was a real door. You chose not to go through it, in the seventh month, with your eyes open, and you told your aunt why. I've seen the line; she showed me." He looked at her over the card. "I don't say that you chose wrong. There's a girl with her leg in a board this morning who'd say otherwise, and she's no friend of yours. I say that it was a choice, and it had a price, and this is the price. I've had patients who wanted me to tell them it was bad luck."

"It wasn't luck."

"No." He wrote at last. It was a short line. "There's two drams of ward-trace sealed at the ash office with your team's name on it. I'll save you the asking. It's no use for this. Keep it for somebody whose hand's only starved."

She looked at the two fingers.

They lay beside the others, a little straighter. They were not white. They were the same color as the rest of her. Nobody who saw that hand on a table would know that there was anything the matter with it, unless they had sat near it in a room and knew how the old wall-holders carried theirs.

No rank would change it. She had never thought that one would, and she found that she had wanted to be told so all the same.

"What do I do?"

"What your aunt did. What that catcher of Sorn's has done for six-and-twenty years." Aske handed her the card. It had six figures in a column, and under them a line ruled across. "You find out which of the other eight will do each thing, and you ask somebody for the rest. I'm told that you've been practicing."

Pike had them throw the sheet in the Graywater hall yard that evening until he had stopped wincing, and it took eleven times.

It was not a shift. Rhea Sorn had sent a boy down with a card, four words long. *Yard. Six. No van.* They had come as they were, the four who had stood the Regional and Pru, with the purse sheet on Perrin's handcart, and found a square grey man under a hooded lamp with his hands behind him.

"Up," said Pike. "Again. Rowan, you're setting that left on the hem as though it could tell you something."

"It can tell me there's a hem."

"Then use it for that and nothing else. Heel of the hand. Last two fingers hooked. The first two straight out, clear of it; they're passengers, and passengers don't hold the rail." He walked round. "You had that grip for a ward, I'm told. From the first-year. It's a catcher's grip, whoever taught it. Up."

She threw it again. The canvas lay flat. She brought the neck in at her elbow, slower by a count, as she had since the summer.

"Now the cord," said Pike. "All three turns. By yourself."

"I can't feel the cross."

"I know that you can't. Do it."

She did it. She made the first turn, and the second, and at the third she had the cord over her two fingers and no way on earth of knowing which side of itself it lay.

"Cord," she said. "Pru. Yours. All of it."

"Mine," said Pru, and had it, and crossed it right.

"There," said Pike. He had stopped beside her. "That's the grip. That's all of it. It isn't in your hand. It's that word, said before you've fumbled and not after." He held out his own scarred right palm, with the groove worn across it and the one finger that had been a passenger since he was thirty. "I've caught six-and-twenty years on this. I've been thought a great hand by myself. I never sealed a neck in my life that somebody wasn't kneeling at the other hem to take it if I slipped. I only didn't say so." He closed the hand. "You'll have to say so. Every time. You'll hate it for about a year."

"And then?"

"Then you'll be a catcher. You'll have noticed that there's no other kind."

They went on. On the eleventh throw he walked the whole way round and said nothing.

There were people on the hall steps who had come out to watch. One of them came down into the yard when the sheet was rolled. She had a broken nose, and her tunic was buttoned to the neck.

"Well," said Greta Stroud.

"Miss Stroud."

"I asked you a thing on a stair. In the sixth month. You said you'd tell me when you knew."

Kiva had not forgotten. She had thought about it, on and off, for three months, and she had never once got an answer ready, since every one that she tried had a shine on it.

"What the dram was for," she said. "I think it was so that I could feel I'd paid. For your hands. It didn't pay for anything. Your company had it covered." She looked at the linen, which she was still carrying in her pocket, out of habit. "This isn't for your hands either. I'd like to say it was. It was for a leg, and it was mine to spend. I only know now what it's like when a thing's actually paid, and it wasn't that."

Stroud looked at her for some time.

"Mm," she said. "That's two things out of you I've half liked. You're getting to be expensive." She jerked her chin. "Your cuff's undone."

It was. Kiva had dressed that morning with eight fingers, and the left cuff had beaten her. A button wanted an edge found, and a hole, and one thread told from two.

"Give it here," said Stroud.

She did up the button. She did it quickly and without gentleness, with two hands that had been six weeks in linen, as one does up a child's.

"I'd somebody do mine till the seventh month," she said. "My coat, and my boots, and a spoon held for me the first fortnight. I've never hated anything so much. I'd have bitten the girl if I'd had the use of my teeth." She let go of the wrist. "Let them. That's all I've got. It's not advice. It's only that I was no good at it, and it took me a month longer than it need have."

She went back up the steps.

Anwen was folding the last of the cord. She had watched all that from the handcart, and she waited until the yard was nearly empty.

"I saw Mace on Monday," she said. "After the board was read. He'd told me to."

"I know. You said he would."

"I haven't told my mother yet. I haven't told anybody." Anwen put the cord down. She looked, for a moment, exactly seventeen. "He opened the drawer. He's got a relief's place on the night van's line for next summer, a whole season of it, and he's put my name on it. Not Stroud's seat. One of my own. He said he'd watched a first-year hold a Flask's face for the best part of twenty minutes with three students beside her and it dipped once, at the end, by a hand, and came up."

"It did. I saw it dip."

"You weren't to be watching."

"Finch told me that as well."

Anwen laughed. It was the first time that Kiva had heard that laugh since a kitchen table in the spring, and she had not known until then how much she had been listening for it.

"I wanted to tell you first," said Anwen. "I've been wanting to since Monday. I found that I could. So that's a thing that's changed." Her face was level again, and very clear. "I'm done minding. I said in the yard that I wasn't, and I wasn't. You asked me on that floor. You said *Pryce, the gate, on back,* with a thing on your shoulder that was going to cost you two fingers, and you still asked. I'm done." She took up the cord again. "I've not forgotten. I looked, and those aren't the same."

"No."

"You're not to say anything handsome."

"I haven't got anything."

"Good." She held out the folded sheet, and Kiva took the other end of it, with her right hand and the heel of her left, and they walked it in.

Mara took her across to the Ferren pump house on Monday, the twenty-sixth, at four, and made her stand in the doorway.

"You're not to come past that sill. It's a bank of four-and-twenty, charged, and you've a certificate that says three paces. I only want you to see what I've been looking at for a week."

It was a long brick room, very clean, that smelled of oil. The three pumps stood at the far end, turning, as they had turned for three hundred years, with a sound like slow breathing. Along the left wall ran the bank. Four-and-twenty cells in a row, each with its drum, each with a needle under glass.

Every needle in the row was nodding. They went up together, and came down together, like a line of people breathing in a church.

Kiva had seen twelve needles do that once, on a load board, for two minutes.

"That's nine days," said Mara. She had gone down the row to the ninth, and laid her left palm on its drum. "It fell in at twenty past two on the seventeenth. I've the keeper's book. It hasn't missed a beat since. The pumps haven't dropped a stroke; I'll say that first, since it's worth more than the rest. The low streets are dry."

"Then what's wrong with it?"

"Nothing, yet. That's what's wrong with it." Mara took her hand away. "A bank that keeps one time is one engine. That's very fine in a refinery, where there's an operator with his hand on it and a spare bank behind. This is a municipal bank. It was built so that a cell could fail and its neighbors never know. Now if one drifts, all four-and-twenty drift. If one's fouled, I don't know what the other three-and-twenty do." She came back up the room. "Rusk has had the Works' best in here since Sunday week. He's had a man from Halden's technical office, who was very civil and of no use at all. They can't take it out. Nobody's ever taken one out. They can quench the lot and fill them fresh, and that's four hours with no pumps, in a wet autumn."

"And they won't."

"They'll have to. But that isn't what I brought you to see."

She had the trunk book under her arm. She opened it on the sill, between them, at a page ruled in her own hand.

"The Brickfields trunk. Every drum on it, from this door to the Compact Quarter. Nineteen went on Wednesday; I've its baseline for six years, and on Wednesday it stopped being a number and became a beat. Twenty went on Friday. Twenty-one last night." Her finger moved down the column. "It's walking. A drum every two days, near enough. It isn't in the copper; they've had links open the whole length. It goes all the same."

Kiva followed the finger.

There were nine more lines below it. At the foot of the page, in Mara's small exact print, was the name of the last drum on the trunk. She knew it. She had sat beside it for six months, on a bench by a cart-shed door.

*Core house, the Crooked Line School.*

"Eighteen days," said Mara. "If it keeps its time. I'd not wager that anything will, this year."

Somewhere over the roofs, toward the Sag, a bell had begun to ring. It rang two strokes and a pause, and two again. As they listened another took it up, farther off, three and three; and then a third, from the river, on a code that Kiva did not know.

"That's mast five," said Mara. "And the weigh-house. And I think that's the weir." She shut the book. "The Home Line posted a notice on Saturday. No tear's to be held for a licensing until further word. I read it twice. It didn't give a reason."

"It wouldn't. Lowen says they're not saying anybody showed them."

"No. I shouldn't, either, if I were they."

Kiva put her right hand flat on the brick of the doorpost.

She could feel it. It came up out of the floor of the pump house, through the sill, slow and even and many. It was the hum of the sheds. It was a bag on an iron bridge at four in the morning, and a white cord across a girl's leg.

She put her left hand beside it.

The palm felt it, and the heel, and the last two fingers. The first two lay on the brick. They told her that there was a wall.

"I can't weigh it," she said.

"No. Nor can I, and I've a license." Mara looked down the long row of nodding needles. "I told a watch officer in the summer that every device in my house had a meter and a way to shut it down. I've found the meter for this. I've been reading it for nine days." She tucked the book under her arm. "I've not found the other."

They walked back along the Brickfields road together, toward the tram.

Vane had come to the shop on Sunday, with his hat off, and had asked her what he should write in the third column, now that her healer knew. She had told him. He had written it standing at the counter, in the back of a ledger older than the school, under the direction that he had given her and the cost that he had put first.

*Cost, after: the first two fingers of the left hand, without fine feeling. Not returned.*

He had held the pen a moment longer. Then he had said that E.K. had gone back in the autumn, and added a line about a roll, and that he had no roll to add her to.

"I've one," Kiva had said.

She had it under her arm now. It was a canvas field book, soft at the corners, with a spring and a summer in it. She had written the line on Sunday night, at the kitchen table, where it would be read. She had put it on the right, in the column that asked for no reason at all, and Mara had laid the brass weight on the page without a word.

*Rowan, K. Catcher. Hand as before.*

The bells were still ringing when the tram came.
