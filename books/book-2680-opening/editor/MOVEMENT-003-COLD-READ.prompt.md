# Chapter 17 — Kiva: Twenty Handfuls

Orla Dane drew a line across the Old Yard lane with the end of her stick.

It was Tuesday night, and the lamp on its hook made the usual yellow room in the dark. The chalk had run out years ago, so the line was only a scrape in the dirt, four paces short of the crooked post. She drew a second mark at the post's foot, a cross, and leaned on the stick to look at the two of them.

"There," she said. "That's the whole of it. You stand on the line. You go from the line to the cross in one bound, on the Ember. You come down on the cross with the still door open and nothing else. And the moment your right foot is on the ground, I push."

She showed Kiva the end of the stick. It had an iron ferrule, worn bright.

"Where?"

"Left shoulder. Hard enough to move a girl. Not hard enough to move a stance." Orla lowered herself onto her crate with her bound leg out. "If I can't move you, and your line stays cold, and there's no metal on your legs when I look, then the speed went across. That's a sequence. One bound of one. It's the smallest one I could think of."

Kiva looked at the four paces. She had crossed forty in the Cellhouse with a husk behind her. They did not look like much.

"One bound," she said. "I can do three."

"You can do three and land on a knee that's been strapped for a month. One. Low. You come down on the right." Orla's crooked face did not change. "And you'll want the words for how it goes wrong, because it will, and I'd like us to mean the same thing. If you shut the first door and stand there a while and then open the second, the speed's gone into your boots and the dirt. You've put the work down. That's a drop. If you open the second while the first is still in the doorway, they'll fight, and your line will tell you so. That's a jam. You've had one of those. It was on a run plate."

"I remember it."

"A drop gets you pushed over. A jam gets you burned. So you've a word, and it's the same word the warden gave you. *Off.* Yours to say, not mine. The line warms, you say it, and you shut everything you've got and fall down like a sack if that's what it takes." She tapped the ferrule on the crate. "I'd rather pick you up than quench you. Is that understood?"

"Yes."

"Then settle your ash, and let's see how bad it is."

The ration came in a wax-stoppered glass no bigger than a thimble. Third Division drew half a dram a week from the Low Lane store, on Mondays, signed for, and it was meant to last through five afternoons of ordinary practice. Kiva had spent a few hundredths of this week's on Monday's drill. She tipped the rest into her left palm now and closed her fingers, and felt the small warmth go up her wrist and settle.

She stood on the line.

*Ember.* The metal came off the cuff on her left forearm and ran, cool and quick, down her sides and her thighs, and closed round both calves in two dull copper struts. She bent her knees. She did not think about the knee.

She went.

It was a good bound. It was low and flat, and the cross came up under her exactly where she had meant it to. She shut the Ember as her right boot touched. She felt the struts let go and the metal start for home, and she counted one, as she had counted it four hundred times in this lane, and reached down for the weight of the ground.

The stick took her in the shoulder.

She sat down in the dirt.

"Drop," said Orla.

"I was planted."

"You were planted a breath after I hit you. I felt you arrive." Orla had not moved from the crate. She had only straightened her arm. "You came down, and you stopped, and then you asked the ground for its weight. That's your drill. It's a very clean drill. Up."

Kiva got up. The seat of her trousers was damp, and her knee had noticed the landing and was thinking it over.

"The stop's the thing I'm meant to carry," she said. "Isn't it? The speed. So I can't stop first."

"I'd have thought not."

"So the still door's got to be open when I land."

"I didn't say that. You said that." Orla looked at her steadily. "Go on, then. Try it your way. You know your word."

She stood on the line again. *Ember.* The struts closed. She went, and this time, in the last arm's length of the bound, with the cross rushing at her, she reached for the stance before she had let the Ember go.

It was like putting a foot down on a stair in the dark and finding another foot already there.

The two of them met in her. The weight came up out of the ground, and the speed had not finished going forward, and each of them wanted the whole of her legs. The struts shivered on her calves. Something tore, lightly, along her breastbone, a thread of heat laid on the skin from the inside.

"One," said Kiva. "*Off.*"

She shut them both. She did it badly, and all at once, and for the length of a stumble she was a girl with no doors at all, going forward at the speed of a Stride into an old black post. She got her right hand up. The post took the rest. It was two hundred years old and banded with iron, and it did not give her anything back.

She stood with her forehead against the wood and breathed.

"Jam," said Orla. "And a called one. You said it inside the breath. That's the only part of that I liked, and I liked it a good deal." A pause. "Is the line still warm?"

Kiva put two fingers of her right hand to her breastbone, through the shirt. The bottom thread was hot, and it was already cooling.

"Going."

"Then sit down till it's gone."

She sat on the other crate. Her hand had gone on up from the breastbone without her leave, to the cord at her collar, and she felt the disk through the cloth. She did not take it out. It was the shape and the cold of a coin left on a windowsill.

"It doesn't know either," said Orla.

Kiva let go of it.

"I wasn't asking it."

"No. You were only seeing whether it had an opinion." Orla's voice was dry, and it was not unkind. "I've sat on this crate for three weeks watching you not do that. Don't start again because the work's got hard. I can tell you a drop from a jam, because I've a stick and two eyes. I can't tell you what the right one feels like. I've never seen one. Neither has that." She nodded at Kiva's collar. "So there are three of us in this yard who don't know, and you're the only one who can find out."

They did it twice more that night.

The third was a drop, because she was frightened of the jam, and she let the Ember go so early that she came down as nothing but herself and had to catch her whole weight on her right leg. The fourth was a jam, because she was ashamed of the drop. She called *off* on the fourth before the line had properly warmed, and fell over the cross on her hands, and lay there for a moment with her cheek in the dirt of the Old Yard, thinking about nothing at all.

"That's enough," said Orla. "How's the glass?"

Kiva did not need to look at the glass. The ash was in her, and she could feel how much. It was the same sense that told her when a bench cell wanted filling.

"More than half gone."

"In four goes."

"The drill takes hardly any." She sat up. "I've done the drill two hundred times on a ration. I didn't know the other way cost."

"Nor did I. I told you. I've never seen it." Orla reached for her stick and got herself onto her feet, slowly, with the grunt she allowed herself after dark. "So now we know one new thing, and it's a dull one. You can afford to be wrong about eight times a week. I'd spend them carefully."

Warden Druce had borrowed a hat stand.

It stood on the cast-room table on Wednesday afternoon, an ugly thing of black iron with a ring of hooks at the top, and it had clearly come from the porter's lodge, because the porter's own umbrella was still hanging on it. To the upright the warden had tied eight flour bags, one above the other, like buttons up the front of a coat. Each bulged. A length of red cord ran through all eight.

He had drawn a face on the top one, in charcoal. It was not a good face.

The benches were full. There were thirty-one teams on the list, and every one of them had sent its blade and its catcher at the least. Kiva sat between Kellan and Anwen with the field book open on her knee. Perrin sat on the end of the row, very upright, in the manner of a boy whose ribs had been strapped that morning by a healer with no sense of humor.

Anwen had come straight from the tram. She had been back from the Ferren culverts for a day, after two nights of carrying a lamp behind Graywater's ward line, and she had the grey, polished look of somebody who has slept in the afternoon and not enjoyed it.

"A Handful," said Druce. He held up one grey-stained finger. "One knot. Four lent legs. You've all cut one or caught one, or you'd not be in this room. Now. A Flask is twenty Handfuls in the yield book. Who wants to tell me what it looks like?"

Nobody did.

"A big dog," said somebody at the back, at last.

"Hm. That's what I'd have said at your age, and that's what's printed on the cover of the penny books, and it's wrong." The warden laid his hand flat on the top bag. "A pattern that holds up a dog won't hold up twenty dogs' worth of ash. It hasn't the strength. So when a tear's wide enough to let twenty Handfuls through at once, the ash doesn't make one large knot. It makes eight. One above the other, on one cord. The Home Line calls it a string. Each knot's about sixty drams by the book, and each lends its own limbs, and the whole affair stands about as high as the roof of a tram."

He let them look at the hat stand.

"The top one reads. That's what I've drawn the face for. It turns to the brightest worked thing it can feel, the same as a Handful does, only it feels a good deal further. Twenty paces. Five-and-twenty. The other seven don't read at all. They walk where the top one's pointed."

Kellan had stopped writing. He was looking at the red cord.

"So you cut the top," he said.

"So you cut the top. Stand up, Mr. Renn, since you've a voice. What happens then?"

"Sixty drams come down. The count opens."

"And the other seven?"

Kellan was silent for a moment. Kiva watched him work it through. She had seen him do the same thing over a load board in the Cellhouse.

"They're still standing," he said slowly. "They've no reader. So it's blind."

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"That's the right answer, and I'm obliged to you for it. The book says a slow hundred. I've seen eighty. After that the next knot down wakes, and it starts to read, and you've a Flask again that's one rung shorter and has just watched you kneel on the floor with your hands full." Druce took a penknife from his apron. "The yield book calls a knot a rung, because a string's a ladder when you come to climb down it. You go from the top. One rung, and you catch it. Then the next. You can't light a blade inside three paces of an open count. So you can't cut the second until the first is sealed or you've walked the thing off somewhere else."

He cut the cord above the top bag.

The charcoal face slumped. The bag split along a seam he must have unpicked beforehand, and sixty drams of dead ash came down the front of the hat stand in a soft grey sheet and heaped on the table, and spilled off it, and went on spilling.

Kiva had never seen sixty drams fall. It was ten of her tins, all at once, and it looked like more.

"Sixty counts," said the warden mildly. "Who's got a pan?"

There was a small unhappy laugh along the benches.

"No. You'd be there till supper. A Flask's caught on a purse sheet. Six paces square, waxed, lead in the hem, and a cord run through the hem like the string of a bag. The catcher sets it and calls it. Three more take the corners. You bring it up round the fall and you haul the cord, three turns and a seal, and that's a bag. Bagged inside the count is tinned, for the book. You carry it off dark and pan it at a table in your own time." He wiped the knife. "Four people to a bag. Which means that while your rung's on the floor, your blade's dark and your ward's dark and your Carry's got a corner in his fist. Your whole team is on its knees. And seven knots are standing behind you with nobody minding them."

He looked along the rows.

"So a Flask isn't done by a team. It's done by a company. Four teams, sixteen hands, and a foreman off a chartered crew who answers for the lot. Your team takes one rung, hold and cut and bag and carry, the whole of it. The other three mind the string while you do. Then you get up and mind it for them. The foreman's own people take the bottom four, because the last two rungs carry the legs, and when the seventh goes, it sits down wherever it happens to be. You'll not be asked to stand under that this year."

"What's the mark?" That was a Stonehand voice.

"Fifty of your sixty, bagged inside the count, at Second or better. Nothing printed. Nothing fouled. Nobody hurt. And then the foreman's four columns, for the company. Closed, hurt, what still works, what was tinned." Druce folded the knife. "It isn't a fight. I want that understood before any of you goes near the Pit. A Handful's a fight, a short one. A Flask is a schedule. The hands that keep it are the catcher's, and the best blade in this room is going to spend most of that morning standing in the dark being told *not yet*."

Kellan sat down.

"I've had practice," he said, to nobody.

The companies were posted on the cast-room door.

There was a crush for it. Kiva let the crush go by, because of the knee, and so she read the fifth sheet over the shoulder of somebody who had got there first and was reading it very carefully.

*Fifth company. Wednesday the 26th, forenoon. The Pit, Brickfields. Foreman: H. Vane, Home Line.*

*T7 (17th). T4 of '78 (18th). T33 (19th). T12 (20th).*

The shoulder was broad, and the head above it was shaved.

"It seems we're a company," said Lowen Brask.

He turned round. He wore a plain grey coat instead of a Hall tunic, and there was a notebook under his arm. He looked exactly as calm as he had looked on his knees in the sand with a line judge counting over him.

"You're on a license team," said Kiva. It was a stupid thing to say. It was what came out.

"Old Four. The year puts a number after it so we don't get your post." He nodded at the sheet. "We tinned twenty and six-tenths two springs ago. Last year the board took nine teams to a Flask. The year before, it took six. We've been eighteenth on somebody's list for a long while." He considered her, mildly. "I'm the Carry. I'd not have picked a company with you in it, if I'd been asked. I don't mean that rudely. I mean I haven't finished thinking about you, and now I'll have to do it quicker."

"We lost to you."

"By two. I've the lane card pinned over my bed." He said it without a smile, and then there was one, slow and a little rueful. "There's a return on the eighteenth. Your court, your book. I've been reading your book. It's a strange one."

"I haven't read it," said Kiva.

"No. Home sides never do." Brask tucked the notebook more firmly under his arm. "I'd read rule nine, Miss Rowan. I mean that kindly as well. Good afternoon."

He went away down the passage at his slow rolling walk. Kiva found that Perrin was beside her, holding his side.

"He taught me to fall down," said Perrin. "When I was twelve. It took him a month. He said I was the only boy he'd ever met who argued with the floor." He looked after the grey coat. "He's told you where to look. He does that. It's maddening. You go and look, and it's true, and you still lose."

"What's rule nine?"

"I'm Stonehand. I can't read your book. It'd be disloyal." He shifted his ribs. "Ask your captain."

Someone laughed behind them. It was short and had no pleasure in it.

Selka Dray was leaning on the passage wall with her arms folded. Her cropped red hair was damp, as if she had come from a lane. She held a slip of yellow card between two fingers.

"Reserve blade," she said, when she saw Kiva look at it. "In case somebody's Ember breaks an ankle. That's what a four-second kill's worth this spring." Her eyes went to the fifth sheet. "The Pit. Did any of you look at where it gets its power? I did. I'd nothing else to do on Sunday. There's a pump bank across the road that keeps half the Ferren streets out of the river." She pushed herself off the wall. "Fifth company. I wonder who thought of that."

She walked off the other way.

Nobody had answered her on the Sag Line either. Kiva looked at the yellow card going away down the passage, and wished very much that she had a thing to say that was not *I know*.

Mara Rowan had brought eleven years of books to the Works in a handcart.

She had not needed the cart. The boy from the bakery had offered to carry them, and Tobin had offered the van, and she had said no to both and pushed the cart through the Thursday morning streets herself. She wanted her hands occupied, and she wanted to arrive knowing how heavy it was.

Examiner Rusk had the books in three stacks by ten o'clock. He was a broad, tidy man of about sixty, with a clerk's linen guards over his cuffs and an operator's thick, scarred thumbs. He had been through every one of them. She could see his slips of paper sticking out of the pages like tongues.

"Operator Rowan," he said. "Sit down. I'm going to say what I've found, in the order I found it, and then what I've decided. If I've a fact wrong, stop me. If you only dislike it, let me finish."

Mara sat.

"The household filing of the thirteenth. A resident minor woke with her hand on your flat panel. The panel took a three-door pattern. The shared core tripped at sixteen minutes to three. You quenched the panel by hand, in the dark, and you were at the records office when it opened to file against yourself." He turned a slip. "I've had thirty years of filings. Most arrive a week late with a lawyer's letter. That goes in your favor, and so I've written it."

He turned another.

"The condition of 2677 persists. You say so in your own hand. The minor has a sponsor's certificate, which I've read. It keeps her off cells. That's a help. It isn't a cure."

"No," said Mara.

"Mast six." Rusk laid a half sheet on the desk between them, covered in a quick slanting hand that she knew. "You declined to recalibrate it. You wrote down why. On the twenty-eighth it rang four minutes ahead of its neighbors, and a Home Line officer has taken the trouble to tell me what he did with the four minutes." He looked at her over the paper. "I spent a morning trying to decide whether that was skill or luck. Then I read your reason again. You hadn't thought of the bell. You'd only refused to hide a line you didn't understand. I've concluded it was neither. It was a habit, and a good one."

Mara said nothing. She had learned on a great many benches that the moment after praise was when the meter fell.

"There's a bench cell behind you," said Rusk. "A plain lamp tune. Read it for me, please."

She got up and went to it. It was a small municipal cell in an oak cradle, humming to itself. She laid her hand on the case, and felt the tune come up slow and even through her fingers, with a little drag in it where somebody had been impatient.

"Lamp tune. Steady. It was laid in a hurry and smoothed afterward. I'd give it eight months before it wants doing again."

"Seven, I'd have said. Near enough." He had not looked at the cell. He was looking at her arm. "That's your left hand, Operator Rowan."

The room was quiet. Down in the yard a meter van was being loaded, and somebody dropped a crate.

She could have said that she favored it. He would have had no way to know.

"The middle of my right palm has had no feeling since the Linden Row cell," said Mara. "Three years. I can hold a wrench with it. I can't read with it. I taught the left." She turned round. "I didn't declare it. I told myself that a reading hand was a reading hand, and the license didn't say which. I knew better than that when I told it to myself."

Rusk wrote for some time.

"Thank you," he said. "I'd guessed in the third book. Your figures don't change. Your handwriting on the cell tags does." He blotted the page. "Here's what I've decided, then. I don't average, Operator Rowan. People ask me to. They say she did one thing well and one thing ill, call it even. A cell doesn't call it even, and I'm not paid to."

He set his thick hands on the desk.

"Your Grade Two is suspended to the autumn sitting. It is not revoked. You'll re-sit the practical in the ninth month, and the left will be entered as your reading hand, and after that nobody will have cause to wonder. Until then you tune nothing, refill nothing, and seal nothing. The flat panel stays quenched and tagged. For three years of an undeclared hand, I'm obliged to fine you a week's fees. I've gone through your books for the smallest week I could find. It was the one with the flood."

Mara breathed in, and out.

A third of the money, until autumn. She had expected to lose it for good. She found that she was not grateful, and not angry either. It was the right size. That was all she had ever asked of a number.

"Repair and reconciliation continue," said Rusk. "And there's this, which is not a kindness, so don't thank me. The Home Line has written to the board about what they're calling a dirty spring. The board wants the baseline books gone through, every mast on the Sag Line and every drum on the Brickfields trunk, by somebody who won't flatten a line to make it sit. I've forty operators who can read a drum. I had exactly one who wrote *recal. would mask trend.*" He pushed a thick canvas-backed book across the desk. "Records rate. It pays about half of what the tuning did. That's the trunk's own book. Start there."

She took it. It was heavier than it looked.

"The Brickfields trunk," said Mara. "That's the Ferren pump bank."

"Twenty-four cells, three pumps, and a branch off to that clay hole the Circuit plays its games in. One link between them. I'd like to know whether its baseline's been creeping as well, and since when." He was already drawing the next file toward him. "That's all. Take your cart round to the side door. The front steps are murder on the wheels."

"Suspended," said Perrin. "Not revoked. I'd like to shake that man's hand. I'd also like to put him in a sack."

"She says it's the right size."

"She would. She's a Rowan. You'd all thank a person for hitting you if he'd measured first."

They were in the back room of the Pryce laundry, among the hampers, with the team book open on an ironing board. It was Thursday evening. Through the wall the big machines were turning over, and the air smelled of starch and hot linen. Kiva had come down from the flat with the taste of a quiet supper still in her mouth. Mara had eaten with the canvas-backed book open beside her plate. On the first page there was a diagram of a bus, a long black line with twenty-four small squares on it, and one thin branch going off through a mark like a hinge.

*S. link. Pinned and locked, 2661.*

Kiva had read it upside down. She read most things upside down.

"Two floors," said Kellan. He had his own paper beside the book. "Seven-tenths each, paid. We've nothing else on the page but the reserve, and that's sealed. So it's these two, and whatever the school gives us for nothing." He looked up. "I'd like the first tomorrow. A skinned one, if the Kennel's still throwing them, and I'd be surprised if it's stopped. I've cut that skin once in my life. I'd like not to be early the second time."

"And the sheet," said Anwen. "The warden will lend us one. I asked. If we're to bag sixty at the Pit, I'd like to have bagged twenty-four somewhere first. All four of us. I've never held a corner."

"I've held four hundred kilos," said Perrin. "I expect a corner's within me."

"Your ribs are within you. That's all the healer wants to hear about for three weeks." She had her list, and she wrote on it. "The second floor we keep. Mace says the Home Line means to let every catcher and every blade on the list watch a real one, if a tear obliges. There've been ten on the district board now. He thinks one will." She looked at Kiva. "I'd sooner spend the second floor after we know what we're looking at."

It was agreed in about a minute. Kiva noticed that. Three weeks ago, it had taken them an evening and half a pot of tea to agree on a catch kit.

Kellan signed. Perrin signed, with a flourish. Anwen signed, and held the pencil out.

"How's the other thing?" she said. "The yard."

"Four tries. Two of each."

"Each what?"

Kiva told them. She told it plainly, the line and the cross and the stick, the drop and the jam. Perrin winced at the post. Kellan asked how long the bound was, and wrote it down.

Anwen did not write. She sat with the pencil across her knees and a small frown, as she sat when a thing reminded her of another.

"The line changes over at two in the morning," she said. "On the culverts. Graywater doesn't let a ward-holder stand more than four hours. So at two there's a relief, and I thought I knew how that would go. One drops, the other raises. I'd have done it that way." She shook her head. "They don't. The new one comes up behind the old one's shoulder and brings her wall up *inside* the first. And the first one leans on it. I watched her do it. She leaned until hers wasn't holding anything, and then she let it go, and there was never a moment with no wall and never a moment with two. Nobody said *now*. Mace says if you can hear the join, it's a bad one."

"Lean into it," said Kiva.

"Till yours isn't doing anything. Yes." Anwen's eyes were on her. "That's two people. I don't know that it's the same."

"It's the nearest thing anyone's told me."

The Old Yard was cold, and Orla had the lamp lit.

Kiva stood on the line. She had thought about it all the way up from Eastbank on the tram. She would not shut the Ember and then open the still door, and she would not grab for the one while holding the other. She would bring the stance up inside the Stride, as a relief brings up a wall, and lean the speed over into it a little at a time.

*Ember.* The struts closed. She went.

In the air she opened the still door, a crack, gently, and began to lean.

For about as long as it takes to blink, it was lovely. The two of them lay alongside each other in her legs like two hands on one rope. She had time to think that Anwen had been right.

Then the bottom line lit like a fuse.

"One. *Off!*"

She came down on her right foot with nothing open and nothing in her head, and Orla's stick, which had been on its way, stopped a finger's width from her shoulder and stayed there.

"That was a jam," said Orla, "with manners. Sit."

"I didn't grab. I leaned it across. Slowly."

"You held two doors and were polite about it. It's still two doors." Orla lowered the stick. "Who told you to lean?"

"Anwen. It's how Graywater relieves a ward."

"It is. I've stood in that line. And it works because the two walls belong to two different women, and neither of them has to hold both." She was quiet for a moment. "It's the right notion in the wrong place. The work goes across. The doors don't overlap. I don't know how you get the one without the other, Rowan. I know that was a stack. Your line knew it before I did."

The thread along her breastbone was cooling. It took longer than it had on Tuesday.

"Again," said Kiva.

"When it's cold."

*Why:* I keep trying to do it with the doors. Orla says the work goes across and the doors don't. I don't know where the work is when it isn't in either one.

"I don't know."

*What went wrong:* Nothing I could see. 446 of 480 bagged.

*Who was hurt:* Nobody.

*What it cost:* Six seconds of light, Kellan says. I don't know what in ash.

She read that over. Then she filled in the last column, and found that she had more for it than would fit.

*I don't know yet which of those is the thing to learn.*

# Chapter 19 — Kiva: Rule Nine

"It's a caller's rule," said Jude Talley.

He had the home book open on the bench in the Low Lane, a fat brown volume with a cracked spine, and he read from it as if he suspected it of making fun of him.

"*Nine. A change called to a count is declared at the count.*" He looked up. "That's all. That's the whole rule. It's from when the school fought everything in fours with a caller, and the caller sang out what was coming so the other three could move on it. *Talley, turning on two.* Everybody else's book says that's telling the enemy. Ours says the enemy was going to find out anyway, and your own side ought to know first."

"Has anyone used it?" said Kiva.

"Not in my three years. Nobody's had anything worth counting in." He shut the book. His chipped tooth showed when he was thinking. "But I've been to see the lane warden, and he's been to see the Circuit, and here's how they read it. You say *Rowan, changing on three,* and the doors. The flag goes up then. Straight off. And you change on your own *three* and at no other time. Go before it, or after it, and it's undeclared."

"So I don't wait for the flag."

"You wait for yourself. Which I'm told you're good at." He nearly smiled. "It gives us a second back. That's all I want it for. Brask found a second in you at the Terrace and lived in it for two carries."

It was Monday afternoon. The old wooden mast ticked in its corner. Pru Finch sat on the drain cover with her knees up, and Noa was threading a new roll of tape with his tongue between his teeth, and on the bench by the door, with her stick across her lap, sat Orla Dane, who had not been in the Low Lane in anyone's memory and had not said why she was there now.

Jude had chalked the New Lane on the brick. It was two rows of dots and a box.

"Now. Saturday. He watched the Wren House walk round you, and he wrote it down. I saw him." He tapped the box that was Donny. "So I'll tell you what a patient man does with that. He lets you plant. He leaves you the litter. And he takes four still-doors and goes hunting the three of us among the posts, where Noa can't stretch his legs and Pru's wall has ends. He'll take a point a minute and never lay a finger on you."

"I could drag it home."

"On that knee? With the door shut? Keld would take it off you like a hat." Jude rubbed his jaw. "No. If they won't come to the stop, the stop goes to them. Second carry, you declare the Ember. You go where the sandbag goes, on the struts, low, one bound at a time, with the bar out for their wrists. And when we've got it somewhere worth holding, you call rule nine. You come down. You stop. You count your three and you plant on it, with the flag already up."

Kiva looked at the chalk. Then she looked at the bench by the door.

"I come down and stop," she said. "And then plant."

"That's what I said."

"I only mean that there's another way of doing it. If the three fell as my foot did."

Nobody in the Low Lane moved. The mast ticked.

"No," said Orla.

She had not raised her voice. She did not need to in a cart shed.

"Not in a lane. Not with a referee and a board and four hundred people. You've not landed one in my yard, Rowan. You've not come near. I'll not have you find out what a jam does on brick with a still-door's palm coming at you." She turned her head. "Captain. You'll want to know what you're hearing. She's trying to carry the speed of a Stride straight into a stance, with nothing between. If she ever does it, you'll have a fighter who arrives already planted. She's tried six times. She's fallen over six times."

"Twice into a post," said Kiva.

"Once into a post. The second was the ground." Orla's eyes came back to her. "I'm not forbidding the count. The count's lawful and your captain wants it. You'll use it the slow way, with a stop in the middle that a child could see. Tell me you will."

"I will."

"Then I've nothing to say about Saturday." She settled the stick. "Mr. Bexley. I'm told that you have an instrument."

Noa nearly dropped the tape.

He read the mast afterward. Those were Orla's terms. Kiva was to stand under the wire cage and do what she was told, with her back to the drum, and he was to say nothing until she had finished.

"I'll not have you watching a needle home," said Orla. "You've only just stopped watching the metal."

The store had opened at noon. The new week's thimble of ash was in her, warm and whole. She did the drill first, the three doors one after another, with a breath between, and heard the pen scratch behind her.

Then she stood on a crack in the brick and made the seventh try.

It was a drop. She knew it in the air. She had been so afraid of the line lighting that she let the Ember go a full stride early and came down as herself, on her right foot, and reached for the ground like someone feeling for a light cord. Orla did not trouble to get up. She leaned over from the bench and pushed with two fingers, and Kiva sat down on the drain cover beside Pru.

"Hello," said Pru. "You've got brick on you."

The eighth was a jam. It was quick and stupid, and she called it inside the breath and shut the lot, and stood with her hands on her knees while the bottom line burned and cooled.

"That's all," said Orla. "Read it, Mr. Bexley."

Noa had the tape in both hands. His narrow face was pale.

"The drill," he said. "All three doors, start to finish. A hundredth and a bit. That's nothing. That's less than Jude burns lighting up." He moved his thumbs along. "The first of the other two. The one where you sat down. Four hundredths."

"For one bound?"

"For one bound and whatever you did after it. And the second." He stopped. He held the tape nearer the window. "Nine. Nine hundredths. In less than a second. The pen went up the paper like a mouse up a curtain." He looked at her over it. "Kiva, the book says a four-minute hold is twelve. You burned nine while falling over."

"That's the two of them fighting," said Kiva. "It's what the bench measured on Classification Day. Only that was for a slow count of ten."

"Three point one," said Noa. He said it softly. He had clearly thought he would never meet that number in the flesh. "Yes. I see."

He wrote the figures into her book himself, at the bench, under the date, and turned it round so she could check them against the tape. Then he wrote them in his own. Jude watched him do it.

"So every time she misses, it costs her a carry's worth of ash," said Jude slowly.

"A third of a carry. Or three-quarters, if she misses the hot way."

"And she's a thimble a week." The captain was quiet. He was not a boy who did sums for pleasure, and he had done this one. "I'd lend you mine, Rowan. I've thought about it since Thursday, when Bexley told me you'd run dry. I've half a dram, same as you, and five meets, and a blade that wants a lit hour before each one." He shook his head. "I can't. I'd like it known that I looked at it properly."

"I wouldn't have taken it."

"I know. That's part of why I looked."

He took a slip of yellow card from his tunic and turned it over in his fingers. It was the same yellow as Selka Dray's.

"Reserve blade," he said, to no one in particular. "For the Pit. I've a first step and no team. Mine broke up two years ago, when one went home to Saltmere and one went to a brewery. If an Ember in any company turns an ankle, I get a rung." He put it away. "Three years, and that's my Flask. Somebody else's ankle. So you'll forgive me if I'm careful with a thimble."

Pru had been unusually silent. Now she drew a breath that seemed to come from her boots.

"I've been called," she said. "Tomorrow. There's a little tear on the river flats, a Handful one, and they've four seats left over from teams that weren't passed fit, and one's mine." Her voice went up. "I've failed the warden twice. I don't call *off*. I say what's happening instead. I can hear myself doing it. I stand there going *oh, it's got round me, oh, it's on the cloth,* like a woman at a window."

"So call off," said Jude.

"What if there's nothing to call it at?"

"Then you'll have called it early, and it costs nothing. The warden said so."

"The warden," said Pru darkly, "has never had to listen to me."

Kellan Renn came to the Old Yard that night with his coat buttoned to the neck.

He had not been asked. He stood at the edge of the lamplight until Orla nodded him to the spare crate, and then he sat, and took out the folded paper, and watched Kiva walk to the line.

"Before she goes," he said. "May I ask something? About the bound."

"Ask her. It's her bound."

"When do you shut it?"

Kiva thought. It was harder to say than to do. "When I'm coming down. Near the end. When I can see the cross."

"Why then?"

"Because I need it till then. I'm in the air."

"You're in the air because you left the ground. The Ember did that at the line. After your feet are off, it isn't doing anything." He said it with the mild puzzlement of somebody pointing out that a shoe was unlaced. "A Stride's a push. It's paid for when you go up. I shut mine at the top of every bound and open it again as I land for the next. That's all the rhythm is. You've been carrying the door open the whole way across for no reason, and then trying to get rid of it in the last yard, when you've the least time."

Orla had turned her head. She said nothing.

"I learned it for the skin cut," said Kellan. "Light after the foot. It's the same habit turned round. You want the metal home before the foot."

"At the top," said Kiva.

"At the top. You'll come down exactly as fast. I promise you. The ground doesn't know whether you've a door open."

She stood on the line and thought about that.

It changed the shape of the whole thing. She had been picturing a doorway, and herself jammed in it with a door in each hand. This was not a doorway. It was a gap, a real one, with air under it: the Ember shut at the top, and nothing open, and the ground coming up. There would be a girl in the air with no doors at all, falling at the speed of a Stride.

And the speed would still be there. It would not be in the Ember. It would only be in her, as it was in a thrown stone.

*That's where the work is,* she thought. *When it isn't in either one.*

"Ember," she said aloud.

The struts closed. She went. At the height of the bound, with the lamp swinging under her and the post tilting up, she shut it.

It was the strangest thing she had ever done on purpose. The metal let go and ran for her cuff, and she had time to feel it arrive. She had time to feel that she had nothing, and that she was still moving.

Then the ground was there, and she felt it through her boot, and reached.

The stick took her in the shoulder.

She did not sit down. She went back two steps and kept her feet. Her knee said one sharp word and was silent.

"Drop," said Orla. But she had sat forward on the crate. "A different drop. Your line?"

"Cold. It never stirred."

"No. It had nothing to quarrel with. That's the first of nine where you weren't at any risk of burning." She looked at Kellan for rather a long moment. "I've taught a Stride to forty students, Mr. Renn. I never told one of them that. I didn't know it needed saying."

"I didn't know it was a thing people didn't do."

"You shut it at the top," said Kiva. She was out of breath, and not from the bound. "And I came down with nothing. But I was late again. I opened the still door when I felt the brick. No. The dirt. When I felt it."

"Then that's already late," said Kellan. "I light after the foot because a cut's allowed to be late. It only has to be clean. Yours has to be there when the weight is." He frowned. "How long were you falling? From shut to ground."

She tried to remember it. There had been time to feel the metal get home. There had been time to be afraid.

"About as long as it takes to say *two*."

"Then it's somewhere inside *two*," said Kellan. He wrote it down.

Orla would not let her go again. The knee had spoken, she said, and she had heard it from the crate. Kiva wrote the ninth in the book, a drop and a cold line, and under it the cost, which Noa had not been there to read and which she could only feel: less than the others. A good deal less.

*Why:* The work is in me in the air. The stance has to be open before the ground and after the Ember. I can't feel the ground before it's there.

She did not know what a person did about that.

The notice came on Tuesday, by the hand of Ysra Crest's clerk.

*Objection to the seating of Rowan, K. (prov.) in any Flask company at the Pit. Entered by Dray, S., Second Division, with eleven signatures. To be heard Friday the 17th at eight.*

Kiva read the eleven names. She knew four of them. Two were on teams below the twentieth place, and one had sat next to her on the Kennel benches for a week and had moved his kit.

That was the day that Pru Finch got her license.

She arrived in the Low Lane at four, with mud to the knees and her scarf on backward, already talking.

"I called it! I called *off*! Twice! The first time it had got its foot on Lund's cloth and I said it, three times, exactly like the book, and everybody went dark and walked out, and the officer said *good.* And the second time there wasn't anything there. I'd seen a dog. A real dog, on the bank. I called off on a spaniel." She sat down on the drain cover. "And he passed us anyway. He said he'd sooner license a girl who calls off at spaniels than one who watches. I've a first step. I've never been so embarrassed in my life."

"That's the best thing I've heard since Sunday," said Jude, and for once the tooth showed because he was laughing.

The rest of the week was a long corridor with Friday at the end of it. Kiva did the drill. She did not bound. Orla had looked at the knee on Tuesday night and prescribed two days of standing.

On Friday at eight she sat in the Placement Chair's office with her leg out, and Selka Dray stood.

She had not expected Dray to be good at it.

"I'm not saying she'd mean to." The cropped red head was up, and the voice was steady. Dray had dressed for it, in a pressed Second Division tunic, and she spoke to Ysra and not once to Kiva. "I don't think she meant to in the Cellhouse. That's my whole point. She took her hand off that drum, and the cell went on climbing by itself for half a minute, and it called husks. Nobody had seen a cell do that. Nobody's said since why it did." She laid a paper on the desk. "The Pit hasn't a bank of its own. It runs off the Ferren pumps by one branch. I copied that out of the Works' public sheet. Twenty-four cells with the river behind them. Has anyone in this building asked what her doors do to a trunk?"

Ysra read the paper.

"Sponsor," she said.

"Her certificate keeps her dark," said Orla. "No door inside three paces of open ash or any charged cell. She's kept it on two floors and a field. I've a referee's card and an officer's slate to say so."

"The Cellhouse wasn't inside three paces," said Dray. "The husks came from the far end of the floor."

It was a fair hit. Kiva saw Orla take it.

"Miss Rowan," said Ysra. "You're permitted to speak. I'd advise you to say only what you know."

Kiva had lain awake on it. She had tried a dozen answers, and every one of them had begun with *but*.

"She's right about the cell," she said. "I can't tell you why it called. I've asked, and nobody knows." She made herself go on, to the long plain face and not to the tunic beside her. "What I know is what was different. In the Cellhouse I had my hand on the drum and three doors open. On the Sag Line I'd nothing open at all, and I knelt beside eight drams with a husk walking past, and the pump that was fouled that day wasn't fouled by me." She felt Dray stiffen. She had not meant it as a blow. It was only where the fact lay. "I can't promise what a cell will do. I can promise where my hands are, and that I'll say so out loud first. That's all I've got. If it isn't enough, I'd rather be told now than on the day."

Ysra Crest was silent for some time.

"The objection is noted," she said at last. "It is not upheld. I can't unseat a licensed catcher on a sponsor's certificate because eleven people are uneasy. I'd have no roster left." She squared Dray's paper on the desk. "But it has a fact in it, Miss Dray, and the fact is a good one, and you were the only person in the Compact who went and looked. So. Two conditions on the event. The catcher's mark for Team Seven will be set no nearer than twenty paces to the Kiln's lattice house. And Miss Rowan's file goes entire to the foreman, who may unseat her on the morning for any cause he'll write down."

"That's Vane," said Dray. "He passed her on the Sag."

"He also failed you there. I shouldn't lean on his partiality in either direction." Ysra's voice did not change. "You may renew this on evidence. Not on signatures."

Selka Dray picked up her copy.

"Then I'll be at the Pit," she said. "I've a card that lets me stand on the floor. I'll go and stand where the evidence is going to be."

She went out. She had not looked at Kiva once. Kiva found, to her surprise, that she would have preferred a glare. A glare would have meant it was about her.

The New Lane was three hundred years old.

It lay behind the school's core house, under a slate roof with a long glass lantern down the ridge, and the brick of its floor had been worn into two shallow troughs by ten generations of feet. It was forty paces long and twelve wide. There were no steps in it, no porches, and no sill. There were posts.

They stood in two rows, nine to a side, three paces in from each long wall and four apart. They were oak, black with age, banded in iron. Between the rows ran a lane six paces wide, and behind each row an aisle hardly broader than a doorway.

Kiva knew those posts. She had hit one with her forehead.

But the school had copied its Old Yard here in the year it had money, and had copied it straight. She looked down the southern row to the ninth post, at the far end, by the away line.

It stood as plumb as a mast. Nobody had thought the lean worth building.

"Fighters to the mark," said the referee.

It was the grey braid, and the voice like a dropped crate. She stood at the center of the lane, between the fifth posts, with Donny on his litter at her feet. He had been given a clean coverall for the occasion. Somebody had inked a mustache on him.

"I'm told I've no court of my own here, so I'll run yours. Circuit rules, the same as the Terrace. Three carries of four minutes. A down's a point, two halves a down, the litter over your own line is three, and at time it scores one to the half it lies in. One dram each." Her eyes went along the western four and stopped. "And a rule I read on the tram this morning, with some astonishment. Nine. Rowan. If you call a change to a count, my flag's up when you've said it. You go on your own *three*. Not a hair to either side. I'll be listening to the word and looking at your feet, and if they disagree, I'll score it undeclared and we'll all go home."

"Yes."

"Doors."

"Talley. Ember."

"Finch. Hand."

"Bexley. Ember."

"Rowan. Still."

Across the litter, four people in Stonehand brown stood like furniture.

Hendry Moss was there, and Idony Keld with her long arms, already looking at the knee. The boy with the ears had been given a name by the board, which was Arrand, and seemed no happier for it. Lowen Brask shook Jude's hand, and then Kiva's.

"You read it," he said.

"My captain did."

"Then we've both done our lessons." His calm brown eyes were friendly. There was no other word for it. "I hope it's a good lane, Miss Rowan."

The gallery ran along both walls behind a rail. It was a home crowd, and loud. The benches nearest the western line were full this time. Kiva had looked, and had seen why. They were full of first-years, a great many of them, leaning on the rail. They had not come out of loyalty. They had come to see whether she fell over.

It was better than a man moving his daughter. She supposed it was.

Mara sat beside Orla, with her gloves. Anwen and Kellan were behind them. Across the lane, in a row of brown, with his arms folded over his strapped ribs and an expression of saintly neutrality, sat Perrin Cade.

"Bell," said the referee.

---

Noa was gone before it stopped ringing.

He had Donny by the foot poles in three bounds and dragged him west, four paces, to the fourth posts. Kiva came down the lane at her own pace. She took the head poles. She let her weight go down, and the old brick took it as it had taken everything else for three hundred years.

"Planted."

Pru's ward went up across the lane behind her. Jude stood at her right hand with his blade low.

The four still-doors walked to the fifth posts. And there, without a word, they divided. Two went left and two went right, and they passed behind the rows of posts into the aisles, and came on down both walls like people walking round a pond.

Nobody came down the lane at all.

"Jude," said Kiva.

"I see it. It's what I said." His voice was tight. "Pru, turn it. North side. Noa, don't let Keld get you against—"

But Noa was an Ember who lived on his legs, and the aisle was three paces wide with a wall on one side and oak on the other. He bounded once. He had nowhere to land but in front of Idony Keld. Her long arm came through the gap between two posts like a gaff.

"*Down!* Crooked Line!"

Kiva could not turn round. She was holding seventy kilos of sand that nobody wanted. She heard the rest of it through the back of her head.

She heard Pru's ward ring, and ring, and Pru's voice going up. "That's Moss, he's leaning, that's four, the other one's walked round the *post,* Jude, he's just—" and the flat sound of a palm. "*Down!* Crooked Line!"

She heard Jude take the boy with the ears, clean, somewhere to her left. "*Down!* Stonehand!" And then three pairs of heavy feet closing on one light pair, and the scrape of a blade on a forearm. "Half!" And a grunt. "*Down!* Crooked Line."

It had taken perhaps ninety seconds.

She knelt in the middle of the lane with her hands full, and there was not one member of her team on his feet.

*Shut it and pull,* she thought. It was sixteen paces to the line. She gave the ground its weight back. *Thank you.* She got her good leg under her and heaved, and Donny came, a hand's width, and another, his foot poles scoring the brick.

She had made two paces when a pair of long hands took the foot poles and stopped them.

Kiva planted. She did not need a flag for it; it was the door she had declared. The litter became the floor again.

Idony Keld let go at once. She stepped back and stood with her arms hanging, a pace off. She did not try to pull. She did not try anything.

"I'm only minding you," she said. It was nearly kind.

"*Time!*"

The board turned over. *CROOKED LINE 2. STONEHAND 3.* And in the last column, by her name: *Dram, 0.10.*

She had spent a tenth of a dram in four minutes and scored a point by not moving. It was the most efficient carry of her life. She had been of no more use in it than the mustache.

---

"So," said Jude.

He had sweat in his eyes and a red mark on his ribs. He did not sound surprised, only as if he would have liked to be.

"That's what it looks like. They'll do that twice more, and it'll be eleven to five." He wiped his face on his sleeve. "Second carry. As chalked. Rowan, you're on the Ember. Stay low, stay on your right, and keep that bar between their hands and the poles. Once he sees struts on you, he'll come down the middle for the sandbag. He'll have to. There's no stop to walk round. That's when Noa and I get to work."

"And the count?"

"When we've got it near home and it sticks. Not before." He looked at her hard. "The slow way."

"The slow way."

"Rowan," she told the referee. "Ember."

Lowen Brask, at the far mark, heard it. He turned his shaved head a little, as a man does who has been waiting for a tram and hears it in the next street.

"Bell."

It began well. It began better than anything she had been part of in a sanctioned lane.

She went down the brick on the struts in two low flat bounds, right foot and right foot, and was at the litter as Noa got his hands on it. The bar closed on her fist, dark and ugly and square. And Stonehand came down the lane. All four, shoulder to shoulder, at that slow rolling walk. Jude had been right.

Keld reached for the foot poles. Kiva laid the flat of the bar across her wrist.

"Half! Crooked Line."

It was not much. But she saw Keld's sharp face change, and she understood what the ugly thing on her hand was for in a lane full of open palms. A blade slipped between hands. A bar was a gate. It was a foot of square iron with nothing sharp about it, and it was wherever she put it, and a still-door who wanted the poles had to come through it or go round.

Moss went round. Jude was there.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

Arrand flinched from Noa's stub of a blade, and flinched again. "Half! Half! That's two. *Down!* Stonehand!"

"Lift!" Jude had the head. Noa had the foot. For the first time all afternoon there were only two brown tunics standing, and Donny came off the brick and went west at a run, between the black posts, toward the white line.

Kiva went with him. One bound. She shut the Ember at the top of it, as Kellan had said, and opened it again as she touched, and the rhythm was so much easier than she had ever known that she nearly laughed.

They were six paces from the line when the litter stopped.

Brask had it. He had not run. He had only walked in a straight line to the place it was going to be, and set both hands on the foot poles, and let his weight go down. Keld was beside him a breath later.

Jude and Noa heaved. They might as well have heaved at the school.

"Rowan!"

She was eight paces east of the head, on the wrong side of Keld's reach, where the bar had taken her. Behind the two at the foot, Moss was getting up from his ten. Arrand had three counts to go.

She saw the whole of it at once.

She saw the slow way. A bound to the head. Down. Stop. *Rowan, changing on three.* One, two, three, and then the stance. It was three seconds and a half, and in three seconds and a half, there would be four still-doors on Donny's foot and Jude would be holding his end of nothing.

And she saw the other.

It was one bound. It was eight paces, flat and low. If she called it from here, and went on *one*, and shut the Ember at the top on *two*, then *three* would fall as her foot did. Her hands would be on the head poles, and the whole speed of a Stride would go down her arms into that litter with the stance behind it. It would be the stop she had put through Lowen Brask at the Terrace, and nobody would have to push her first.

Nine tries. Nine misses.

She had promised.

*It's somewhere inside two,* Kellan had said.

"Rowan, changing on three!" It was out of her mouth. "Ember to still!"

The white flag went up.

"*One—*"

She went. The brick fell away. The black posts went by on either hand.

"*Two—*"

She shut the Ember at the top. The metal ran home. She had nothing, and she was moving, and the two poles were coming at her hands with Jude's white face above them.

At the far end of the litter, Lowen Brask looked up.

He had heard it. Everyone in the New Lane had heard it; that was what the rule was for. He had heard *three* coming as a man hears a train. And he did not brace. He did not set himself to meet it, as he had set himself at the Terrace with both hands on Old Tom's chest.

He lifted.

He raised his end of the litter a hand's width off the brick and swung it one pace to the side. Keld went with him without being told. It was as neat and unhurried as two people moving a ladder out of the way of a door.

The head poles tore out of Jude's fists.

"*Three,*" said Kiva, to nothing.

Her hands closed on air. The stance opened under her, wide and deep, exactly on the word, and there was nothing in the world for it to stop. The speed was still in her. It had been meant for a sandbag, and the sandbag had gone.

So it went the only way that was left.

Her left foot found the brick first.

She heard the knee before she felt it. Then the lane turned white from wall to wall, and she was on her side on the floor of the New Lane with her cheek in one of the two troughs, saying *off, off,* though the Ember had been shut a full second and the line on her breastbone had never so much as warmed.

A palm came down between her shoulder blades. It was not hard. It did not need to be.

"*Down!* Crooked Line!"

*One,* said the line judge. *Two.*

She could not kneel on the left. She got onto the right, with the other leg stuck out before her like a broom, and held it in both hands.

*Three. Four.*

They did not run with it. They never ran. They took Donny up between them, Brask and Keld at the foot and Moss at the head, and walked him east down the middle of the lane. Thirty-four paces. Jude went after them and could not get past Arrand. Noa went after them and was slapped away. Pru's ward rose in their road, and Moss leaned on it without breaking step, and it rang four times and splashed.

*Eight. Nine.*

From her one knee, Kiva watched a sandbag with an inked mustache go over the far white line.

"Litter home. Three points, Stonehand."

*Ten.*

The board turned.

*CROOKED LINE 4. STONEHAND 7.*

*Rowan, K.: one change, called to a count. Dram, 0.44.*

There was a silence along the western rail, where the first-years were. It did not last. Somebody up there laughed, the short surprised laugh of a person who has got what he came for.

She did not look up at the fourth row. She knew exactly who was sitting in it, with a stick across her lap, and what she had been told on Monday.

# Chapter 20 — Kiva: Mine, Yours

Lowen Brask sat down on the away line and let Hendry have the water first.

He always did. A captain who drank first had a team that noticed. He had been taught that at thirteen by a woman with one eye who ran the junior lanes at the Hall, and it was one of perhaps eleven things he knew for certain.

"Seven to four," said Keld. She was pleased, and trying not to show it. She had never been good at that. "And she's finished. Did you see it go? She can't stand on that."

"She can stand on it. She can't land on it." He looked up the lane. "Those are different."

The girl was sitting against the fourth post on the far side, with her leg out and a healer's boy kneeling over it. She was not writing in the book. He had half expected her to be. Her captain stood over her with his arms folded, and the small one in the scarf was talking, and nobody was listening to the small one in the scarf.

He had moved a ladder. That was all.

He went over it again, honestly, as he would have gone over a first-year's fall. He had read rule nine on a tram a fortnight ago and laughed out loud, and an old man opposite had looked at him. A school that told you what it was going to do and when. No wonder his Hall had been beating it for thirty years.

So he had planned for the count. If she called a change to a count, she would be coming at something, and a stop wants a thing to stop. Take the thing away. He had drilled it with Keld on Thursday, with a bench. *When you hear two, lift.* There was no cruelty in it. If you ran at a door, and the door was opened, the floor on the other side was your own affair.

It had worked to the inch.

And that would have been the end of his thinking, and a pleasant end, if he had not spent six years on a mat teaching children to come down.

He had watched four hundred landings. He knew a late door when he saw one. He knew what it looked like when a boy opened his stance in a panic with something else still running, because he had seen her do exactly that on a run plate, twice through, with a pencil. The Ember's metal had been on her calves in the Cellhouse when she planted. It had been ugly, and it had held once, and he had written *don't expect it again.*

There had been nothing on her shins just now.

He had been looking. Nobody else on that floor would have been; they were looking at her face, or the flag. He had been looking at her legs, because that was where a landing told the truth. The struts had gone before she was halfway down. She had come through the air with nothing open at all, a plain girl falling fast, and on the word *three* one door had opened, the right one, by itself.

It had been late by about the width of a breath. And it had been aimed at his sandbag.

"What are you chewing?" said Keld.

"I'm trying to remember whether I've ever seen anyone get that part right and fall over anyway."

"What part?"

"Never mind." He took the water from Hendry. "It's usually the other way about."

He had a Hold that had stopped at seven and a half two springs ago and would not be coaxed. He was not strong. He had come to terms with that. Stonehand's upper teams wanted plates lifted, and he could not lift them, and so he was a third-year in a Third Division tunic who taught twelve-year-olds to argue with the floor. He did not mind it as much as people supposed. But the Home Line took its hold crews from hands with a second step, and Old Four had been eighteenth on a list for two years.

On Wednesday week he would stand in a clay pit with sixteen people and a string of eight knots. One of the sixteen was a girl who had just done a new thing wrong in front of four hundred witnesses.

He ought to think about that. He would, on the tram.

"Third carry," he said. "They're three behind. They have to come and get it. Talley's proud, and the Bexley boy can't keep still to save his life, so they'll run. We divide at the fifth posts, same as the first. Hendry, the ward. Idony, the boy. Arrand, you stay on my shoulder and stop apologizing to people."

"And Rowan?"

"Leave her the sandbag."

He stood up. Across the lane, the healer's boy had finished, and the girl was getting her good leg under her with a hand on the post.

*I should have liked to see it land,* thought Lowen Brask. It surprised him. He put it away, with the other things he would think about on the tram, and went to the mark.

"I'm not going to say it," said Jude.

"Say it. I'd rather."

"You told her you wouldn't. In front of me. With my book on the bench." He had his arms folded so tightly that his knuckles were white. "And I'm three behind in my own lane, with a fighter on one leg. No. I'm not going to say it. I haven't the time."

The knee was the size of a small loaf. The healer's boy had strapped it and told her that she could stand on it and should not, and gone away. Kiva held the post and tried her weight. It bore. It felt as if somebody had packed the joint with hot gravel.

"I can plant," she said. "I can walk a few paces. I can't bound. I'm sorry, Jude. I mean it, and I know it isn't any use to you."

"It isn't." He breathed out through his nose. "So. Third carry. He'll do what he did in the first. He'll leave you the sandbag and hunt."

"He won't hunt."

They all looked at her. Even Pru stopped.

"He didn't hunt us in the first one," said Kiva. She had been going over it against the post, because it was better than going over the other thing. "It looked like it. But think where they went. Round the sides, slowly, where we'd have to come to them. And then in the second he came straight down the middle the moment I had struts on. He isn't walking round me because he's afraid of the stop. He's waiting for me to travel. The whole afternoon. Every point they've had came off one of us moving."

"Noa bounded," said Pru slowly. "Into Keld."

"I ran at three of them," said Jude. "With my back to nothing."

"And I went eight paces through the air and told him when I'd arrive." It tasted of brick. "He hasn't beaten anybody who was standing still. I don't think he's tried."

Noa had his tape. He had been to the board. He looked from the strip of paper to the chalk dots on the floor of his mind, and she watched it reach him.

"Then don't," he said. "Any of us."

"We're three behind," said Jude. "If we sit on it, it lies in our half at time, and that's one. We lose by two instead of three."

"So we take it home." Kiva pointed down the south row. "Along the posts. One post at a time. I plant at a post with my heel on its shoe, and that's a riser. Pru ties the gate across from oak to oak, so there aren't any ends. When it's clear, I let go and you two carry it four paces, and I walk, and I plant at the next. I never change doors. Nobody's ever in the air. And every time it changes hands, somebody says so. Out loud. *Yours. Mine.* So there's never a moment when it's nobody's."

"That's four paces at a time," said Jude. "It's sixteen to the line from where Noa gets it."

"Four goes."

"With four still-doors on the other end of it."

"They have to come and take hold first. That's the part I like." She met his eye. "You said it on Monday. Our book says your own side ought to know first."

Jude Talley looked down the lane, at the nine black posts and the white line beyond them. Then, for the first time since the fall, something eased in his jaw.

"Nobody runs," he said. "Nothing changes. And we say everything. Pru, can you tie that wall to wood?"

"I've never tried. It's always had ends. I've always thought the ends were the ward's own fault." Pru considered. "I could lean it on the posts. Like a plank across a ditch."

"Then be a plank."

"Rowan," said Kiva to the referee. "Still."

The grey-braided woman looked at the strapped knee, and at her face, and wrote it down.

"Bell."

Noa went once. It was the only run of the carry, and he made it count. He had Donny off the mark and four paces west, hard against the foot of the southern fourth post, before the brown tunics had left their line. Then he let go and stood still, with his hands open, and it was plainly the hardest thing he had done all year.

Kiva came down the lane on her own two feet. It took a long time. She sat rather than knelt, with the bad leg out, and set her right heel against the iron shoe of the post, and took the head poles with her right hand and the crook of her left elbow. She did not trust the two fingers. She had not trusted them since the Terrace.

She let her weight go down.

"Mine," she said.

"Plank," said Pru. The ward came up across the lane a pace east of Donny's feet, low and leaning, and its two ends came to rest against black oak. It hummed there. It had never hummed before. "Oh. It likes that. Jude, it *likes* it."

Jude stood at the mouth of the south aisle with his blade down. Noa stood at the north.

Stonehand reached the fifth posts and divided, two and two, and went into the aisles.

They came along both walls at that slow, rolling walk. They came round the ends of the rows, looking for somebody to hunt, and found four people standing still behind a wall with a sandbag.

Lowen Brask stopped.

He put up one hand, and the other three stopped with him. He looked at the gate, and at the heel on the post, and at Jude Talley, who was not running anywhere. Kiva saw him read it. It took him about as long as it took to read a tram ticket.

Then he folded his arms.

For the space of ten breaths, eight people in the New Lane looked at one another across six paces of brick, and nobody did anything at all. The gallery had begun to mutter. Somebody in brown called out to get on with it.

"He's ahead," said Jude, without moving his lips. "He can stand there till the glass runs."

"Then we go home," said Kiva. "Call it."

"Yours?"

"Yours."

She gave the ground its weight back. Jude and Noa had the poles as her hands opened, and Donny came up off the brick and went west, four paces, at a walk. Pru's ward folded and rose again at the third posts. Kiva limped after it, and sat, and set her heel.

"Mine."

It was twelve paces to the line.

Lowen Brask unfolded his arms. He said one word to his team, and all four came down the lane.

---

They did it properly. She would say that for them afterward.

Moss and the boy with the ears walked up to Pru's wall and leaned on it, side by side, like men at a bar. Keld went into the north aisle after Noa. And the captain came down the south, behind the posts, to where Jude stood.

He did not try to pass him. He stopped a pace short. Then he reached through the gap between two posts with both broad hands, and took hold of Donny's foot poles, and pushed.

Kiva felt it arrive in her wrists.

She had been waiting three weeks to feel that again. It was the Terrace. It was a strong man leaning on a door. "*Now!*"

Jude's blade was already going.

It met a forearm. Brask had pushed with one hand, she saw, too late, and kept the other back, and he had been stepping away before the word was out of her mouth. His open palm turned the dark edge aside as a person turns a branch on a path.

"Half! Stonehand. On the arm."

Jude swore. "He wasn't there. You said *now* and he wasn't there."

"There was nothing in it." She was staring at her own hands. "Jude, it stopped at my wrists. It never went through. At the Terrace I felt him in the step. That was a knock on a door by somebody who'd already walked off."

Lowen Brask, three paces away behind an oak post, inclined his head to her very slightly.

"Seven," said Pru. Her voice was climbing. "That's seven taps. There's two of them, it's going twice as quick, I can feel it going soft in the middle—Jude—"

"Pru." Kiva did not look round. She kept her eyes on the poles. "Don't let them break it. Drop it yourself. On your own count."

"Drop it? They'll fall on you!"

"Yes. Call it. Call it to me."

There was a small, frantic silence.

"Gate on three!" said Pru Finch, in a voice that cracked. "One! Two! *Three!*"

The silver went out like a blown candle.

Hendry Moss had been leaning on it with the whole of his considerable self. He came through the place where it had been as a man comes through a door that somebody has opened from inside. Two great stumbling steps, with his arms spread, and his chest wide, straight down upon the head of the litter and the girl sitting at it.

She had the poles in the crook of her left arm. She took her right hand off the wood. She held it up, open, and he arrived on it.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

He knelt. He went down on the brick an arm's length from her, a large placid shape folding at the edge of her eye, and everything in her that had ever counted on a cloth turned toward him.

"Staying," said Kiva, through her teeth.

She had her hand back on the pole before the line judge said *two*.

It was the first time she had ever put anyone down. She noticed that some while afterward. In the moment there was only the boy with the ears standing alone where a gate had been, with his mouth open, and nobody on the foot poles at all.

"Yours!"

"Ours," said Jude, and they had it up. Four paces. The second posts. Pru's plank dropped across the lane behind them and hummed.

"Mine."

Eight paces to the line.

That was where it stuck.

Idony Keld had got Noa. Kiva heard it go, in the north aisle, the long arm and the flat call. "*Down!* Crooked Line!" Then Keld came round the end of the gate by the wall, where there was just room, and she and the captain and the boy with the ears all laid hold of Donny's feet together.

They did not push. Brask had finished with pushing. They pulled.

It came on slowly, three still-doors leaning back from the poles with their heels set, and then four, when Moss had served his ten. Kiva felt it come through her arms and her ribs and her hips, and through her right heel into the iron shoe of a post that had stood there three hundred years. It stopped there. She was not going to be moved.

But the poles were wood, and her hands were hands.

"Fifty seconds," called the referee.

Jude could not get a touch. Each time his blade went toward one set of ribs, another's palm was waiting behind it. Pru had her gate up, between nobody and nothing. The board said eight to five.

Brask pushed. A short, quick shove, with the weight of a breath.

"No," said Kiva. Jude's blade, which had twitched, stayed where it was.

"Ah," said Lowen Brask. He sounded like a man whose pupil has given the right answer a term early. Then he leaned back with the others and went on pulling, and his calm brown eyes came down to the crook of her left elbow.

He was watching the hook. He had told her that he would.

Her right hand had slipped a finger's width. The left arm was shaking. He would have her in a fourth carry, he had said. There was not going to be a fourth carry. He would have her in thirty seconds instead.

Kiva looked at the four of them.

She looked properly, as she would have looked at a cell with a fault in it. Four people, leaning backward at the far end of a sandbag. All their weight was hung on those two poles. They were not standing on the brick. They were standing on her.

*As a man leans on a door he expects to open.*

She had been the door, once, at the Terrace. She had been shut, and he had put everything on her, and for one instant none of it had been in him.

It was the other way about now. She only had to open.

"Jude," she said. "Get a hand on the head. Pru, the other. On three, it's theirs."

"It's *what*?"

"Yours first. Then theirs. One."

She did not whisper it. A call was no good whispered. It went down the lane and up into the lantern, and Lowen Brask's eyes came up from her elbow to her mouth.

"*Two.*"

She saw him understand. It took him half a second, which was half a second quicker than anybody else in brown. He took both hands off the poles, cleanly, and stepped back, and stood on his own feet. His mouth opened.

"Let—"

"*Three.*"

She gave the ground its weight back.

Donny went east as if he had been kicked. He went a full pace, with nothing whatever holding him, and at the far end of him, three still-doors who had been leaning their whole selves on a rooted girl found that there was no girl there.

The boy with the ears sat down. Moss went back four steps, windmilling. Keld kept her feet, because she was Keld, but her long arms were flung wide to do it, and her ribs were as open as a book on a table.

Jude Talley had started on *two*.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

"Mine!" That was Pru, with both fists on a head pole. "Is it mine? I've got it! Kiva, I've got one!"

Kiva had the other. She had no door open at all. She was a girl on one good leg, hauling seventy kilos of sand backward with a first-year who was narrating.

Nine paces.

They made three of them. Then the litter stopped as if it had been nailed.

Lowen Brask had bent, unhurried, and taken the foot poles in his two hands, and let his weight go down. One still-door. Seven and a half. He looked along the length of Donny at her, and there was nothing unfriendly in it.

"That's mine," he said.

"I know."

Jude's blade came for his ribs.

He had to take a hand off to turn it. "Half! Crooked Line." With one hand he could not hold both poles. The litter slewed, and Kiva and Pru dragged the head round like the hand of a clock. Jude came again, and stood between.

Brask looked at the dark edge. He looked at the girl walking backward behind it, with her hands full and her leg dragging. One step would have put his palm on her. It would have put his own ribs on Jude's flat on the way.

"No," he said gravely. "Thank you. Not that one."

He let go.

They went over the white line backward, the three of them, and fell down in a heap on the far side of it, with Donny on top.

"Litter home! Three points, Crooked Line!"

"*Time,*" said the referee, eleven seconds later, to a lane in which nobody could hear her.

The board turned over.

*CROOKED LINE 9. STONEHAND 8.*

Kiva did it sitting on the brick. Hendry Moss shook her hand with great goodwill and told her that nobody had ever opened a door on him before. Keld took it briefly, looked at the knee, and said, "I told you I'd strap that." The boy with the ears wished to be assured that it had been allowed. Noa assured him.

"Nine to eight," said Lowen Brask. "I'll have to take your lane card down from over my bed and put up a worse one."

He lowered himself to the brick beside her, quite naturally, as a man sits down next to someone on a wall.

"You told me," he said. "*On three, it's theirs.* I heard it. I got my own hands off. I couldn't get theirs off, and I've been teaching Idony for three years." He shook his head. Your whole side went on a thing I'd heard as plainly as they had."

"That's what the rule's for."

"So I've found. I read it as a rule that tells me where you'll be. It tells them as well. I only read the half I liked." He was silent a moment. "I'm not going to apologize for the ladder."

"No."

"But I've taught landings for six years, and I'll tell you what I saw, since nobody else in this building was looking at your shins." He held up a finger and thumb, a hair apart. "You'd nothing on them. The metal was home. You came down on one door, and it was the right one, and it was late by about that much. I've seen a great many people get that wrong. I've never seen anybody get it right and fall over."

Kiva did not know what to say. Her knee was beating like a second heart.

"Where was it meant to go?" he said. "The speed."

"Into the litter. Into you."

"Mm. And I moved the litter." He looked at the strapping. "I wonder where it would have gone if I hadn't. All of it. That's a good deal to put into a sandbag." He got up, slowly. "I won't."

"Good. I believe you, which is more than your captain does at present." He nodded, and went away after his team.

The referee had finished the card. She turned it on the table.

*Rowan, K. (prov.). Door declared each carry. One change, called to a count under home rule nine; made on the count; fell. No undeclared change. No incident.*

"A fall isn't an incident," she said. "I've written what I wrote last time on the back, word for word. A fighter who's flagged from door to door is supervised." She blotted it. "I've added a line. You were on your face in my lane with nothing lit, and you called your own *off*. I hadn't asked. I've put that down as well. It isn't *safe*, mind. I don't want it mistaken."

---

Orla was waiting in the passage behind the lane, where the buckets were kept.

She had sent Mara on to the tram. Kiva saw that and knew what it meant, and came down the passage on Anwen's arm, and then let go of the arm.

"I'll be outside," said Anwen. She went.

Orla Dane stood with both hands on her stick. Her voice, when it came, was quiet.

"On Monday you told me you would do it the slow way."

"Yes."

"In front of your captain."

"Yes."

"My name is on a form in Ysra Crest's office. If your line had lit out there, that's an incident, and it's my standing and my contract. I'd have paid. I said I would the day I signed, and I meant it." The lamp-oil eyes did not leave her. "But you don't spend it without asking me. Not once. Not because it's eight paces and it's right there. It will always be right there, Rowan. That's what it's going to be like for the rest of your life."

Kiva had nothing to hold. She held the wall.

"I'm sorry."

"I know you are. You're a great deal sorrier than you were on your knees with that leg, and so you ought to be. I'm not finished." Orla breathed. "The yard's shut till the healer's seen that. And I'll tell you now, so you needn't wonder, that I've seen ten, and I wouldn't put my name to one of them."

It was fair. That was the worst part. She would almost rather have been shouted at by somebody who was wrong.

"Now," said Orla. Her voice had changed. It had gone dry and exact, the voice of the crate. "You've paid for a lesson. I'll not have you go home without it. Your shins were bare. I saw. The order was right, for the first time in ten. So what did you do wrong?"

"I was late."

"By a hair. That isn't what put you on the brick. Where was the speed to go?"

It was the question Brask had asked. She gave the same answer, and heard it this time.

"Into the litter."

"And when he moved the litter?"

"Into my knee."

"Into the *ground*," said Orla. "It was only ever going into the ground. Through you. That's what a stance is; you've known it since you were thirteen. The litter was in the way." She tapped the ferrule once on the stone. "You tried to spend it on him. A sequence isn't a thing you do to somebody. He hadn't to be there for it at all. You proved that. He left, and it went into the ground anyhow, by the worst road there was."

Kiva stood in the passage among the buckets.

She had thought of it as a blow. She saw that now. From the first night on the line she had thought of the speed as a thing to hit with, a Stride made heavy, because that was what would have been wonderful.

It was not a blow. It was only weight, arriving. It had to be given somewhere to arrive.

"Go home," said Orla. "Ice it. And Rowan. That last carry." She had turned to the door, and she did not turn back. "Six times that sandbag changed hands, and it was never once nobody's. I counted. I don't know that you did."

---

The healer at the school infirmary gave her a fortnight.

"No landings," she said. She was a brisk woman, with her sleeves pinned back. "None. You may stand on it. You may walk on it, with the strapping. If you come down on that leg from any height before the second of next month, I shall hear of it, and I shall send the account to your sponsor."

"There's a Flask on the twenty-sixth."

"Are you the blade?"

"The catcher."

"Then kneel on the other one."

So when the yard opened again on Monday night, with a new week's thimble of ash in her, Orla took away the bound.

"A step," she said. "One. Off the line. Struts for a single push, no higher than a curb and no longer than a pace. If you miss, you've taken a step. I'd like to see your healer make a bill out of that."

Perrin Cade had come. He sat on the spare crate with a hand on his ribs, and he watched Kiva stand on the line and look at the cross. It was four paces off still. Nobody had moved it. It was only that she was not going there tonight.

"Can I say a thing?" he said. "It's about falling. It's the only subject I'm any good at."

"Say it," said Orla.

"Brask taught me. When I was twelve. A month, I told you. And the trick was that you don't land and then let go. You're let go already when you get there. He used to say it every morning, till I could have hit him." Perrin put on a mild, patient voice that was not at all a bad likeness. "*Arrive before you're there, Cade.* I thought it was the stupidest thing a person ever said. Then I fell off the practice wall onto my back and didn't feel it. You set the door for the weight that's coming. Not the weight you've got."

"I can't feel the ground till it's there."

"No. Nobody can. You don't wait to feel it. You'd be late." He shrugged, and winced. "It's like. I don't know what it's like. It's like holding out your arms for a baby somebody's about to hand you."

Kiva stood very still.

"It's a pan," she said.

"A what?"

"You don't chase ash." She was speaking slowly, because it was coming slowly, from a long way down. "It falls where it's going to fall. If you wait to feel it land, you're sixty counts behind. You put the pan under first. Before. You set it where the fall's going, and then the ash arrives, and it's in the pan." She looked at her own two hands. "I've done that four hundred times. With the bad fingers. In the dark. I've never once waited for it."

Orla said nothing at all.

*Ember.* The struts closed on her calves. She pushed off the line, a curb's height and a pace, a thing a child might have done over a puddle. She shut the Ember at the top of it. The metal ran home.

And she set the still door under herself, as she would have set a pan.

Her right foot came down into it.

There was no jar. There was no ground, exactly. It was like stepping off a curb onto a mattress that was the whole of the Old Yard. The step went through her and out, and she had never in her life landed so softly on anything.

*Caught,* she thought, and felt her hands do what they had done four hundred times after a catch.

She put the lid on.

The stance shut. The stick arrived. She went over sideways into the dirt.

"Well," said Orla, after a moment. Her voice was not quite steady. "You caught it. And then you tinned it. I asked for a stance, Rowan, not a seal check."

"It was soft." Kiva lay on her back and looked at the lamp. "Orla. It was *soft*. My knee didn't know."

"I saw. Is your line cold?"

"It never moved."

"Then that's eleven, and the first that didn't hurt, and it's a miss. You caught the speed and stood on nothing afterward. Up."

The twelfth was the same. She caught it, and this time kept the door open by main force, and the stick moved her a foot across the lane as a hand moves a pudding. She had taken the landing in and had nothing left over to stand with. Orla called that a miss as well.

On Wednesday night she made two more.

The thirteenth put her back two steps. She wrote it down.

The fourteenth she caught, and stayed open, and stayed. The iron ferrule came in at her left shoulder with all of Orla's arm behind it. And it went through her. She felt it go, as she had felt Lowen Brask go at the Terrace: in at the shoulder and down, through her hip and her right heel, and into the dirt of the lane, and gone.

She had not moved.

*I've done it,* thought Kiva, and the joy of it went up through her like a struck match.

The bottom line lit.

"One! *Off!*"

She stood with her hands on her knees while it cooled. She did not need to be told. She had felt the Ember come up through the middle of the gladness, without being asked, to see what the fuss was about.

"One count," said Orla. She had got to her feet. She had not done that for any of the thirteen. "The work went across. I felt it go; it nearly took the stick out of my hand. And then you were pleased, and I watched you being pleased, and your other door came to see." She sat down again, heavily. "You weren't counting."

"I was listening for the ground."

"You've told me yourself what happens when you stop counting. It's in your own book." A pause. "Write it as a miss."

"It was nearly—"

"Nearly is a miss with a better seat. Fourteen. I've watched every one of them, and I'd sign none. Not for a lane. Not for a floor." Orla's crooked face was grave in the yellow light. "You'll go to the Pit on the paper you've got. Recovery. Dark hands. You'll kneel."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so that I've said it."

---

Between those two nights there had been the second floor.

They spent it on Tuesday, at four, on the Kennel's brick. It was the last thing but a sealed dram on Team Seven's page. Kiva had walked the floor, and set the borrowed sheet between the first tank and the second, and then she had stood up and said the thing she had been turning over since the lane.

"When I call dark, I want it from everybody. Not inside three paces. The whole floor. Nothing lit anywhere till I say sealed."

"The rule's three," said Kellan.

"The rule was three on the Sag Line. Dray's ash went forty and found a pump." She had the field book open at Saturday. "The Pit's a bowl. I don't know what the air does in a bowl. And there's this."

She read them the last column.

*Why:* We stopped feeding him. Every point they had came off one of us moving. When nobody ran and nothing changed, he had nothing to hunt, and he had to come to us.

"That's a man," said Anwen. "At the weir they kept the wall lit. Ten paces off, the whole way through."

"At the weir they had eight Home Line wards round the outside and a meadow. We'll have a floor and three teams of students." Kiva shut the book. "If nothing's lit, there's nothing for open ash to lean at. That's all I mean. It's the same thing. Give it nothing."

"The old woman said it likes a gate," said Perrin.

"It can have the lattice. That's what the one on the Sag did when we all went dark. It went and leaned on the hold like a cow."

Nobody could find the hole in it. Kiva could not find one herself, and she had looked.

So they did it her way. The husk that came up out of the dark was a plain one, for once. Kellan cut it at the foot of Anwen's ward. *Dark,* said Kiva, and the ward went out, and there was not a lit thing on the settling floor from the yellow line to the gutter. Four people came to four corners. She had told Anwen beforehand that the cord's third turn was hers.

"Yours on three. One. Two. Three."

"Mine," said Anwen, and crossed it over, not under.

"Sealed. Forty-four."

It was fourteen counts better than the first floor. It weighed twenty-three and six-tenths, and there was no hair-bright line in a grain of it.

"Forty-four," said Warden Druce. "Hm." He turned the bag's neck in his grey fingers. "Whole floor dark. Whose notion?"

"Mine."

"And if there'd been seven more of it standing behind you with nothing to look at?"

"It would have gone and leaned on your lattice."

"On my floor, it would. I've a lattice sixty years old in the next room, and it's the loudest thing in this building." He set the bag on the scale and did not look at her. "I'd ask myself what the loudest thing in the Pit is, Miss Rowan."

"The Kiln."

"I'd have thought so. Mind, I've not stood a Flask in that hole for nine years, and nobody's stood one there with a company of first-years. Put it to your foreman. It's his floor." He wrote the weight in the Kennel book. "That's a tidy catch. I'll say so. I'd only say that a tidy catch on a Handful has never yet told anybody anything about a string."

She wrote that down too, on Tuesday night, under the forty-four.

She did not underline it. She had a number that was fourteen counts better, and a reason that had won a lane, and a warden who had said *hm*. On Tuesday night that had seemed like most of an answer.

# Chapter 21 — Kiva: The Pit

Brickfields had dug its bricks out of one hole for two hundred years, and then, finding itself with a hole, had faced it with them.

The Pit lay off the Brickfields road behind a wall of yellow brick, and Kiva came down into it on Thursday afternoon by the keeper's gate, one step at a time, with her knee strapped and the field book under her arm. It was an oval. Brick benches went up its sides in terraces, twelve deep, all the way round to the rim, where forty lamps stood on iron posts against the sky. The floor at the bottom was rammed cinder, seventy paces from north to south and forty across, and it was empty.

It was the largest floor she had ever stood on. She felt like a pea in a roasting tin.

"Fifth company," said Hollis Vane. "To me."

He stood at the middle of it with his slate on its cord. The weathered face was as she remembered it. Bettan stood a little behind him with her arms folded. Sixteen candidates gathered in from the gate in four knots, and the four knots did not quite touch.

Lowen Brask was there, in the grey coat. He had three people with him whom Kiva did not know: a lanky Crooked Line third-year with a sardonic mouth, a small stern girl in Wren House green, and a quiet boy carrying a catch roll. Team Thirty-Three had tinned in fifty-two on the Sag Line, and she knew their faces from the berm. Team Twelve she did not know at all.

"I'm your foreman on Wednesday," said Vane. "Today I'm a guide. You'll walk this ground with me once, and then by yourselves until you can draw it in the dark. I'll tell you what things are. I'll not tell you what to do about them." His pale eyes went round the sixteen. "North first."

At the north end stood the Kiln.

It had been a brick kiln once, a great beehive of a thing, twice the height of a house. In its side, facing down the floor, was a doorway as wide as a cart, and in the doorway hung a slab of cast iron as thick as a hand. A pattern had been cast into the face of it, ridge inside ridge, like the rings of a tree. The iron hung in a frame on two chains that ran up over wheels to a stone platform beside the Kiln, where a capstan stood with four long bars.

Kiva felt the thing behind the iron before she was within thirty paces of it. It was the flat of a hand on her neck, as at the weir, and it was pressing through a wall.

"That's the lid," said Vane. "It's two hundred years old. The lattice behind it is younger and better, and it runs on current. If the current fails, the lattice fails, and the lid's all there is. So the lid's kept. It comes down in two minutes with four still-doors on the bars, or in ten with eight ordinary men." He nodded at a low brick shed built against the Kiln's flank. "Lattice house. Warden Tilling's. She opens the bottle as wide as she's told and no wider. On Wednesday she'll be told a Flask."

"Has it ever let two through?" That was the lanky third-year.

"Not in Tilling's time. Ask me a better question, Mr. Tolliver."

"What happens if it does?"

"Then I've a professional crew at the rail and you've a long walk to the gate." Vane turned. "South."

---

There was a line of iron gratings down the middle of the floor.

Each was a pace wide, and they ran end to end, as straight as a ruled line, from the foot of the Kiln to the far wall. Kiva had crossed them twice already without looking. Now she stopped on one and looked down through the bars. There was a brick channel underneath, an arm deep. Along the bottom of it lay two bars of copper, each as thick as her wrist, dull and clean.

She knew them at once. She had seen them drawn, as one long black line.

"Keeper calls it the race," said Vane. "That's the Pit's branch. Everything in this hole that hums runs off those two bars. The lattice. The lamp ring. The board. The lifts."

"Where are the cells?" said Kiva.

She had not meant to ask it so quickly. Several people looked at her. Selka Dray's objection had been posted on the roster board for two days, and there was nobody in the fifth company who had not read it.

"That's the better question," said Vane. "Keeper."

An old man had come out from the east wall. He wore a Works coat gone green at the seams, and a ring of keys hung at his belt, and he walked with his head forward like a heron. He had been sitting on a stool beneath a glass-fronted case with twelve dials in it.

"There isn't one," he said. "Not a cell on this floor. Never has been. Dunmore, miss. I've kept this branch since 'fifty-nine." He pointed south with his chin. "It all comes from over the road. Ferren pump bank. Four-and-twenty cells, and three pumps that keep the low streets dry, and one branch off for us, because the Circuit was too mean to buy its own. Comes under the road in a tunnel and up that wall."

"So that's a bus," said Kiva. "Under the gratings. Not a cell."

"That's a bus."

She wrote it down. *Race = bus. No cells on the floor. Nearest cell: across the road.* It mattered. Her paper said three paces from any charged cell, and she had been ready to walk this whole floor measuring.

The south wall had no benches. It went straight up, four times the height of a man, sheer yellow brick, with the road somewhere above it. At its foot the floor stopped at a trench.

It was three paces wide. It ran the whole width of the south end, and it was a man and a half deep. The line of gratings ended at its lip. The two copper bars came out of the race there, bare, and ran down a brick ramp and across the bottom of the trench, and vanished into a round black mouth in the foot of the wall.

"Sump," said Dunmore. "Floor drains to it. And that's my link."

Kiva looked up.

On the wall beyond the trench, a little above the height of her head, an iron shelf was bolted to the brick. It was a grating, a pace deep and four long. Above the shelf two copper jaws stood out of the wall, each as long as a forearm, and a copper blade lay bedded between them. From the blade's hinge a lever came down along the wall, an iron bar as long as a man, with a crossbar at its end like the handle of a spade.

There was a spring behind it as thick as her leg.

"That's the only thing between this hole and the bank," said the keeper. "Blade's in, we're on. Haul the lever down a quarter turn and the blade lifts out, and everything north of here goes dark. It's sprung to shut. Has to be. If aught breaks, I want it closed, not flapping." He scratched his jaw. "Two-man lever by the book. One hauls it down and stands on it. The other drops a pin through the lower eye. There's the pin, on its chain. It's been one man and a prayer since the Works cut my mate in 'seventy-one."

"It's locked," said Kiva. "Pinned and locked. Since twenty-six sixty-one."

Dunmore's head came round like a heron's.

"Who told you that?"

"It's in the trunk book. My aunt's reconciling it."

"Rowan. Aye. She was at my drums on Tuesday." He looked at her with new interest and no more warmth. "It's locked three hundred and fifty days of the year. Day the Kiln's opened, I draw the lock at dawn and stand by. Circuit's own rule. If that lattice runs away, somebody's got to be able to drop the branch."

"From where?"

"From the board. That's where trouble shows first." He jerked his thumb at the glass case, sixty paces off. "Then out my gate, up the rim stair, along the road, down the road stair, and through that."

There was a small iron door in the wall, behind the shelf.

"Four minutes," said Dunmore. "I've timed it. I was quicker in 'sixty."

"How does a man get to it from the floor?" said Lowen Brask.

"He doesn't. That's the notion. There's no way onto that shelf but my door. So no lad off the floor can get at that lever in a temper and put the Kiln on its lid in the middle of a lane." The keeper sniffed. "They would, you know. I've met them. And he'd do himself no good if he did. It wants four hundredweight on it to bring it level for the pin. One good still-door with his boot on the shelf, or two big men."

Kiva looked at the trench. It was three paces, with bare copper at the bottom. She looked at the shelf, one pace deep, with a wall behind it and two open copper jaws at the height of a person's chest.

*Nobody could land on that,* she thought. It was an idle thought. She had a knee the size of a loaf and a paper that said *kneel.*

She wrote: *S. link. Shelf across the sump. No way on but the keeper's door. Unlocked on Kiln days. Wants a still-door. 4 min round by road.* Then she went to set her mark.

---

"I've read your file," said Vane.

He had waited until she was alone, on the eastern half of the floor, with a twist of dead ash in her fingers.

"All of it. It took me a tram ride, and I'm obliged to the Chair for the reading. Twenty paces from the lattice house. I've paced it. That's my heel mark." He pointed at a scrape in the cinder. "Anywhere south of that. I'm to unseat you on Wednesday morning for any cause I'll write down. I'd like you to know that I will, and that I've no cause yet."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Set your mark."

She dropped a pinch.

It fell straight for most of the way. In the last hand's width it slid. She dropped another, two paces on, and it did the same thing, and the same way. South.

"The floor falls," she said. "To the sump. The cold air's lying in it and going downhill."

"It does that every morning till the sun's on the west terrace."

"Then an open heap will want to creep south." She walked. She dropped a third pinch, and a fourth. She looked at the line of gratings in the middle of the floor, and at the east wall, where a cable in an iron pipe came down from the lamp ring to the keeper's glass case. "And I don't want it near the wall. That's the board's feeder. So the middle of this half. As far from the wall as from the race."

She stood on it. It was ten paces from the gratings and ten from the terrace, and two paces south of his heel mark.

"Here. Twenty-two from the lattice house. Sheet squared north and south."

Vane chalked it. He did not say whether it was a good mark. He never had.

The sixteen of them sat on the lowest terrace afterward, in the thin sun, and for the first time the four knots became one.

"Rungs go by the list," said Lowen Brask. Nobody had made him the one who spoke. He had only begun, in his mild way, and nobody had stopped him. "Seven has the top. We've the second. Thirty-Three the third, Twelve the fourth. Whoever isn't cutting is minding. Four wards make the wall for every rung, with the cutting team's ward in the middle calling the step. Is that how everyone had it?"

It was.

"Then there's one thing I'd like said aloud before Wednesday. The count." He turned his shaved head. "Miss Rowan has a notion. I've an interest in it. I'd rather she put it."

Kiva had not expected that. She opened the book on her good knee.

"Whole floor dark," she said. "During every count. Not three paces. Everyone. From *dark* until the catcher says *sealed.*"

"The book says three," said Tolliver.

"The book said three on the Sag Line." She gave them the pump, and the forty paces. She gave them the cold air sliding south under her pinch of ash. Then she gave them the lane, because it was the best reason she had. "We were three behind on Saturday. Every point Stonehand had came off one of us moving. So we stopped. We gave them nothing to come at, and they had to come to us, and that's when it turned. Open ash leans at a pattern. If there's no pattern lit on the floor, there's nothing for it to lean at."

"And the string?" said the stern girl in green. Her name was Merrin. "It's blind for a hundred counts. If the wall's dark, it's got nothing to stand against."

"It goes to the Kiln. It's the loudest thing here. The one on the Sag went and leaned on the hold."

"At the weir they left a gate," said Anwen quietly.

She was sitting two steps down, with her list. Kiva looked at her.

"I know they did."

"I was behind it. Ten paces off, every rung." Anwen did not look round. "I'm not saying you're wrong. I'm only saying I'd want to know why they bother, before I didn't."

"Foreman," said Brask.

Hollis Vane had been standing at the foot of the terrace with his back to them. He turned round.

"I'm not a vote," he said. "I'm a slate. I'll tell you one thing, because Warden Druce sent word that you'd ask, and because I don't keep traps." He took the chalk from behind his ear. "My own crews leave a gate lit. Ten paces, twelve. I'd leave one. I've never had to say why. It's only done, the same as you tie a boat up at both ends."

Nobody spoke.

"It isn't in the Circuit's book. This is the Circuit's floor. In twenty years I've not seen a blind string do anything with a dark field but walk to the loudest pattern there is and lean on it, and in this hole that's the Kiln, and the Kiln's stood worse." He looked along the row. "So it's inside the book, and it's yours to call. I'll write down what you tell me, and I'll write down why."

Kiva's mouth was dry. A Home Line officer had said he would leave one. He had also said that it was hers.

"I'd have left the gate, a week ago," said Lowen Brask slowly. "I'd have said the Home Line's been doing this for five hundred years and I've been doing it for none." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Then four people stood still in a brick lane and took a point off me for every step I made toward them. I don't know that a string's a captain. But I know what it cost me to have nothing to hunt."

"And if it doesn't go to the Kiln?" said Tolliver.

"Where else is there?" said Kiva. "There isn't a cell on the floor. I asked."

They put it to hands. Thirty-Three went with her, all four. Old Four went three to one, Tolliver against. Team Twelve looked at one another and would not say either way. On her own team Kellan's hand went up at once, and Perrin's after it. Anwen's came up last, and she wrote something on her list as it did.

"Company's call," said Vane.

He wrote it, and turned the slate so that they could read it.

*5th Co. No gate left lit during counts. Whole floor dark, catcher's word. Reason given: wind-borne foul at Sag Line; floor falls south; "nothing to lean at." Foreman would leave one. Not forbidden.*

Bettan had not said a word all afternoon. She was looking at the floor. Kiva thought she was looking at the cinders.

Up at the rim, where the reserves' rail ran above the keeper's gate, a figure with cropped red hair had been leaning on her elbows for an hour. She was too far away to have heard any of it. She had watched Kiva pace out a mark and stand on it, and then she had taken a pencil from behind her ear and written on a yellow card.

Monday's two companies were the top eight teams on the list, and they made it look like a thing that people did.

The student blades went up the back of the string on their struts, dark, and lit at the top of a bound, and came down dark. One was a First Division girl from Kellan's own lane, and she was lit for so short a time that Kiva saw only the knot open.

"Is quick enough?"

"It was for her." He watched the First Division girl back off with her hands open. "I'd like it noted that she asked her catcher. She's never asked anyone anything."

The bags closed in fifty, in fifty-five. One team missed its mark by a single dram, forty-nine and not fifty, and sat on the terrace afterward without speaking. The mark was real. Kiva wrote that down as carefully as anything else.

Not one company tried a dark floor.

"It isn't in the book," said Kellan. "I looked on Thursday night. The book says three paces and stops."

"I know it isn't."

"I'm only saying I looked."

On Tuesday morning the third company went in, and one of its teams was short an Ember. The boy had come down wrong in the high lane on Sunday and turned his ankle. The board put up a name in his place, with *res.* after it.

*Talley, J.*

Kiva did not breathe for the whole of the third rung.

He had never cut anything but a Kennel dog. He went up the back of a six-knot string in two bounds, with his dark blade low, and a boy he had met that morning said *now,* and Jude lit. It was not four-tenths. It was nearer a second, and it wobbled, and the knot opened.

They bagged fifty-one and four-tenths.

"I've a second step," said Jude Talley.

"I saw."

She didn't say it as if it were a compliment." He was silent. "I'll not be your captain in the autumn, Rowan. I don't know what I'll be. I thought I ought to say it before I'd had time to feel clever about it."

"Whoever's left. Pru, at this rate." The chipped tooth showed. "She'd narrate them to death."

"The Hall's put me on the lid," he said. "Monday and today. It's a duty; they send four still-doors every Kiln day to wind the thing up and stand by to wind it down. I was told it was light work for a boy with ribs. It's four bars and you walk in a circle." He swallowed. "I said a donkey could do it. Brask said the donkey had been promoted."

"Does it hurt?"

"Only when I push. So I lean, and the bar goes round, and I say *ow* quietly. I know that capstan like my mother's mangle." He looked down the floor at the iron slab in its frame. "It's a strange thing to stand beside. You can feel what's behind it in your teeth."

And Anwen had her answer from Graywater, though it was not an answer she could use.

"It's an offer now. He said the word. A summer line. Six weeks from the tenth of next month, the culverts and the Sag, reliefs and lamps." She had the letter folded very small. "It isn't a draft. It's work, with a number on it and a stipend. And I'd miss the school's summer card, all of it. And anything Seven does in those six weeks, I'm not there for."

They both laughed. It did not last, and neither of them said the other thing.

"You settle that?" said Mara. "With no door declared?"

"Then I say *off* three times and walk to the gate."

Mara nodded slowly. She was looking at the vial.

"So he told me. And sits sixty paces off with the key." Mara made the small sound she made at bad wiring. "Eat. You've an early tram."

The Pit held eleven hundred people, and on Wednesday morning it held all of them.

The noise came down the terraces like water down steps. Kiva stood on the cinder at the keeper's gate with fifteen others and broke the wax on her vial. She tipped the grey into her left palm and closed her hand. The warmth went up her wrist and into her chest, and settled, and lay there whole.

A Circuit clerk ticked her name. Up on the eastern rim, beside the great board, a sensing mast stood taller than any she had seen, with a wire cage at its top like a birdcage, and a glass drum at its foot with sixteen pens.

There was a narrow boy on a stool beside the drum, with a letter in his hand and a roll of tape.

Noa Bexley saw her looking. He held up the letter, so that she could see the Chair's stamp on it, and then, because he was Noa, he pointed at one of the sixteen pens and at her, and spread his hands. *Yours. Doing nothing.*

She very nearly waved.

Below the board, in the first row with a rail before it, sat Ysra Crest. Orla was next to her with the stick. They were not speaking. Along the rail at floor level stood the reserves with their yellow cards, and one of them had red hair.

And on the west side, at a gate of their own, stood ten people in slate blue.

Kiva counted them without wishing to. Doran Mace, bald and still. Four in a line with their hands low, and the third had a broken nose. Two catchers. Line-hands. And a tall woman with her hands in her coat pockets, who was looking at the Kiln and at nothing else.

"That's the safety crew," said Kellan. "Graywater has Wednesday."

"I know who it is."

"Company," said Hollis Vane.

They went out onto the floor.

It was fourteen paces from the gate to the chalk. Kiva walked them on a strapped knee with Perrin beside her, carrying the roll, and when she reached the mark she dropped one more pinch out of habit. It fell, and slid south.

They unrolled the sheet. She squared it north and south. She knelt at the east hem on her right knee, with the left leg out, and laid her hand on the cord where it came out of the brass eye.

"Roles," said Vane. "Seven first."

"Renn. Kill."

"Pryce. Hold."

"Cade. Carry."

"Rowan. Catch. Door declared: none."

"For the slate. By condition of the Placement Chair, Team Seven's mark is set at twenty-two paces from the lattice house. I've walked it. I have the catcher's file, and I have found no cause." He turned to the next knot. "Old Four."

The other twelve declared. Four wards came to the head of the sheet and stood in a row, with Anwen second from the left, where the middle was. Merrin stood at her shoulder. Kellan had gone already, round to the north, and Kiva could not see him.

Up on the stone platform by the Kiln, four still-doors in Stonehand brown bent to the capstan bars.

The iron slab went up.

It rose in its frame with a long grinding groan, link by link, and behind it there was no dark. There was a blue so deep and so tired that it made the morning look thin. The flat hand on Kiva's neck became a weight. In the low shed against the Kiln's flank, somebody did something that she felt as a note dropping, a hum going down a tone, like a hand taken off a string.

For a moment nothing came.

Then it unfolded.

She had seen it at the weir from the top of a bank, sixty paces off. She was twenty-two paces from this one, and on her knees. The legs came down first, six of them, and the cinder jumped under each. The long body followed. Then the column went up into the bright air above the Kiln mouth, and up, knot over knot, eight of them, until the top was higher than the lid's iron frame.

The top one had frost on it.

"Skin," said Kiva. Her voice worked. She was glad.

"Seen." That was Kellan, from somewhere behind it.

"Wall," said Anwen.

Four wards rose as one. They were not Graywater. She could see the seams. But they came up under that barrel of a knot together, low and leaning, and the frosted face swung, and found them, and rang.

"Eight," said Kiva. Her voice was a croak. "*Nine.*"

She gave them the numbers. She gave them to the Ember and to the hand door, one each, like crusts to dogs. She kept her eyes on the hole in the bar. The left arm had started to shake at the elbow.

Something landed beside her.

It was as light as a cat on a wall. The shelf hardly spoke. Dull yellow struts flexed and held, on the last half pace of grating that there was, and a hand shot past her shoulder for the pin on its chain.

"It's high," said Selka Dray. She was not out of breath. "It won't go. It's a thumb off. Get it down."

"I can't."

"Then move your elbow."

Two hands came down on the crossbar, outside her own. Dray picked her feet up off the shelf. She hung there with all sixty kilos of herself, like a child on a gate.

The thumb's width closed.

"Hold that. Hold it—"

One of the hands let go. There was a scrape of steel.

*Clack.*

"It's in." Dray's boots were on the iron again. "It's pinned. Let go."

"Yours?"

"It's the *pin's,* you—yes. Mine. Let go of it, Rowan!"

"Ten," said Kiva. "Eleven."

Up the floor, a wall of silver had come up like a sunrise. Three wards, bright and seamless, walked in to ten paces. The frosted face wavered. It hung between a girl on a shelf and three women in blue.

She gave the wall its weight back.

*Thank you.*

She said it to two hundred years of yellow brick. She shut the still door behind it, cleanly, with nothing left in the doorway, and stood on an iron shelf with no doors open at all.

The lever stayed where it was.

The Flask turned its face to the Graywater line, and walked north.

---

For a moment she only stood.

There was a very great deal the matter with her. She took it in order, as she would have read down a board. Her left arm hung from the shoulder like a wet towel, and the first two fingers were bone white. Her legs were shaking. There was something running over her lip.

And the dram was gone.

It was not all gone. But she could feel the place where it had been whole, an hour ago, at a gate. A third of it was simply missing. It had not gone into the bound. A bound cost nothing. It had not gone into the stance. She had stood four minutes on a stance for a tenth.

It had gone at the top. It had gone in the gap, in the time it took to say *two,* when she had shut one door and set the other and been nothing in between. Something had carried a Stride across that gap. Whatever it was had drunk a third of a dram to do it.

The lines on her chest were not burning. She put her right hand up to be sure. They had not fought. They ached, all three, the whole length to her arm, with the deep, stupid ache of a muscle that has been used for the first time in its life.

Her fingers were on her breastbone. They were on the lines.

There was something else there.

It was small. It was exactly round. It lay between the bottom line and the next, where it had lain for four years, and it was warm.

It was not the warmth of her own skin. She knew what her skin felt like through a shirt. This was warm as a hand is, when somebody lays it flat on your chest to feel whether you are breathing.

Kiva closed her fist round the disk through the cloth.

She did not ask it anything. She noticed that. She had nothing to ask. She only stood on a shelf with blood on her lip and held it, as you would hold a cup that somebody had put into your hands.

And the shelf went away.

---

She was high up. Very high.

They were coming. From every road. She could not see their faces, only their light, and no two lights were alike.

This time there was sound.

Each of them had a note. She had not known that before, or had not kept it. They were not in tune. They came on toward one another over a dark country, each humming its one note, old and bright and unlike.

She tried to count them.

She got as far as four and lost the number. She began again. It would not stay. She, who had counted through a husk walking at her, could not hold a figure in her head for the length of a breath.

The lights touched.

The notes did not make a chord. They made one note.

She looked up. There was no sky.

And the one note went on, with nothing over it, and what was above came down into it, full and enormous and paying attention.

Below her, very small and very exact, something answered.

*Tick.*

"—seventeen. Eighteen. Rowan. *Rowan!*"

Somebody had her by the belt.

She was against the wall. The brick was rough on her cheek. A fist was knotted in the back of her belt and another in her collar, and both were hauling her inward, hard, away from the edge of an iron grating that she was a good deal nearer to than she had been.

"You stopped counting," said Selka Dray, in her ear. Her voice was furious. "So I did it. Nineteen. Don't you dare go in that ditch. I've only just pinned this."

Kiva got her feet under her. The shelf was there. The Pit was there, dark and roaring.

"How long?"

"I don't know. Three counts. Four. You went white and started to lean." The grip on her collar did not ease. "Sit down. Against the wall. *Down.*"

She sat. There was nowhere else. She sat on the grating with her back to the brick and her bad leg sticking out over the sump, and found that her right fist was still closed at her chest.

It was still warm.

Dray was looking at the fist. Then she was looking at Kiva's face.

"You're bleeding," she said. "Your nose."

"I know."

"What have you got?"

"Nothing. It's—" She could not think of a lie. She had never been any good at them. "It's a thing I wear. It's warm. It isn't, generally."

Dray plainly did not think that was an answer. She let go of the collar, slowly, and stood with her back to the wall, a pace away, with her yellow struts gone home and her arms folded tight.

"The lamps jumped," she said.

"What?"

"Just now. When you went white. All of them, round the top. Once." Dray jerked her chin at the rim, where forty dead lamps stood on their posts against the morning. "There's no current in them. I pinned it myself. They jumped anyway."

Kiva looked up at the lamps. They were only lamps.

She had no answer to that either. She put it with the plate in the meadow and the tick in the bag, and held on to the disk.

Perrin Cade had asked, once, to be told before he was rescued.

He thought about that on the capstan. It was a poor time to think of anything, but his mind had always wandered when his body was busy, and his body just then was a boy leaning on an oak bar with two cracked ribs.

"Two to a bar," said Lowen Brask. "Cade, with me. Walk it. Nobody plants till I say."

There were eight of them. Four were the Hall's duty crew, third-years with necks, and two were strangers from the east wall, and one was Perrin, and one was the man who had taught him to fall. They walked the capstan round against its pawl, and the chains paid out over the wheels, and the iron lid came down in its frame a link at a time. *Clack. Clack.*

He knew this capstan like his mother's mangle. It took two minutes with four. With eight it ought to take one.

It was a third of the way down when he heard her.

It came the whole length of the Pit, thin and cracked and unmistakable. *Rowan, changing on three!*

He could not turn round. The bar was across his chest.

*She's telling everyone,* thought Perrin. *All eleven hundred. She might have just told me.*

"One," said the far voice. "Two."

He did not hear *three.* He heard the lamps go.

They went all together. One moment there was a ring of pale lights round the rim and a note in his back teeth that he had stopped noticing on Monday. The next there was neither. The note stopped as if it had been cut with scissors.

It was a great deal worse without it.

"Lattice is off!" That was the warden, somewhere in her shed. "It's off, it's off, I've nothing!"

The gap under the lid was as high as a man's waist. There was blue in it.

Then there was grey.

It came out under the iron low and blunt, a leg as thick as a quay bollard, feeling for the cinder. Another came beside it. Behind them, the deep tired blue bulged outward into the morning.

"That's a second one," said one of the duty crew. His voice had gone up like a girl's. "Brask, that's a second—"

"I see it. Walk."

They walked. The lid came down two more links, and met the top of a grey leg, and stopped.

The bar kicked back into Perrin's chest. Something in his side said a thing that he did not let out of his mouth.

"Plant," said Lowen Brask. "All of you. Now."

Eight still doors opened at once.

Perrin felt the platform take them. He had never been part of that before. It was like standing in a wall. The bar stopped kicking. The lid sat on the grey leg with the weight of two hundred years of cast iron and eight people who could not be moved, and the leg shoved upward, and nothing went either way.

"It's pushing," said Brask. He sounded interested. He always did. "Good. It's got its whole self on that. Listen to me, the lot of you. This is a caller's rule, and it isn't ours, and I learned what it's worth on Saturday. On three we give it the bar. One step back, all together. Then we take it again and we don't stop. Cade, what did I tell you every morning for a month?"

"Arrive before you're there," said Perrin, through his teeth.

"That's the one. One. Two. Three. *Give.*"

They gave.

The capstan spun back a quarter turn. The lid jumped up a hand's width in its frame. Beneath it, the leg that had been straining against a ton of iron found suddenly that there was no iron, and shot upward and out, and lost its footing on the cinder.

"*Take!*"

Eight still-doors drove the bars.

Perrin did not plant for it. Brask had not asked for a stance. He had asked for a walk with a wall's weight in it, and Perrin gave him that, and his ribs gave him the rest.

The lid came down. It came down the hand's width and a yard more, in one long rattle of chain, onto a thing that was off balance and half out of a doorway. It did not stop this time.

It seated in its stone sill with a boom that he felt in the soles of his feet.

On the cinder outside, a piece of grey as long as his arm lay by itself. It twitched once. Then it let go and poured, and was a heap of warm ash on the open floor with nobody to catch it.

"Sixty," said somebody, stupidly. "Somebody ought to count."

"That's sweepings," said Brask. "Let it lie. Don't any of you go lit near it." He had not taken his hands off the bar. "Stay planted. All of you. I'd like four minutes of standing on this before I believe it."

Perrin stood on it.

He had said *ow.* He was fairly sure that he had said it loudly. There was a hot wire in his side where two ribs had nearly finished knitting, and he suspected that they would now have to begin again.

A hand came down on his shoulder, broad and careful.

"I'm telling you first," said Lowen Brask. "I'm going to ask you to stand here four minutes more, on those ribs, because you're the only one on this bar who didn't have to be told twice. After that I'll carry you to the healer myself."

"I'd like it said that I mind."

"It's said."

Far off at the dark south end, a girl's voice was counting. It was not Kiva's. It sounded cross.

Perrin listened to it, and stood.

The lamps went out, and the thing in front of Rhea Sorn remembered that it was a husk.

She saw it happen. The shine drew in off the whole column and sat on one face of the top knot, a thick dull bloom. It was thicker than it had any right to be. It had not broken when the boy cut the first rung. It had slid, and she had seen it slide, and thought, *that's new,* and then the floor had gone dark and there had been no leisure for the thought.

It swung south. It had found something on the wall.

"Mace."

"I see it. Line, walk in. Ten paces. *Brighter.*"

He had three. Stroud was on a stretcher at the gate with her hands in wet cloth. Three Torch wards made a wall that was bright enough and not wide enough, and Rhea heard Mace say a name that was not on his roster.

"Pryce. Behind Imrie's shoulder. Hands below your belt. You're not in the line. If she says *take,* you take, for ten, and you give it back."

A girl in a plain dark coat came across the cinder at a walk and stood where she had been put.

The face came round to the wall. Rhea let out a breath.

She did not look south again. She had seen what there was to see, and she had a Flask. But it sat at the back of her eyes while she worked: a girl crossing three paces of air and stopping on iron as if she had been set there with a hand. There had been nothing on her shins.

Rhea had run a plate three times, a month ago, to be sure what a jam looked like. That had not been one.

*Not to be expected twice,* she had written. She had been right. It had not happened twice. This was a different thing, and it had happened once.

"Whose sheet's nearest?" she said.

"Old Four's." Hollis was at her elbow with his slate. "Their catcher's a third-year. He's waited two springs for this."

"Then he can have it."

The quiet boy was kneeling at his hem with a face like paper. She walked round the back of the string, a long pace off, and stopped.

"Catcher?"

He started as if she had struck him. She saw him swallow. He looked at his canvas, and at the cinder, and not once at her.

"Not yet," he said. It shook. "It's two paces north of me. I need it two paces north."

"Mace."

"Step. Step."

"Now."

Her left foot came down. Her right.

She had never had a Stride. She had stood under knots for twenty-three years. She raised her fist and let the blade be as long as it needed to be, a line of white from her knuckles to the place behind the frost, and took it away again.

Sixty drams came down. The skin slid.

It did it again, as it had for the boy. It tipped and went down one rung and sat on the next knot, and it was thicker.

"Dark. One."

The line drew off. Ten paces down the floor, three wards and a student's shoulder stood burning in the dead grey morning, and the blind thing swayed, and walked to them, and leaned.

*It likes a gate.*

Somebody had told sixteen children to put out every light on this floor. It was inside the book. Hollis had let them, and written it down, and she knew him well enough to know that he would have his own hide for it before the board did. She would have left a wall. So would he.

Neither of them would have guessed what it would find instead. She was honest enough to grant that.

She cut the third. She cut the fourth, with a student team from the east wall bagging under Bettan's eye, and on the fourth she heard Imrie's breath go ragged.

"Take," said Imrie.

Rhea was looking at the knot. She did not see the join. She listened for it, as she always did, and for ten slow counts there was a place in the Graywater wall that was held by a seventeen-year-old in a laundry coat.

She did not hear it.

"Give," said the girl. Her voice was steady. "Yours."

"Mine."

*Well,* thought Rhea, and cut the fifth.

By the sixth she could not find the edge of the skin by eye.

It had come down five rungs without breaking, and every rung it had kept what it had. It lay on the reader now like a second knot, a shell as thick as her wrist. A blade went in behind the edge of a skin. This one had hardly any behind left.

She found it by ear. She stood under it with her fist dark and listened to it ring on Mace's wall, and where the note was truest, she put the line.

It went. But it had been lit for a full second. She had not been lit for a full second since the Ferren floods.

"Two left," said Hollis. "The sit-down. I've no second blade, Rhea. Mine's at Brickfields with a broken wrist, and I'll not put a student under that."

"No."

She looked at what remained. It was low and wide, six legs and two great knots in its belly, side by side. The skin had come down with nowhere further to go. It lay over both of them like a lid on a pan.

Two knots. The book said two blades, together, or it would fall wherever it liked.

She had cut two with one line twice in her life. Once it had not worked, and a catcher had lost a foot.

"I want it turned," she said. "Broadside to me. Both knots in a row, edge on, so that one line goes through the pair. Mace, walk your wall round to the east. Slowly. Bettan, I want a double sheet under it, and I want your word and nobody else's."

"You'll have it when I've got it," said Bettan.

It took the best part of a minute. The wall went round in an arc, a step at a time, and the low grey thing shuffled after it, turning. Rhea stood with her fist at her side and did nothing whatever in front of eleven hundred people. She had long since stopped minding that.

She had time to look south.

There were two of them on the shelf. The red-haired one was standing against the brick with her arms folded. The other sat with her back to the wall and one leg stuck out over the ditch, and there was blood on her chin.

Her fist was closed at her collar.

Rhea had been nineteen. She had been in brown water to her chest, in a culvert, in the dark, and a tall woman with a scarred jaw had got an arm round her and held on. With the other hand that woman had been gripping something at her own throat, hard, as a person grips a rail.

She had never asked what Ilse Corran felt in her fist that night. She did not know what the girl felt in hers.

She knew what it looked like from across a floor.

"Now," said Bettan.

Her left foot. Her right.

The line went out level, at the height of her hip, as long as a tram pole. It went in at the edge of the shell and through one knot and through the other, and she held it lit until she felt both open. A second. A second and a half.

It sat down.

It went onto the double sheet with a sigh, all of it, and four catchers were on it. One of them was a student, with brass on his collar and tears running openly down his face, hauling a cord.

Rhea let the metal run home. Her arm ached to the shoulder.

She took a card from her coat while the count was still open, and a stub of pencil, and wrote on it standing up.

*Flask, the Pit, the 26th. Company left no gate; string took the bus. Rowan opened the south link on two doors, one after the other. Nothing dropped between. About ten seconds. Then down. That is not what I saw on the plate. Still NOT for draft. Attach Seven to Graywater. I'll take the shift myself. — R.S.*

She read it over. She did not turn it and write on the back. There was a private card in her other pocket that had been there for six weeks, with two words on it.

She thought that she might not be able to keep it private very much longer.

Kiva put her hand inside her collar.

The disk was cooling. She could feel it go. By the time she had limped the length of the wall to the gate, it was the cold of a coin on a windowsill, and she could not have proved to anyone that it had ever been otherwise.

# Chapter 24 — Kiva: What Was Tinned

Healer Aske had the coldest thumbs in Meridian, and on Wednesday morning they were in the back of a cart.

It was the Wren House cart that stood at the west gate on every Kiln day, a covered wagon with two cots and a locked chest. On one cot lay Greta Stroud, grey in the face, with both her hands wrapped to the wrists in wet cloth. Kiva was put on the other.

"Left hand," said Aske. "Palm up. Don't help me."

The cold thumbs went along her first two fingers, joint by joint. Kiva watched them press. She felt the third finger and the fourth. She felt the first two some while afterward, faintly, as one hears a neighbor through a wall.

"When?"

"On the lever. Forty minutes ago. A little more."

"Then you're inside the hour, and somebody remembered what was said." The grey eyes went briefly to Anwen, who was standing at the tailboard with the team book open in both hands. "Is that page signed?"

"Four names. One dram, ward-trace, sealed at the ash office. To Healer Aske, for K. Rowan." Anwen held it out. "She signed with her right."

"That's what the page is for. I ease now, and I draw against it tomorrow." Aske unlocked the chest. "Sleeve."

The ash came out of a jar no bigger than an egg cup. It was the pale silver-grey that Kiva had seen once before, in a grading cup, and Aske did not settle it. The healer laid it along the inside of Kiva's forearm in a thin line from the elbow to the wrist, and then set both hands over it, and shut those grey eyes.

It was like having a cold, wet string drawn slowly through the arm.

It did not hurt. It went down from the elbow, through the wrist, and out along the two dull fingers to their tips. Where it passed, the deep ache went quiet. The trembling in her hand slowed, and stopped.

"There," said Aske, after a long time. The ash on her arm had gone the color of chalk. "Now. I told you a month ago that this nerve had been burned once and wouldn't forgive a second. I've been looking for the second. It isn't there."

Kiva let her breath go.

"It isn't burned. It's starved. Something went through that arm this morning a great deal faster than it has ever been asked to carry anything, and it took what there was. I don't know what you did. I'm told half the Compact watched and nobody else knows either." The lid of the jar went on. "They'll sulk for a week. Then they'll be where they were on Tuesday, which is slow, and late, and not finished mending. No better. I want that understood. I've eased it. I haven't cured anything."

"And if I'd come tomorrow?"

"Then I'd be saying something else, and you'd not have liked it." Aske turned to the other cot. "A fortnight. No lanes, no floors, nothing lit. The knee was already owed that. Tell your captain."

Greta Stroud had turned her head on the pillow. Her broken nose was very white.

"You're the one on the shelf," she said hoarsely.

"Yes."

"I'd my eyes shut for most of it. They tell me it turned round and walked off." She looked down at her wrapped hands. "I slid a wall under a Flask because a woman I'd follow anywhere said it might do. It was a fair notion. I'd have had it myself." Her mouth twisted. "Your girl stood my place in the line for ten. Mace says he never heard the join."

"She's not my girl," said Kiva. "She's her own."

"Good," said Stroud, and shut her eyes.

They were waiting at the foot of the cart.

Mara had the canvas-backed book under her arm. Orla had both hands on the stick. Neither had tried to climb the step. Kiva got down it by herself, slowly, and stood between them in the thin sun with her sleeve still rolled.

She told them.

She had meant to choose her words. There were not enough of them to choose among. She said that the disk had been warm. She said when, and for how long, and what her fist had been doing. She said that it was cold again by the keeper's gate.

Then she told them about the shelf going away.

Mara's face did not change. It had not changed in a hallway at a quarter to three in the morning either. Only her hand moved, and came to rest flat on the cover of the book, as it rested on a case she was reading.

"Standing up," she said. "In daylight. Over an open sump."

"Dray had me by the belt."

"Then I owe that girl a thing I don't know how to pay." She breathed. "The same as before?"

"There was sound. I'll tell you all of it tonight. At the table. That's the rule."

"It is." Mara looked at her for a moment longer. "I was in the pump house. I had my hand on cell nine. The bank went into step at twenty-two minutes past ten, all four-and-twenty, like a room full of clocks. It came out of it at twenty-four." She tapped the book. "Not one fouled. The pumps never dropped a stroke. I wrote that down before I knew who'd done it."

The fever came after dark.

It was not like the last. That had been hot sand in the lines. This was a slow heat everywhere, a banked stove, and a headache that sat behind her eyes. She lay on top of the blanket with a wet cloth across her chest and the disk cold on the cloth.

There was no dream. She waited for one, and it did not come.

There was only the note.

She could not hum it. She tried, quietly, with her teeth shut, and what came out was not it. But it was there, the way the after-ring of a bell is there. It was one note that had been a great many.

She got up at ten and went down the passage to the kitchen. The rule said the same night.

Mara had the lamp trimmed and a clean page.

Kiva told it. She told the height, and the roads, and the lights that were all unlike. She told the notes. She told about trying to count, and losing four. She told the lights touching, and the one note, and looking up.

"And under it," she said. "At the very end. Something small. It ticked. Once."

Mara's pencil stopped.

"Like the bag."

"Like the bag. Like a drum pen." Kiva pressed the cloth to her chest. "I don't know that it's the same. I only know it's the word I'd use for both."

"Then that's what I'll write. *The word she'd use for both.*" Mara wrote it. She sat back. "I looked at the hall panel when we came in. Did you see me?"

"Yes."

"It's quenched. It's been quenched since the thirteenth. There's nothing in it to touch. I looked anyway." She was quiet. "I shall probably go on looking."

The stove ticked. Kiva drank the water that had been set out for her.

"That I'd missed."

"I heard a girl tell eleven hundred people exactly what she couldn't do, and then be let do it by somebody whose business it was to decide. That's a meter, and a shutdown path, and a book. "I should hope so. So was I. I was across the road with my hand on a cell." She pushed the water jug nearer. "Drink that. And then you'll help me with something, if your head will stand it. I've the keeper's book for Wednesday. I'm to reconcile it."

It lay under the brass weight, a smaller book than the trunk's, black with handling.

"Not tonight," said Mara. "Saturday. I want to read it twice by myself first. There's a line in it I'd like to be sure of before I show it to anyone."

Noa Bexley had the tape pinned along the edge of a drawing board, and he had been looking at it for two days.

He was in the mast room at the Pit, a brick cupboard under the eastern rim. The keeper had let him in with a grunt. The Chair's letter said *sanctioned instrument,* and *copy to this desk,* and Noa had made the copy on Thursday, and then he had gone on sitting there.

Sixteen pens. He had looked at fifteen of them once.

The sixteenth lay dead flat along its line from the gate until nearly twenty-four minutes past ten. He had pointed at it on Wednesday morning. *Yours. Doing nothing.* A whole dram, unspent, for ninety minutes.

Then it moved.

There was a small neat step. That was the bound. He had measured a great many bounds, his own among them, and that one was two hundredths, as it ought to be.

After the step the pen should have run level. She had shut the Ember at the top. Everyone said so. She said so. Shut meant nothing drawing.

It did not run level.

It went straight up the paper.

He had put a glass on it. The line climbed twenty-six hundredths in a fifth of a second. There was no slope to it. It was a cliff. The pen had gone up so hard that it had left a tiny blot at the top, where the nib had jarred.

And then, from the top of the cliff, a gentle slope. Nine seconds and a little, three hundredths, a tired stance on a heavy lever. That was all.

"Two, and twenty-six, and three," said Noa aloud. "Thirty-one."

He had written it into her book on Thursday, in the cart shed, and into his own. He had worked the Draw because he could not stop himself, and it had come out near twelve. The bench had frightened a Placement Chair with three point one.

Twelve. For the only thing she had ever done that worked.

It was the cliff that he could not leave alone. The mast drew a pen for a fighter, not a door. It could not say what had been open. But he had the keeper's word, and the foreman's slate, and a Graywater Edge's draft card that the Chair had let him read, and they all said the same. Her shins had been bare. Her foot had not yet come down.

By every account, there had been nothing open at all when twenty-six hundredths went out of her.

*The work is in me in the air,* she had written. He had seen it. She let him read the *why* column now. *Where is it when it isn't in either one?*

He did not know. He knew what it cost to get it across.

He turned to the notebook's other page. *Things that are probably nothing.* It had grown. He read down it, as he did most nights. The tears. The skins. The class. Her still door. A plate in a meadow. A seventh reed.

He wrote at the bottom, in pencil.

*The Pit, 26th. Every lamp on a dead ring, once. Keeper's book says T-0. She has three more of those in hers.*

"Mr. Bexley?"

He had not heard the door.

The man was about forty, and pleasant, and wore a black coat with a narrow gold cord at the cuff. He stood with his hat in his hand as if the mast room had been somebody's parlor.

"I'm told you're the one who reads for the Chair. I'm with the Halden technical office. We've an interest in efficient patterning, as you'd expect." He smiled. "I understand that there's a tape."

"There's a sanctioned tape," said Noa. His mouth was dry. "It goes to the Placement Chair. I can't show it."

"Of course not. And your own notes? I'm told you've a paper in hand. We fund a good many papers. It can make a difference to a young man in Third Division." The smile did not alter. "Your name would be first on it, naturally."

Noa looked at the drawing board. He looked at the cliff, with its tiny blot.

He had wanted a paper since he was eleven. He had wanted it in the roster corridor, among the drawers, so badly that he had had to sit down. *Second,* he had told her, at the foot of a stone porch. *After yours.*

"They're her numbers," he said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Every one of them. I measure. I don't own." It came out steadier than he had expected. "She sees the sheet first, and her sponsor does. If she says stop, I stop. You'd have to ask her. I don't think you'd like what her aunt would say."

"I see." The pleasant man put his hat on. "The Circuit's tapes are open to chartered parties thirty days after the standings post. You'll know that. So I shall be reading this one on the twenty-eighth of next month, in any case." He nodded at the board. "I only thought it might be agreeable to read it in company. Good evening."

The door shut.

Noa sat for a while with his hands flat on his knees.

Thirty days. Anybody with a charter could walk in here in thirty days and put a glass on that cliff. They would not need him. They would not need her leave.

He took the pencil, and under the last line on the page that was probably nothing, he wrote one more.

*They can look now. So I'd better find out first.*
