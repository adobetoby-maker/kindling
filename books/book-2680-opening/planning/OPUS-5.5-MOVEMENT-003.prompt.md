## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of

# Chapter 16 — Kiva: What Carries

The referee's flag stayed on the table.

Kiva saw Lowen Brask notice that. He had come down to the sill for the third carry with his eyes already on the white cloth, as a man looks at the clock he means to catch a train by, and when she said *still* for the second time that afternoon, his calm brown gaze came round to her and rested.

He did not look worried. He looked interested.

"Bell," said the referee.

Noa went. This time Jude went with him, on his own struts, which were shorter and clumsier and dug a trench in the raked sand. They had Old Tom by a pole each before the four still-doors were off their bottom step, and they did not try to lift him. They ran him backward across the western half like two boys dragging a sledge.

Kiva went down her own two steps and no further.

It was no distance at all, and she took it at her own speed, because nobody on the eastern side was going to arrive anywhere quickly. She turned at the bottom step. She set her right heel against the stone riser, hard, the good leg, and felt the whole weight of the Hall behind it.

"Here," she said.

The litter slid to a halt at her feet in a spray of sand. She bent and took the two head poles, and let herself go down.

The still door opened. The sand took her, and the step took what the sand did not, and Old Tom became part of the floor.

"Thirteen hundredths," said Noa, at her ear, and was gone.

They came in a row, the four of them, walking.

It was a long time to stand and watch four people walk. She had done harder things that month. She had knelt with a pan in each hand while a husk went past her shoulder. But this was different, because a husk had not been thinking, and every one of the four faces coming toward her across that sand was working something out.

"Pru," said Jude. "The long-armed one. She's yours. Don't stop her. Make her go round, and when she goes round, go round with her."

"That's not a ward. That's a gate."

"Then be a gate."

Pru's ward came up low and wide and curved, with her weight under it. It rang as Idony Keld reached it. Keld looked for the end, as she had before, and stepped to the right, and the end went with her. She stepped left. The silver swung like a door on a hinge.

"One," said Pru breathlessly. "That's one she hasn't got round. Two. Oh, she doesn't like it. Three."

Moss and the boy with the ears had taken the foot poles. They heaved. Kiva felt it come up through her arms and into her shoulders and down, through her hips and her planted knee and the heel on the stone, and it went into the step and stopped there.

Nothing moved. Hendry Moss looked at the poles in his fists as if they had been nailed to the court.

"Four," said Pru. "Five."

Lowen Brask had not taken a pole. He had come to stand at the side of the litter, a pace from Kiva's right elbow, and he was looking down at her heel.

"That's mine," he said mildly.

"I know. Thank you."

"I taught that to a boy with shoulders like a wardrobe. I never thought I'd be shown it by a Crooked Line first-year." He glanced along the litter at Moss, and gave the smallest nod.

Moss let go of his pole. He straightened, and walked round the foot of the litter to her other side, and stood there. His big arms hung wide. His chest was open.

Kiva kept her eyes on Brask's hands. She could not look at Moss. If she looked, she would see the shape of him going down.

"Jude," she said.

"I see him." Jude Talley was three paces off, with his dark blade low. He looked at the great open chest in front of him. He looked at it for the length of a breath, like a hungry man looking at a pie on someone else's windowsill. "No. Thank you. Not that one."

He turned his shoulder to Moss and faced the captain.

For the first time that afternoon, something moved in Lowen Brask's face. It was not much. It was the look of a man who has put his foot on a stair that was not there.

"Eleven," said Pru, from the middle of the court. Her voice had gone up. "Twelve, she's leaning on it, Jude—"

Brask did not wait for another plan. He was a third-year with a Hold that stopped at seven and a half, and he had the litter to move, and she was six and two. He set both broad hands flat on Old Tom's chest, as he had before, and he pushed.

It was a real push. It was the whole of him. She felt it arrive in the poles as she had felt it in the second carry, slow and steady and patient, a loaded wagon with a strong man behind it.

And she felt it go through her.

It went down her arms and through her ribs and her hips. It went through the stance, which took it, and through her right heel into two hundred years of Stonehand stone. And there it stopped. It did not push her back by the width of a finger. All that weight had come out of him, across the litter, into her and out again into the step, and for one instant it was not in him at all. It was in the wall. He was leaning on the far end of it with nothing held back, as a man leans on a door he expects to open.

She did not think it. There was no time to think it. She knew it the way she knew a needle had stopped.

"*Now,*" said Kiva.

Jude's blade was already there.

It lay across Brask's ribs, flat and dark and neat, in the very breath when he had nothing left to guard them with. It did not come after the stop. It came in it. From inside the stance, she could not have said where her holding ended and his striking began. It was one thing, and two people had done it.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

Lowen Brask knelt in the sand at the foot of the litter.

And Kiva's hands began to go.

She felt it start. The stance turning to grease. Her eyes dragging down toward the kneeling shape. Three weeks of it, in her knees and her wrists. *Dark. Count.*

"Fourteen!" yelled Pru, from fifteen paces off, at the top of her lungs. "Fifteen! Sixteen! That's five touches, Kiva, that's five, we're on five—"

"Staying," said Kiva.

She said it aloud, through her teeth, to nobody. It was not a number. It did the same work. She kept her eyes on the two poles in her fists, and she did not look at the sand, and the still door held.

Moss had seized the foot poles again. He hauled. He was a great deal stronger than the boy with the ears, and he had been waiting for her to go soft.

The litter did not move.

From his knees, with the line judge counting over his head, Lowen Brask looked up at her. He was smiling. It was the slow, pleased, rueful smile of a teacher who has been caught out by a pupil.

"You didn't look," he said.

"Not this time."

"No," said Brask, as if she had handed him something. He bowed his shaved head and waited for his ten.

---

The rest of it was long, and it was not pretty, and she held on.

Keld got round Pru on the count of nineteen. She did it by not going round at all. She put her palm flat against the ward and leaned, and went on leaning, a tall thin girl with all the patience in the world, and on the ninth ring the silver went with a splash. Her other hand took Pru Finch in the shoulder before the cold air had cleared.

"*Down!* Crooked Line!"

"Nine taps!" said Pru, from her knees, in a voice of pure wonder. "It took *nine*! Jude, did you see, it's never gone past four—"

Then there were three of them on Old Tom's foot poles, because Brask had risen from his ten and taken the left one, and Keld had come to help, and Kiva began to learn what her arms were made of.

She was not being moved. That was the strange part. The step had her heel and the sand had her knees. But the poles were only wood, and her hands were only hands, and three still-doors were trying to draw seventy kilos of sand out of her grip a hair at a time. Her fingers burned. The two slow ones on the left burned least, because they could not properly feel it, and she watched them, coldly, to be sure they had not opened without telling her.

Jude could not get a clean touch. They were guarding now. Noa darted in and out at the boy with the ears, who had been left with nothing to pull on and was trying to get a hand to the canvas.

"Half! Crooked Line."

"A minute," called the referee.

"Seven hundredths!" shouted Noa. He had been to the board and back. "Kiva, you've got seven!"

She had the hand door. It was right there, as it always was. A ward across the middle of that litter, between her fists and theirs, even one the size of a tea tray, and three pairs of hands would have had nothing to hold. It was exactly the right door. It would take two seconds and a white flag, and whatever she had left in the dram.

She had the bar. Flat across those three sets of knuckles.

She had both of them, and the stance besides. For half a breath, with her arms shaking and her knee on fire, she thought about how it had felt on the Cellhouse floor to stand in the jam of two doors and not be moved by anything.

The bottom heat-line warmed.

"One," said Kiva.

"You could change," said Lowen Brask. He said it quietly, across the length of Old Tom, between heaves. He was not taunting her. He was a teacher, and he wanted to know. "You've a blade. I've seen it on a plate. It would stop the lot of us."

"I know what I've got."

"Then why not?"

"Because I can't get to it." It came out in pieces, between breaths. "Not without letting go of this. I can open any of them. I can't get from one to the next without putting everything down in between. So I've really only got the one I'm standing on."

He considered that, with his hands full of pole.

"That's the most sensible thing I've heard a first-year say in my court," said Brask. "Pull, Hendry."

They pulled. Her right hand slipped a finger's width on the wood, and she clamped it.

"Half!" said the line judge. "Crooked Line. That's two on the same man. *Down!* Stonehand!"

The boy with the ears sat down in the sand with a look of deep reproach. Noa whooped.

"Thirty seconds," said the referee.

It was the longest thirty seconds of the month. She counted them, because there was nothing else she could do with them. At twelve, her left hand came off the pole entirely. The two dull fingers had simply stopped, without warning, the way a tired spring stops. She caught the wood again in the crook of her elbow and hung on with her whole forearm, and the litter jerked a hand's width east and stuck.

"Five," said Kiva. "Four."

"*Time!*"

The three of them let go at once, so that she nearly went over backward.

Old Tom lay on the sand at the foot of the western steps, with his arms folded and his embroidered name the right way up. He had not been carried anywhere. He was a hand's width from where she had first taken hold of him.

"Litter in the western half," said the referee. "One point, Crooked Line."

The board turned over its figures for the last time.

*STONEHAND 9. CROOKED LINE 7.*

*Third carry: Crooked Line 3, Stonehand 1.*

And beside her own name, in the last column: *Dram, 0.98. No overspend.*

---

They shook hands at the sill.

Kiva's would hardly close. Idony Keld took it anyway, briefly, and looked at the knee, and said, "I'd strap that," which from her seemed to be warmth. Hendry Moss shook it as though it might come off. The boy with the ears shook Noa's and said that it had not been fair, two halves, and Noa agreed with him earnestly and at length.

"Nine to seven," said Lowen Brask to Jude. "It was eleven to three in the autumn."

"I remember."

"You turned Hendry down. I've been offering that boy to third teams for two years, and nobody's ever said *no thank you*." Brask shook his head. "I'll have to think of something else. I'm rather annoyed." He turned to Kiva. "You stopped telling me where you were going."

"I stopped going anywhere."

"Yes. I noticed that as well." He looked at her for a moment with that mild, careful attention. "I'd have had you, you know. In a fourth carry. Your left hand had gone. I saw it go."

"I know. So did I."

"Good," said Brask. "Then next time it won't be news." And he went away up the eastern steps, with his team behind him, to where the gallery was stamping.

The referee was filling in the lane card. She wrote for some time. Then she turned it on the table so that Kiva could read the last line without having to ask.

*Rowan, K. (prov.). Door declared each carry. Three changes, each declared, none before the flag. No undeclared change. No incident.*

"That goes to the Chair tonight," said the referee. Her voice was as much like a dropped crate as ever. "I had you in the Terrace Ring last month. You'd lit all three by the first touch, and you fell over a step. That's a different fighter's card, and I've signed it clean." She blotted it. "I'll tell you what else goes up with it, since I don't write behind people's backs. I told the Chair when she placed you that a fighter who has to be flagged from one door to the next isn't safe. She's supervised. I've seen nothing today to change my mind, and I've said so on the back."

"We lost," said Kiva. It was all she could find.

"You did. By two. Go and sit down before that knee does it for you."

She did not get to sit down. Noa Bexley was standing in front of her with a strip of tape in each hand and an expression of such painful resolve that she thought for a moment he had been hurt.

"I've got to ask you something," he said. "Properly. I said I'd do it after, and it's after."

"Ask."

"I want to measure you. Not just the tape. Everything. Output, draw, how long each door takes to come back, what they do when they're shut." It came out in a rush. He had clearly said it to himself a good many times. "I think the bench has been wrong about you for four years, and I think I can show how. If I'm right, it'll be a paper, and I want my name on it. Second. After yours. I'd rather say that now than have you find out later that I wanted it." He swallowed. "They're your numbers. If you say no, I won't file anything. I'll still measure, because I can't not. But it stays in my notebook."

Kiva looked at him. She thought about a tag on a mast, and a quench box, and a clerk with spectacles who had written down three point one.

"Every figure goes in my book as well," she said. "The same day. I see the sheet before anybody else does, and so does Orla. And if I say stop filing, you stop."

"Yes. All of that. Yes."

"Then yes."

Noa let out his breath. "Good. Then come to the hold posts. Now, before you've rested. The referee says we may. I asked her at the interval, in case."

---

Stonehand kept its hold posts in an alcove behind the eastern porch, three steel uprights exactly like the ones in the Measure Hall.

He made all four of them do it. Jude went first, grumbling, and lit his edge inside the ring of sensing wire, and Noa counted it and read the gauge.

"Four and one. You were six and four at the Hall in the spring." He wrote. "Down a bit more than a third. That's right. That's what twelve minutes of lane does. Pru."

Pru's ward, which had held Keld for nine taps, read three and eight on the post. She had been five and nine. Noa tested himself, with the air of a man taking his own temperature, and reported two and seven against four flat, and said that it served him right.

"Now you," he said. "Edge first."

Kiva raised her right fist. Her arm trembled. The metal came off the cuff and closed into the bar, and it looked exactly as ugly as it always had.

"Light."

The edge-light came on along the one sharp side, dull orange. It shivered at the corner. She held it.

Noa counted aloud. She had held it for eleven on Classification Day, with a gallery watching and three doors open. She was so tired now that the alcove swam.

"Eleven," said Noa. "Twelve. Thirteen—"

It guttered out.

Nobody spoke. Noa read the gauge. He read it again, and wrote, and his pencil was not quite steady.

"Ward," he said.

Seven taps. It had been six.

"Stance."

The steel arm came forward and pressed against her breastbone, over the disk, and she let her weight go down. It went. But it went like water into a cracked jug. Her knees shook, and the plate under her boots slid, and the gauge climbed sluggishly and stopped well short of where she knew it ought to.

"Four and nine," said Noa. "From six and two."

He lowered the notebook.

"You've fought twelve minutes," he said slowly. "The same twelve as Jude. Your stance is down by a fifth, a bit more, because you stood on it for most of them. That's right. That's what it ought to do." He turned back a page. "But your Edge is up. It's better than it was at the Hall. And you had the bar out for nearly four minutes in the first two carries."

"I didn't use it in the third."

"That's four minutes' rest. Nobody gets a hold back in four minutes. I've a year of this team's tapes. It takes Jude a quarter of an hour, sitting down with his eyes shut, to get back half of what he's spent, and he can't do it while he's doing anything else." Noa's narrow face had gone very intent. "You got yours back while you were holding a litter against three still-doors."

"The ash was nearly gone," said Kiva. "I had two hundredths left. That's the same as anyone."

"The ash is one pot. Yes. You drank it to the bottom, exactly like everybody else." He tapped the page. "It's not the pot. It's the doors. Jude's got one, and when it's tired, he's tired. You're not one tired fighter. You're three, and two of them were sitting out." He stopped, and she saw him catch himself, as he had in the Low Lane. "That's one reading. I'd want twenty. I'd want it on different days, in a different order, with someone else holding the rod."

"But it's a sanctioned board," said Jude. He had been listening with his arms folded. "With a referee's stamp on the tape."

"Yes," said Noa. "It's that as well."

Kiva let her arms hang. She was too tired to feel much of anything. But a thought had come to her, small and clear, and she turned it over with care, as she would a part she did not recognize.

Every time she had ever fought, she had tried to be three doors at once and been beaten by people with one. And for four years, Orla had said *sequence*, and she had heard it as a rule about safety, a thing you did so the cells would not scream.

Nobody had ever told her that a door she had shut cleanly was a door that was resting. She did not think anybody had known.

---

Rhea Sorn did not go down to the sand.

She had thought about it. She sat on in the chair with the arms while the gallery emptied round her and the old man with the chisel went on cutting names into brass at the north end, and she watched four tired children in grey troop off toward an alcove with a boy who was waving a strip of paper.

Doran had gone to find his placement. He had been pleased with the Pryce girl all week, in his quiet way, and had said twice that a Home Line officer did not write a note like the one Vane had written unless he meant it.

It was not Pryce that Rhea had come for.

She had watched the first two carries with her arms folded and had not much liked what she saw. It was what she had expected from the plate. The girl had too much, and it was all loose. She had opened a door for whatever stood in front of her, as a careless traveler opens every bag on the platform, and a patient third-year with a shaved head had robbed her each time she set one down.

And then the third.

Rhea was Edge only. She had been Edge only for twenty-three years, not by any choice of hers, and she had made a life of it. She knew to the ounce what one door could carry. She had also stood beside a good many people who had been given more than one could carry, by talent or by money or by a famous name, and she had seen what most of them did when the thing in front of them was heavy and the right tool was lying within reach.

They reached. It was the most natural thing in the world.

The girl had stood at the foot of those steps for four minutes with a blade to hand and a ward to hand and a captain asking her, to her face, why she did not use them. Rhea had been close enough to see her lips. *Because I can't get to it.* And she had gone on standing there, on the one door, with her left hand failing, until the glass ran out. And lost.

That was not a fighter either. But it was nearer to something Rhea could use.

There had been a cord at the girl's collar the whole time. Nothing had swung out on it. Rhea had looked, and had been ashamed of looking, and had looked again.

She took a draft card from her coat. The first one was in the board's tray on the Brickfields road, and she did not mean to take it back. She wrote a second.

*Meet, Stonehand, the 4th. Lost 9–7. Three declared changes in two carries. Every one cost her and she saw why by the interval. Third carry: one door, no change, held their captain on a braced stance. Teammate scored off the stop. Asked to her face to open a second and did not. Still NOT for draft. Watch the Flask. — R.S.*

She read it over. Then she turned the card and wrote a line on the back, for the board's eye, because it had been sitting at the back of her mind since the Brickfields muster on Thursday.

*Nine tears on the district board since the thaw. A usual spring is four or five. Three of ours threw Handfuls with a skin.*

Rhea put the card away.

Ilse Corran had carried that thing on its cord for thirty years and had never explained it to a living soul. She had pulled a drowning probationer out of a culvert and told her nothing. Rhea could wait a season to ask a seventeen-year-old what she wore around her neck.

She could watch the Flask first.

---

They found her on the Hall steps at dusk, all three of them, because she could not get any further on the knee.

Kellan had won. Crooked Line's first team had beaten Stonehand's by eleven to six in the last lane of the card, and he had put four people down and been touched by none of them. He sat on the step below hers and told her so in two sentences, as a person might report the weather. Then he took the folded paper out of his pocket.

"Your third carry. Eleven hundredths in four minutes, with two of them on the poles and then three." He had copied it off the board. "My best carry was nineteen. I've never been outspent downward by anyone in a sanctioned lane. I'd like to know how."

"I lost, Kellan."

"I'm aware of that. It's a different column." He frowned at the paper. "I'm not saying it's good. I'm saying it's low, and I can't account for it. The boy with the tape says it's the door."

"He says a lot of things. He's got one reading."

"Then I'd like to see the next nineteen," said Kellan, and wrote something down.

Anwen had her coat on and a canvas bag at her feet.

"Mace wants me at Brickfields by six tomorrow evening," she said. "The night watch on the Ferren culverts. It's only a shadow. I carry the lamp and hold what I'm told to hold." She was trying to say it flatly, and her whole face was giving her away. "I'll be back on Tuesday. So I won't be at your review."

"I know."

"I'd rather be on the culvert. I want that said. If they'd offered me the choice, I'd have picked the culvert."

"It's said." Kiva found that she was smiling, tired as she was. "Anwen. Go and hold a ward for Graywater. I'll tell you what Ysra says."

"Write it down. Then I can read it." Anwen picked up her bag. She hesitated, which was not like her. "You taught that girl to lean. The one with the scarf. I watched her do it."

"You taught me."

"I know I did. I'm only saying I saw where it went." She went down the steps without looking back, quick and neat, with the bag bumping at her hip.

Perrin had come out last. He lowered himself onto the stone with great care for his ribs and stretched his legs out.

"My Hall won your lane," he said. "It lost Kellan's, so I'm told it comes out even. I'd like to be congratulated for mine. By somebody. I don't mind who."

"Congratulations, Perrin."

"Thank you. It tastes like a mouthful of sweepings." He leaned his head back against the step above. "Brask found me afterward. He said I'd sat through the whole of the second carry with my hands between my knees like a man in church. He said he'd seen me from the sand. And then he said *thank you for not shouting*, and I didn't know where to look."

Kiva had been watching the Hall doors. Through them, at the far north end, a lamp was burning over a long brass plate, and an old man on a stool was still at work with a mallet.

She had seen that plate from the porch. She had not thought about it. She thought about it now, and about a boy with a scarf round his ribs who had said nothing all week about why the Hall healer had laughed at him.

She counted the bright new lines. Three.

She opened her mouth.

"Don't," said Perrin. He had not moved, and his eyes were shut. "It's in my book. I wrote it down the same night, with the weight, and what I think I'd have held. It's a good page. It's not going in yours."

"Perrin—"

"I stood where I meant to stand. I'd stand there again." He opened one eye. "And I mind. I've decided I'm allowed both. You taught me that. It's extremely annoying of you."

She did not say it. She put her hand on his shoulder instead, the way she had on a couch three weeks ago, and he let her.

---

Ysra Crest read the lane card on Monday morning as she had read the field book, from the first word to the last.

She had a good deal else on the desk. Kiva, sitting very upright with her knee strapped under her trousers, could see Hollis Vane's slanting hand on a half sheet, and a page copied from the Kennel book, and Orla's four lines. Orla was in the chair by the window with her bound leg out and her stick across her lap. She had not slept much. Neither of them had.

"A loss," said Ysra at last.

"Nine to seven."

"I can read a board, Miss Rowan." She set the card down and squared it. "Three changes of door in a sanctioned lane. Each one said aloud, to a referee I'd trust with my own file, and none made before her flag. Not one undeclared. No incident." She turned the card over, read what was written on the back, and turned it face up again without remark. Then she drew the half sheet toward her. "A field response on the Sag Line. *Catch sealed at fifty-one. Nothing fouled.* And the catcher, according to an officer who has failed better teams than yours for less, called her own blade dark with a husk walking at her." She looked up. "Door declared: none. I've been Placement Chair for nineteen years, and I have never before had a candidate enter a sanctioned event on no door at all. I had to ask a clerk whether it was permitted."

"Was it?"

"There was no rule against it. Nobody had thought to write one." Ysra made a small mark in the margin of her own notes, from which Kiva gathered that somebody was about to. "I told you on the thirteenth that you'd be reviewed after your first sanctioned meet. This is that review, and it is short. You were given one condition above all the others, which was not to have an incident, and you haven't. Your provisional place is retained. Third Division, Crooked Line, third team. The conditions stand as written. I see no reason to loosen any of them, and you have given me no reason to tighten them."

Kiva breathed out.

"It's still the lowest defensible place," said Ysra. "Don't carry it out of this room as anything more. You lost a Third Division lane. Your team is where it was. You are certified to kneel beside ash and open nothing." Her long plain face was perfectly grave. "I hope you still hate it."

"I do."

"Good." She put the card in the folder. "There's a student in my corridor. He's been there since eight. He has a sheet of paper, and he tells my clerk that you've seen it."

"I've seen it. It's in my book as well. My name's first."

"Fetch him."

Noa Bexley came in as though the carpet might be wired. He gave her the sheet with both hands. Ysra read it, and Kiva watched her read it, and saw the moment when the iron-grey head stopped moving.

It was a long moment.

"This is the Stonehand board," said Ysra.

"Yes, Chair."

"I certified that board in the autumn. My office's stamp is on the tape. The lane had a Circuit referee." She turned the sheet over, found nothing on the back, and turned it again. "Draw on the still door alone, across four minutes of a contested hold. Eleven hundredths and a hair on the tape, set against the book's twelve for that work. Nought point nine three. My bench, in my hall, on Classification Day, gave her three point one." She set it down very gently. "And three hold readings taken directly after, by leave of that same referee, which say that a door she had shut was fresher at the end of twelve minutes than it was on the day I deferred her."

"It's one day's readings," said Noa. His voice shook, and he kept on. "I've said so at the bottom. I'd want twenty."

"So you have. I can see where you've said it." Ysra looked at him over the paper. "Most people who bring me something like this leave that sentence off. Why did you ask her first?"

Noa went red.

"They're her numbers," he said.

"So they are," said Ysra. She was quiet for a time. Outside the window, a lift craft crossed the roofs of the Compact Quarter with its rings shining. "I can't dismiss this. I should like to be clear that I looked for a way. It comes off my own instruments. If I say the Stonehand board is wrong, I've voided every lane fought on it this season, and if I say it's right, then my bench has been measuring that girl's quarrel with herself for four years and writing it down as her." She folded her hands on the sheet. "I am not going to rule on what it means. I haven't twenty readings, and nor have you. But you'll go on taking them, Mr. Bexley, under my office's notice, on sanctioned instruments, with her consent in writing every month. And a copy comes to this desk."

"Yes, Chair."

"That's all. You may wait outside. Try not to tell my clerk about it."

When the door had shut, she did not at once go on. She drew two other papers from beneath the folder and laid them side by side, and Kiva, who could read a page upside down as well as anyone raised in a records shop, saw the Kennel warden's large soft hand on one and a column of figures on the other.

"You had better both hear what comes next, and what it rests on," said Ysra. She touched the column of figures. "My own office tells me that I have signed more Fire readings this spring than in the ten before it put together, and that the median Hold of the entering class is the highest in the forty years we've kept the figure. So I have more first-years fit to try for a Flask than the calendar allowed for." She touched the other. "Warden Druce and the Wren House graders tell me that the Kennel has thrown husks in the past month that it never threw in sixty years, and that one came off a real line on the twenty-eighth, with your name on the tag, Miss Rowan. The Home Line calls it a dirty spring, and has written to the Compact board to say so. Nine tears in the district since the thaw. They want more licensed hands by midsummer than the calendar was going to give them."

Orla had lifted her head.

"The board met on Saturday night," said Ysra, and put both papers away.

"The Flask qualifiers," said Orla.

"Were set for the last week of next month. They have been moved to the last week of this one. Every team holding a first step is on the list, in order of yield." Ysra's dry level voice did not change. "Twenty and eight-tenths puts Team Seven seventeenth of thirty-one. You are not last, Miss Rowan. I'd advise you not to enjoy it. A Flask is not a Handful with more ash in it, and you have three weeks."

---

The Old Yard was the same that evening as it had always been, which was a comfort, and also a rebuke.

Orla sat on her crate with the lamp on its hook. Kiva gave her the book, open at Saturday, and sat on the other crate with her leg out, and the two of them made a matching pair.

Orla read the page. She read it twice. Then she put her finger on a line near the bottom and turned the book round.

*Third carry. Heel on the riser. He pushed and it all went into the step and for a second none of it was in him. Jude's touch landed in the stop. Not after. In. I couldn't tell where mine ended.*

"Do you know what that is?" said Orla.

"A point."

"It's a hand-off." Her crooked face was very still in the yellow light. "The stop of one door going straight into the strike of another, with nothing dropped in between. I told you about it on a Friday night in this yard, and I told you I'd never seen it done across doors." She tapped the page. "I still haven't. That was across people. A still-door and an Ember, on two different pairs of feet. The Home Line's done that for five hundred years. It's the whole of what a team is."

"So it isn't a sequence."

"No. It isn't yours. Don't write it down as yours." Orla handed the book back. "But on Saturday morning you didn't know what it feels like from the inside when the work goes across and doesn't spill. And now you do. You've been one half of one." She leaned back. "I couldn't have given you that with a year of talking. I'd thought I might have to."

Kiva looked down the lane. The posts stood black in their two rows. At the far end, the last of them leaned out of the line and into the dark.

"We lost," she said. "I keep saying it. Everybody keeps telling me it's a different column."

"It is a different column. It's also a loss. Your captain's still Third, and he'll be Third in the autumn, and that sandbag's been over the wrong line twice more." Orla did not soften it. "You had three doors on that sand, and for two carries all they did was get you knocked down. You had access to more than anyone in the Hall, and the only thing you had control of was a stance and a step. That's the true account. I'd be a poor sponsor if I let Bexley's tape talk you out of it."

"I know."

"I know you know. It's in the last column." Orla nearly smiled. "Three weeks to a Flask. You'll kneel for it. I've nothing else to sign you for, and I won't until I see something. Now. Up. The knee can stand, and your arms can sulk. Once, and I'll count."

Kiva stood in the lane.

She raised her right fist and asked for Edge. The bar came, ugly and dark. She looked at the crooked post, and let the metal go, and did not watch it home. She breathed out. One, two. The ward, curved, leaning, folded. One, two. The weight of the old ground through her boots, taken and given back.

"Eight and four," said Orla.

It was two-tenths. It was nothing, set beside a boy who could change his whole shape between one footfall and the next. But it was the third time that number had come down in a fortnight, and not once had it come down because she hurried.

She put her hand inside her collar.

The disk was cold. She held it a moment in her palm, with the nine grooves against her skin and the old Kennel ash still caught in three of them. It had been cold in the Cellhouse, and cold on the Sag Line, and cold at the foot of a stone step with a litter in her hands. Somebody had carried it for thirty years and chosen her, and it had never yet told her whether she was getting warmer.

She had stopped asking it. She noticed that, standing there. She could not have said when.

She tucked it back, and sat down on the crate, and opened the book on her good knee.

*Exercise:* The Carry. Stonehand, the Long Terrace. Third Division lane.

*What went wrong:* Played it like a floor. Went dark when one of theirs went down beside me. Twice. Their captain saw it and used it. Changed doors three times and told him each time where I'd be empty. Flag adds a second.

*Who was hurt:* Me. Knee, again. Left hand gave out at the end. Pru, shoulder, not badly.

*What it cost:* 0.98 of a dram. Two carries. The lane, 9 to 7.

*How long to come back:* Stance, down to 4.9 after. Edge and ward were up. Noa says the doors I shut were resting. One reading.

*Second go:* One door. No changes. Heel on the riser. Pru counted for me. Stayed planted when he went down. Won the carry 3 to 1.

She looked at the last column for a while. The lamp hissed. Orla, on the other crate, had shut her eyes.

*Why:* I can open three. I can only stand on one. The others are for the people next to me, until I can carry something across without putting it down.

She read it over. It was true. It was not all of it.

Under it, smaller, she wrote: *Flask in three weeks. Kneeling.*

Then she closed the book and got up, on a knee that did not want to, and went and stood in the lane again.

"Orla," she said. "Count."

## Combat progression, Riftspawn, and earned ash (`LOCKED 2680`)

The visible progression engine joins student combat, field service, and the ash
economy. It should be experienced repeatedly on the page rather than explained once.

1. **Train:** discipline schools teach body mechanics, door control, team roles,
   spawn anatomy, terrain, containment, quench procedure, casualty extraction, and
   ash recovery. Several Book One chapters must show students learning specifically
   how to kill Riftspawn, not merely how to duel other students.
2. **Contest:** school teams fight one another in Measures, brackets, and mixed-role
   lanes. A human opponent adapts, remembers, and exposes habits that a spawn does not.
3. **Hunt or respond:** students graduate through licenses from contained Handful
   releases to supervised live rifts. Riftspawn are dangerous animals or expressions,
   not training dummies. A clean kill still leaves containment and rescue work.
4. **Recover:** defeated Riftspawn dissolve into finite ownerless ash. The team must
   catch, quarantine, grade, and reconcile it before it can be used. Contaminated or
   badly captured ash can poison a cell, attract another rift, or injure an operator.
5. **Invest:** the public authority takes its civic and casualty shares; the school,
   team, and licensed fighters receive recorded yield shares. Earned ash pays for
   training cells, equipment, travel, medical reserve, and harder sanctioned entries.
6. **Advance:** rank is not an automatic reward for a kill. The soul grows through
   repeated owned exertion, correction, recovery, and reproducible control. Ash lets a
   student attempt more difficult work, but cannot purchase judgment or replace what
   the soul has learned.

This produces two linked scoreboards: personal capability (rank, Hold, Draw, control,
and Call) and team yield (clean ash recovered after rescue, closure, and public shares).
Kill count alone is an immature metric and a persistent source of institutional abuse,
but kills and usable ash must remain materially important to students and companies.

**Draw** is efficiency: ash spent on a defined piece of work divided by the governing
book or bench expectation. `1.0` is standard and lower is more efficient. The expected
cost is task-specific; the accepted book figure for a contested four-minute hold is
twelve hundredths of a dram, so eleven hundredths and a hair records as `0.93`.

Recovered ash has three separate measurements. **Yield** records quantity on the
Handful -> Flask -> Barrel -> Well ladder. **Grade** records purity and stability;
fraudulently stamped ash can kill an operator even when the quantity is correct.
**Resonance** records the defeated spawn's expression and therefore which training,
medical, containment, transit, or industrial patterns can use the ash efficiently.
A rare yield is valuable because of what it can safely fund, not because it grants the
killer a packaged new power. Student teams carry metered combat ash for permitted
techniques and equipment, so decisions about spending or saving an earned share have
visible consequences in the next match and the next rift.

ways, and reach higher yield classes. Early institutions read the two trends

## Ashcraft and electrical power (`LOCKED 2680`)

Centuries of work have turned ash from carried healer fuel into the rebuilt world's
principal finite energy commodity.

- **Ash induction:** refined ash sits in a warded cell. A trained operator impresses
  a stable magical pattern on it; the induction assembly converts the controlled
  release into ordinary electrical current. Once tuned, a modern cell runs without
  continuous soul expenditure, but never without finite fuel, maintenance, metering,
  or risk.
- **Magic supplies form; ash supplies fuel.** A soul cannot be replaced by ash because
  ash has no owner, judgment, or intent. Ash cannot safely do complex work without a
  pattern held by a person or preserved in a carefully built device.
- **Traditions remain distinct:** Edge-derived work defines conductive paths and
  precise release; Ward-derived work contains reaction and redirects force;
  cultivated/internal work holds resonance, distributes load, and prevents collapse.

  gives Kiva possibility, not instant mastery.

  medical machinery, tools, transit, station gates, arenas, and rift sensing.
  Critical services retain mechanical and hardened backups.
- **Failure modes:** contaminated ash, false grading, pattern drift, runaway heat,
  operator backwash, grid damage, and rift attraction. Every serious device has a
  meter, a shutdown path, and a responsible ledger.
- **Glory scale:** a Glory master can stabilize industrial patterns that lesser
  practitioners can only maintain in teams or for short intervals. This has produced
  powerful industrial houses—titans whose refineries, grids, transit works, and
  professional companies shape governments—as well as near-monastic school orders

## Energy revolution after Meridian (`LOCKED 2680 HISTORY`)

The decisive technological leap occurred during the first century after the first
Home Line, then compounded for more than four hundred years.

1. **Ash as emergency fuel:** Meridian-era practitioners used ash mainly for healing,
   training, lamps, and survival work. Its finite nature and dangerous grading were
   understood before its industrial potential.
2. **Radiation return:** Sustain-trained Kindled learned to remove active radiation
   burden from living workers while medicine treated exposure and fixed damage.
   They could enter zones ordinary crews could not, recover records and components,
   and work in short, measured rotations. This never made radiation harmless or
   reversed tissue already destroyed.
3. **Nuclear recovery:** engineers combined ordinary shielding, remote machinery,
   disciplined rotations, ash containment patterns, and Glory-scale oversight to
   reopen surviving nuclear sites and build safer compact reactors. Ward does not
   move radiation; the breakthrough is containment, control, monitoring, and safe
   human access—not a quiet rewrite of the old power rules.
4. **Tamar Vey's question:** an itinerant Home Line teacher and storyteller visits
   an early recovery workshop whose engineers keep trying to make ash burn longer.
   With an ordinary bowl, a conductor, a measured pinch of ash, and a short-held
   pattern, Tamar demonstrates that ash can preserve an instruction after the
   operator releases it. The lesson is conceptual: the reactor's fire already knows
   how to make heat; perhaps ash should hold the rules around the fire. Tamar does
   not design the reactor or hand civilization a finished device. Engineers spend
   years turning that question into safe, reproducible work.
5. **Ash-nuclear synthesis:** ash patterns stabilize reaction geometry, manage heat
   and load, and improve electrical conversion. Nuclear fuel supplies dependable
   baseline energy; ash supplies control, high-density response, and compactness.
   Both fuels remain finite. Waste and failure still matter.
6. **Distributed power:** licensed Glory-impressed patterns allow factory-built
   small reactors and ash cells to run homes, buildings, farms, hospitals, and
   workshops without a Glory master standing beside each one. Local technicians
   maintain them; public ledgers track fuel and failures.
7. **Displacement:** within roughly a century of Meridian, ash induction and compact
   nuclear-ash systems displaced fossil fuels and large solar fields as primary
   energy. Legacy wind, water, solar, combustion, and mechanical systems remain as
   local backups rather than vanishing from knowledge.
8. **Flight and advanced equipment:** ash-electric lift craft, personal lift rigs for
   suitably trained Kindled, powered armor/tools, precision medicine, climate control,
   high-speed fabrication, and hardened communications become ordinary enough to
   reshape cities. Personal flight remains skill- and fuel-limited, not universal.
9. **Far-future society:** four more centuries of refinement make 2680 materially
   beyond most pre-Fall dreams. Its abundance is real but uneven. Grid access, clean
   ash, reactor licensing, professional protection, and Glory-pattern ownership are
   central political and class questions.

Tamar's role is remembered unevenly. Some accounts make him an inventor; others call
him a fraud who happened to ask the right question. Canonically he is mortal, human,
original to this world, and catalytic rather than omniscient. The science belongs to
the generations who test, fail, record, and build after him.

## Ash-electric civilization and Glory

- Refined ash is the dominant finite energy source. **Ash induction** converts a
  controlled ash reaction into ordinary current for lights, pumps, heat,
  communications, medicine, workshops, transit, arenas, and rift sensing.
- Ash does not create free energy. Cells are graded, metered, consumed, reconciled,
  and vulnerable to contamination or runaway.
- **Ashcraft** blends magic and ash: a trained soul supplies pattern, intention,
  and control; ownerless ash supplies most of the fuel. Different traditions shape,
  contain, conduct, or sustain the reaction differently.
- Modern cells continue running after a Kindled operator starts and tunes them, but
  still require maintenance, replacement ash, and emergency shutdown.
- At **Glory**, a rare master can impress stable patterns at civic or industrial
  scale. Glory does not make ash infinite; it expands safe throughput, duration,
  precision, and the complexity of blended systems.
- That capability produces ash-power industrial dynasties as well as school orders
  whose masters pursue near-monastic devotion to one tradition. Some orders reject
  corporate ownership; some industry houses sponsor them; some Glory masters move
  between both worlds.
- Pro rift companies feed the ash grid. This creates prestige and public service,
  but also incentives to overhunt, hide risk, manipulate ash grades, or protect
  wealthy districts first.
- A sanctioned kill produces a recorded yield. Civic power, casualty reserve, school,
  and team shares are removed in that order. Student shares fund training cells,
  metered combat techniques, equipment, travel, and medical reserve; they are not
  unmetered personal treasure.
- Ash is recorded by **yield** (Handful, Flask, Barrel, Well), **grade** (purity and
  stability), and **resonance** (which patterns can use that spawn expression safely).
  A large dirty yield may be worth less than a small stable one.
- Killing does not grant rank automatically. Real combat can deepen capacity because
  the practitioner works at the edge of owned control and must later reproduce the
  lesson. Ash funds the next threshold; training makes the threshold survivable.
- Filter thinning increases both human potential and Riftspawn pressure. The opening
  book presents this first as unusually capable students and an unusually varied rift
  season. Later books prove the common Glory-pressure cause.

in the first two fingers of her left hand. Healing closes the injury but does not
restore exact touch. The cost is planted before it occurs and changes how she grips,
trains, and asks for help. Rank cannot erase it.

## 4. World facts established on the page

- **Two team structures.** A student's roster place (school and division) is for meets and
  Measures. The **field license** is taken in the mixed trial teams, which keep their
  numbers and hold the yield book. First step ("the Handful license"): contained floors at
  the Kennel until the warden passes a team as fit, then one field Handful at a **held
  tear** under a chartered officer, called in trial-score order. The Home Line holds a
  small tear open inside a four-person ward square for a day or so, and each team takes
  one husk as the tear gives it (about every forty minutes on the 28th). Three held tears
  licensed the cohort this spring: Ferren culverts, 14th–15th (teams 1–12); river flats,
  22nd (13–26); Sag Line, 28th (call positions 27–40: fourteen teams, Team Nineteen's repeat at 39th
  standing in the place of a team the warden had not passed fit; Team Eleven's reseat was
  to follow them and did not get a floor). Team numbers and call positions are unrelated:
  Nineteen stood 18th, Eleven 9th, Seven 40th. First Division fighters need the first step to enter live lanes.
- **"Live-cell events"** in Ysra's condition, as her clerk reads it: any charged cell, a
  held rift, and the Kennel lattice ("a rift in a cage"). Stated once, by Kellan, in
  chapter 9.
- **Draw**, defined on the page by Noa: what a fighter burned on a piece of work, set
  against what the book says that work ought to cost. One is standard; lower is thriftier.
  It is not raw drams.
- **The Kennel book** hangs by a chain and hook at the cast-room desk and is unhooked to
  be carried down to the floor.
- **The husk.** Nothing inside; a pattern pressed into loose ash with nobody home, tied at
  **the knot** (heart height on a dog). A lit Edge through the knot is the clean kill; a
  hard dark blow can shake the shape out of a Handful. Limbs are "lent." It reads rather
  than sees, turns to the brightest worked power within about twelve paces, goes straight,
  does not feint, and does not remember. With nothing lit, it stops and then seeks the
  nearest pattern (the Kennel lattice; a hold wall).
- **The four roles:** Kill, Hold, Carry, Catch, declared aloud. A team that does three has
  failed its response. **Abort:** any member, any time, *off* three times; everyone goes
  dark and walks to the line; called early it costs nothing.
- **The count.** From the cut, fresh ash is **open** for about sixty counts and will take
  the first pattern that touches it. Inside the count and inside three paces nothing is
  lit: **dark hands**. Inside the count the catcher owns the floor and may call *dark*.
  A lit door near open ash **prints** it (a hair-bright line in the color of that person's
  light; the tin hums and is warm after a night); printed ash is salted. **Loose ash finds
  the nearest lamp:** open ash leans and drifts toward the nearest worked pattern, and in
  wind it travels; it fouled the allotment pump cell on the 28th. Ash tinned inside the
  count settles clean; ash still lying open at sixty settles to what it lies on and is
  **sweepings**, Grade Third at best, not counted toward the mark. All tins spend a night
  in a cold room before grading. This also explains the Cellhouse yield line *T7, tin 4:
  salted, unread*.
- **Kit:** waxed cloth two paces square with a lead weight in each corner; two steel pans
  and a brush; squat tins of six drams with waxed-cord seals and paper tags; a box of
  **wet** quench salt.
- **Measures.** A Handful is **24 drams** by the book. **Pass:** 20 tinned at Grade Second
  or better, nothing printed, nothing fouled, nobody hurt. **Yield** by scale. **Grade** by
  how long a pinch holds a test tune: First past 100 counts, Second past 60, Third below,
  road-lamp ash under 30. **Resonance** on "the comb," nine reeds cut to families of
  pattern (edge, stride, ward, healer's read, stance, and four unnamed); Handful ash has
  always read **plain**. Resonance says which work the ash will take to; it is not a
