## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual

| Metric | Target |

| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

# Chapter 32 — Kiva: Held of the Line

Healer Aske turned her hand over on the towel on Tuesday and did not reach for his pin.

"I'm told that it went away," he said. "For forty-one counts in a yard on the twentieth, two fingers. For the better part of an hour on a wharf on Sunday morning, the whole of it, to the wrist. Your sponsor wrote me the first. A Graywater catcher wrote me the second, in pencil, on the back of a tin tag. I've never had a letter from a catcher before."

Kiva looked at the hand on the towel. An hour was a better number than the true one. It was in somebody else's pencil, written by a man who had seen the wharf and nothing after it, and she had only to sit still.

"It was longer," she said. "He only saw the wharf. It went at the sixth rung, a little after three, and it was supper before it came back. Fifteen hours, about."

Aske did not move for a moment.

"Fifteen," said Aske. He looked at her over the hand. "And you were in two minds whether to say so. Don't trouble to answer; I've a face in front of me." He turned the wrist toward the window. "Well. Now I've the right number, which is the only kind I can do anything with."

"Is it burned?"

"No. I've looked for that twice now and I'd tell you." The cold thumbs went along the two fingers and stopped at the knuckle. "It's a nerve that's being asked a question it can't answer quickly. Every time you do whatever it is that you do on that mast, something goes through here at a rate it was never built to carry. It doesn't scorch. It starves, and then it sulks, and each time it sulks a little longer. The wharf wasn't the mast. That was four bag necks hauled across the crook of an elbow, over a nerve that had been emptied on the Wednesday and hadn't filled. The mast leaves it hungry. The cord found it hungry." He let go. "I can ease it. I can't tell you how many more times it will come back to where it was. I'd be guessing, and I don't."

"Somebody's didn't come back. Two hundred years ago. A whole hand."

"So your sponsor tells me. She sent me the line." He wrote on her card. "No go until Monday. After that, one in a week, and I see the hand the next morning, every time, before you're let have another. That's not a ban. It's a price, and I'm setting it where you can read it."

Orla Dane had sat on a great many benches in her life, and she thought that this was the hardest.

It stood in the passage of the Home Line hall, against a whitewashed wall, opposite a door. It was a plain door. There was no plate on it. She had walked past it perhaps four hundred times in twenty years as a field officer, with mud to the knee and a slate at her belt, and had not once been asked through.

*Your sponsor as far as the door.* Hollis had been as good as his word. He had come out for the girl himself at four, with his hat off, and had held the door for her, and had looked at Orla over her head with an expression that said a number of things and apologized for none of them. Then it had shut.

That was a quarter of an hour ago.

She did not mind. She had examined that carefully, as she would have examined a candidate's stance, pushing at it from several sides. She had expected to mind a good deal. She had saved a favor for twenty-five years to get this near that room, and had spent it, and here she sat with her stick between her boots like a parent outside a school.

It was only that the wrong person had been asking, all that time.

She had wanted to read the book because Ilse was in it. That was the whole of her reason, and it had never been enough to open a door, and she saw now that it ought not to have been. The girl had a better one. She was in it herself.

A boy went by with a bucket. A bell rang somewhere for the change of the levee watch.

There had been a visitor to the Old Yard, a week and a half ago, on a night when there was no go.

Mara Rowan had come down the lane at half past eight in her work apron, with her sleeves still rolled. She had not sat. She had stood beside the spare crate and said two sentences, in the voice she would have used to a meter.

*You kept a thing from my table. Don't do it twice.*

*I shan't,* Orla had said.

*Good. Then that's all. I've a re-sit in the ninth month and no room in me for a second grievance.* And she had looked at the cross, and at the brass head on its three legs, and at the crooked post, for about as long as it takes to read a drum. *She came down on that mark on Friday. She told me at the table. She was a good deal more pleased about the one that she called off.*

Then she had gone home. Orla had sat on for an hour afterward and found that she had been let off nothing and forgiven nothing, and that she could work with it.

There was a paper in her coat now, too. It had come from the Chair that morning, in answer to Monday's book. *Noted: one stack, a third of a second, landed, called a miss by the candidate's reader. I shall want the tape, the reader, and the sponsor with the rest. Y.C.* It was not a reprimand. Ysra Crest did not write reprimands. She wrote appointments.

She knew what would be asked in that room. A boy with a strip of paper would be made to say, in front of a Placement Chair, whether a Torch could tell a crossing from a stack with a stick, and he would have to say no.

She would have to say it too. She found that she did not mind that either.

*Two,* she thought. *Four to show. And the hand goes away a little longer each time.*

She had read E.K.'s line until she had it by heart. *Nor to anyone I had not watched miss.* She had watched this one miss sixteen times. She had watched her call *off* in the air with a door ready under her. It was not the landings that Orla would put her name to, when the day came. It was that.

The door across the passage stayed shut.

Orla Dane settled her stick, and her bad leg, and waited, as she had learned to wait on a tear watch, without leaning on the time.

The room was smaller than Kiva had imagined, and there was nothing in it but a table and a window.

The window looked out over the Ferren levee. The table was oak, and old, and on it lay a flat box of black wood with a brass hasp. Hollis Vane lifted the lid and turned it back, so that she could see the inside.

A sheet of paper had been pasted there, and renewed, and pasted over. It was a list. It was not long. *Officer of the watch, Sag Line. Officer of the watch, Ferren. Officer of the watch, river flats.* There were seven such lines, each an office and never a name. Under the seven, a little apart, in the same faded print:

*And the bearer.*

"You've been on that since you were thirteen," said Vane. "Nobody told you. The appointment was sealed, and the one person whose business it was to tell you had been dead three years when the seal was cut." He did not excuse it. "Your sponsor's known of this book for thirty years and couldn't be shown it. You could have walked in any morning and asked. I'd like to say we're a careful service. We're a service that copies what's in front of it."

He lifted the book out and laid it on the oak.

It was bound in canvas, like a field book, and the corners were soft. He opened it at the first leaf and stood back.

It was in print, the old uneven print of the fourth *Founders' History,* and it was short.

*One plate, dark, of nine rings, on a cord. Held of the Line. Released to a bearer.*

*The bearer is named by the bearer before, and by nobody else. The bearer writes why. The watch witnesses, and does not choose.*

*It is not a rank. It is not a reward. We do not know what it is for.*

*Write down what it does.*

And below, in a smaller letter:

*How it came to the Line is in the first book. The first book is not here. This is copied from the second, which did not say where the first had gone.*

Kiva read it twice. Then she put up her right hand, without hurry, and drew the cord out of her collar, and laid the disk on the table beside the page.

It was the first time that she had ever taken it out in front of a stranger on purpose. It lay on the oak, dark and smooth, the width of her palm. There was still a thread of grey Kennel ash in three of the grooves.

Vane looked at it. He did not touch it, and he did not ask to.

"That's it," he said quietly. "I've stood four watches in this room and I'd never seen it. Corran kept it in her shirt." He cleared his throat. "Turn over. It's ruled like one of ours."

It was. There were five columns, and she knew the shape of them before she read the headings, as she would have known the shape of her own kitchen in the dark.

*Bearer. Received, and from whom. Why. What it does. Released, and to whom.*

There were a great many names.

She did not try to keep them. She let her eye go down the leaves as Vane turned them. The hands changed every thirty years or so; the ink went from brown to black. Some of the reasons were a line long and some a paragraph. One said only, *She asked.* One, in a hand like a row of bent pins, she could not read at all.

"Nor can I," said Vane. "It's the same script as the other. I have to ask seven people's leave before I may trace a line out of this book. I've asked."

It was the fourth column that she could not stop looking at.

*Nothing.*

*Nothing.*

*Cold.*

*Nothing, in thirty-four years.*

It went down the page like a drip. Leaf after leaf, bearer after bearer, each of them had been told on the first page to write down what it did, and each had carried it for half a lifetime and come back with the same word. Once, halfway through, a narrow upright hand had written, *Warm? In a fever. It may have been me.* And beside it, in the margin, in different ink: *Not entered as fact. Watch.*

"Five hundred years," said Kiva.

"Of people who were asked a question and were honest enough to write that they'd no answer." Vane's voice was dry. "I thought when I first read it that it was the dullest book in the hall. I've come round."

He turned one more leaf, and took his hand away.

*Corran, I. Received 2643, from M. Gill, at his death.*

Under *Why,* in a square old-fashioned hand that must have been Gill's: *She went into a culvert after a child and came out counting who was still in it.*

Under *What it does,* there were three entries, years apart, in a big loose scrawl that Kiva had seen on birthday cards. *Nothing.* And then: *Nothing. I talk to it. It's a poor listener.* And last, in ink that was hardly faded:

*Nothing. I've stopped asking it. I.C.*

Kiva's eyes stung. She had thought that she had found that out for herself, in the Old Yard, beside the crate, with the thing cold in her palm.

*Released: to K. Rowan, by sealed appointment, 2669. To be delivered on her Kindling day and not before. Witnessed by the watch, which did not choose.*

And under *Why.*

*Ione Rowan's girl. I sat an hour with her on a stair after the Tallow fire. She is six. She asked me one thing the whole time, which was whether anybody had written down what her mother did. I said that the Works would. She said, "Will they put why?"*

*I have looked for a better reason than that for a month and have not found one. I do not know that it wants one. I.C.*

Kiva read it standing, with her hands flat on an oak table.

She did not remember saying it. She remembered the stair. She remembered a tall woman's coat, and that it had smelled of rain, and that nobody had made her talk. She had been six, and her mother had pulled a quench by hand, and she had wanted to know whether it was going to be in a book.

It was not a prophecy. There was nothing in it about doors. A woman who had carried a cold thing for twenty-six years had needed to give it to somebody, and had chosen a child who asked the only question that Ilse Corran had ever thought worth asking. She had written down that she had no better reason, and had signed it.

There was one line more, beneath, in a clerk's hand.

*Guardian (M. Rowan, aunt) was asked, and said no. Appointment sealed regardless; it is the bearer's right. The Line owes that household an answer and has not got one. Watch.*

"She said no," said Kiva.

"She did. And we went round her, by our own rule, and wrote down that we had." Vane's face was set. "That's eleven years old and nobody's been to Coper Street. I'll come myself. Not to defend it. To say it's there."

"She'll give you tea and not forgive you."

"I'd expect both."

The last line on the leaf was her own.

*Rowan, K. Received 2676, by courier, sealed, from I. Corran (d. 2673).*

There was nothing after it. The *why* was above. The fourth column was empty, a clean white space the width of three fingers, under five hundred years of one word.

"I'm obliged to tell you two things before you pick up that pen," said Vane. "The Reader at the Library has a way of doing it; I've borrowed her manner." He turned back to the first leaf and put his finger at the foot. There was a line there that she had not read, added in a later ink. "*The watch may recall the plate from any bearer, on a vote of the seven, for cause written.* It's been done once. A man sold it, in 2590, for forty drams. They had it back in a week. That's what *held of the Line* means. It's yours to carry. It was never yours to keep from us, or ours to keep from you."

"And the second?"

"Anything written in that fourth column that isn't *nothing,* I read aloud to the watch at its next sitting. That's three days off." He met her eye. "You've enough coming at you. I wouldn't add to it without saying so. You may leave that space white and walk out, and I'll enter that the bearer read her line. Nobody could fault it."

Kiva looked at the white space.

She thought of a page at a kitchen table, with one word crossed out. She thought of a request book that was open because that was what it was for. She thought of a clerk called R., who had been told to leave something out of a column, and had put it at the side in a hand that his Warden could not read, and of how long it had taken anybody to come down three flights of stairs and ask.

She thought of Mrs. Cobb on the wharf, pressing hard with a stub of pencil. *Now it's somebody's.*

"Is there a rule about how much I may put?"

"Only what it did."

She took the pen.

Her left hand held the page flat, the last two fingers doing the work. She wrote slowly, in the square pencil hand turned to ink, and she did not let herself hurry the dull parts.

*Warm, once. Wednesday the 29th of last month, at the Pit, a little before half past ten in the forenoon. First felt after I had shut the still door, at the end of a crossing, Ember to still. I do not know when it began. It lasted from the south shelf to the top of the road stair. Cold by the keeper's gate.*

*Cold since. Cold through two crossings more, on a sanctioned mast, the 15th and the 20th.*

*I did not ask it anything. It did not do anything. K.R.*

She blotted it. It looked very long, under all that *nothing.* It looked like somebody making a great deal of a small matter.

"You've used his word," said Vane. He had read it upside down, as a watch officer reads everything. "*Crossing.*"

"It was there first. I only found it."

"Mm." He took the pen from her, and under her initials he wrote, small: *Entered in the bearer's hand, in the presence of the watch officer, Sag Line, who did not prompt. H.V.* "There. Now it's a fact, and seven people hear it at the sitting, and I can't tell you what they'll do with it. I can tell you I'll say I gave a field direction to that hand and would again."

"May I copy my own line?"

"The bearer may copy her own, and the line that gave it to her, and show them where she likes. Not another's."

She copied them into the field book. She copied Ilse's reason last, and when she came to the six-year-old's question she put it in the right-hand column, by itself, because there was nowhere else in any book of hers that it could go.

Then she picked the disk up off the table. It was cold. She put the cord over her head, and tucked it in, and it lay against her breastbone where it had lain for four years.

It weighed what it had always weighed. She had rather expected that it would feel different, now that she knew whose it was.

---

Orla got up from the bench when the door opened. It took her a moment, and she did not hide that it did.

Kiva did not say anything. She held out the field book, open, turned the right way round.

Orla read it standing in the whitewashed passage, with her stick hooked over her arm. She read Kiva's own line first, and the long entry under it, and her eyebrows went up a little at one word. Then she came to the square beneath.

*She said, "Will they put why?"*

The crooked face did something that Kiva had never seen it do. It did not last. Orla shut her mouth hard, and looked for a while at the wall.

"That's Ilse," she said. Her voice was rough. "That's her to the life. She never could find a grand reason for anything. She'd sooner have a true small one and be laughed at." She gave the book back. "I'd wondered for four years what was in that seal. I'd made up a dozen things. Not one of them was as plain as that, or as hard to argue with."

"It says they don't know what it's for. On the first page. In print."

"I'd guessed that much."

"And that it's held of the Line. They can call it back, on a vote."

"Can they." Orla took her stick in her hand. "Then there's one more office with an opinion. A watch on Sunday, and the same day a man in a black coat with a glass on your tape. The Chair on Tuesday, with a house and a street and a shelf all wanting one bag. And an Order with a letter." She began to walk, slowly, toward the daylight at the end of the passage. "You wrote in it."

"He told me what it would cost first."

"I don't doubt it. He's become very scrupulous since somebody read him a ledger." She glanced sideways. "You've your answer ready as to why you did it, I suppose."

Kiva opened her mouth.

She had. It was about open books, and a clerk called R., and it was rather good. She shut her mouth, and walked four paces, and found something behind it that was smaller.

"I didn't want to be the next *nothing,*" she said. "Not when it wasn't true."

"Hm," said Orla. "That'll do."

They came out onto the levee. It was a grey, mild evening, with the water low in the Ferren and the first lamps showing in the streets beyond. Somewhere over there was a pump house that had never dropped a stroke, and a street with a cracked lintel in it, and a yard with a brass head on three legs that had not been asked to tick since the twentieth.

"What was she like?" said Kiva.

It was a plain question. She had never put it to anybody. She had a coat that smelled of rain, and a laugh in a kitchen, and a line in a book.

Orla did not answer at once. She stood with both hands on her stick, looking down the levee to the place where it bent.

"Loud," she said. "She was the loudest officer on the Line, and she never raised her voice at a tear in her life. She laughed in other people's kitchens. She fell asleep on watch once, standing up, and kept her count." A pause. "She took me down my first culvert. I've told people for twenty-five years that I took Hollis down his. I never said who took me."

"You never told me that."

"You never asked. You've only lately begun asking things you've not already answered." It was said without any edge at all. "Come along. There's a tram at the half hour, and your aunt will want that page on her table tonight, and I'd give a good deal to see her face at the line about the tea."

They went down the levee steps together, the one on a stick and the other on a strapped knee, at about the same pace.

Kiva had the book under her arm. In it were four old lamps and a clerk's margin; a dead woman's name for a thing that she had not understood either; a tag copied into a tenement's ledger; and a child's question, in ink, with a witness who had not chosen. On Sunday a watch would hear what the disk had done, and a man in a black coat would put a glass on a cliff. On Tuesday there would be a room.

She had two, and four to show, and ash for four.

It was not hers. It had been put into her hands by a tired woman who could find no better reason, and written down, and she found, going down the steps, that she would sooner carry that than any of the things that she had made up.

  houses and tenement cell boxes are not stepped**; each cell keeps its own time. Named before
  the patent *counting ash* (Wren House cards 40 to 44, superseded). The claim: *The Proprietor
  claims the Step, and all ash exhibiting the Step, however produced.* The caution: *Not to be
  introduced to an unstepped bank under load.*

  Pit). **The last two knots of the Flask, the pair that carry the legs, read I, full:** the
