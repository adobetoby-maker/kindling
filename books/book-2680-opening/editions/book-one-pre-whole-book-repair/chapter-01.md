# Chapter 1 — Kiva: There Isn't a Column

*Four years later — 2680*

The draw meter on the wall of the Measure Hall had a sticky needle.

Kiva Rowan had noticed it twenty minutes ago, while she stood in the queue with three hundred other seventeen-year-olds and tried not to think about her chest. Every time a candidate at the first bench finished a reading, the needle dropped back toward zero, caught at the two, and hung there for half a breath before it fell the rest of the way. Nobody else seemed to see it. The assessors wrote down the numbers, the projection boards lit up, and the crowd in the gallery murmured or clapped, depending.

"You're doing it again," said Anwen.

"Doing what?"

"Staring at a meter like it owes you money."

"It's sticking at the two, so either the pivot's dirty or the hairspring's tired."

"Kiva."

"Mara would have it open in a minute."

"Kiva." Anwen Pryce turned her head and gave her the flat, level look she had, like a carpenter checking a shelf. "That is the least important thing in this room."

It was not, actually. A sticky needle meant every reading at the first bench was a little high on the way down, and although probably nobody's rank would change because of it, probably was not the same as nobody. But Kiva did not say that, because Anwen was right in the way that mattered. She was staring at the meter because the meter was easier to look at than the boards.

There were four projection boards, one over each rig, and each was a tall sheet of pale glass that lit up with columns when a candidate stood in the frame. Kiva had been watching them all morning, and they were simple once you knew how to read them, because every board showed the same four columns for the same four axes.

*Rank* was how much of you was lit, and it ran up the old ladder from Kindled through Ember, Flame, Fire and Torch to the two words people said quietly, which were Blaze and Glory.

*Hold* was how well you could control what you had, and it came as a number out of ten.

*Draw* was how much ash you burned to do a standard piece of work, measured against a standard, so that one meant average and lower was better. A girl from the Weighbridge had pulled a seven-tenths an hour ago, and the gallery had stood up.

*Call* was the column that always stayed dark on Classification Day. Call was judgment, and you could not measure judgment in a person standing alone in a cage of wires. You measured it in teams, in trials, under pressure.

Under the four columns, in letters a hand high, the board showed the thing everybody was really waiting for.

*First door.*

Ember. Hand. Still.

"Next," called the clerk at the far rig, and the queue shuffled forward three paces.

Kiva shuffled with it. Her left forearm itched under the leather cuff where her Ember slab sat, still the dull copper it had turned four years ago, in her hands, in the Old Yard. It had never been any other color. Under her shirt, against her breastbone, the disk lay cold.

And under the disk, from the middle of her chest to her left shoulder, the three heat-lines lay quiet. For now.

They were faint these days, most of the time, three fine pink streaks that were neither straight nor even, like scratches from a cat that had been thinking about something else. You could only see them if you were close, and you could only feel them if the doors began to argue.

They would argue today. They always did in the frame.

"What's your number?" said Anwen.

"Two-forty-one."

"I'm two-thirty-eight, so I go before you." Anwen took a folded paper from her coat pocket and opened it, and Kiva saw that it was a list, because Anwen always had a list. "Afterward I'm going straight to the Wren House desk to file for the field track. Then lunch. Then I'm going to find out which trial slot I drew. Then I'm going to the Graywater table to ask them how many field placements they're taking this year, because last year they said four and took three."

"You're going to ask a rift company to their faces why they lied?"

"I'm going to ask them to count." Anwen folded the list. "It's different."

---

The candidates ahead of them were mostly from the Compact's own preparatory lanes, which meant they had trained for this since they were thirteen, and you could tell. They stood in the frames with their shoulders loose and their faces calm, like people who knew what the rigs wanted.

A tall boy with close-cut black hair stepped up to the near bench, and the gallery went quiet.

"That's Kellan Renn," said someone behind Kiva.

"I know who it is."

Kiva knew who it was too. Everybody in the Crooked Line School's junior lanes knew Kellan Renn, who had been the best Ember-door candidate in the Eastbank wards for three years running and had never placed lower than first in a junior Measure. People said Graywater Company had sent someone to watch him spar when he was fifteen.

He did not look like a person who knew any of that. He looked like a person waiting for a bus.

The first rig was the draw bench, where a small sealed lamp-cell sat on a steel plate, wired to the sticky meter on the wall. Every candidate did the same thing. You put your Ember, or your hands, or your stance on the plate, depending on your door, and you held a standard pattern on the cell for sixty seconds while the cell lit and the meter measured how much ash it burned.

Kellan laid his left forearm on the plate. His Ember sat on a steel cuff, a dark slab gone a deep blue-grey, and he did not close his eyes to use it. He just watched the lamp.

It lit steadily, with a calm white glow that had no flicker in it at all, and the needle on the wall climbed, found a place, and sat there without twitching.

At sixty seconds, the clerk called time. Kellan lifted his arm, and the needle fell, caught at the two for half a breath, and dropped to zero.

*Draw: 0.82.*

The gallery clapped. Kellan nodded once, as if somebody had told him the time, and walked to the hold posts.

The hold posts were three steel uprights with clamps and gauges, and each tested one door. For an Ember-door candidate, the test was Edge: you formed your weapon shape and held it inside a ring of sensing wire while a clerk counted, and then, if you could, you lit the edge-light and held that too.

Kellan raised his right hand. The metal came off his cuff in a smooth grey flow, ran over his fist, and closed into a blade that was long and narrow and single-edged, with a very slight curve and no ornament at all. It looked like the shape a person would choose if they had thought about blades for years and decided to stop being interested in anything else.

"Light," said the clerk.

A line of light came on along the edge, thin and blue-white and perfectly steady. It did not shiver, and it did not gutter at the tip the way most people's did.

The clerk counted to thirty. Then forty. Then sixty, which was the end of the test.

*Hold: 9.1.*

Then the frame. Kellan stepped inside the tall cage of wires, and the projection board over it rose into four columns, and Rank climbed and climbed and stopped, steady, three rungs up.

*Rank: Fire.*

*First door: Ember.*

The applause this time was real, and Kiva found she was clapping too.

Fire at seventeen. Most people did not reach Fire until their twenties, if they reached it at all, because Fire was real output, the kind of fighter companies sent to an open rift. And he had done it with a Hold over nine and a Draw near eight-tenths, which meant he was not just bright. He was careful, and he burned less ash than almost anybody in the hall.

He came out of the frame and walked straight toward the queue, because the exit was past it. As he went by, he glanced at Kiva, and then he stopped.

"You're the three-door," he said.

"I'm Kiva."

"I know." He said it plainly, not unkindly. "Everyone knows. You've been read four years running."

"This is the fourth."

"Anything changed?"

"I don't know yet."

Kellan studied her a moment longer. He had very dark, very steady eyes, and he did not seem to be in any hurry to stop using them.

"I hope they find a category for you," he said. "Honestly. Then we'll know what we're comparing."

He walked on, and Kiva watched him go.

"Was that rude?" said Anwen.

"I don't think so." Kiva thought about it. "I think he meant it."

"That's worse."

"I know."

---

Anwen's reading took six minutes. She was hand-door and always had been. On the draw bench she held her palms an inch above the lamp-cell and lit it with a slow, warm, amber light that wavered a little at the start and then steadied. *Draw: 0.95.* At the hold posts she raised a ward the size of a dinner plate between her palms and kept it there, faintly shimmering, while a clerk tapped it with a padded rod. *Hold: 7.4.* In the frame, her rank column stopped two rungs up.

*Rank: Flame.*

*First door: Hand.*

Anwen walked out of the frame, wrote something on her list, and went to wait by the wall without glancing at the gallery once.

"Two-forty-one," called the clerk.

Kiva went to the draw bench.

She could feel the gallery shift as she came up. It was not loud. It was like the change in a room when a cell starts to hum the wrong note, and everybody who knows cells looks up at once. She heard her name, and she heard *three-door* and *Rowan* and *that's the one from the Old Yard*. She heard someone say *the disk*, and she kept her face very still.

The clerk at the bench was a young man with spectacles, who checked her number against his ledger and frowned.

"Which door do you want to read on?"

"Ember," said Kiva. "The standard is Ember first, then hand, then still. That's what they did last year."

"Right." He did not look happy. "Ember first."

She laid her left forearm on the plate.

The copper slab touched the steel, and the lamp-cell under it woke. She felt it the way she always felt cells, as a faint warm hum in her wrist bones, like a cat purring through a wall. She knew this kind of cell, if not this exact one, because Mara serviced a hundred of them a year for the municipal lamps. It had a three-ring induction coil and a standard Grade First charge, and somebody had tuned it a little cold.

She held the pattern.

The lamp lit a dull orange, which was the color of her Ember's light and always had been. It flickered, and she steadied it, and it held.

Then her hand door woke up to see what she was doing.

It did not ask, because it never asked. It was like a dog that heard the cupboard open in another room. She felt it lift inside her, cool and close, reaching for the cell through her palm because the cell was right there and the hand door liked cells, and the instant it reached, the Ember door pushed back.

The heat-lines on her chest lit up.

Nobody else could see them under her shirt, but she felt all three at once, thin and hot, like wires drawn tight from her breastbone toward her shoulder. The lamp flared yellow and then orange. Then it stuttered, and the needle on the wall jumped and swung.

She shut the hand door. It went, sulkily, and the lamp settled.

At sixty seconds the clerk called time.

*Draw: 3.1.*

Somebody in the gallery laughed, more surprised than cruel. Three point one meant she had burned more than three times the ash a standard candidate would, for the same minute of light.

"It's the needle," Kiva said. "It sticks at the two. It reads high on the drop."

The clerk squinted at the meter and then at her. "It's not two. It's three."

"I know. I'm just saying it sticks."

He wrote down 3.1.

---

The hold posts were worse, and better, and then worse again.

At the Ember post, she raised her right fist and asked for Edge. The metal came off her cuff and ran over her knuckles, and formed the shape it had formed every single time for four years.

It was not a blade, exactly. It was a short, flat, square-ended bar about the length of her forearm, with an edge on one side and a blunt back on the other, like a cleaver someone had run over with a cart. It had no point and no curve, and it was ugly in a way that made people politely look somewhere else.

She had tried for four years to make it into anything else: a knife, a short sword, and once a spear-point, which had lasted half a second and gone out. Every time she asked for a different shape, the metal hesitated, guttered, and fell back into the bar.

"Light," said the clerk.

The edge-light came on along the bar's one sharp side, dull orange and shivering at the corner. She held it. It guttered, caught, held.

*Nine,* counted the clerk. *Ten.* At eleven it went out.

*Hold (Edge): 3.0.*

At the hand post, she made a ward. It came up between her palms the size of a tea tray, faint and silvery, and when the clerk tapped it with his rod, it rang like a pan and held for six taps before it popped. *Hold (Ward): 2.6.*

At the still post, there was a steel arm on a spring, which pressed against your chest with a slowly increasing weight while you stood on a plate that measured how far you slid. Most hand-door and Ember-door candidates slid at once. Still-door candidates sank their weight and held.

Kiva stood on the plate, and the arm came forward and touched her breastbone, right over the disk.

She did the thing she had done in the Old Yard. She stopped holding the rail.

Her feet went heavy, and her knees went soft and sure, and the push went down through her into the plate as if the floor had agreed to take it for her. The arm pressed harder. The gauge climbed. She did not slide.

It was the one thing she could always do, and she did not know why. She had never trained still-door properly, not in a school. She had trained it in Mara's shop, carrying crates of cells up the cellar stairs, and on the trams, standing without holding the rail because Mara said it was good for her balance.

*Hold (Stance): 6.2.*

Six point two was respectable. Six point two was a still-door candidate on a decent day.

The gallery murmured, and she heard someone say *that's not bad* in a tone of surprise that she decided not to mind.

Then the frame.

She stepped into the tall cage of wires, where the air always felt faintly thick, like the air before a storm. The clerk at the instrument box threw his switches, and above her the projection board woke.

Four columns.

Rank rose. It rose in the Ember band, one rung, and paused. Then the hand band beside it lit, and rose, and the Ember column dropped as if somebody had kicked it, and then the still band surged up past both of them.

And the heat-lines caught fire.

That was what it felt like, three hot wires inside her, drawn from her breastbone toward her left shoulder and pulled tight, with three doors open at once and each one pulling on the same blanket from a different side. Her breath went short. Her hands curled at her sides.

On the board, the columns would not hold. They traded places, and climbed and fell, and the Rank total at the bottom flickered between *Ember* and *Flame* and a row of dashes. Under *First door*, the letters stuttered through the first half of each word and never finished one.

"It's the rig," said the clerk.

"It's not the rig," said a woman's voice.

Kiva knew the voice, because everybody in the Compact knew it. She looked up through the wires and saw Ysra Crest walking across the floor from the assessors' table.

---

Ysra Crest was the Placement Chair of the Meridian Collegiate Circuit, which meant that every roster in every division of every school in the Compact passed across her desk before it was posted. She was a tall woman of about sixty, with iron-grey hair cut very short and a long, plain face, and she wore Compact grey with no pins. She was Torch-rank, hand-door, a Wren House ward-holder before she had gone into administration. People said she had once held a ward across a broken water main for forty minutes while a crew got three children out from under it, and had walked into the hospital afterward on her own feet.

She did not spare the gallery a glance. Her eyes were on the board.

"Shut it down," she said.

The clerk threw the switch, and the board went dark. Inside the frame the thick air thinned, and the three hot wires in Kiva's chest loosened, slowly, and lay down.

"Step out, please, Miss Rowan."

Kiva stepped out on legs that were a little unsteady. She put her hand flat on her chest over her shirt and pressed, the way Mara had taught her, until the burning faded to an ache.

Ysra Crest picked up the clerk's ledger and read the morning's numbers. Draw 3.1. Hold 3.0, 2.6, 6.2. Rank: unsettled. First door: none recorded.

"Same as last year," she said.

"The still number's up," said Kiva. "It was five-eight last year."

Ysra regarded her over the ledger. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of a person checking a weight against a scale.

"So it is," she said. "Four tenths. Well done." She closed the ledger. "And your draw is worse, up from two-nine. And your Edge hold is the same. And when the frame wakes all three doors at once, the board cannot tell me what you are." She handed the ledger back to the clerk. "Classification deferred. Fourth year. Write it, please."

The clerk wrote.

"Come with me, Miss Rowan," said Ysra Crest. "There's someone who wants to argue with me, and I'd rather she did it with you in the room."

---

The someone was Orla Dane.

She was waiting in a small office off the main hall, sitting on the edge of a desk that was not hers, with her bad left leg stretched out straight in front of her and her stick leaning against the wall. She was a compact, weathered woman in her fifties, with a crooked nose and grey-blond hair tied back in a knot, and she taught mixed-path method at the Crooked Line School. She had been a Home Line field officer for twenty years before her knee went. And she had been teaching Kiva in the evenings, quietly, three nights a week, for four years.

She stood up when they came in. Kiva saw her take in the way Kiva was holding her chest, and saw her mouth tighten.

"The frame again," said Orla.

"The frame again," said Ysra, and she sat behind the desk. "Sit down, both of you."

Kiva sat. Orla stayed standing.

"You know what I'm going to say," said Orla.

"I know what you're going to ask." Ysra folded her hands. "And you know what I'm going to answer. So let's have it on the record for Miss Rowan's sake, because I don't think anyone has ever put it to her in order." She turned to Kiva. "Do you know why you can't be placed?"

"Because I don't have a first door."

"That's what the board says. It isn't the reason." Ysra leaned back. "The Circuit is not a game, Miss Rowan, though it looks like one from the gallery, with the flags and the standings and the people cheering. Every roster I post decides what a young person is allowed to do with live ash, near other people, under pressure, in public. A first door tells me which rules to put around you. It tells me which events you may enter, which cells you may touch, and which teammates you can be trusted beside when something goes wrong."

"I can touch cells. I touch cells every day. My aunt—"

"Your aunt's shop filed an incident report three years ago," said Ysra quietly. "A cell ran hot under your hands and burned her palm. And last spring, in a training lane at this school, your Ember slab discharged when you tried to ward and stride at once. Nobody was hurt that time. The lane's sensing mast was."

Kiva felt her face go hot, and she stared at the floor.

"Both of those are in your file," said Ysra, "and the file is what I am permitted to rule on. Item one, three open doors that pull against each other, in a person who cannot yet stop them from doing it. Item two, a set of rules built for people who have one." She paused. "Your rank is not the problem. If anything, it's too early to say whether your rank is a problem at all. Your control is the problem. Rank and control are different things. You know that."

"I know that."

"Good." Ysra turned to Orla. "Now you."

Orla took a breath. "The combined trial," she said. "The Cellhouse. Nine days from now. Candidates without a standard placement can be entered on a lecturer's request, and the board rules on the result. I want her in it."

"You want her in it," said Ysra. "On a lecturer's request."

"Yes."

"Not a sponsorship."

Orla's jaw set. "I'm her lecturer. I'm not her sponsor. You know that."

"You could be," said Ysra. "A senior lecturer with twenty years of Home Line field record can sponsor any candidate she likes. It takes one form and my stamp. And then her name goes on the roster beside the candidate's, and if the candidate burns down a cellhouse, her name goes on the incident report too." She held Orla's eyes steadily. "You've had four years. You haven't filed."

There was a short silence, in which Kiva looked from one woman to the other. She had never seen Orla Dane lose an argument. She had never seen her not have the next sentence ready.

Orla did not have it now.

"Request granted," said Ysra at last. She pulled a form from a drawer and began to fill it in. "Miss Rowan enters the combined placement trial at the Cellhouse on the twelfth. Team assignment by the board. Ruling by the board." She wrote for a while. "Two conditions. One. A candidate under eighteen who has an ash-incident record needs her guardian's countersignature to enter any trial involving live cells. Mara Rowan signs, or Miss Rowan does not run." She signed the form and turned it around. "Two. Miss Rowan, if you wake more than one door in that trial, and anything goes wrong, you will be withdrawn and your file will be closed for the season. Do you understand?"

Kiva read the form. Her name was on it. Under *Supervising lecturer*, it said *O. Dane*. Under *Sponsor*, the line was empty.

"I understand," she said.

"Good luck, then," said Ysra Crest. "I mean that. I'd very much like to know what you are."

---

Anwen and Perrin were waiting on the Compact steps.

The steps ran down from the Measure Hall to the plaza in long white flights, and on Classification Day they were packed. Families sat in clumps eating lunch out of paper, and recruiters from the chartered rift companies stood under striped awnings at the bottom, each with a table, a banner, and a board of last season's standings. Kiva could see the Graywater banner, slate blue with a white wave on it, and Saltmere's green, and the black and gold of the Halden companies, which were not rift companies exactly but which bought half the ash the rift companies brought home. Over the plaza hung the long anniversary banners the city had put up in the spring. *SIX HUNDRED YEARS. WE WROTE IT DOWN.*

Perrin Cade was sitting on the third step with a meat bun in each hand.

He was big. He was the biggest person Kiva knew who was still technically a teenager, broad and soft-looking in a way that was completely untrue, with sandy hair and a sunburned nose. He was still-door, Stonehand Hall, Flame rank and pushing at the top of it. He had been coming to Orla's Thursday-night lanes for a year, because Orla let anyone come, and he had decided on the second night that Kiva and Anwen were his friends and that there was nothing they could do about it.

He held out one of the buns.

"I got you lunch," he said. "Because I knew you'd be in a room. With important people. Being important."

Kiva took it. "It wasn't like that."

"It was a little like that." He bit into the other bun. "Ysra Crest. In a private room. Your own personal Orla Dane, arguing for you. I got a number on a board." He chewed. "It was a good number. Six-eight on hold. Flame, upper band. Nobody took me into a little room about it."

"Perrin," said Anwen.

"I'm not complaining. I'm observing." He swallowed. "I'm observing with my whole heart and a small amount of bitterness."

Kiva sat down beside him. The bun was hot and greasy and she had not eaten since six, so she ate half of it before she said anything.

"Classification deferred," she said. "Fourth year. They're putting me in the Cellhouse trial. On Orla's request."

"Request?" said Anwen. "Not sponsorship?"

"Not sponsorship."

Anwen said nothing. She took out her list, wrote something on it, and folded it again.

"What did you write?" said Kiva.

"Nothing."

"Anwen."

"I wrote *ask Orla why.*" Anwen put the list away. "I drew the Cellhouse too. Field track. Afternoon slot. Graywater's taking four placements this year. They said four last year and took three, so I asked them why." She gazed out over the plaza. "They said one candidate dropped out. I asked who, and they wouldn't tell me. I don't believe them."

"You think they'll do it again?"

"I think they'll take three again if three are good enough. And I intend to be one of the three." She glanced sideways at Kiva. "I'm not running for you, in that trial. You know that? If they put us on the same team, I'm running for the field track. I'll help. I'm not carrying you."

"I know."

"I just want it said."

"It's said," said Kiva. And then, because Anwen was still watching her, "Good. I don't want to be carried."

Perrin finished his bun and licked his fingers. "I drew the Cellhouse," he said. "Morning slot. If I score above the line, I get the Hall bursary. If I don't, I go back to the tram yards with my father and fix brake shoes for the rest of my life. Which is a fine life. My father is very happy. He tells me so every morning while he fixes the brake shoes." He squinted at the crowd. "Six kids in our flat. The bursary's the only way any of us gets a Compact place. So I'll be thinking about that. Instead of you."

"Good," said Kiva.

"Good," agreed Perrin. Then he bumped her shoulder with his, quite hard, the way he did when he was finished being honest and wanted to be friends again. "You'll be fine. You've got the disk."

Kiva stopped chewing.

"Don't," she said.

"What? Everybody knows you've got it."

"It doesn't do anything."

"Right, but it's *old.* It's Home Line. Somebody picked you." He shrugged. "Nobody ever picked me for anything. That was a joke. It was meant to be a joke when it started."

Kiva put her hand flat against her chest, over the disk, without meaning to. It was cold, as it always was. In four years it had never once been warm, not when she slept with it, not when she held it in her fist, not on the worst nights when she woke up with the heat-lines burning and the pieces of the dream sliding away from her in the dark. It did not hum like a cell or answer like a door. It was a cold dark circle with nine grooves cut in it, and she did not know what it was for.

She knew what people thought it was for.

She sat on the step and watched the recruiters under their striped awnings, the Graywater blue and the Saltmere green and the Halden black and gold, and the Fire-rank boy with the steady eyes shaking hands at every table. And she decided something she had been circling around for most of a year.

She was not going to wait any longer for a board to tell her what she was. She was going to go into the Cellhouse and do something that a board could count.

"I'm going home," she said, and stood up.

---

The Eastbank tram was crowded and warm and smelled of other people's lunches.

Kiva stood without holding the rail, which she did without thinking now. The car swayed through the Compact Quarter, past the white arenas and the glass-fronted lecture halls, and over the river on the long iron bridge. Below, the water was brown with spring, and above, on the flight lanes, the lift craft moved in their slow lines toward the station with their rings glinting blue-white in the sun: a Saltmere boat, a medical lifter, and a long freight hauler with a Halden stripe.

At the front of the car, over the driver's seat, hung the car's cell meter, with its needle sitting at three-quarters. Every tram in Meridian ran on ash induction, from cells charged at the Eastbank core and tuned every morning by the yard Kindled. Kiva had once sat with Mara for a whole afternoon while she rebuilt one of those meters from a box of parts, and she knew every jewel and spring inside it.

She watched the needle all the way across the river. It did not stick.

The tram let her off at Coper Street. The shop was halfway down, with a laundry on one side and a bakery on the other, and the sign over the door said *ROWAN INSTRUMENTS & RECORDS* in faded gold. In the window, a row of repaired meters sat on a shelf with paper tags tied to them, waiting for the municipal van.

Through the glass, she could see Mara at the bench.

Her aunt was bent over a district pressure gauge with its face off, working on something inside it with a pair of fine tweezers, with her reading glasses pushed up into her hair. Beside her elbow, a stack of paper ledgers sat under a brass weight, and a cup of tea had gone cold without being drunk. The light from the bench lamp lay across her right hand.

Kiva could see the scar from the door. It ran across the palm and up the inside of the wrist, shiny and pale and slightly puckered. Mara never hid it, and she had never once, in three years, said a word about it.

The form was in Kiva's bag. *Guardian's countersignature.*

She stood on the step with her hand on the door and did not open it.

Inside, Mara looked up and saw Kiva through the glass. She put down the tweezers, pulled off her glasses, and reached under the ledgers.

She drew out a long envelope with the Compact seal on it, already opened, and laid it flat on the bench.

Kiva went in. The bell over the door rang.

"They sent me a copy," said Mara. "By the noon post. Very efficient, the Compact." She did not so much as glance at the envelope. Her eyes stayed on Kiva. "Fourth year?"

"Fourth year."

"And this trial."

"The Cellhouse. Nine days." Kiva took a breath. "Orla asked for it. Ysra Crest granted it. It needs you to sign."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. In the back of the shop, a cell on the test rack ticked as it cooled, and out in the street a lift-truck rolled by.

"No," said Mara.
