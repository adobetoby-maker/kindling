# Chapter 15 — Kiva: The Carry

Stonehand Hall did not believe in flat ground, and the Long Terrace was the proof of it.

It lay at the heart of the Hall, under a roof of glass and iron, and from the gallery rail it looked like a dry dock with the ship taken out. The court was sunk a full story below the benches. It ran forty paces from end to end and fourteen from side to side, and nearly every part of it had been built to trip somebody.

At each end stood a stone platform, two knee-high steps above the floor, which the Hall called a porch. A single low step ran the whole length of both long walls. The floor between was coarse, pale sand, raked into lines, and straight across the middle of it lay a curb of worn stone no higher than an ankle, which the Hall called the sill. On the sill, on its canvas litter, with its arms folded on its chest, lay a sandbag in a coverall.

"That's theirs," said Pru Finch, at Kiva's elbow. "They've got one called Old Tom. He's been carried over our line so many times they've had his name embroidered."

"Pru," said Jude.

"I'm only telling her. She ought to know who she's rescuing."

It was Saturday afternoon. The gallery was full to the roof, and the noise of it came down into the court like weather. Kiva stood on the western porch with the other three, in a grey Third Division tunic that was a size too large, and let herself look up once.

It was full everywhere but in the rows nearest the western porch. Those had been full too, when the team came out. Then the board had put up four names, with *prov.* after the last, and a man in the second row had taken his small daughter by the hand and moved her up to the back, and a dozen people had found reasons to follow him. Nobody had shouted anything. She would almost rather they had.

Mara was there. She had said nothing about coming. She had put on her good coat after dinner and walked to the tram, and now she was sitting in the fourth row beside Orla Dane, very upright, holding her gloves in her lap like a woman at a hearing. Orla's bound leg stuck out into the aisle. Two rows behind them sat Anwen, with her list, and Kellan, whose own lane was the last on the card and who had come three hours early to watch this one with a pencil.

Further along, where the chairs had arms, there were coats of slate blue. One of the people wearing them was a tall woman with a white streak through her dark hair. Kiva knew that streak. She had looked at it for six years on a poster on Anwen's door, and she had seen it once in the flesh, pressed close to the glass of another gallery, on the worst afternoon of her life.

She did not know why Rhea Sorn should come to a Third Division lane. She put the question away with the others and brought her eyes back down.

"Fighters to the sill," said the referee.

---

It was the same referee. Kiva recognized the grey braid and the voice like a dropped crate, and by the look she got, the recognition was mutual.

The two teams met at the stone curb with Old Tom between them.

She had never seen four still-doors stand in a row before. They did not look like fighters. They looked like furniture that somebody had carried in and set down. The largest of them, a boy with a neck as thick as his head, was introduced as Hendry Moss, and beside him stood a long-armed, sharp-faced girl called Idony Keld, who was already looking at Kiva's left knee. The fourth was a first-year with ears like jug handles, who seemed more frightened than Pru.

Their captain put out his hand to Jude across the litter, and then, to her surprise, to her.

"Lowen Brask."

He was broad and unhurried, with a shaved head and very calm brown eyes, and his grip was careful. He had bare forearms and no cuff on either. None of the four wore an Ember.

"I watched your Cellhouse plate," he said. "Twice. You root better than half my second-years, and you'd had no teaching. I thought you ought to be told I'd looked." He let go of her hand. "It didn't seem right to know more about you than you knew I knew."

"Thank you," said Kiva, and found that she meant it, and that it frightened her rather more than if he had sneered.

"The Carry," said the referee. "For the benefit of the gallery and the two first-years. Three carries. Four minutes to each, by my glass. You begin on your own porch. The litter begins here. Put it on your own porch, the whole of it, and that's three points and the carry's done." She held up a thick hand and folded down the fingers. "A clean contact on the body or the head puts a fighter down. That's one point. A fighter who's down kneels where they are for a count of ten from the line judge. They don't touch the litter, and nobody touches them. A contact on a limb is a half, and two halves on one fighter in one carry make a down. Still-doors score with the open hand, or with a throw that puts both shoulders on the sand. Embers stay dark. Light an edge in my court and you'll be watching the rest from up there."

"And at time?" said Jude, who knew, but who had seen Kiva's face.

"If the glass runs out with the litter on the sand, it's one point to whichever half it lies in. Draw board's on the east wall. One standard dram each for the whole lane, three carries, and if you overspend it, that's a violation and a point off." Her eyes moved along the western four. "Doors."

"Talley. Ember."

"Finch. Hand."

"Bexley. Ember."

"Rowan. Still."

The referee did not write that one down straight away.

"I've a note about you from the Placement Chair," she said. "It says you'll tell me before you change. Is that right?"

"Yes. *Rowan, changing,* and then the door."

"Then here's how it goes in my court. You say it. I put up this flag." She showed a square of white cloth on a short stick. "You don't open the new one until the flag's up. Not when you've said it. When I've heard it. If you go before the flag, that's undeclared, and I'll score it so. I'm not having it argued afterward that you whispered."

It was fair. It was also a whole second longer.

She saw Lowen Brask hear it. He did not smile. He only looked at the white flag, and then at her, with the mild attention of a man reading the label on a jar.

---

The bell went, and Noa was off the porch before it had finished ringing.

He went down the two steps on his struts and across the sand in four long bounds, and Kiva saw the metal dig in at every stride and saw him ride it, light and crooked, like a boy running on a mattress. He had Old Tom by the foot poles while the four still-doors were still coming down their own steps.

They did not run. Jude had said so. They walked, shoulder to shoulder, with a slow rolling heavy tread, as if the four of them were carrying a piano that was not there.

"Drag!" said Jude. "Pru, the middle. Kiva, with me."

She could not Stride. She went down the steps on her own feet, minding the knee, and across fifteen paces of raked sand at something between a walk and a run. Noa had dragged the litter four paces into their half and was hauling at it with his heels dug in. She dropped to it. She took the two head poles in her fists and let her weight go down.

The still door opened like a cellar. The sand took her. She felt her knees go soft and sure, and the litter under her hands became the heaviest thing in the Hall.

"Planted," she said.

Pru's ward went up across the court behind her, wide and curved and leaning.

For about the space of ten breaths, it worked exactly as Jude had chalked it.

Moss reached the ward first, and leaned on it. It rang, and rang, and held, and Kiva heard Pru give a small astonished squeak. Keld did not lean on anything. She looked at the silver, and then she walked four paces to the right, to where it ended, and went round the end of it as a person goes round a puddle.

"She's just walked *round*," said Pru. "Jude, she's—"

"Hold Moss. I have her."

But he did not have her, because he was watching the other two. Brask and the boy with the ears had come straight on down the middle while the ward was busy, and they had arrived. The boy bent for the foot poles. Noa darted in and slapped his arm with a dark, stubby half-formed blade.

"Half! Crooked Line."

The boy flinched from it. As he flinched, his ribs came open, and Jude Talley, who had been waiting three years for exactly that, laid the flat of his dark edge across them as neatly as buttering bread.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

And Kiva's hands let go.

They did not come off the poles. But for three weeks, on a brick floor and a cinder path and in the long hot hours at the kitchen table, a shape had gone down beside her, and a voice had shouted, and every part of her had done one thing. *Dark. Count.* The stance eased under her as if it had been greased. Her eyes went to the sand at her right, where a grey shape was folding to its knees, to see where it would fall.

It lasted one breath.

Lowen Brask was standing at the foot of the litter with a pole in each hand. He did not snatch. He simply straightened his back.

The poles slid through her slack fingers. Old Tom went up at the far end and came away from her, and by the time her weight dropped back into the sand, there was nothing in her fists but air, and the litter was a pace off and moving, its head end plowing a furrow.

"No," said Kiva.

She was rooted on nothing. The still door was wide open under her, holding up nobody, and Keld had come round and caught the trailing poles and lifted, and now two of them had it between them and were walking east.

She tried to turn. It was like turning a cart.

Jude had gone after them. He could not get past Keld's long arm. Noa was circling like a terrier. Nobody was going to stop that litter with a blade, and the only person on her side who could put a shoulder to it was planted fifteen paces behind with the wrong door open.

"Rowan, changing!" she shouted. "Ember!"

She saw the white flag go up. It seemed to take a week.

She gave the ground its weight back. *Thank you.* She shut the still door behind it and counted one, and reached for the slab, and the metal came off her cuff and ran across to her right fist and began to close—

She had been watching the flag. She had not been watching Idony Keld.

Keld had set her end of the litter down the moment the word *changing* left Kiva's mouth. She had covered six paces of sand in the time it took a referee to raise a stick. Her palm arrived, flat and hard and exactly on time, in the middle of Kiva's ribs, while the bar was still soft on her knuckles.

"*Down!* Crooked Line!"

Kiva knelt in the sand with the breath knocked out of her.

*One,* said the line judge. *Two.*

She counted with him. There was nothing else to do. From her knees she watched Jude put Hendry Moss down with a neat touch under the arm, and watched Moss kneel as placidly as a man sitting down to supper. She watched the boy with the ears finish his own ten and get up and trot to the litter. She watched Brask and Keld walk Old Tom up two stone steps without breaking stride, as people walk upstairs with the washing.

"*Carried!* Stonehand!"

The glass on the referee's table had more than a minute of sand left in it.

The board on the east wall turned over its figures. *STONEHAND 4. CROOKED LINE 2.* And beneath, in the column by her name: *Dram, 0.38.*

---

Perrin Cade had both wrapped hands clamped between his knees so that he would not do anything with them.

He was in the front row of the gallery, with the Hall's first-years, in the Hall's colors. He had sat there because it was where he belonged. His bursary had the Hall's seal on it. So did his tunic. The people around him had been stamping their feet since the word *carried*, and he had stamped twice, and stopped, and felt like a liar in both directions.

Brask had seen it. Perrin had watched him see it.

It was a thing Brask used to say in the junior lane, on wet mornings, to rows of thirteen-year-olds who were not listening. *Everybody's ready for a thing to start. Watch what they do when it stops.* He would shove a boy, and the boy would brace, and Brask would nod. Then he would clap his hands and say *good, that's all*, and shove him again as he relaxed, and the boy would sit down in the sand. Perrin had sat down in that sand perhaps forty times before he had learned.

Kiva had never been taught by Lowen Brask. She had been taught, for three weeks, by a brick floor, that when a thing went down beside her, the right and clever and disciplined answer was to go soft and look at the ground.

He had seen her do it on the Sag Line. He had thought it was the best thing he had ever watched.

Down on the western porch, the four of them had their heads together. Talley was chalking on the stone. Kiva had one hand flat against her ribs. From here she looked very small in that tunic.

On the eastern porch, Brask had not gathered anyone. He was sitting on the top step with his forearms on his knees, saying something short to Moss. Moss nodded. Then Brask's eyes went across forty paces of sand and rested on Kiva Rowan's hands.

*He's going to do it on purpose,* thought Perrin. *He's going to give her one.*

He could have shouted it. He had the lungs. It was not against any rule that he knew of, and a year ago he would not have thought twice.

But that was his captain down there, on his Hall's sand, who had taught him to thank the ground. And an hour ago, on the rack at the north end, in front of this same gallery, three first-years had put their names where Perrin's ought to have been.

He had not let himself look at the wall since. He looked now.

The brass plate was as long as a door. It carried the Hall's best first-year hold for every year since the rack was built, and the same line had headed it since long before Perrin was born. *340 kilos, thirty counts. 2649.* Thirty-one years. Masters had grown old under that number.

Today a girl from the Weighbridge had held three hundred and fifty. Then a boy Perrin shared a bench with had held three fifty-five. Then a third, a quiet lad nobody had thought much of, had stood under three hundred and sixty-two with his face perfectly calm, and the old master with the chisel had laughed out loud and said it was a strong year, and that he would need a longer plate.

Perrin had held four hundred. On his back, on his palms, for forty seconds, with a winch dead above him. Everyone in Meridian had seen it. But it had not been on the rack, and so it was not a hold, and he had been down to try for the plate today until a husk walked through him in a potato bed.

The Hall healer had looked at the strapping and laughed too, less kindly.

He had not told Kiva. He did not intend to. She would write it in that book of hers, under *what it cost*, and look at him with that awful steady face, and he would rather have the ribs.

*I'd do it again,* he thought, and it was true. *And I mind.* That was true as well. The two things sat side by side in his chest like a pair of badly matched boots, and he was getting used to the walk.

He kept his hands between his knees. He was Stonehand. He would be Stonehand tomorrow.

But when the bell went for the second carry, Perrin found that he was not watching the litter at all. He was watching Hendry Moss, who was coming down the eastern steps with his big arms hanging loose and open, like a man walking out to be measured for a coat.

---

"Rowan. Ember," she told the referee.

Jude had changed the chalk. It was simple enough. Stonehand walked through wards and round them, and they did not care about being touched. So the litter would not sit. It would move. Noa and Pru would lift it between them and carry it home, and Jude and Kiva would stand between the carriers and anything that came.

"The bar stops a hand as well as it stops a blade," Jude had said. "Kellan Renn couldn't get round it. Let's see if a girl with long arms can."

The bell went.

Noa was first again. Pru ran, which Kiva had never seen her do, with her scarf coming undone, and reached him as he got the foot poles up. They had Old Tom off the sill and swinging before the four still-doors were halfway. Pru's face was already crimson. It was twenty paces to the western porch.

Idony Keld came straight for the carriers, and Kiva stepped into her road.

The bar was on her fist, dark, forearm-long and ugly. Keld's palm came at her chest, and she brought the flat of the metal across, and the palm stopped dead against it with a smack like a butcher's slab.

Keld's eyebrows went up. She struck again, with the other hand, lower. The bar was there.

It was the best that Kiva had felt on that sand all day. *This* was what the thing was for. It could not cut and it could not reach, but it was as broad as a shovel, and behind it the litter was moving west, a pace and another pace, with Pru gasping and Noa shouting at her to mind the furrow.

Keld's eyes dropped to Kiva's left knee.

Her weight shifted. Her right hand swept in low and fast at the outside of that leg.

Kiva had seen a husk come straight at her forty times that month. Not one of them had ever started to go one way and gone another. She turned the bar down, and put her weight on the knee to do it, and the knee told her, with a flash straight up the thigh, what it thought of the idea. Keld's right hand had never been coming at all. Her left clipped Kiva smartly on the upper arm.

"Half! Stonehand."

"Kiva! Their captain!"

She looked round.

Lowen Brask had not gone to the litter. He had not gone to anyone. He had walked, at that same rolling unhurried pace, the whole length of the court, and now he stood at the foot of the western steps with his heels against the bottom riser.

He was planted. She could feel it from ten paces, a weight in the sand like a boulder under a lawn. And the litter, with Noa and Pru staggering at either end, was coming straight toward him, because that was where home was.

Jude went at him. The dark edge flicked at his ribs and found a forearm in the way.

"Half! Crooked Line."

It flicked again, and Brask turned his shoulder, and it slid off. He did not hit back. He did not need to. He only stood there, guarded, immovable, with the step at his back, and waited for the litter to walk into him.

"I can't get a clean one while he's set," said Jude. He was breathing hard. "Somebody's got to unset him. Or go through."

There was one person on the western side who could carry a litter into a planted still-door and not be stopped by it. She had done it twelve days ago, for four minutes, for nine hundredths of a dram.

She had the wrong door open.

"Rowan, changing!" It came out cracked. "Still!"

Somebody in the Hall's rows had learned the word in the first carry. *Changing!* a voice sang back at her, high and pleased, and a dozen more took it up along the benches.

The flag. She did not watch the metal. She looked at Brask's shaved head and felt the copper take the weight, and breathed out, and counted one, and reached under her feet.

She was open for two seconds. Keld knew it. Kiva saw the long arms start toward her, and saw Jude step across and take the palm on his own bar, and thought, *he's covered me.* And then she thought, *and so nobody's on Brask.*

The still door came up. She took the head poles out of Pru's shaking hands.

"Go and ward," she said. "I've got him."

The weight went down into the sand. She walked.

It was the wading walk, a little taken and a little given back at every step, and her knee burned, and the litter came on behind her like a barge. Brask watched it come. When the front of the canvas was a pace from him, he put both broad hands flat on Old Tom's chest and leaned.

They stopped.

She had never felt another person's stance from the inside of her own. It was like putting a shoulder to a loaded wagon and discovering that somebody on the other side was doing the same. He was stronger than she was. She knew it in the first breath. Her hold was six and two on a good day and his was seven and a half every day, and the difference came up through the poles into her wrists as a slow, steady, patient pressure. She was not going forward. She was, by the width of a finger at a time, going back.

But she was holding. And he was held. His hands were on the litter and his weight was in it, and for as long as that lasted, he could not guard his ribs.

"Jude!" she said, through her teeth. "*Now*—"

Something large moved at the edge of her eye.

Hendry Moss had walked up beside her. He was close enough to touch. He was not looking at her, or at the litter, or at anything. His great arms hung at his sides, wide and loose, and his chest was as open as a barn door.

Jude's blade lay across it before he had finished arriving.

"*Down!* Stonehand!"

Moss knelt. He knelt at her right elbow, slowly, a big grey shape going down to the sand.

And Kiva went dark.

She knew what she was doing while she did it. That was the horror of it. She felt the stance ease. She felt her eyes pull down and to the right, to mark the fall, and she had time to think, quite clearly, *he's not ash*, and it made no difference at all. The hands had been taught. For one breath, the still door held nothing.

Lowen Brask pushed.

Seventy kilos of sand and canvas came back through her arms like a ram. The poles took her in the hips. Her heels went, and her bad knee folded, and the sky over the Long Terrace was made of glass and iron and was suddenly all she could see.

Both shoulders. She felt them hit.

"*Down!* Crooked Line!"

---

She lay on her back and listened to the count.

*One. Two.* Somewhere above her head, feet were moving on sand, heavy and unhurried. The litter went past. She saw the underside of it, and Old Tom's embroidered name, upside down.

*Five. Six.*

She had been given him. That was what she could not stop turning over. Moss had not been beaten. He had walked up and held his arms out. Brask had handed Jude a point, as you hand a dog a biscuit, so that a shape would go down at her elbow, because he had watched her in the first carry and had known to the half breath what she would do.

A husk did not do that. A husk came straight. A husk did not remember you from one minute to the next.

*Nine. Ten.*

She got up. The litter was thirty paces off, nearly at the eastern steps, with Brask at one end and the boy with the ears at the other. Jude and Noa were worrying at them. Keld was holding both of them off with her long arms, and her face was a little bored.

There was no still-door answer to thirty paces. There was nothing to plant on.

"Rowan, changing. Ember."

Half the gallery said it with her.

The flag. The gap. Nobody came for her this time. They did not have to.

She ran, on a knee that would not properly bend, with the bar on her fist. She reached the steps as Jude finally got a clean touch through Keld's guard, and the referee's voice said *down, Stonehand*, and it did not matter, because Old Tom was already lying on the eastern porch with his arms folded.

"*Carried!* Stonehand!"

The gallery roared. The board turned over.

*STONEHAND 8. CROOKED LINE 4.*

*Rowan. Dram, 0.87.*

---

Nobody on the western porch said anything for a while.

Pru sat on the top step with her head between her knees. Jude stood with his hands on his hips, looking at the board as a man looks at a bill. Kiva lowered herself onto the stone and stretched out her left leg, and under her tunic, the bottom heat-line ached, one long dull thread. Only the one. She had not lit two all afternoon. It seemed a very small thing to have managed.

"He gave us Moss," said Pru, into her knees. "He just *gave* him to us. Like a parcel."

"He's been giving us points since the first bell," said Jude. He rubbed the chipped tooth. "Four touches to their two. I thought we were winning the fights."

"We were," said Kiva. "It isn't a fight."

They looked at her.

She had not known what she was going to say until she had begun. But it had been laying itself out in her head since she lay on her back in the sand, piece by piece, as a fault lays itself out on the bench once you have the case off.

"It's what the Home Line officer said on Sunday. He doesn't keep a kill column. Brask doesn't either. He'll take a touch any time, if it buys him the litter. We've been playing it like a floor. Kill it, hold it, carry it." She pressed her hand to her ribs. "But a husk can't choose to lose a leg to get past you. He can."

"That's the plan," said Jude slowly. "That's my plan you're talking about."

"It's a good plan. I thought so too." She made herself go on. "The rest is me. Two things. When one of them goes down beside me, I go soft. I look at the ground. I've spent three weeks learning to do exactly that, and I can't seem to stop it. He saw it in the first carry, and in the second he made it happen." She held up a second finger. "And every time I change doors, I have to say so, and wait for a flag. That's two seconds where I've got nothing open, and I shout to the whole court when it's about to start. The first time, she hit me. The second time, you had to come off their captain to stop her. The third time, they didn't even bother."

"You're allowed three doors," said Pru. "I've only got the one. I'd have thought—"

"So did I," said Kiva. "For four years. I thought if they'd only let me use them, I'd be worth three people." It was oddly easy to say, sitting on a stone step with sand down the back of her neck. "I've used all three today. Every one of them was the right door for what was in front of me. And all it's given him is three different places to stand while I'm busy getting from one to the next."

Noa had not been listening. He had been at the east wall, at the foot of the draw board, where the mast's paper drum turned behind glass with eight pens on it. He came back along the side step at a trot with a length of tape pinched between his fingers.

"Look," he said. He knelt and spread it on the stone. "That's you. Fourth pen. I asked the line judge and he let me tear it. First carry. See how flat it is while you're planted? Hardly anything. Then here." His inky finger stopped at a sharp peak. "That's *changing*. And here it stays up, the whole time you've got the bar. Second carry. Up from the bell, because you started on Ember. Then a spike, down for forty seconds, another spike, up again." He looked up at her, and his narrow face was alight. "It's not you that's dear. It's the Ember door, and it's the getting there. On the still door, you spent two hundredths in forty seconds with their captain leaning on you. Two hundredths."

"How much have I got left?"

"Thirteen."

"For four minutes."

"On the bar, you'd be dry inside one. On the still door—" He did the sum with his lips. "You'd finish with something over. Not much."

Kiva looked across the sand. Brask was sitting on his top step again. He was not looking at her hands this time. He was looking at the flag on the referee's table.

If they had bought a meet reserve, she would have had half a dram more. She would have gone out for the third carry with the bar on her fist and all three doors to hand, and done precisely what she had done in the first two, for a little longer.

"I'm declaring still," she said. "And I'm not changing. Not once, whatever happens. If the litter goes thirty paces off, then it goes, and I can't chase it. If somebody swings at me, I can't block." She turned to Jude. "So you'd have to be the bar. Pru's the ward. Noa's the legs. I can be the one thing they can't walk through, and that's all I can be."

Jude Talley was quiet for a moment. Then, for the first time that day, he showed the chipped tooth.

"Then we're your other doors," he said. "I've been called worse." He crouched and rubbed out his chalk with the heel of his hand. "Where do you want to be?"

That was a floor question, and she had a floor answer.

"There." She pointed to the foot of their own steps, where Brask had stood. "With my heel on the bottom riser. He used it. It's free. It doesn't cost anything to lean on a step." She thought of Kellan, saying *huh* very softly on another stretch of sand. "They have to bring the litter past me to take it home. Don't carry it to our porch. We can't lift it up two steps with them hanging on. Bring it to me. I'll plant on it. Then they have to come and get it."

"And when they send Moss over to be touched?"

She had been coming to that.

"I need somebody to say something," she said. "When one of them goes down near me. Straight away, before I've had time to go soft. It doesn't matter what, as long as it's got a number in it."

"A number," said Jude.

"She counts," said Noa, as if that explained it. "It's in her tape. I can show you."

"I'll do it," said Pru. She had lifted her head. Her face was blotched and her scarf was gone entirely, and she looked like a person who has been told she may, after all, be useful. "I'm good at counting disasters. I've been doing it under my breath all afternoon."

Down on the sill, the referee turned her glass.

"Third carry," she said. "Doors."

Kiva stood up. The knee held.

"Rowan," she said. "Still."
