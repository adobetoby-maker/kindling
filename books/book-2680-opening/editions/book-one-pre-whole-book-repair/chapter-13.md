# Chapter 13 — Kiva: License Day

The tear was not dark.

Kiva had always imagined that it would be. Every drawing in every schoolbook showed a rift as a black slit, like a cut in a curtain. The thing that hung over Agnes Roake's potato bed at first light on Sunday was the color the sky goes in the last minute before the street lamps come on, a deep, clear, tired blue, and it was no bigger than a cellar door laid on its side. It floated at about the height of her knee. The air around its edge shivered the way air shivers over a stove.

She stood on the Sag Line road with the kit roll under her arm, sixty paces off and four above, and felt it in the back of her neck like a thumb.

Under her shirt, the three lines lay quiet. She had half expected them to answer it. They did not, and she was grateful, and a little ashamed of being grateful.

"Small," said Mara, beside her. She had the mast's book in one hand and her tool roll in the other, and she was looking at the tear as she would have looked at a fault in someone else's wiring. "I've seen four. That's the smallest. It'll be making Handfuls and nothing else." She turned her back on it. "Go and find your team. I've a drum to read."

The allotments ran south from the foot of the berm in long narrow strips, grey with dew, each with its shed and its water butt and its rows of poles standing ready for beans that had not yet been planted. A drainage cut two paces wide ran along the bottom of the berm between the road and the plots. Somebody had staked a square of white tape around the corner plot, twenty paces to a side, and at each corner of the square stood a figure in an undyed canvas coat with both hands raised.

Between those four pairs of hands, faint as breath on a window, hung four walls of silver.

Kiva had seen Anwen hold a ward the size of a dinner plate. She had seen Ysra Crest's name in a story about a water main. She had never seen four people hold one twenty paces long, in the open, in the cold, and from the set of their shoulders they had been doing it since three in the morning.

Inside the square were the tear, a tarred shed, a strip of cinder path, and the dug black earth of the potato bed. Nothing else moved.

"Rowan Instruments?"

The man who had come up the berm steps behind them was lean and about forty-five, with a face so weathered that his eyes looked pale in it. He wore the same canvas coat with the same tar-black band. A slate hung from his belt on a cord, and there was a stub of chalk behind his ear. He did not raise his voice. Kiva noticed that everyone near him had lowered theirs.

"Hollis Vane. I've the watch." He nodded at the steel box at the foot of mast six. "That's the one that rang first. I want to know whether to thank it or pull it out."

---

Mara had the box open in under a minute.

She unpinned the drum roll and laid it along the lid, and beside it she laid two more, which Vane's courier had brought down the line from masts five and seven. She weighted the corners with her wrenches. Kiva had seen her do exactly that on a cold bright Thursday a little more than three weeks ago, in this same place, with four rolls and nobody watching.

"There," said Mara. She set her finger on the inked line of mast six. It ran flat along the roll, a little under halfway between the second grid mark and the third, and then it jumped. "Ten to three. Clean rise, no stutter. And here." She moved to the other two. Their lines ran flat exactly on the second mark, and jumped in the same shape. "Six minutes to. Both of them. Four minutes behind."

"Five and seven were recalibrated in the winter," said Vane. "Wren House sent someone. It's in their books. Six wasn't."

"No."

"Why not?"

Mara turned the mast's cell book round on the lid and opened it to the entry. She did not explain it. She let him read.

*Baseline elevated, consistent with prior quarters. Cell clean. Drum true. No fault found. Recal. would mask trend. Not recalibrated. M.R.*

Hollis Vane read it without hurry.

"So it's reading high," he said.

"It's reading what's there." Mara's voice was level. "The other two are reading what's there, less a correction somebody put in to make the line sit where the manual says it ought to. The bell's set to a mark on the grid. Drop the baseline, and the line has further to climb before it rings. I didn't think of four minutes when I wrote that. I only thought that if I flattened it, nobody would see it creeping." She took her finger off the paper. "I ought to tell you that my tuning license is under review with the Works. If you'd rather somebody else read your mast."

"I didn't ask who tuned it. I asked who left it alone."

He unhooked the slate from his belt and wrote on it, three lines, in a quick slanting hand.

"My first ward-holder reached that north corner about ninety seconds before the first husk stood up," he said, writing. "I timed it, afterward, because I'd thought we were going to lose it. With five and seven alone, I'd have had no hold. I'd have had a Handful loose in forty allotments in the dark, and a cold-cupboard cell in every shed that's got one." He looked up. "Rowan, M. I'll want that in the report. Whoever is reviewing you may have a copy, if a copy's any use."

Mara did not answer at once. Kiva, who knew her aunt's face better than any other thing in the world, saw the corner of her mouth tighten the way it did when a fault turned out to be exactly where she had said.

"It might be," said Mara. "Thank you. I'll write up the drum."

She bent over the rolls again. Vane hooked the slate back on his belt, and his pale eyes went to the kit under Kiva's arm, and then to her face.

"One of mine?"

"Team Seven."

"Last on the call." He said it without sympathy and without anything else. "There's only a reseat behind you. You'll have a long day to watch other people's mistakes. I'd use it. The hold comes down at six this evening whether you've had your turn or not. My people have been leaning on that tear since before you were awake, and there's a closing crew on its way from Brickfields who'll want it shut by dark."

"How often does it make one?"

"Every forty minutes, about. It's been regular as a tram." He started down the steps toward the plank bridge. "So far."

---

By noon she had filled two pages.

Fourteen teams had mustered at the plank bridge. Thirteen had stood at the bottom of the call since the trial, and the fourteenth was Team Nineteen, sent back to try again in the place of a team the warden had not passed fit for a field. A fifteenth sat at the end of the row with one new face in it. They waited on the grass of the berm with their kits between their feet, like passengers waiting for a ferry. Each team went in through a gap that the western ward-holders opened by lowering their hands. Each declared its roles to Hollis Vane. Each had one husk.

Kiva wrote down every fall.

She had not planned to. She had opened the field book to make a note of the wind, which at six in the morning was no wind at all, and then the first team had gone in, and its Ember had cut the knot on the cinder path, and she had found her pencil drawing a little square for the plot and a cross where the ash came down. After that she could not have stopped. She put a cross for every kill and an arrow for which way it ran, and beside each a count and a word.

*T33. Path, by shed. Still air. Tinned in 52. Clean.*

*T14. Potato bed. Soft. Husk sank to the hocks, slow. Ash fell in the furrows. Catcher dug for it. Count 60+, sweepings.*

*T26. Path. Vane said "Lund, you're down" to their ward. They kept going without her. Ember got knocked flat. Off called late. Nobody hurt much.*

"He did it again," said Perrin, on the fourth. He was lying on his back in the grass with his hands on his chest and a dock leaf over his eyes. "I heard it. *You're down.* Who was it?"

"Their catcher."

"What did they do?"

"The Carry picked up the pans." Kiva wrote. "He was slow. Thirty-one in the count before the first tin. But they got fourteen."

"I'd have got fifteen," said Perrin, to the dock leaf.

Kellan sat a little apart, with his arms round his knees, watching the blades. He had said perhaps ten words since dawn. Once, when a Stonehand team's Ember had finished a husk and stepped back with her edge still lit, Kiva had seen his whole body lean forward, and had seen him lean back again when Vane's voice came flat across the plots: "Dark, Miss Corwen. You're a lamp."

Anwen was not sitting with them at all. She was at the white tape, as close as the hold would let her stand, and she had not taken her eyes off the four ward-holders for an hour.

A man in slate blue had come down the berm steps at eight, bald and brown and unhurried, and had stood beside her for a minute. Kiva had been too far away to hear it. She had seen Doran Mace ask something, and seen Anwen answer in one word, and seen him nod and walk on along the row to where Team Eleven sat with their Wren House reserve.

The one word had been short. From where Kiva sat, it had looked a good deal like *Seven*.

---

The wind got up a little after two.

She felt it first on the back of her neck, and then she saw it, in the dead grass along the cut, all leaning the same way. It came from the west. It came down the length of the berm like water down a gutter, along the cut and over the plots, and it did not gust. It simply leaned, steadily, on everything.

She licked her pencil and wrote *Wind W to E, 2 o'clock, steady,* and then she looked east to see where it was going.

Forty paces beyond the white tape, on the bank of the cut, stood a small brick hut with a tin roof. A fat iron pipe ran from it down into the water and another ran from it up to a tank on stilts. It had been thumping quietly to itself all morning, a slow heartbeat that she had stopped hearing.

"Is that a pump?" she said.

"That's my pump," said a voice above her.

An old woman was standing on the berm behind the row of candidates, in a man's overcoat tied round the middle with string. She had been there since before sunrise, Kiva realized. She had taken her for one of the Home Line's people. She had a face like a walnut and a trowel sticking out of one pocket.

"That's the allotment pump, and I'm the pump warden this year, for my sins. And that's my potato bed they've got their door hanging over, and my shed, and those are my bean poles." She did not sound angry. She sounded like somebody reading out an inventory after a flood. "Agnes Roake. Plot nineteen. I had four hundred seedlings pricked out to go in this week. They're in trays behind the shed. I'd be obliged if nobody trod on them."

"I'll tell my team," said Kiva.

"You do that." Agnes Roake looked down at the open book on Kiva's knee, at the little squares and crosses. "What's the pencil for?"

"Where they fall. I'm the one who has to pick it up."

"Hm," said Agnes Roake. It sounded exactly like Warden Druce. "Then you'll want to know the path's firm as far as the shed and soft after. I put two barrows of cinder down in the autumn and ran out."

Kiva wrote that down too.

Team Nineteen went in at half past three, thirteenth of fourteen.

She knew before the gap closed behind them how it would go, and she hated knowing. Selka Dray walked to the north end of the plot, to the bank of the cut, which was the nearest firm ground to the tear. She bounced on her toes. Her catcher laid the cloth on the cinder path, by the shed, as nine catchers before him had done, and looked at it, and looked at Dray, and said something. Dray shook her head.

The husk stood up out of the blue at twenty to four, and Dray lit.

It took her four seconds. It was the same lovely four seconds. The thing came for her in a line straight across the dug earth, and she met it on the bank in one Stride and cut the knot at a run, and the whole grey shape let go on the very lip of the cut, in the wind.

It did not pour. The wind took it.

Kiva watched a Handful of open ash go up like smoke off a bonfire. Half of it sheeted down into the brown water and was gone. The rest streamed east along the bank in a long low grey scarf, over the white tape, through the silver of the hold as if the ward were not there at all. And it did not thin out and settle on the grass, as dust ought to. It gathered. It drew in on itself as it went, narrowing, like a thread being pulled through the eye of a needle.

The eye of the needle was a small brick hut with a tin roof.

"*Dark!*" That was Vane, and for the first time all day it was a shout. "Dark, Dray, and get your catcher to that pump house—"

It was far too late. The grey scarf went in under the eaves of the tin roof. The slow thumping heartbeat ran on for three more beats, and then it stumbled, and raced, and stopped.

The allotments were very quiet.

"There now," said Agnes Roake softly.

---

Hollis Vane wrote the result on his slate where everyone could watch him do it. Then he read it out.

"Team Nineteen. Kill, four seconds from sight. Yield, none. One civic device fouled. Response failed." He hung the slate on his belt. "Nobody hurt. I'll say that first, because it's worth more than the rest. Miss Dray, that was as quick a blade as I've seen this year."

Selka Dray was standing at the gap in the tape with her fists clenched and her face scarlet. "I *killed* it."

"You did. Now come and see what else you did. All of you who haven't been in yet. Seven, Eleven. Bring your catchers."

He walked them along the bank of the cut to the pump house, a dozen candidates in a straggling line, with Agnes Roake stumping along behind in her overcoat. A Wren House tuner in a grey apron was already there, kneeling at the open door with a lamp.

The cell was no bigger than the one in a tram. It sat in a cradle of copper coils beside the silent pump, and the little meter on its case was doing a thing Kiva had never seen a meter do. The needle was wandering. It drifted up to three-quarters, hung, sank to a half, and crept up again, like a drunk man trying to find a curb.

She was two paces from the door, with her hands behind her back, and she could feel it anyway.

It had been a plain municipal tune, a slow, even pump tune that some yard operator had laid in years ago and that had gone on thumping ever since. Now there was a streak through it. It was hot and quick and yellow, and it was the exact color of Selka Dray's edge-light. The tune was trying to hold its shape around it and could not, in the way a woven thing cannot hold its shape around a snapped thread.

"That's a lit blade in a pump cell," said Vane. "A few drams of it. It was open when she cut it, and she was still lit when it went, and the wind carried it to the nearest pattern, and the pattern took it in. You can't press that out. You can't tune round it." He nodded to the tuner. "Go on."

The tuner broke the wire seal and pulled the red handle. There was a clank and a hiss.

"Four hundred seedlings," said Agnes Roake, "and a bucket." She looked Selka Dray up and down. "You'll not have known. I don't suppose anyone told you where the wind went."

"Nobody told me anything." Dray's voice shook. She was not looking at the old woman. She was looking along the line, at Kellan, and then at Kiva with the field book under her arm. "I cut it in four seconds. Clean. And I'm to stand here and be shown a pump, while *she* fouled a cell in the Cellhouse in front of half the Compact, and got a roster place for it, and the best blade in the year to stand dark at her back." She laughed, a short hard sound. "She's on the list after me. I hope somebody's warned the pumps."

Nobody answered her. Vane did not appear to have heard. Kiva found that she had nothing to say, because the part about the cell was true, and Kellan, beside her, went on looking at the dead meter and did not move a muscle of his face.

Dray's catcher was standing behind her with the empty carrier, and Kiva saw him look at the cloth he had laid on the path, forty paces back, with nothing on it.

"The casualty reserve will refill that cell," said Vane. "In about a month, when the claim's been through three offices. In the meantime, plot nineteen through plot fifty-eight carry their water from the cut." He turned so that he was speaking to all of them. "The Home Line took the kill column out of its book before I was born. We keep four. Was it closed. Who was hurt. What still works in the morning. What we tinned. That order. The districts let us stand in their potato beds for exactly as long as their pumps still run when we've gone, and not one day longer." He looked along the line of faces and stopped, very briefly, at Kellan's. "Seven. You're next. You've about half an hour."

---

Kiva used the half hour.

She went in through the gap as soon as Vane would let her, with the cloth under her arm and a twist of dead ash from the Kennel in her pocket, and she walked the plot the way Orla had taught her to walk the lane.

The tear hung over the middle of the potato bed. The earth beneath it had been dug that week and was as soft as cake. South of the bed stood the tarred shed, with its back to the west and the wind. The cinder path ran north and south along the shed's eastern side, firm as far as the shed's far corner, exactly as Agnes Roake had said, and soft beyond.

She stood in the lee of the shed and dropped a pinch.

It fell straight down. She took two steps north, past the corner, and dropped another, and the wind had it before it reached her knee.

"Here," she said. "On the path, against the east wall. The shed breaks the wind. It's the only place inside the tape that does."

Kellan had come in behind her. He was not looking at the path. He was looking at the potato bed, and he went and set one foot on it, and watched his boot sink to the laces.

"I can't Stride on that."

"No."

"It's the sand again. Worse." He drew his foot out. There was no complaint in it. He was reading a meter. "And I've three paces of cinder to come up on, from the shed's north corner, and it'll have to walk past the end of that corner to get onto your cloth. So it'll be turning as it comes. Pryce will have to hold its face square the whole way down."

"I can hold it," said Anwen. She was pale, and she had her list folded very small in one fist, and she had been watching four Torch-rank ward-holders for nine hours. "I've been looking at how they lean. They don't stand behind it. They stand under it. I think I've been holding mine like a tray as well."

"And I'm by the bean poles," said Perrin. He had sewn the missing weights into the Stonehand cloth himself, on Saturday night, with his mother's needle, and he had shown the stitching to everyone on the berm who would look. "With nobody to carry, I hope. I notice he hasn't given us a Gerald."

"He doesn't use a sandbag," said Kiva. "He uses us."

They all looked across the plot, to where Hollis Vane stood at the tape with his chalk.

"Roles," he said.

"Renn. Kill."

"Pryce. Hold."

"Cade. Carry."

"Rowan. Catch." She drew a breath. "Door declared: none."

Vane's chalk paused.

"None?"

"None. Dark hands. It's the only thing I'm certified for." She had the form in her coat and held it out. He read it at arm's length, and she saw his eyes stop on the signature.

"Dane," he said. "Orla Dane. She took me down my first culvert, when I was nineteen and knew everything." Something passed over the weathered face that was nearly an expression. "She fell in it." He handed the paper back. "That's the first declaration I've had today that I entirely believe. Places."

---

The husk stood up out of the blue at twenty-two minutes past four.

It came the way they came up the chute, in a heap, and it found its legs as it rose. It had a skin. Kiva had known that it would. She could not have said why. The faint bloom lay over its blunt front like frost on a turnip.

"Skin," she said.

"Seen," said Kellan, from the shed's north corner. His hands were open at his sides.

Anwen's ward came up. It was not flat, and it was not held out in front of her. She had her hands low and wide and her weight forward, and the silver leaned from her like a door she was holding shut with her shoulder.

The husk read it and came. It went into the soft earth of the bed to its hocks, as the others had, and plowed forward regardless, and its skinned face met the ward at the edge of the cinders with that thin glass-rim note.

"Seven paces," said Kiva. She was on her knees at the east hem of the cloth, with the bean poles at her back. "Six. It's on the path."

"Renn," said Hollis Vane, from the tape. He did not raise his voice. "You're down. Right leg, at the knee."

Nobody moved.

Kiva saw it go through Kellan like a current. He was the best blade in his year. He was standing with a clean line to the thing's shoulder, and he had spent a day and a night learning how to cut it. For one long instant she watched him not sit down.

Then he sat down. He did it all at once, on the cinders, with his right leg straight in front of him, and his face shut like a book.

"*Off!*" said Anwen. "Off! Off!"

The ward folded. Kiva was already rolling the cloth, pans inside it, because a cloth left on the ground was a cloth a husk would walk across. Perrin went past her at a heavy run.

"I can walk," said Kellan.

"You're down." Perrin had him by one arm and the belt. He bent his knees, and Kiva felt the ground take the weight as the still door opened under him, and Kellan Renn went up across those broad shoulders like a rolled carpet. "Down people don't have opinions. I've wanted to do this for two weeks. Keep your leg straight, you're meant to have broken it."

"Cade—"

"Quiet. You're a casualty. Casualties groan."

They went out through the gap in a line, Anwen first and Kiva last with the kit in her arms. Behind them, the husk had already lost them. It stood on the cinder path with its blind face swinging, and then it found the nearest of the four silver walls and went and leaned on it, patiently, like a cow at a gate.

Perrin set Kellan down on the grass outside the tape with a great deal more care than he had picked him up.

Hollis Vane was looking at his slate.

"Nine seconds," he said. "From *down* to *off*. I had a Second Division team this morning who took forty, and tried to finish it with a ward and a still-door and a prayer." He chalked a line. "Whose plan was that?"

"Hers," said Kellan, getting up. He nodded at Anwen. "She wrote it out last night. I told her what to put beside my name."

"Did you." Vane considered him. "Your leg's mended, Mr. Renn. I'd say you've until that thing wears a hole in my south corner, and she's been standing there since three. In."

---

They went in.

Kiva laid the cloth again, in the lee of the shed, squared against the tarred boards. She set out four tins and the spare. She put the wet salt where her right hand would find it without looking.

Then she knelt and put a pan in each hand, and Anwen raised her ward.

The husk came off the south wall as if it had been called. It came up the cinder path toward the brighter silver with its skin shining dully in the long light, and the ward rang, and Anwen began to walk backward, slowly, with her weight under it.

It would pass the end of the shed. It would pass Kiva. She had laid the cloth so that it must.

"Five paces," she said. "Four."

It was very much bigger out of doors. She did not know why that should be. In the Kennel there had been brick all round and a warden at a lever. Here there was grass, and a tin roof, and somebody's seedlings in trays, and a blind grey thing the size of a mastiff walking down a garden path toward her with nobody's hand on anything.

The Ember came up. She had a number in its way before it reached the door.

"Three. One line. Two paces. It's at the corner—"

It was at the corner. It drew level with the shed's north end, where Kellan stood flat against the tar with his fist down, and it did not so much as flicker toward him. It walked on. Its thick limb came down on the waxed canvas a forearm from her knee, and the cloth dented.

"On," said Kiva. "All four feet. It's on."

Anwen stopped. Her heels were at the south hem. She did not step off. She stood, with a husk's blind face pressed to her ward an arm's length from her own, and did not give it an inch.

Kellan left the corner.

He came up the three paces of cinder behind it, dark. Kiva watched his feet. The left came down. The right came down. His shoulder dropped. And she saw, because she was watching the part that moved least, the precise instant when ten years of habit reached for the light and did not get it.

His foot landed.

Then the blade lit.

It was a short hard blue-white line and it was already moving. It went down behind the edge of the skin, where the grey was only grey, and into the place where the red cord would have been, and it was dark again and running home before the husk had begun to turn.

The skin did not burst. It stood for a heartbeat by itself, a bright shallow curve like the lid of a pot, with nothing under it. Then it dropped straight down and broke on the canvas, and the rest of the husk poured after it, warm, in a heap, in the middle of the cloth.

"*Count!*"

"Dark," said Kiva. "One. Two."

"One. Two." Kellan was backing away up the path. Six paces. His hands were open. "Three."

Anwen's ward folded like a hand closing. She dropped to her knees at the south hem, and her fingers were already on the corner weights.

The heap lifted and settled. It was listening. The hand door rose cold and wild and Kiva gave it *four* and *five* and set the pan beneath the ash, and for the space of six counts it was exactly like the Kennel, and she thought, with a rush of something very like joy, *I can do this.*

She heard herself think it. She knew what came after that sort of thought.

"Six," she said, louder. "Seven."

The wind came round the shed.

It was not the wind itself. It was a curl of it, an eddy off the north corner, and it lifted the top of the heap in a thin grey veil that stood up off the canvas and began to lean toward the east.

"Corners!" said Kiva. "Up, all four, make a bag of it—Perrin! I need a wall. West side. Stand there. No. There."

"I have trained," said Perrin, planting himself between the shed corner and the cloth with his coat held open like a pair of wings, "my entire life. For this."

The veil sank. Anwen had two corners gathered in her fists and Kiva had the third under her knee. She panned out of the canvas as out of a sack. First tin at sixteen. "Seal check."

"Sound."

Second at twenty-seven. Her left hand was slow, and she let it be slow. The skin ash was in there. She could feel it, a few grains in every panful, already faintly curved. She did not stop to think about that. She had a number.

"Thirty," she said. "Thirty-one—"

"Seven." Hollis Vane's voice came across the plot, and there was an edge in it she had not heard all day. "There's a second one coming. It's early. It's not yours. Hold your catch."

Kiva looked up.

The blue over the potato bed was bulging.

---

It had been forty minutes between them since dawn. She had written it in the book thirteen times. This one had come in eleven.

It heaped out onto the dug earth and stood. It had no skin. It was plain and grey and it swung its blind front end in a slow half circle, reading.

There was nothing lit inside the tape. Anwen was dark. Kellan was dark. Perrin was a boy with his coat open. And so the husk's face went on turning until it found the nearest pattern there was, which was the silver wall on the east side of the hold and the ward-holder behind it, at the corner past the bean poles.

It started toward her.

A straight line from the tear to that corner ran across the potato bed and across the cinder path. It ran across the middle of Kiva's cloth.

"Thirty-three," said Kiva. Her voice had gone up. "Thirty-four."

She saw Kellan see it. He was six paces up the path, on the far side of the line, and he had only to raise his fist. A lit blade at six paces would be the brightest thing in that field. The husk would turn to him like a needle to north. He could meet it on firm cinder with his feet set, and it had no skin, and it would take him one breath.

And for that one breath there would be a lit Edge six paces from eight drams of open ash, with the wind behind it.

His right hand had started to come up.

"*Dark,*" said Kiva. She did not shout it. She said it straight at him. "Stay dark. Thirty-five. Kellan. Count."

His hand stopped at his hip.

It shook. She saw it shake.

"Thirty-six," said Kellan Renn, through his teeth. His hand opened. "Thirty-seven."

The husk came on across the bed. It was slow in the soft earth. Somewhere outside the tape a Home Line blade was running along the east side toward the corner, a woman with her coat flying, and she was not going to be in time to do anything about a cloth.

"Kiva." Perrin had dropped his coat. He stepped over the hem of the canvas, between her and the potato bed, and stood there with his back to her. "I'm standing in front of you now. You're being rescued. I said I'd like to be told. I thought you would too."

"Perrin, you can't plant, you'll print it—"

"I know I can't plant. I'm not planting." He sounded quite cheerful, and not at all steady. "I'm just a large boy in a field. Enjoy it. Thirty-eight."

"Thirty-eight," said Kiva. "Thirty-nine."

She put her head down and tinned. It was the hardest thing she had done all that month. Every part of her that had gone over a rail was shouting, and the still door was heaving under her knees with its whole weight, wanting to be good. She gave it a number. She pressed the third lid and listened for it.

The husk came out of the soft earth onto the cinders, and it did not slow, and it did not see him. It walked into Perrin Cade exactly as it would have walked into a gatepost.

He had no stance. He had only himself. It took him in the hip and the ribs with its shoulder, and he went sideways off his feet with a grunt like a struck drum, into the bean poles, which went down in a long clattering row.

But a gatepost bends a line. The husk's blind front slewed a pace to the south as it shouldered through him. One thick grey limb came down on the corner weight of the cloth, a hand's width from the gathered ash, and the next came down on cinder, and it was past.

"Forty-four. Seal check!"

"Sound," said Anwen. Her voice was shaking. Her thumb was not.

"Anwen. Take the left pan. My hand's too slow, I'll lose it."

Anwen took the left pan. They brought the last of it up between them, out of the folded canvas, grey and warm and still listening, and Kiva ran the lazy finger round the rim and wound the cord and pulled it through.

"Fifty-one. Sealed."

At the east corner of the hold, the silver wall went out like a pinched wick. The ward-holder there had dropped her hands and stepped back three long paces, and the walls to either side of her swung inward and met behind the husk, so that it stood outside the hold with nothing lit in front of it. In the dark she had left, a short red blade lit and fell, once, and ran home. The second husk came apart over a cloth already spread on the grass. A grey-haired woman dropped to her knees beside it, and Kiva, who had thought the warden quick, watched two pans move like a card trick.

"Fifty-nine," said Kiva. "Sixty."

The listening stopped.

She put the pan down. Her hands would not hold it any longer.

---

Perrin was sitting up among the bean poles with one hand pressed to his side.

"I'm alive," he said, before anyone could ask. "I was told to count and I counted. Somebody write that down. I lost my place at forty. I think it was the flying." He drew a careful breath and winced. "That's a rib. Maybe two. It didn't even look at me, Kiva. I've never been so thoroughly ignored."

Anwen was beside him before Kiva could get her legs under her. She laid her palms an inch above his ribs and shut her eyes, and her face went still and inward, the way it had gone over a sandbag in the dark. "Two," she said. "Cracked, not broken through. Nothing underneath. Your hands have split again." She opened her eyes. "You great fool."

"I'd like it said that I'm a great fool who got told first."

"It's said."

Kellan had come down the path. He stood at the edge of the cloth and looked at the four sealed tins in the carrier, and at the bare canvas, and then at the print of one thick foot on its corner weight.

"I'd have lit," he said.

"I know," said Kiva.

"I had my hand up. I'd decided." He was not looking at her. "It would have been a clean kill. It would have been the cleanest thing I did all day, and it would have printed the lot, or blown it into the next plot." He stopped. "You said *count*."

"It was all I could think of."

"It worked." He said it as if it annoyed him, and then, more slowly, as if it did not. "I heard a number and my mouth said the next one. I didn't know it would do that."

The white tape lifted. Hollis Vane ducked under it and came across the plot with his slate, and behind him came the grey-haired woman with a brass balance in a leather case exactly like Orla's.

"Bettan weighs on the bank," said Vane. "The graders will have their say tomorrow, when your tins have had a night in the cold. I like to know what I'm signing for today."

The woman called Bettan knelt and set the tins on the pan one by one. She did not hurry. She moved the little weights with a fingernail.

"Twenty," she said, "and eight-tenths."

Nobody spoke. Anwen's hand had closed on Kiva's sleeve.

"The mark's twenty," said Kellan, to no one.

Vane wrote. When he had finished, he turned the slate round and held it where the four of them could read it.

*T7. Abort 1, clean, 9 sec. Kill on 2nd entry. Catch sealed at 51. Nothing fouled. One hurt: Cade, ribs, by own choice, dark. Yield 20.8 on the bank, to grade.*

*Passed, subject to the tins.*

"I've four things to say, and then I've a tear to shut," he said. "It's stopped keeping time, and I don't put students in front of a clock I can't read. Cade. That was a stupid thing to do, and it's the only way that cloth was getting saved, and you're the first Carry I've had all year who looked after the one member of his team nobody tells him to. Get those ribs strapped. Pryce. You called *off* in nine seconds on a thing you'd planned for, and you held a skinned Handful's face for a slow count of four without stepping back. Mace has told me who you are. I'll tell him what I saw."

Anwen went scarlet to the hair.

"Renn." Vane took his time. "You're the best blade that has come through this gap today, and I've had fourteen. You sat down when you were told. You waited for your foot. And at the end you were one breath from fouling your own team's catch to show me a kill I never asked for, and a provisional Third on her knees said *no*, and you listened." His pale eyes were level. "Which of those do you suppose I'm going to write down?"

Kellan met them.

"All of it," he said.

"All of it. Good. I wouldn't have you otherwise." He turned to Kiva last. "Who taught you to go dark?"

"Orla Dane. And the warden. And him." She nodded at Kellan. "He hit me with a blade for a week."

"Then they did it properly." He glanced at the tins. "You called that floor from your knees with a live one walking at you. I've known chartered catchers who couldn't. What I can't make out is the tin record on your certificate. Six drams of dead ash and somebody swinging at you. Whose notion was that?"

"Hers. Orla's."

"It would be." He chalked something small in the corner of the slate. "I might steal it."

"There's one more thing." Kiva had the pencil in her hand already, for the tags, and she was afraid that if she did not say it now she would talk herself out of it. "The skin. When it broke. Some of that ash had something in it before I touched it, and nobody was lit, I looked. It's in all four tins, a little. It isn't a print." She made herself finish. "The warden had me say it three times and wrote it in the Kennel book. It feels curved."

Hollis Vane did not chalk that. He looked at her for rather a long time.

"Bettan," he said.

"I heard." The grey-haired woman had stopped with the balance half folded. "I had one off the Ferren hold, the fourteenth. With a skin. I thought my hands were tired."

Vane took the first of Kiva's tags out of her fingers. He turned it over, and on the back, in his quick slanting hand, he wrote a line and initialed it.

*Catcher reports skin ash "curved" inside the count, no lit door near. Second such report this month. H.V.*

"Put that one on top," he said, and gave it back.

---

The closing crew came down from the Brickfields road at half past five, six people with a handcart, and Hollis Vane went to meet them.

There would be no fifteenth floor. Team Eleven had gone up the berm steps a quarter of an hour before, with their kit unopened and their Wren House reserve walking a little behind the other three. Vane had told her to her face that her seat would keep until the next tear. She had nodded, and gone on looking at the potato bed. Anwen had watched them to the top of the steps, and she had not taken out her list.

Selka Dray had gone before that. She had stood at the tape with her arms folded until Bettan called the weight, and then she had walked up to the road by herself, and Kiva did not think that twenty and eight-tenths had changed her mind about anything.

Kiva sat on the bank of the cut with her boots off and her feet in the cold brown water. She had written the four tags. *Sag Line, plot 19. The 28th. Team Seven. One Handful, skinned. Sealed at 16, 27, 44, 51.* And on each, on the line where the form asked for it, a thing she had never before had cause to write.

*Catcher: K. Rowan.*

It was only a paper tag on a tin. It would go in a cold room overnight, and tomorrow a grader would cut the cord and throw it in a basket. But it was the first time her name had been written on anything in the Compact without *unclassified* or *provisional* or *failed* somewhere near it.

Mara came down the berm steps with her tool roll under her arm.

She did not say that she had watched. She had told Kiva, on a tram stop a fortnight ago, that she would never be able to sit in a gallery, and Kiva had believed her. But the mast stood at the top of the berm with the whole of plot nineteen laid out below it like a map, and Mara had been at the foot of that mast with a drum roll for eleven hours, and there was cinder dust on her knees where she had knelt, at some point, at the edge of the road.

She sat down on the bank. After a moment she unlaced her own boots.

"You said *dark*," she said.

"Twice."

"I heard it from up there. Both times." Mara put her feet in the water beside Kiva's and hissed through her teeth at the cold. "I heard that boy say he was going to stand in front of you. I thought, *that's Cade's lad, he'll plant, he'll ruin it.* And then he didn't." She was quiet. "I nearly came down the bank. I had one foot over the edge of the road."

"What stopped you?"

"You were counting." Mara looked at the brown water. "I could hear you. Thirty-eight, thirty-nine. And I thought, if she can count, she doesn't need me shouting about quench handles."

Kiva leaned against her. She had not done that since she was small. Her aunt's shoulder was thin and hard and smelled of machine oil, and it did not move.

On the far side of the white tape, the six people from Brickfields had made a ring around the tear. She could not see what they did. She felt it, a slow tightening in the back of her neck, as if the thumb that had rested there all day were being lifted, a little at a time.

Agnes Roake was standing among her fallen bean poles with her hands on her hips. Perrin, strapped round the middle with somebody's scarf, was trying to stand one of them up again with his elbows.

"Leave it, you daft lump," Kiva heard her say. "You'll do yourself a mischief. I've a nephew."

"I knocked them down. I'd like to put them up. It's the only thing I've knocked down all month that I can."

"Then hold that one straight and stop talking."

The blue over the potato bed went grey, and then it was only air.

Kiva put her hand to her chest, out of habit. Through her shirt the disk lay where it always lay. It was cold. She found that she had not expected it to be anything else, and that today, for once, the cold did not feel like a verdict.

She had opened nothing at all. She had four tins.
