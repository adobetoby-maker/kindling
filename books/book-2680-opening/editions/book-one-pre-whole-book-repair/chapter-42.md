# Chapter 42 — Kiva: The Ninth Post

Healer Aske saw the hand on Friday at seven, and for the first time since the spring he did not take out the pin.

"There's no use my pricking those two," he said. "I know what they'll say. I'll look at the rest." He turned the hand over under the lamp, and pressed the palm, and the root of the thumb, and each of the last two fingers in turn. "When. When. When. Good. That's a hand with eight parts and it's had a fortnight, all but a day."

"So I'm passed."

"For what?" He let go. "You're passed to catch. You always were. You're passed for one door at a time, dark hands, from this morning; a step, a stance, a ward for a tap. I'm told that you test the ground before you'll stand on it. I approve of that." He took a fresh card, and wrote. "From tomorrow, the first, the fortnight's up. So I've to write something about the other, and I've thought about what."

He wrote for some time. Then he read it to her, as he read everything.

"*A crossing, one, in seven days, as before. The longer thing: not on my card. If somebody with a slate means to direct it, he sends for me and I say the price to his face, before, with the candidate listening. I'll not have it read off a card that I wrote in a quiet room.*" He capped the pen. "I don't know what the price is now. That's the truth of it. The two fingers have nothing left to lose. It took them first because they'd been hurt first. I don't know what it takes second."

"You could guess."

"I could. I shan't, at seven in the morning, to a girl who'd write it in a book." He gave her the card. "I'd as soon that nobody sent for me. I've no curiosity in the matter whatever."

She put the card in her tunic, with the other.

---

Mara had forty corks in a string bag, and she went up Stopgate with them like a woman delivering eggs.

It was a clear cold morning. The hill was quieter than it ought to have been. The two dry drums had six hundred households on their boxes, most of them in the streets behind. On the hill itself it was the middle stretch, and one could tell which houses by the windows, which had the dim brownish look of lamps turned down to save a can. A cart from the casualty reserve stood at the rail of the twenty-sixth drum house with its tailboard down.

"Hold that," said Mara.

It was the slate. Kiva held it against her chest with her left forearm and chalked with her right.

They did it the same way at each grating. Mara knelt in the road between the tram rails, and lowered a cork on a measured cord until it floated, and tied the cord off to the bars. Then she let down the lamp and scratched a line on the wet brick with a nail on a stick, at the place to which the water rose, and another at the place to which it fell.

"Twenty-five. Rise and fall, two fingers and a half."

Kiva chalked it.

"It was two on Tuesday."

"So it was. Write the Tuesday figure beside it, in a ring, and don't join them with anything. I'll join them when I've a third."

They went on up. At twenty-six, under a dry drum, the water rose and fell by two fingers. At twenty-seven it was one and a half. A tram went by them, clanging, and the motorman leaned out to look at a Grade Two operator on her knees in his road.

"I'd a thought in the night," said Mara, at the twenty-eighth. She was a little out of breath. "I'll tell it you, since you'll not make more of it than it is. If I'm right, and the water's doing it, then every one of these holes is a place where what's underneath can get at what's on top. I don't like a hole. I shall ask Rusk for planks."

"For forty gratings?"

"For every one on this hill, and sandbags to weight them. It'll flood the gutters if it rains. I'd sooner have wet boots." She let the cork down. "A finger. And a bit. What did your flagstone say on Thursday?"

"That something was pushing. Very small."

"It's got a figure now. I'm obliged to it."

Twenty-nine was near the top, where the houses grew taller and had brass on their doors. Half a finger. Above that there was only the last grating, at the very head of the street, and beyond it the wall.

Kiva had come up that hill every morning for six months and had never properly looked at where it ended. Stopgate did not turn, or open into a square. It simply stopped, against thirty feet of old dark brick. The tram line bent away to the east along the foot of the wall. In the middle of the wall was a cart gate of black oak, with a wicket in it, and over the gate a stone with the School's device, which was a line with a kink in it.

"A hair," said Mara, at the last grating. "I can see that it moves. I couldn't put a rule to it." She got up, with a hand on the rail. "Now. By the Works' plan the main goes on under that gate, straight as a pipe, across the bottom yard and up the School's hill. There's a rain gully in the yard that sits right over it. I want a cork in that gully."

The wicket was open. There was a short woman standing in it with a ring of keys at her belt.

"I thought you'd be up," said the keeper, Ansty. "I've had the kettle on since eight. You'll want the gully by the cart shed. Mind the trunks."

There were a great many trunks.

The bottom yard was a cobbled square, thirty paces each way, and that morning it was full of first-years going home. They sat on boxes and stood about in knots, in their walking coats, with the look of people who have been told that they are not in trouble. A carrier's dray was being loaded at the gate.

Kiva knew the yard as she knew her own stair. On the east side stood the core house, square and windowless, with its one iron door and the hum coming off it like warmth off a stove. The Low Lane was built against its south wall. Behind it, to the north, was the long slate roof of the New Lane. On the west side there was only a low wall with a gap in it, and through the gap one could see packed dirt, and a shed, and the end of a row of black posts.

"There," said Ansty.

The gully was an iron grid in the cobbles, ten paces from the core house wall. Mara knelt at it. She let the cork down.

She let it down a long way.

"That's dry," she said.

"It's a rain gully. It's dry when it isn't raining."

"It's on the main. There ought to be a foot of water under it, summer and winter, and that water ought to be moving a hair." Mara drew the cork up. It was not so much as damp. She looked at it, and then at the plan, and then at the black gate behind her, through which a thing that rose and fell was supposed to have come. "Mrs. Ansty. Where does the water in this yard go?"

"Down that. And then away."

"Away where?"

"I've never asked it," said Ansty. "I keep a core. I'm told there's a man at the Works who keeps drains."

Kiva had left them. She had the slate under her arm, and nothing to chalk.

She had not meant to walk anywhere in particular. She found that she had gone through the gap in the low wall and was standing at the east end of the Old Yard, in daylight, which she had hardly ever done.

It looked smaller by day. It was a long strip of packed ground under the bottom wall, forty paces from the gap to the sheds at the far end, with two rows of black posts down it. Somebody's scraped line was there still. So was the cross, four paces beyond it, scratched over and over in the dirt.

And the last post of the south row leaned over the cross, as it always had.

"Why's that one crooked?"

Kiva looked round.

It was a child. She was perhaps thirteen, small and sandy, with a plait and a junior's grey smock, and she was carrying a quench box in both arms that was plainly not hers. She had the fixed, careful look of somebody who has been Kindled since the spring and has not yet set anything on fire.

"I'm to take this to the shed," said the child. "They're sending the first-years home and nobody's told the juniors anything. I've been coming to this yard since the fifth month. All the others are straight. That one isn't. I asked Miss Dane, and she said it wasn't in the manual anymore."

"She says that to everybody."

"Do you know?"

Kiva considered the post. It was black, and split down one side, and bound with two iron bands. It stood a full pace out of its row, and it leaned into the lane: to the left, if one stood at the sheds and looked along the row.

She had put her hand on it when she was thirteen. She had come down beside it, with nothing open, and paid a quarter of a dram each time. She had never once been able to answer that question.

"No," she said. "I don't. I've wondered for four years."

"Oh." The child shifted the box. She seemed, if anything, relieved. "I thought it was only that I was new."

She went on down the lane toward the sheds. Kiva watched her go round the leaning post without looking at it, as everybody did, by a little sideways step that she had plainly learned in her first week.

Pike had them in the Graywater hall yard that evening, and it was the worst hour that Kiva had spent on a sheet since the spring.

The new one had come up from the ash office at four. It was stiff, and it smelled of wax, and Pru had carried it the whole length of the Brickfields road on her shoulder rather than put it on Perrin's handcart.

"Finch catches," said Pike. "Rowan, you're a corner."

"Which?"

"That's hers to say. You'll have noticed that it's her cloth."

Pru stood in the middle of the yard with the sheet at her feet and her round face perfectly white.

"North-east," she said. "Please. I mean, north-east. Rowan."

Kiva went to the north-east corner. She knelt, and took the hem.

It was a strange place to be. She had been the one in the middle since the fifth month. She had set the mark and thrown the cloth and called the count, and four people had done as she said. Now she had a lead weight in her right hand and the heel of her left on a hem, and a first-year behind her was reading the ground.

"Cade's the rung," said Pike. "Gerald, I should say. Off the hoist, from the height of a man. When he lands, he's sixty drams and he's open, and you've sixty counts. Finch."

"Mark," said Pru. Her voice shook. "Here. A pace south, for the wind. There isn't any wind. I'm saying it for practice." She threw the sheet.

It did not lie flat. One corner had folded under.

"Again," said Pike.

She threw it nine times. On the ninth it lay like a tablecloth, and Perrin let Gerald go from the hoist, and a hundredweight of sand hit the middle of the canvas with a sound like a dropped mattress.

"*Dark!*" said Pru. It came out as a squeak. "One! Corners!"

Kiva hauled.

She did it as she had done it a hundred times. She brought her corner in toward the heap, low, with her right hand, and she felt for the slack of the hem along her left side to tell her when the next corner was coming up to meet her.

There was no slack. There was nothing there at all. Her two fingers lay on the canvas and told her that there was canvas.

"*Rowan!*" That was Pru. "Your left! It's proud! It's standing up like a tent!"

She looked. Along her left side the hem had lifted off the ground in a long loose fold, a hand high, where a corner that had not waited for its neighbor had pulled the cloth up. Anything open would have run out under it.

"That's a lost rung," said Pike. He did not raise his voice. "Not hers. Yours. What happened?"

"I felt for the hem. On the left."

"With what?"

Kiva did not answer.

"With what, Rowan?"

"With two fingers that can't tell me there's a hem."

"So you asked a thing of a hand that it hasn't got, and you didn't say so to your catcher, and she found it out with the bag open." Pike squatted down beside her, heavily. He did not look angry. He looked like a man checking a knot. "I told you that you'd hate it for a year. This is what the year's made of. You knew about the cord, since that's the one everybody's watching. What else is there? Say them. All of them. To her, not to me."

It was not a comfortable minute. She knelt in a hall yard, in front of four people and a sandbag, and went through her own left hand like a clerk going through a drawer.

"The cord. All three turns. That's yours already." She made herself look at Pru and not at the ground. "The slack of a hem on my left side. I can't feel it come. So I want a right-hand corner, every time, where the slack's on my good side. Or somebody on my left who calls it to me."

"North-west, then," said Pru. "Or south-east. I'll write it on my hand."

"Tin lids. The cold-room kind, with the lip. I can't find the lip." She thought. "The pin of a seal. Whether wax has gone off. I'd have to look, and at night I can't."

"Wax is mine," said Anwen. She was sitting on the handcart with the green book shut on her knee. "I've done wax since I was eight. It's laundry."

"And the count of a thing." Kiva had not known that she was going to say it. "If a thing's keeping time, a hem or a cord, I used to feel it in both hands and take the middle of the two. I've only the one now that hears properly. I got the hold's breath at the Pit because Brask called it for me. I'd not have had it by myself."

Pike's square head came up a little.

"That's the one I was waiting for," he said. "I'd not have thought you'd find it tonight. Who has that?"

"I don't know. Nobody. Brask, if he's there."

"*If he's there* isn't a name. Find one. Before you want it." He stood. "Finch. Throw it again. Rowan, south-east. And this time, when you want her corner in, you tell her so. You've a voice. I've heard it called shrill."

"I'm not shrill," said Pru. "I'm carrying."

They threw it until the lamp was lit. It grew a little less dreadful. On the fourteenth, Pru said "Rowan, in a hand" in the tone in which one asks for the salt, and Kiva brought her corner in a hand. The neck closed. Pru took the cord at her elbow and made three turns, and looked at the third, and did not think about it.

"Sealed," she said. "Fifty-one." Then she sat down on the stiff new canvas and put her face in her hands.

"That's a catcher's count," said Pike. "On her own cloth. Rowan was a corner in it and did as she was bid." He rolled the cord. "I'd not have wagered a penny on either of those in the fifth month."

Kiva's knees ached. She had been corrected eleven times by a girl who had once run from a spaniel.

She found, somewhat to her surprise, that she could bear it. It was a good deal like being a still door. One was not the thing that was happening. One was only what it stood on.

Hollis Vane walked his plan on Saturday night, and he began it at ten with every lamp on Stopgate turned down.

"I'll say the reason for the dark," he said. He had the wardens at the foot of the hill, eleven of them with their handbells, and behind them the fours. "If a tear opens on this street, I'll want one thing lit on it, and that's a gate. Everything else that's bright is a thing for it to walk to. So you'll learn the road with your feet. There's a white tape up the east curb. There'll be a voice at every corner."

There were sixty-two houses on the hill itself. The rest of the dry drums' six hundred households lay in the streets to either side, and had roads of their own. He had the list. Fourteen had somebody in them who could not walk. For tonight a Circuit dummy lay in each of those fourteen beds, by leave of the household, and in nine of the households the family had stayed up to watch.

"The bottom forty go down. To the Brickfields road, and the tram shed, where the Wren House has its carts. The top twenty-two can't come down past whatever's in the road. They go up. Through the School's gate, across the yard, along the old practice lane under the wall, and out by the side gate to the square." He looked at the bursar. "I've been shown it by daylight. I'm told that it's the shortest way off that hill that doesn't cross a drain."

"It doesn't cross the main at all," said Mara. She was there for Rusk, with the plan. "If the main's where it's drawn."

"Is it?"

"Ask me tomorrow."

The gate stood at the head of the street, a hundred paces below the wall, silver from curb to curb. It was Doran Mace's. He had brought six of the night van's wards up from the Brickfields road and four students to stand among them, and he walked behind the line with his board under his arm, looking at boots.

Anwen was at the end of it.

Kiva saw that from where she stood. It was the east end, with the curb on one side of her and Tibb on the other, and nothing beyond her but an area railing. She had her hands low and her face shut, and the silver between them did not flicker.

"Rowan," said Vane. "You're recovery. So you've no litter and no wall. I've a use for somebody who reads ground. You'll stand in the School's yard, at the turn, and tell me what goes wrong there. Not mend it. Tell me."

"Yes, sir."

"Finch has the lamp, shuttered. Bexley's on the School's mast, on the core house roof, with his eyes on a drum and not on a bell. If that bell rings and he doesn't shout, it's the mark and not the drum, and nobody's to stop walking." He raised his voice. "*Carry! On the wardens' bells! Go!*"

Eleven handbells went off down the hill like a dropped tray.

She stood in the gap of the low wall, with Pru beside her, and listened to a street being emptied in the dark.

It was a queer, slow, shuffling noise. There were doors, and feet on steps, and a great many people saying *mind* to one another. Somewhere a child was laughing, since nobody had told it not to. A warden's voice went along the top of the hill, cracked and steady. *Odd numbers, by the tape. Evens, wait for your bell.*

The first litter came through the black gate at a trot.

It was Pettit at the head and a Stonehand first-year at the foot, and a dummy between them in a blanket. They had been told the road. Across the cobbles, through the gap, along the lane.

"Through here," said Kiva. "Lane's ahead. Posts both sides."

"I've got it," said Pettit.

He had not got it.

He went through the gap fast, with his head down, and he did what anybody does who is carrying a weight along a wall in the dark. He kept to the wall. The south row of posts stood in its line, a yard out from the brick, and he meant to run along the lane side of it as a man runs along a fence.

The nearest post of that row was not in the line. It was the last of the row, as the lane was counted from the sheds, and so the first that anybody met from the gap. It stood a pace out, eight paces in, leaning into the road at the height of a man's hip.

There was a sound like an axe going into a log.

"*Down!*" said Pettit, from the ground, in a voice of pure surprise. The dummy had gone over his head. The first-year at the foot had sat down on it. "Who put a post there? There's a post in the road!"

"It's always been there," said Pru.

"Then it's always been in the road!"

He had it on the shin. He got up, swearing in a careful undertone like a boy who has been taught not to, and they gathered the dummy and went on, limping. Kiva counted. It had cost them forty counts.

"That's one," she said.

The second litter was two third-years of the School, who had walked past that post for three years. They went round it without a check. The third was Old Four's.

Lowen Brask had the head of it. He did not trot. He came through the gap at his long, even walk, with the dummy lying in the poles as though it were asleep, and he stopped.

He stopped dead, two paces short of the post, and stood there.

"There's something here," he said, to the dark. "I can hear the wall's come nearer. Wace, a pace right. Slowly." They went round. "Rowan. Is that yours to tell?"

"It's a post. It's out of its row. It leans."

"I know which it is. I've fought in this yard." He was already past. "I'd not thought of it with a litter. Somebody ought to say so at the gap."

"I did say posts."

"You said posts both sides. That's true of all of them." His voice came back along the lane, quite without malice. "It's the one that isn't like the others that wants saying."

The fourth litter hit it.

It was two Wren House second-years with a real person, an old man from number fifty-eight who had declined to be a dummy and insisted on being carried in his own chair. He was not hurt. The chair's leg was. He made a great deal of noise about it, and a bird in a cage on his knee made more.

"Two," said Kiva. She had her book out, on her forearm, and she was writing in it by the edge of Pru's shutter. "Forty counts, and then about ninety. That's two in four, and the two that didn't were people who knew."

"You could stand at it," said Pru. "With the lamp."

"He said nothing lit."

"Then you could just stand at it. And say."

It was what she wanted to do. It was exactly what she had done at the Pit, and on a street, and in the lane on her first morning. She would plant herself where the trouble was and call it for everybody, in a carrying voice, until the last litter was through. She had her mouth open to say *I'll take it.*

*Tell me what goes wrong. Not mend it.*

She shut her mouth.

It was not that she could not do it. It was that if she stood at that post, then the post was hers, and on the night that mattered she would be somewhere else. She would be four paces off with a hand gone, as she had told a kitchen table on Wednesday, and there would be a post in the road and nobody at it.

"No," she said slowly. "It's not a thing for one person to stand at. It's a thing for whoever's carrying to say."

The fifth litter was Perrin's.

She heard him before she saw him. He was telling the dummy that it was being rescued.

"Perrin! Stop at the gap!"

He stopped. He had Hendry Moss of his own Hall behind him, a boy with a neck as thick as his head, and they stood there in the dark with the poles on their shoulders.

"There's a post," said Kiva. "The last of the south row. It's a pace out, into the lane, on your left as you go in. I'm not going to stand at it. I want you to call it. Aloud, before you're through the gap. *Post, left. Keep right.* And then I want Moss to say it back."

"Post, left. Keep right," said Perrin obediently.

"Post left, keep right," said Moss.

"And when you're past it, you say it over your shoulder. To whoever's behind. Whether you can see them or not."

"What if there's nobody behind?"

"Then you've told a wall, and it's cost you nothing."

Perrin thought about that.

"It's a lane call," he said. "It's *Yours* and *Mine.* It's only that it's for a post." He hitched the poles. "I call that rather good. *Post, left! Keep right!*"

They went through. They did not touch it. She heard him bawl it backward down the lane, in the voice that he had been saving since the spring, and a moment later she heard somebody at the black gate take it up who had not been told to. *Post left. Keep right.*

By the ninth litter it was coming up the hill ahead of the bearers. By the fourteenth a warden with a handbell was calling it at the wicket, and had no idea why.

She wrote that down. *Told one team. The call went on by itself.*

Then the bell rang.

It was the School's own, on the core house roof, and it rang right over their heads, two and two and two, as loud as a dray on cobbles. Every foot in the yard stopped.

It lasted perhaps three seconds. There was a litter halted in the gateway, and a warden with her bell held up and her mouth open, and forty people on a dark hill all turning their faces the same way.

"*It's the mark!*" That was Noa. His thin cracked voice came down off the roof. "*It's the mark, not the drum! I've got the needle! Walk! Go on walking!*"

"*Walk!*" said Vane, from somewhere below. "*You heard the roof! That's what you've a roof for!*"

They walked.

It was done by twenty past eleven. Vane read the time off his watch at the black gate, with the wardens round him and the fours sitting on the cobbles.

"An hour and twenty minutes. For sixty-two houses, fourteen litters and a school, with nothing chasing us." He did not sound displeased. He did not sound pleased. "I'd want it in forty. We'll walk it again on Tuesday. Now. What went wrong? Bottom of the hill first."

They told him. A warden had rung evens before odds. The tape had come off the curb at number thirty. Twenty-Eight's ward had heard a join in Mace's gate and had said so, and had been right.

"The yard," said Vane. "Rowan."

"A post," said Kiva. She stood up. "The last of the south row in the old lane. It's a pace out of line, and it leans into the road at hip height. Two litters in the first four struck it. Forty counts and ninety. The two that missed it were people who'd known it for years, so it wasn't that they saw it." She read it off the page. "Brask stopped short of it and felt his way. Then I had one team call it at the gap and call it back over the shoulder, and after that nobody struck it. I didn't stand at it. I thought I shouldn't be there to."

"A post," said Vane.

"Yes, sir."

"In the middle of my casualty road." He turned his head. "Bursar. What is it doing there?"

The thin man in the gown had been sitting on a trunk. He got up.

"It is retained," he said, "for ceremonial continuity. That's the manual's phrase. It's the oldest thing in the School. It's said to be older than the School."

"Is it holding anything up?"

"I shouldn't think so."

"Then it can be ceremonial somewhere that I'm not carrying an old man in a chair. I'll have it out by Tuesday. I've a man with a saw."

"No," said Orla Dane.

She had been in the yard the whole night, on her crate, by the first shed, where she had sat for one-and-twenty years. Nobody had asked her for anything. She had her stick between her boots, and she did not raise her voice.

"You'll not put a saw to that, Hollis. Not on a bursar's word, nor on mine."

"Give me a reason."

"I haven't one." The crooked face was grave. "That's what I've to say, and I've said it to every first-year who ever asked. There used to be one. It was printed. It was taken out of the manual seventy-seven years ago by a committee, and something civil was put in its place, and nobody now living at this School has read what was there before." She shifted the stick. "I've sat under that post since before you had a slate. I've wondered what it was for most nights. I've never once thought that the answer was *nothing.*"

"That's not a reason. That's a feeling about one."

"It is. I'm not a girl; I know the difference." Orla looked along the fours until she found the one she wanted. "You've a ledger in your hall that's older than this School. You found a hand in the back of it that had been tidied away, and you put it back, and I thought the better of you. I'm asking you to look once more before you cut. If there's nothing, I'll hold the lamp for your man myself."

Vane was silent.

He was a watch officer. He had stood on the cinders of the Pit and read a column aloud that somebody had ruled out of a book. Kiva saw him weigh the one thing against the other, a post and a litter, and she saw that he did not enjoy it.

"Tuesday," he said. "Noon. It's in the road, Dane, and a girl of seventeen has just shown me how to get forty people round it with their voices, which I'll grant you is more than the saw would have taught them. That buys it three days. Bring me why it's there, or it comes out." He put his watch away. "And I'll look in my own. I'd be a poor sort of man to ask it of your shelf and not of mine."

"Bexley," said Orla.

Noa had come down off the roof. He had a roll of tape under his arm and his pencil behind his ear, and he was already looking at the post as he looked at a notch.

"The superseded stacks," he said. "Three flights down. I know the stair. The Reader shut at six." He swallowed. "She opens at eight on a Monday. I shall be on the step."

It rained a little before dawn, and Mara was on the hill by seven on Sunday to see what the rain had done to her corks.

It had done nothing. A wet drain breathed exactly as a dry one had. She read them from the bottom, with Kiva chalking, and the slate filled slowly with pairs of figures, a Friday and a Sunday, each in its ring.

"Twenty-five. Two and a half, and two and a half. Six-and-twenty, two, and two. Seven, one and a half, and a little over." She knelt at the next. She was a long time. "Eight-and-twenty. It was a finger and a bit. It's a finger and a half."

"It's growing."

"It's growing at the top of the hill and standing still at the bottom. Like a tide making up a creek." Mara sat back on her heels in the road. "Tomorrow's the third. If that drum does what I wrote down, I shall be believed, and I find that I've no stomach for it."

They went on. Nine-and-twenty was a full finger. The last grating, under the wall, was more than a hair now. One could have put a rule to it.

Ansty let them in at the wicket. The yard was empty and wet, and the trunks were gone.

Mara went straight to the gully by the cart shed. She let the cork down, and drew it up, and turned it over in her fingers.

"Wet," she said. "From the rain. There's an inch of rainwater in the bottom of that, lying as flat as a plate." She did not get up. "It isn't moving. Not a hair. I've a main that breathes a rule's width on one side of that gate, and ten paces inside it I've a puddle."

"Then it isn't under here."

"It's drawn under here." She had the plan out. She laid it on the wet cobbles and put her knee on a corner of it. "There. A blue line, from the gate, north, across this yard, past the core house, and on up the hill. It's as straight as if somebody had done it with a ruler."

Kiva looked at the line. It was very neat. It had a small note beside it, in a clerk's round hand.

"*Recopied from the worn sheet, 2553,*" she read.

"So it was done with a ruler." Mara's voice was quite level. "A clerk had a sheet that was coming to pieces, and he copied it fair, and where the old line wandered he made it sensible. I've done that reconciling for eleven years. I know that hand. It's the hand of a man who thinks that he's being helpful."

She shut her eyes for a moment.

"Somebody's tidied my drain," she said.

Kiva had her palm on the cobbles already.

There was nothing. There was wet stone, and under it the dead flat silence of ground with no hollow in it. She moved a pace toward the gate, and knelt again. Nothing. A pace west. Nothing.

She worked along the inside of the bottom wall. She did it on her knees, in the wet, with her right palm and the heel of her left, as a person feels along a shelf in the dark for a thing that has rolled. Mara watched her and did not say a word.

At the gap in the low wall she felt it.

It was very small. It was the least thing that she had ever called a feeling. It came up through the packed dirt on the far side of the gap, slow and even, and it was not coming from the north.

She went through on her knees. She followed it. It was like following the warmth of a pipe along a wall. It ran west from the gate, close under the brick, and it grew a little as she went, and then, eight paces into the Old Yard, it stopped being ahead of her.

It was underneath. It was directly underneath, and stronger than anywhere, and beyond that place it went off at a slant, along the lane, toward the sheds.

She took her hands off the ground and looked up.

There was a cross in the dirt in front of her knees, scratched over and over. There was a black post beside it, split, with two iron bands.

"It's here," she said. Her voice came out oddly. "It doesn't go north. It comes in under the gate and turns. It runs along under the wall to here, and then it goes off down the lane, on the south side, about where the posts are." She sat back. "I can't weigh it. It's what comes through dirt. You'd have to dig."

"I can't dig up a school on a palm."

"No."

"I can't dig it up on a cork, either, since I've no hole to put a cork down." Mara had come to the gap. She stood looking at the lane, and the two rows of posts, and the one at the end that was a pace out of its line. "There'll be a sheet older than that plan. There always is. Somebody drew the worn one before it was worn."

"Noa's at the Library tomorrow at eight. About the post."

"Then he may ask about a drain while he's there. It's the same stair."

She went back to her gully, and her puddle, to write down what she had not found.

Kiva stayed where she was.

She had come down on that cross again and again since the summer. She had stood on it in the still door, with the whole of the Old Yard under one heel, and felt the ground take her. It had never boomed. It had never been anything but ground. Whatever was down there was a long way down, and bricked over, and it had been there for longer than anybody had kept the reason.

She looked at the post.

It leaned out over the lane. She had always thought of it as leaning away from its row, like a tooth gone bad. A child with a plait had gone round it on Friday by a little sideways step, without looking, and so had every student who had ever used that yard.

It struck her now, for the first time, that it might not be leaning away from anything. It might be leaning toward.
