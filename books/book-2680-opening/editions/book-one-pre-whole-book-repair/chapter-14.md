# Chapter 14 — Kiva: A Handful, Less a Pinch

The Wren House graded ash in a long north-facing room with no lamps in it.

Kiva stood in the doorway on Monday afternoon with the rest of Team Seven, and it took her a moment to understand what was missing. There was not a cell in the place, no lamp, no meter with a lit dial, no humming box on any wall. The scales were brass and the light was daylight.

"Nothing worked within thirty paces," said Kellan quietly. "It's the same on the Halden floor. My mother's bench has nine cups. This one's got seven."

He was looking at the room the way Perrin looked at a meat bun. He had spoken very little since the Sag Line. But he had been at the Wren House gate a quarter of an hour before any of them, and when the grader came down the row with their carrier in her hands, he stood up straight, as he would for a master.

Anwen had not taken out her list. *Passed, subject to the tins,* the Home Line officer had written, and she had carried the second half of that sentence since Sunday like a stone in her boot. If one tin had taken a print in the night, or if a pinch of it would not hold a tune to sixty, there was no license, and no Graywater, and Kellan was back at the bottom of a call.

The grader was a brisk, freckled woman called Halloran, with a jeweler's glass on a cord round her neck and her sleeves pinned back to the elbow. She set the four tins on the slate, glanced at the tags, and stopped at the top one. She turned it over and read the back.

"Which of you is the catcher?"

"I am."

"Are you, now." Halloran looked at her a moment longer than was comfortable. "We'll come to that. First things first. They've had their night. Put a hand on them, if you like. I always let a team do that."

Kiva laid her palm on each lid in turn. They were cold, as cold as the slate, and through the steel she felt nothing at all, no heat and no hum and no thread. It was only ash, settled and quiet and nobody's.

She had not known how tightly she was braced until it let go.

"Not printed," said Halloran. "No. I'd have had you in the salt room at eight if they were. Now. There are three things I'm paid to tell you, and people are forever mixing them up, so I'll do them one at a time."

She cut the cords. She emptied the four tins, one after another, into a wide brass pan on the long arm of the scale, and the ash went in like grey water. She slid the weight along the beam with one finger until it hung level.

"Yield. That's how much. Twenty drams and eight-tenths, and your Home Line weigher on the bank was dead right, as they generally are. The book gives a Handful twenty-four, so you left three and a bit in a potato bed." She wrote it on a card. "Over the mark."

"Next?" said Kellan.

"Grade. That's how clean, and how steady." She took a pinch from the pan and dropped it into one of the seven small cups, which sat in a ring of bare copper wire. Then she laid two fingers on the wire, and Kiva felt the hand door stir, far off, and lie down again. It was the smallest tune she had ever felt anyone lay, a single plain shape no bigger than a thimble. "I put a test pattern on it and let go, and I count how long it holds true before it starts to wander. First grade holds past a hundred. Second past sixty. Under that it's Third, and under thirty it's road-lamp ash." Her lips moved. The room was very still, and Anwen's hand had shut on the edge of the slate bench. "Fifty-nine," said Halloran aloud, as if she knew exactly which number they were waiting for. "Sixty." The hand came open. She counted on under her breath, and then aloud again. "Seventy-nine. Eighty. Eighty-one, and there it goes. Second."

"What took it off First?" said Kellan. He asked it the way another boy might have asked the score of a match.

"Cinder. Look." She held out the jeweler's glass. Through it, the pinch in the cup was a field of soft grey boulders, and among them lay three or four hard black glittering ones. "You tinned a little of somebody's garden path along with your husk. It always happens out of doors, and chartered catchers have given a week's pay for a Second off a windy bank." She took the glass back.

Perrin, who was strapped so tightly round the ribs that he had to turn his whole body to look at anything, said, "And the third thing?"

"The third thing is why I asked for the catcher."

---

She brought it from a cupboard, in a wooden case lined with green cloth.

It was a brass frame about the length of Kiva's forearm, and across it were strung nine reeds of thin bright metal, each a little shorter than the last, like the teeth of a comb. Under the reeds lay a narrow plate of polished steel.

"The comb," said Halloran. "Resonance. This is the one nobody outside this building understands, so listen." She set it on the slate. "Every husk was shaped by something. I don't mean somebody. I mean that whatever pressed it had a lean to it, and sometimes a little of that lean stays in the ash after the shape's gone. Each of these reeds is cut to answer one family of pattern. An edge. A stride. A ward. A healer's read. A stance. Four others you won't have met." She ran her thumbnail lightly down them, and they chimed in a falling scale. "Put ash on the plate, and if it has a lean, the reed that matches it will sing. We write that down as the resonance. It tells a tuner which kinds of work that ash will take to, and which it'll fight."

"And a Handful?" said Anwen.

"A Handful doesn't sing. That's what *plain* means. Handful ash is plain, and has been for as long as there's been a comb to put it on, which is three hundred years and some. A husk isn't enough of anything to have a lean. You start to get a note at Flask, sometimes. At Barrel, always." Halloran took a pinch from the brass pan. "That's why a big yield from an odd spawn is worth more than its weight. It isn't more fuel. It's fuel that already wants to do one particular job."

She let the pinch fall on the steel plate.

For a moment there was nothing. Then, very faintly, the third reed from the long end began to hum.

It was hardly a sound at all. Kiva would not have heard it in the shop, over the tick of the test rack. In that silent lampless room it was as clear as a fly on a windowpane, one thin steady silver note, and she knew it. She had heard it on a cinder path the day before, and in a brick hall the day before that. It was the note of a wet finger on the rim of a glass.

"Ward," said Halloran. "A trace of it. Not a tenth of what I'd get from a skinned Flask, but it's there, and it's not a print, because a print reads as one person and this doesn't read as anyone." She was watching Kiva. "You wrote *curved*. On the back of a tag, in a field, with no comb. The warden sent me a note on Saturday to say you'd told him the same thing on his floor, three times running, in the same words." She lifted the plate, and the reed fell silent. "I've been a grader nineteen years. I can't feel that through a tin. How did you know?"

Kiva did not know how to answer. Everyone was looking at her.

"I've always been able to feel what's in a cell," she said. "Without a door. It's just my hands. My aunt can do it too, a bit. In the count, the ash is all open, and it's like a room full of people waiting. And some of this wasn't waiting. It was already leaning on something." She felt her ears go hot. "I didn't know it was a ward. I only knew it was the shape Anwen's makes, when she holds it properly."

Halloran did not say whether she believed a word of it. She wrote on the card, three letters and a word. *W, trace.* "Come and see my shelf."

---

The shelf was at the far end of the room, between two windows.

It ran from floor to ceiling, and it held hundreds of small stoppered jars, each with a pinch of grey in the bottom and a card tied round its neck. They were sorted by yield and then by note. Kiva read the brass labels going up. *FLASK, EDGE. FLASK, WARD. BARREL, STRIDE.* Some of the cards were brown with age.

The lowest shelf of all was labeled *HANDFUL, PLAIN*, and it held a single enormous jar, the size of a bread crock, with one card on it that said only *As ever.*

Beside that crock, somebody had cleared a hand's width of bare wood and propped up a new label. It was not brass. It was a piece of white card, lettered in ink.

*HANDFUL, W-TRACE. First entered the 5th of this month.*

Four small jars stood behind it.

"Kennel, the fourth," said Halloran, touching them in turn. "Kennel, the twelfth. Kennel, the seventeenth. Kennel, Saturday. That last one's yours as well, off the warden's floor, and I did it this morning." She folded her arms. "Four in a month, from one lattice that's given plain husks since my grandmother was a girl. The board's had my note for three weeks. Do you know what they wrote back?"

"That it's the lattice," said Kellan.

She looked at him sharply.

"It's what I'd write, if I were sitting in an office and wanted it to be nothing," he said. "One tear. One sixty-year-old pattern holding it. If the pattern's begun to drift, every husk out of it would carry the drift. It's the cheapest explanation, and you can't disprove it with four jars from the same place."

"No," said Halloran. "I can't." She nodded at the brass pan on the scale, at the end of the long room. "But that didn't come out of the Kennel. That came out of a hole in the air over an allotment, three miles from any lattice. It's the first field Handful I've ever heard sing." She let that stand a moment. "I'd like half a dram for this shelf. I can't require it. It comes out of your team share, not the city's, and I know what a first share is. But a jar on this shelf with a Home Line officer's initials on its tag is something the board will have to answer. A note from me is something the board can file."

Nobody said anything. Half a dram did not sound like much. Kiva had spilled more than that off the edge of a cloth without noticing.

"We'll tell you tomorrow," said Anwen. "When we've seen what the share is."

"That's fair." Halloran took the card from the scale and handed it to Kellan, since his was the hand that had come out for it. "Rowan. Across the passage, third door. Healer Aske wants a look at two fingers, and said to send you over while the cups were running. Go on. They'll wait."

Healer Aske was small and grey-eyed and had the coldest thumbs Kiva had ever met.

The examination was mostly pressing. A pin, lightly. A feather. "Eyes shut. Which am I touching?"

"First. No. Second."

"Mm." Aske let go of her hand. "They're slow. They aren't stopped. The first is very nearly true and the second's a week or so behind it, and both are reporting late, like a clerk who has the right answer and takes his time bringing it. Another month, and you'll have to think about it to notice. Now listen to me. That nerve has been burned once along the whole of its length. It's forgiven you. It won't do it twice. If there's a next time, you come here the same hour, not the next day, and I'll want ward-trace ash for the easing, because nothing else takes to a nerve half so kindly." There was a short dry laugh. "Until this month I'd have had to beg that from the Flask shelf, a pinch at a time. And now a catcher walks in off a potato bed with a whole Handful of it. The world has a sense of humor. I've never much cared for it."

---

The Compact ash office was in the basement of the Measure Hall, behind a door as thick as her arm.

On Tuesday at noon a clerk with ink to the wrists brought out a grey ledger and a slate. He had done the sum already. He turned the slate to face them across the counter and ran the end of his pen down it, too quickly to be argued with, as he must have done forty times that month.

*Yield at grade: 20.8. Second. W-trace.*
*Civic power, the half: 10.4.*
*Casualty reserve, a quarter of the rest: 2.6.*
*Schools, half of what is left, by head: 3.9.*
*Team Seven: 3.9 drams.*

"Is that all?" said Perrin.

It came out before he could stop it. He went red to the ears.

"That's all," said the clerk. He did not look up, and he did not sound as though the question interested him. "It's a Handful. Less the city, less the hurt, less the schools. A Flask is twenty of those. I'll hear complaints then." He opened the ledger. The first page had been ruled and headed, and under the heading were four names in a copperplate hand. *TEAM SEVEN. YIELD BOOK. Renn, K. Pryce, A. Cade, P. Rowan, K. (prov.)* "Would you like to see it?"

He fetched it from the strongroom. It was a squat tin like Druce's, but only half the size, with the Compact's seal pressed into the lid and a paper band round it. He broke the band and lifted the lid, and the four of them leaned over the counter.

Three drams and nine-tenths of ash lay in the bottom. It would have fitted in an egg cup.

Kiva looked at it for a long time. She thought of forty minutes on her knees in a brick hall, and a tin that opened when a man turned it over, and the weight of a dark blade on her ribs seven nights running. She thought of Perrin going sideways into the bean poles. It did not look like any of that. It looked like what you swept out from behind a stove.

She found that she did not mind. It was the first ash she had ever touched in her life that she had not been afraid of.

"Spend, save, or return," said the clerk. He closed the lid. "It isn't yours to carry about. It's the team's, on the book, and any entry wants all four signatures. There's a list on the wall of what a dram will buy. Take your time. I've had teams stand in that passage and shout at each other till four."

---

They did not shout. They did it at the Rowan kitchen table that evening, because it was the only table any of them had that was not in a laundry or a tram yard.

Mara gave them the lamp and a pot of tea and went down to the shop. "I'd have opinions," she said on the stairs. "And it isn't mine."

So there were four of them and a teapot, and in the middle of the table Anwen's copy of the wall list, in her small neat hand.

*Kennel floor, one release: 0.7.*
*Catch kit, new, school store: 1.0.*
*Meet reserve: up to 0.5 per fighter, above the standard dram, any sanctioned lane.*
*Healer's easing, per sitting: 1.0.*
*Travel warrant, regional qualifier: 2.0 a head.*
*Return to the reference shelf or to civic stock: at par, credited to the team's name.*

"I'll go first," said Kellan. "I want floors. All of it, if you'd let me, which you won't. Five releases and a bit." He had the grader's card in front of him, squared to the edge of the table. "We've cut a skin cleanly once. Once is an accident. The Flask qualifier is whenever the board says, and a skinned Flask will not let me be half a breath early. I want to have done it right three times before it's a thing that can kill Pryce."

"That's a reason," said Anwen. "It's not the only one."

"No." He did not pretend otherwise. "First Division's live lanes open next week. I'm behind every blade in my year by exactly the length of time I spent at the bottom of a call. Floors are how I get that back. It's for me as well as for us. I'd rather you knew which part was which."

"Kit," said Perrin.

He had been turning his cup round and round on the table.

"I know what you'll say. I know it was me. I brought a cloth with two corners and a box of table salt to a live floor, and if Anwen hadn't had a flask, we'd have had nothing to kill a print with. I've written it down. It's the worst page in my book." He put the cup down. "But I only brought that kit because we haven't got one. We've borrowed the warden's, and we've borrowed my Hall's, and next time we'll be borrowing somebody else's with something else wrong with it. I want a cloth that's ours. I want tins I've turned over myself." He breathed in, carefully, around the strapping. "And I'll tell you the rest, since we're telling. When the man showed us that tin today, I thought, *is that all*, and I was ashamed, and I still think it. It's the first thing I ever earned that wasn't a number on somebody else's board. I'd like to be able to pick some of it up."

Nobody laughed. Kiva saw Kellan look at him with something that was almost recognition.

"Save," said Anwen. "At least a dram. Sealed, on the book, and nobody touches it." She had her list beside the other one, and for once she was not looking at either. "I stood at the tape of a Home Line hold all day on Sunday, watching. Doran Mace told me a thing there that I've not been able to put down. He said a new crew that spends its first share is the crew that can't afford its second mistake. Something will go wrong. It did on Sunday, and all it cost us was two of Perrin's ribs, because it was a Handful." Her level eyes went to Kiva's left hand, lying on the table. "Healer Aske said *next time, the same hour, and I'll want ward-trace.* I was in the passage. I heard. We've got ward-trace. I think we'd be mad to burn it on floors."

"Not for my fingers," said Kiva.

"For whoever's fingers. Yours are just the ones we know about."

"Then my interest's the same as Kellan's, turned round," Anwen went on, before Kiva could answer. "My placement wants a license, and I've got it now. What it wants next is for nobody on this team to be carried off a line this season with my name on the card. I'm not being generous. I'm being careful with something that's mine."

They all looked at Kiva.

She had known all day what she wanted, and it was not on the wall list in the order she wanted it.

"The shelf," she said. "The half dram she asked for."

"That's the one thing on there that buys us nothing," said Perrin.

"I know."

"I'd have put it first," said Kellan.

Kiva turned to him. So did the others.

"I was going to say so when it came round to me again." He was frowning at the grader's card. "When that supervisor changed the stamp on my mother's batch, the book saved her. I told you that. The book said what she'd measured. But the book was only her word, written down, until they went to the shelf. Every batch she'd ever flagged, she'd put a pinch of it in a jar, with the date, because that's the rule and she keeps rules. They took the jar down and measured it again, in front of everybody, eleven months later, and it read what she'd said it read." He put one finger on the card. "A number somebody can check is worth ten you have to be believed about. That jar is the only thing on this table that's still worth something if we turn out to be wrong about all the rest."

"So that's two for the shelf," said Anwen slowly, "from the two people at this table I'd have bet on to argue for floors and for herself."

"I'm not arguing against floors," said Kellan. "I'm saying which half dram I'd cut first, and it isn't that one."

Anwen picked up her pencil.

It took them the rest of the pot. In the end it was Perrin who did the sum, on the back of his hand, because he said he wanted to be the one who made it come out even.

"Half to the shelf. That's the jar. One dram sealed, and it stays ward-trace, and nobody so much as breathes on it unless a healer says so." He counted on his green and yellow fingers. "One for a kit. A whole one. With four corners. That's two and a half. Which leaves one and four-tenths, and at seven-tenths a go, that's two floors for Kellan to practice not being early." He looked round the table. "Three and nine. Nothing over. It comes out exact, which I take as a sign."

"There's nothing for a meet reserve," said Anwen. She was looking at Kiva. "Yours is Saturday. The list says half a dram. Your draw—"

"I know what my draw is."

"You'll run dry. In a team lane, three rounds, on one standard dram. You ran through nearly a whole one in a single bout in the Ring."

"I know." Kiva had turned this over on the tram, all the way home. "And if I do, I ought to find that out on the board, where it gets written down. Not have you three buy me out of it with ash you bled for. Saturday isn't Team Seven's. It's the Crooked Line third team's, and mine." She made herself say the rest. "If I need more ash than everyone else on that sand just to stay standing, then that's a true thing about me, and I'd rather know it than hide it."

Perrin gave a long sigh.

"Thank goodness," he said. "Because it's Stonehand you're fighting. My Hall. My own third team. And if you'd asked, I'd have had to sit here and vote to buy you ash to go and beat them with, and I would have done it, Kiva, I'd have put my name to it. And then I'd have complained every day for a month, to all of you, at length."

"You'll complain anyway," said Kellan.

"I shall. But now it'll be about ribs."

They signed it on Wednesday morning, at the counter, in the grey ledger. Four names, one under another. The clerk blotted them and wrote the entries out beneath in his copperplate, and Kiva watched three drams and nine-tenths of ash turn into four lines of ink.

It did not feel like spending. It felt like ruling columns in an empty book.

---

Noa Bexley had been in the roster corridor since the Measure Hall opened, and he had not yet looked at a single roster.

The frames went back two hundred years along both walls, and everybody who came up here looked at those. Nobody looked at the long flat drawers beneath them, where the Chair's office kept the Classification Day summary sheets, one ruled page to a year. Noa's was the only signature on the card since the autumn before last.

He had forty of them spread along the floor in order, weighted at the corners with his boots.

He had come to check one thing. He kept the team's tapes because a tape said what had happened and not who had won, and if the draw bench had been wrong about Kiva Rowan by a factor of three, he wanted to know whether it had been drifting for everyone. So he had pulled the class median for Draw, year by year. It had not drifted at all. Forty years of it lay within a hundredth of one point nought, as flat as a millpond.

It was the next column over that had stopped him.

*Median Hold.* Five and three-tenths. Five and four. Five and three. It went on like that down the whole length of the corridor, a long quiet line of fives, until the sheet under his left boot, which was this spring's. *Median Hold: 6.1.*

He had done the median again by hand from three hundred and six names, because a clerk could slip, and it had come out six and one.

He read down the rest of that last sheet with his pencil. Fire readings at seventeen: three, where the ten years before had two between them. Lowest Draw in the class: nought point seven, and he had to go back to the sheet for 2651 to find a better. At the foot, in the space where the Chair's office noted anything out of the way, somebody had written in a long plain hand: *Stonehand reports eleven candidates at or above upper Flame. Usual: three or four. Confirmed on re-read.*

A strong year. That was what anyone would say, and they might be right. Three hundred people could throw up a freak median the way a handful of dice could throw up five sixes.

He took the notebook out of his coat. Near the back there was a page he had headed *Things that are probably nothing.* The fourth line, dated the twenty-second, said *Two tears in nine days. Usual, 4–5 a spring.* The fifth was copied off a notice the Kennel warden had pinned to the Cellhouse gate: *Skinned Handfuls: four on the Kennel floor this month, one in the field. None prior, in sixty years of book.* The sixth was a number from the Low Lane mast with a question mark after it.

He wrote a seventh. *Class of 2680: median Hold 6.1 against forty years at 5.3–5.5. Three Fire at seventeen. Chair's office has noticed.*

He looked at the page for a while. He drew no line between anything on it. Then he turned to the other page, the one he had not shown to Jude.

It had her name at the top and five ruled columns beneath. *Door. Output. Draw. Recovery. Behavior.* Two rows were filled in, and there was a great deal of blank paper.

He wanted to fill it, the way other people in his year wanted a First Division band. He had never been near a question that nobody else had asked yet, and this one had walked into the Low Lane with her arm in a sling and sat down under the next peg. There would be a paper in it, with his name on the paper. He knew that he wanted that very much, and that wanting it was not the same as being owed it.

Because they were her numbers, which was inconvenient. The Chair had ordered her tape pulled. He could fill those five columns without asking her one thing, and take them up these stairs, and she would find out when somebody sent for her.

He had seen how the school looked at her. A quench box in front of a peg. He did not much want to be one more person who had decided what Kiva Rowan was without mentioning it to her.

Noa gathered up the forty sheets, in order, and put them back in their drawers. On the card he signed his name beneath his name.

He would ask on Saturday, after the meet, when there would be a sanctioned board with a referee's stamp on it. He would say what he wanted and why, and that he would like his name on it second, after hers. If she said no, he would go on measuring, because he could not help that, and file nothing.

He thought she would probably say yes. He wrote that down too, under *probably nothing*, and then crossed it out, because it was not evidence.

---

"It's a response," said Jude.

He had chalked the Long Terrace on the Low Lane's brick floor, a rectangle forty paces by fourteen shrunk to fit, with a line across the middle and a box at each end. He stood on the line with a litter pole in his hand, like a pointer.

"That's how we'll play it, because it's the only way any of us has ever worked as four. You've all had a month on the warden's floor. So. Stonehand comes out of that box, four still-doors, in a row. They don't run. They've never run. They walk to the litter like a dray and pick it up and walk it home, and anything in the way gets leaned on." He tapped the middle line. "Noa's quicker than all four of them put together. He gets to Donny first and drags him our way. Three paces, four, whatever he can. That's the Carry."

"I can't lift him alone," said Noa. "Struts sink under a load. I can drag."

"Drag, then. Pru. Ward across the middle, here, the moment they're close. That's the Hold. You're keeping them where we want them."

"They'll walk through it," said Pru. "I had one of them walk through it last year. He said *excuse me*."

"They'll walk into it. That's a second and a half they're not walking into Noa. I take the touches." He put the pole to his own chest. "Every one of them that reaches for a handle has to open up to do it. That's my Kill. A point a time, and ten counts on their knees for each." He looked at Kiva. "And you're on the litter. Still door, both hands on the poles, planted on top of him like a hen. They can't carry what they can't lift. I want you to be the heaviest thing on that sand."

It was a good plan. She could see every part of it. It had four jobs in it, and each of them had been hers or her friends' on a brick floor that same month.

"Still," she said. "That's what I'll declare."

"And if you have to change?"

"I say so first. Out loud. To the referee. *Rowan, changing,* and the door." She had practiced the words in front of the lane warden until he had asked her, wearily, to stop. "It takes me about two seconds from the one to the other, clean. I can't do it faster yet."

Jude rubbed his chipped tooth.

"Two seconds is a long time on sand," he said. "Try not to need it."

She spent most of Thursday with Pru Finch, at the far end of the lane, because Pru's ward came up flat.

It was a good ward, bigger than Kiva's own and a great deal steadier. But Pru held it out before her at the length of both arms, like a woman carrying a tray of glasses across a crowded room, and when Jude leaned his shoulder into the edge of it, it rang twice and went with a splash of cold air.

"That's what he did," said Pru. "The Stonehand one. Only he was bigger."

"You're standing behind it," said Kiva. "Try standing under it."

She was not the right person to teach this. She said so. Her own ward was the size of a tea tray and her Hold on it was two and six-tenths. But she had knelt a forearm's length from Anwen Pryce on a cinder path while Anwen held a skinned husk's whole weight on a ward for a slow count of four, and she had watched every inch of how Anwen did it, because there had been nothing else in the world to look at.

"Hands low. Lower. Wide, like you're pushing a cart. And don't hold it up. Lean on it. It isn't a thing you're carrying. It's a door, and you're keeping it shut with your shoulder."

Pru tried it. The ward came up crooked, wobbled, and curved.

Jude leaned on it. It rang, and rang, and rang again. He had to step round the end of it in the finish.

"Oh," said Pru faintly. "Oh, that's *horrible*. It's so much easier. I've done it wrong for three years. Who told you that?"

"A friend," said Kiva. "She told me I splash. I still do."

---

Perrin was waiting at the Stonehand gate on Friday when she brought the Hall's old kit back.

He took it from her with his elbows, since his hands were bound again. Then he held up, between two wrapped fingers, a brown paper parcel tied with string.

"Ours," he said. "I went to your school's store this morning and stood in the queue with the team book. Open it. I can't."

She undid the string. Inside lay a waxed cloth, new and stiff and smelling of linseed, with a lead weight sewn fast into each corner. There were two pans without a dent in them, and a brush, and a wet-salt box with a rubber seal in the lid, and six squat tins with their cords coiled white inside.

"Four," said Perrin. "Corners. I counted them in the shop, and then I made the man count them." He was pink with pride. "And I've wet the salt. It's so wet it's nearly soup."

"It's lovely, Perrin."

"It is, isn't it?" He looked at it a moment longer, and then his face changed, and he glanced back over his shoulder at the long stone front of the Hall. "Listen. About tomorrow. I'm going to say one thing, and then I'm not going to say another word, and you're not to ask me."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"I know you weren't. That's why I'm saying one thing." He lowered his voice. "Their captain's Lowen Brask. Third-year. He ran the junior lanes when I came, and he's the one who taught me to say thank you to the ground. He's the best teacher in the Hall, and he's been Third for three years because his Hold stops at seven and a half and won't go higher, and he's stopped minding." Perrin swallowed. "He reads people. That's what he does instead. He'll read you like a tag on a tin. That's all. I've said too much, and it's my Hall, and I'm going to go and feel disloyal to two places at once."

He went, with the old kit under one arm, and did not look back.

At home, the Works notice had company beneath the brass weight. A second paper lay on top of it, a half sheet in a quick slanting hand that she knew at once.

*To whom it concerns at the Works. Mast six, Sag Line: left uncorrected by its instrument-keeper, with her reason written in its book. On the 28th it rang four minutes ahead of five and seven. Hold established with ninety seconds in hand. H. Vane, Home Line watch.*

"It came by the noon post," said Mara. She was mending a kitchen meter, with her glasses on and the tweezers in her left hand. "It isn't a license. It's a man's handwriting on half a sheet of paper." She did not look up. "I've known that to count for something. We'll see on the ninth."

That night, in the Old Yard, Orla sat on her crate with her knee bound in three thicknesses of flannel and read the week's pages.

She read them by the lamp, slowly, one finger moving down the columns. She read the Kennel and the cold room and the Sag Line. She read the kitchen table. At the end she closed the book and held it on her lap.

"Stonehand, tomorrow," she said.

"Yes."

"And your captain's built it like a floor."

"Kill, Hold, Carry, Catch. It's a good plan."

"It's a very good plan for a husk." Orla handed the book back. "I've fought people and I've fought spawn for forty years, and I could tell you tonight, in about six sentences, what the difference is going to do to you. You'd nod. You'd write it down. And tomorrow you'd do exactly what your hands have been taught these three weeks, because that's what hands do." She shook her head. "It'll keep better if you find it yourself. Go and lose it properly. Bring me the page."

"You think we'll lose."

"I think you've a sanctioned meet with a referee and a draw board, and one incident ends your season and my standing, and you're to declare every door before you use it." Her crooked face was not unkind. "Lose it by the rules, Kiva. That's all I'll ask. Now do me the drill once before I'm too stiff to count it."

She did the drill. She looked at the crooked post and not at the metal.

"Eight and six," said Orla.
