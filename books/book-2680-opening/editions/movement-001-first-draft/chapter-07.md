# Chapter 7 — Letting Go

"Taking," said Kiva. "Ember."

The hooked bar came off her knuckles in two lurches, the way it always did. She loosened her fingers on the band, a little, and the heaviness at the end evened out along the metal.

"Hold it," said Orla. "Don't do anything with it. Just know it's there."

Kiva held it. She stood in the middle of the Crooked Line shed on the packed earth floor with the practice Ember on her right fist and the ugly bar standing out from it, dark, and she counted to five. The right-hand coal under her breastbone was warm and steady. The other two were quiet.

"Now let go," said Orla. "Say it first."

"Letting go."

She opened her hand. She spread her fingers wide, as wide as they would go, and breathed out all the way.

The bar did not move.

It stood out from the band on her open hand, exactly as ugly and exactly as solid as before. Kiva stared at it. She had opened the hand. Everything she had been taught said that was the end of it: no fist, no grip, nothing for the metal to close around.

"It's still there," she said.

"I can see that," said Orla. "What did you let go of?"

"My hand."

"Is the door in your hand?"

Kiva thought about it. The right-hand coal under her breastbone was still warm. It had not changed at all when she opened her fingers. "No," she said slowly. "It's in me. My hand's just where it comes out."

"So."

"So letting go of my hand isn't letting go of the door." Kiva looked at the bar. "It's just letting go of my hand."

Orla nodded, very slightly. She said nothing else.

Anwen sat on an upturned crate by the stove with a small sand-glass in her lap, the kind the Wren House healers used for counting a dose. She had been assigned to Kiva's practice on the thirteenth morning, by a note from the rotation mistress that said *bench hours, logged*, and she had arrived at the shed with the sand-glass and a notebook and the air of someone who intended to write everything down.

"What does it feel like?" she said. "When you open it?"

"Like stopping not doing something," said Kiva, without looking away from the bar. "Like when you've been holding your breath without noticing, and you stop."

"So closing it would be the other way round," said Anwen. "Starting not doing something."

Kiva frowned. She looked at the bar on her open hand. Then she turned her hand over, palm up, so the bar stood out from the back of her fist toward the floor, and she thought about the right-hand coal under her breastbone. She did not think about closing it. She thought about putting it down, the way you put down a cup you have been holding for a long time without noticing your fingers have gone stiff around it.

The bar slid back into the band.

It went slowly, the way water goes back into sand. It did not lurch. It simply sank, from the hooked end toward the root, until there was only the plain dull ring on her knuckles again.

"There," said Orla.

Kiva did not move. She stood with her hand palm up and waited, and felt for the coal under her breastbone.

It was still warm.

"It's not closed," she said. "The bar's gone, but it's still—it's still open. It's still warm."

"Then wait," said Orla. "Don't ask for anything. Don't open anything else. Wait."

Anwen turned the sand-glass.

Kiva waited. The shed was quiet. She could hear the stove ticking and a cart going past outside on the lane road, and Anwen breathing. She stood with her hand palm up and felt the warmth under her breastbone, and did nothing at all.

It went slowly. It did not go out like a lamp. It cooled, like a cup that somebody has set down on a table and walked away from, from hot to warm to only the warmth of her own body, until she could not tell where the coal had been.

It was a kind of quiet. Orla had been right about that.

"Now," said Kiva.

Anwen looked at the sand-glass. "Fifty-one breaths," she said. "Near enough." She wrote it down.

"Fifty-one," said Orla. "From letting go to closed. That's how long the Ember door takes to go quiet in you, after you've used it for five breaths." She took the practice Ember off Kiva's fist herself and hung it on its peg. "It'll get shorter, maybe. Or it won't. Either way, you'll know it's there."

Kiva looked at her empty hand. Fifty-one breaths. It seemed like an enormous amount of time to spend doing nothing.

"That's the part nobody watches," said Orla, as if she had heard. "Everybody watches the opening. Nobody watches the waiting. But if you open the next door before that one's quiet, they'll find each other in the middle, and they'll fight." She sat down on the bench along the wall. "Rift door next. When you're ready. Say it first."

---

The rift door's letting go was different.

When Kiva held her left palm out and said "Taking, rift," and the Ward came up flat and warm against the air, she tried turning her palm over the way she had with the Ember. Nothing happened. The Ward simply turned with her hand, and stood out from the back of it instead, pushing gently at nothing.

"It's not the same," she said.

"No reason it should be," said Orla.

Kiva tried opening her fingers wide. Nothing. She tried closing them, softly, and felt the Ward tighten and push harder, and stopped at once. She tried breathing out. The Ward wavered, and came back.

It was Anwen who saw it.

"When I let mine go," she said, from the crate, "I put my hand down. Like this." She held her own left hand out, palm forward, and opened her rift door, and Kiva saw the dust on the shed floor stir under a small flat Ward. Then Anwen let her hand fall to her side, palm toward the ground, loosely, the way you would let your hand fall if somebody you trusted had taken whatever you were holding out of it. The dust settled. "The healers teach it at Wren House. Palm to the floor. They say the Ward goes down into the ground and stops."

"Does it?"

"No idea," said Anwen. "It's just what they teach. It works for me."

Kiva let her left hand fall, palm toward the floor, loosely.

The Ward went. She felt it go, not out like the Ember's bar, but down, as if it were water poured from a bowl onto dry earth: a quick warm sliding pressure past her knees, into the ground, and gone. Then the waiting, with her hand at her side and the left-hand coal under her breastbone cooling. Anwen counted. Thirty-eight breaths.

"Faster than the Ember," said Anwen.

"Because I didn't hold it as long?"

"Because it's a different door," said Orla. "Don't make rules yet. Write it down."

Anwen wrote it down.

The still door was the strangest of all. It opened the moment Kiva let her weight settle into her heels, and it went down through her boots into the earth floor and the wooden underfloor beneath the earth, deep and dull and heavy. When she said "Letting go," she did not know what to let go of. She tried breathing out. She tried softening her knees. She stood in the middle of the shed rooted like a post, and could not find the edge of it.

"Step off," said Orla.

"There's nothing to step off. It's just the floor."

"Step off anyway."

Kiva lifted her right heel.

It was like pulling a boot out of deep mud. For a moment the still door held it, and she felt the heel resist, and then it came free, and the instant it came free the whole deep weight under her loosened. She lifted her other heel. She stood on the balls of her feet, swaying slightly, with the still door going quiet under her like a pond settling after a stone.

"Forty-four," said Anwen.

Kiva put her heels down again, very carefully. The floor was just a floor.

"Ember, fifty-one," said Orla. "Rift, thirty-eight. Still, forty-four. Open hand, palm down, step off. That's three doors." She looked at Kiva. "Now do it again, in order, without stopping in between except to wait. And don't go faster."

---

Kiva went faster.

She did not mean to. It was the fourth time through, late on the thirteenth afternoon, and she was tired, and the Ember door had just gone quiet after forty-seven breaths, which was better than before, and she was pleased about that. Anwen had said "Rest," and Kiva had nodded, and in the half-breath before she could think about it she was already holding out her left palm and saying "Taking, rift."

The Ember door was not as closed as she had thought.

She felt it the moment the rift door opened. The right-hand coal flared, as if someone had blown on an ember she had thought was out. The two doors found each other somewhere in the middle of her chest, the way Orla had said they would, and pushed. On her empty right fist there was nothing for the Ember to open onto, so the hand clenched on its own, hard, nails into her palm. The Ward on her left palm doubled in an instant and shoved outward, and the crate Anwen was sitting on slid back a hand's width across the earth floor.

The three lines under the healing linen on her shoulder went hot.

*Not warm.*

"Hot," said Kiva.

She said it out loud, clearly, before she had time to think about whether she should. It was the first time she had ever said it.

Orla was on her feet. "Stop. Stop the reading." There was no reading, no frame, no gauge, only the sand-glass, but she said it anyway, as if there were.

She did not step in. She could not have; she was an Ember Torch, and nothing in an Ember could meet a Ward.

But Anwen was already off the crate.

She did not run at Kiva. She stood where she was, at arm's length on Kiva's left, and put her own left palm out flat toward Kiva's Ward, and opened her door. Kiva felt Anwen's Ward meet hers in the air between their hands: a narrow, steady, cool pressure against the hot one, not pushing back exactly, just there. A place for the pushing to go.

"Mine," said Anwen.

The pressure in Kiva's palm had somewhere to go now. It went into Anwen's Ward and stopped there, held, the way the iron had stopped on Anwen's shelf in the old lane. Kiva's left arm stopped shaking.

"Letting go," said Kiva, and let her left hand fall, palm down.

Her Ward went into the ground. Anwen's stayed for a breath, then two, and then Anwen let her own hand fall too, and the air between them was only air.

The right-hand coal was still flaring. Kiva's right hand was still clenched. She breathed out, all the way, and waited, and did not ask for anything. After a long time, the coal cooled. Her fingers loosened on their own, one at a time, and she opened her hand and looked at the four red crescents in the palm.

"How hot?" said Orla.

"Hot." Kiva put her fingers to the linen on her shoulder. Under it, the long line was throbbing. "Not like the lane. But hot."

"Sit down."

She sat down on the floor of the shed, where she was. Orla came and crouched in front of her and looked at her face and then at her collar. Anwen stood behind Orla with her left hand still loose at her side, breathing hard.

"You said it," said Orla quietly.

"I know."

"Before you thought about it."

"I know." Kiva looked up at Anwen. "And you said *mine*. And it worked."

"It worked on a Ward," said Anwen. She sounded shaken. "I don't know if it'd work on anything else. I can't hold an Ember. I can't hold your feet."

"No," said Orla. "That's going to be the hard part." She stood up. "But it worked on a Ward. Write that down."

Anwen, after a moment, sat back down on her crate, and turned the sand-glass, and wrote it down.

---

That evening, at the bench, Mara asked which order.

Kiva had known she would. Mara read everything twice and asked about the parts that weren't written. Orla's protocol for the fifteenth day had come in the afternoon, a single sheet in her narrow upright hand, and Mara had read it twice before supper and once after, and now she had it flat on the bench beside the box with *Rowan, K. — doors* filed inside.

"*In an order the candidate chooses*," Mara read aloud. "Which order?"

"Ember first," said Kiva. "Then rift. Then still."

"Why?"

"Because the Ember's the one that goes first anyway." Kiva pointed to the shelf, where Mara's copy of the Kindling trace was rolled. "On the trace. You said. It lifted first, by a hair. So it's the one that's closest to the top. If I open it first on purpose, it's not waiting to jump in while I'm doing something else." She thought. "And when it's done, they can take the Ember off my hand. Then there's nothing for it to open onto. That helps."

Mara nodded slowly.

"Rift in the middle, because it's quickest to close. And still last, because the still door's what holds me up." Kiva looked at her hands. "If anything goes wrong in the middle, I want to be standing on my own feet. And when it's done, stepping off is the end. Stepping off the stone."

Mara looked at her for a long time.

"That's a reason," she said at last.

"Is it a good one?"

"It's yours," said Mara. "And you can say it. That's most of what makes a reason good." She folded the protocol. "Write it on the card."

Kiva took the card out of the box. In the remarks column, under Orla's line, she wrote in her own hand: *Order: Ember, rift, still. Ember first because it lifts first. Rift middle because it closes fastest. Still last so I'm standing.*

Then she filed it, and they went up to bed.

---

Perrin was waiting at the yard gate on the fifteenth morning, with his ankle in a lighter bandage and his right hand in a leather glove.

"Stonehand sent me," he said. "Well. I asked. They needed somebody to do the push on your still door, and the Warden won't let a second-year anywhere near you, and I said I'd been pushed every dawn for four days and I knew exactly how it was supposed to feel." He looked very pleased with himself. "So I'm the push."

"Your hand," said Kiva.

"Left hand. I'll push with the left." He held it up. "I've been practising. On my brother. He's furious."

The yard was closed. It was the second hour, cold and grey, and the stewards had put the ropes up at the gate so that nobody could come in from Gauge Row or the canal side. Inside, the Founders' Yard was very big and very empty. The banners were long gone. The Table on the south wall was dark. Only the Assay frame stood at the south end with its canvas pulled back, a ring of brass and dark iron on three legs, with the slab in its middle and the Ember on its rest and the lead case on its post.

A man Kiva did not know was kneeling at its south leg with a toolbag: the district's second repairer. He did not look at her. Mara stood at the rail by the gate with the other guardians' places empty around her, and did not look at the frame at all.

Ysra Crest was at her table on the platform, with the clerk beside her. The Wren House senior healer was at a bench by the frame's east leg with her jar and her balance. Anwen stood beside her, in green, with the sand-glass in her hand.

Orla stood at the edge of the ring, where she had stood on Kindling Day.

At the rail on the east side, halfway down the yard, alone, stood Kellan Renn.

He had his grey coat buttoned to the collar and his hands in his pockets. He was not on any list. Nobody had sent him. He had simply come and stood at the rail, where anybody could stand, and he was watching the frame.

"Why's he here?" said Perrin, under his breath.

"He asked to walk the lane against me," said Kiva. "When I'm placed. Anwen told me."

"And?"

"And I suppose he wants to see what he'd be walking against."

Perrin looked at Kellan for a while. "That's fair," he said, sounding surprised.

"I know," said Kiva. "That's what I thought."

---

The belt went on over her shirt, the same old brown leather with its three brass plates. Its cord ran to the reel and from the reel to the frame, so that the frame and the belt would both read at once. The clerk tugged it, once, to check the fit.

Kiva stepped onto the slab.

Her feet still did not fit the hollows. She stood with her heels on the lip, as before. She looked down at the stone, the grey worn surface, and thought about all the feet that had stood here and gone down into the ground for the first time, and the ones that had not.

"Candidate," said Ysra. "Declare your order."

"Ember. Rift. Still."

"Protocol, Lane-Mistress?"

"One door at a time," said Orla, clearly, for the clerk to write. "Each declared *taking* before it opens and *letting go* before it closes. Each held against the frame's lowest load for a count, then released. After each release, the candidate waits for the door to go quiet, and the frame's needle to rest. The clerk calls *rest* when the needle's on its stop. Nothing opens until the clerk calls rest." She paused. "The abort line is on record. If the candidate says *hot*, the reading stops. Whoever's nearest says *mine*."

"Recorded," said Ysra. "Frame to lowest load. Begin when ready."

Kiva put her right fist through the Ember's ring.

It was the issued one, the same one as on Kindling Day, heavier than the practice Ember and slightly warmer. She felt the metal against her knuckles. She did not reach for the door. She let it know there was an Ember there.

"Taking," she said. "Ember."

The right-hand coal warmed. The bar came, in two lurches, short and thick and hooked at the end. She loosened her fingers on the band, a little. The heaviness evened out.

On the frame, the right gauge's needle woke and climbed. Two marks. It stopped and stayed.

"Ember door," said the clerk. "Holding load."

The frame's lever moved, the lowest notch. Kiva felt it press, very gently, against the bar on her fist, the way a hand presses on a door to see if it is shut. She held. She did not think about her left hand or her feet. She thought about the bar, and the coal, and nothing else.

"Three," said the clerk. "Four. Five. Six. Seven."

On eight, the bar shivered.

"Letting go," said Kiva.

She turned her hand over, palm up. She thought about putting down the cup. The bar sank, slowly, from the hook toward the root, back into the band.

"Ember door," said the clerk. "Short, seven. Released."

A steward came and slid the Ember's ring off her hand, carefully, and set it on its rest.

Then the waiting.

Kiva stood on the stone with her empty right hand palm up and felt the coal cool. Hot to warm to her own warmth. It was very quiet in the yard. She could hear the canal, faintly, beyond the wall, and the second repairer breathing at the south leg, and the slow trickle of the sand in Anwen's glass. She looked at nobody. She did not count. She simply waited until there was nothing left to wait for.

"Rest," said the clerk.

Kiva let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

"Taking," she said. "Rift."

She lifted her left palm. She stopped not doing it. The left-hand coal warmed, and the warmth ran down her arm and out, and the air in front of her hand went hard: a small flat Ward, the size of a dinner plate, warm and steady.

She had practised the size for two hours on the fourteenth. *Small. Not all of you. Just a plate.* It had been the hardest thing of all, harder than the waiting. Every time she opened the rift door, it wanted to come all at once.

It came as a plate.

"Rift door," said the clerk. "Holding load."

The frame's load paddle swung forward on its arm and touched the Ward, gently. Kiva held. Her right hand twitched once at her side. She felt the right-hand coal stir, very faintly, as if it had heard its name in another room. Her three lines warmed under the linen.

"Warm," she said. "Not hot."

"Recorded," said Ysra, from the platform.

Orla did not move. Anwen, by the healer's bench, had lifted her own left hand a little from her side, and held it there.

Kiva breathed out. The stir in the right-hand coal settled. She did not let it pull her attention away from the plate. *Just this.*

"Six. Seven. Eight."

On nine, the Ward thinned.

"Letting go."

She let her left hand fall, palm toward the stone. The plate of hard air slid down past her knees into the slab and was gone.

"Rift door. Steady, eight. Released."

The waiting again. Shorter, this time. The left-hand coal cooled fast, the way it had in the shed, and when it was gone she stood with her hand loose at her side and felt for the other two, and found them both quiet.

"Rest," said the clerk.

Kiva looked at Perrin.

He was standing at the edge of the ring on her right, where the protocol said the push should stand, as far from her left side as possible. He had pulled off his glove and was flexing his left hand.

"Taking," said Kiva. "Still."

She let her weight settle into her heels.

The still door opened as it always did, the moment it had permission. It went down through her boots and the stone and the copper plate under the stone and on into the deep ground of the yard, heavy and slow. The third gauge woke and climbed.

"Still door," said the clerk. "Push."

Perrin stepped into the ring. He put his left palm flat against her right shoulder, the way the second-years did at Stonehand, and set his feet, and pushed.

She did not move.

He pushed harder. She heard him grunt. She felt his weight come against her shoulder, and go down through her and into the stone, and stop there. Her feet did not slide. Her knees did not bend.

"I've been doing dawn load for four days," said Perrin through his teeth, still pushing. "Four days. I can push a second-year off a stone now. One of the small ones."

"Nine," said the clerk. "Ten. Eleven."

On twelve, Kiva felt the roots begin to loosen of their own accord, a slow giving way under her heels.

"Letting go," she said.

She lifted her right heel.

Perrin was still leaning on her with all his weight. When the still door released, there was suddenly nothing holding her up against him, and he came forward into her with a startled noise, and they both lurched toward the edge of the slab. Kiva got her left foot down on the stone just in time. Perrin did not. He went down on one knee on the slab's edge, with one hand clutching her sleeve.

Anwen was there. She caught Perrin under the arm with both hands, without a Ward, just her hands, and hauled him up.

"Still door," said the clerk, sounding as if he were trying not to laugh. "Steady, eleven. Released."

"I'm all right," said Perrin. "I'm all right. That was intentional. That was part of the push." He let go of Kiva's sleeve. His face was red. "Tell me that wasn't the best push you've ever had."

"It was the best push I've ever had," said Kiva.

She was laughing. She had not known she was going to laugh. She stood on the stone on the balls of her feet with her heels lifted and her hands loose, and Perrin was laughing too, and Anwen was holding him up by the elbow with an expression of deep long-suffering patience, and under Kiva's breastbone all three coals were cooling at once, separately, each in its own place.

"Rest," said the clerk.

---

The projector lamp clicked.

On the south wall, the Table woke. It wrote three rows in pale gold, one after another, each beginning only after the last had ended.

*Ember door. Reach 2. Hold short, 7. Released.*

*Rift door. Reach 2. Hold steady, 8. Released.*

*Still door. Reach 2. Hold steady, 11. Released.*

Ysra Crest stood up behind her table and looked at it.

She looked at the trace drum next, where the clerk had unwound the morning's length onto the desk in front of her. Kiva could not see the paper from the slab. But she could see Ysra's face as she ran one finger along it, very slowly, from left to right: three humps, one after another, each falling back to nothing before the next began.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Recorded," said Ysra at last. Her voice was exactly as it always was. "A complete sequence. Three doors, declared, separate, each released before the next." She sat down again and picked up her pen. "I've never seen that on a trace before."

At the rail on the east side of the yard, Kellan Renn took his hands out of his pockets.

Kiva stepped down off the slab.

She did it carefully, on the balls of her feet, her heels still lifted from the still door's release. She put one boot on the cold earth of the yard, and then the other. She stood for a moment at the edge of the ring, breathing, and looked up at the three gold rows on the south wall. They were ordinary readings, the kind that had filled three hundred rows of the Table on Kindling Day. Nobody would have looked twice at any one of them.

They were hers, every one.

Then something under her shirt went warm.

She gasped. She did not mean to; it simply came out of her, a small sharp sound. Her right hand went to her chest by itself, flat over her breastbone, over the cord. For one terrible moment she thought it was a door, a fourth coal she had never felt before, opening without asking.

It was not a door. All three coals were quiet. She could feel them, cool and separate, each in its own place.

It was the disk.

It lay under her palm against her breastbone, and it was warm. Not hot. Not like the lines. It was the warmth of a cup held in both hands for a while, the warmth of a stone after an hour in the sun. It had been cold every day for four years. It had been cold on the slab a moment ago. She was sure of it. She had been paying attention to every part of herself, and it had been cold.

Now it was warm.

"Kiva," said Orla.

She was standing three paces away. She had not moved. But she was looking at Kiva's hand, flat on her chest over the cord, and her long patient face had changed. Kiva could not have said exactly how. It was as if a door somewhere behind her eyes had opened a hand's width.

"Hot?" said Orla quietly.

"No." Kiva swallowed. "It's not the lines. It's—"

She stopped. Ysra was writing. The clerk was winding the trace. The second repairer was resetting the load lever, and Perrin was telling Anwen about his best push, and nobody was looking at her except Orla.

Orla came closer, until only Kiva could hear her.

"Not here," she said. "Show me after. With your aunt."

Kiva nodded.

Orla stepped back, and turned to the platform, and said something to Ysra about the protocol in her ordinary level voice, as if nothing had happened at all. But her hands, Kiva saw, were not quite at her sides. The left one had closed, very slightly, and then opened again.

---

They went to the Crooked Line shed at noon, the three of them. Orla shut the door behind them and did not light the stove.

"Show me," she said.

Kiva drew the cord out of her collar. The disk came up into the grey light from the high window, near-black, two thumbs wide, its nine grooves catching the light on the face that turned toward them. She held it out on her palm. She did not take the cord off.

Orla did not touch it.

She bent and looked at it for a long time, very closely, without breathing on it, the way Mara looked at a needle she did not trust. She looked at the grooves. She looked at the edge.

"It's still warm," said Kiva. "A little. Less than before."

"When did it start?"

"After." Kiva thought carefully. "When I stepped off. My second boot was on the ground. It was cold before that. It was cold the whole time I was on the stone."

"You're sure."

"I was paying attention to everything," said Kiva. "It was cold."

"Has it ever been warm before? Ever, in four years?"

"Never," said Mara.

Orla straightened.

She stood in the middle of the shed with her hands at her sides and looked at the racks of practice bars, the coils of rope, the dented helmets, as if she were reading something written on them. She was silent for so long that Kiva began to be afraid.

"What is it?" said Kiva. "What did it do?"

"I don't know what it did," said Orla. "I don't think it did anything. You did the sequence. It went warm after." She looked at Kiva. "That's what you saw. That's what you should say. Don't let anybody tell you it was the other way round. Not me. Not anyone."

"Do you know what it is?"

Orla looked at the disk for a moment longer. "I know it's old," she said. "And I know it's entrusted. You know that too." She paused. "I'd like to know more than that before I say anything else. I'd rather say nothing than say something I'll have to take back."

It was not an answer. Kiva knew it was not an answer. But it was the same kind of not-answer as Ysra's *I'm not going to guess*, and she found that she could hold it.

The disk was cooling on her palm. She could feel it going, the way the coals went after letting go: warm to faintly warm to only the warmth of her own hand.

"Write it down," said Orla, to Mara.

Mara already had the card out. She had brought it from the house in her coat pocket, Kiva realized, the way she brought everything. Not *Rowan, K. — doors.* The other one. The old one.

*Disk, near-black, two thumbs wide, nine grooves each face.*

The instrument column had been empty for four years.

Mara took out her pen and uncapped it, and set the card on the end of the workbench under the high window, and wrote in the empty column for the first time, in her small flat hand.

*Day 15 after the Kindling. Warm, after a complete three-door sequence. Not during. Cold before. Faded by noon.*

She looked at it for a long while. Then, in the remarks, under *Appointment sealed*, she added one line.

*Seen by: K. Rowan, O. Dane, M. Rowan.*

Orla read it over her shoulder. Then she nodded, once, and went to the door, and opened it, and stood in the doorway with her back to them for a moment, looking out at the empty yard.

"Go home," she said, without turning around. "Eat. Sleep. Crest will rule in three days." Her voice was quite level. "And Kiva. Well done."

She went out, and left the door open behind her, and they heard her footsteps go away across the yard toward the old lane, not toward the Crooked Line gate.

Kiva tucked the disk back under her shirt. It was cold again. Or nearly. She could not quite tell anymore, and she stood in the doorway of the shed beside Mara for a long time, with her hand flat over it, trying to tell.
