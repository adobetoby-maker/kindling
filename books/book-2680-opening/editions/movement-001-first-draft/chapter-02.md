# Chapter 2 — What the Needle Wrote

Wren House kept its new Kindled in a long room on the second floor, with eighteen beds down each wall and a window at the end that looked over the canal.

Kiva had the bed nearest the window, because the night healer wanted her where the light was. The other beds were filling with the day's Wren House first-years, children in green ribbons who sat on their blankets and whispered and kept looking at their hands. None of them looked at Kiva for long. When they did, it was at her collar.

"Lie back," said the night healer. "Shirt open to here. I'm going to touch them."

She was an older woman with heavy forearms and a calm, flat way of speaking, as if she had said everything she was ever going to say at least once already. She had a jar of salve that smelled of mint and something sharper underneath. She put two fingers into it and laid them against the top line, the shortest, very lightly.

Kiva flinched.

"Tender," said the healer.

"Yes."

"Good. Tender's honest." She moved to the second line, and then the third, the long one that curved toward Kiva's arm. "Hot, still. Not blistered. They're not on the skin, you understand. They're under it. The skin's just telling us where they are."

"What are they?"

The healer wiped her fingers on a cloth and considered the question. "There's a name for them in the old books," she said. "Competing-door marks. When more than one door draws at once, it pulls on you from more than one direction, and sometimes the pulling shows." She capped the salve. "I've read about them. I've never seen them before tonight."

"Will they go away?"

"I don't know."

Kiva looked at the ceiling. It was painted pale green, and somebody had painted a single small fish in one corner, in darker green, where you would only see it if you were lying down. She wondered how many children had lain in this bed looking at that fish.

"You're at your floor," said the healer. "Do you know what that means?"

"A little."

"Tell me what you know, and I'll tell you where you're wrong."

Kiva thought about the lessons in the lower school, the diagrams on the slate board, and the things Mara had said at the bench. "The soul is what fills," she said. "Not the Ember, not the glass. You. Your door is how it fills. And how it empties, when you use it."

"Go on."

"It fills slowly. At Kindled, it fills very slowly. And if you use more than comes in, you go down." She touched the edge of the blanket. "The floor is as low as you can go and still be all right."

"And under the floor?"

"You get sick."

"You get sick," the healer agreed. "Then you get worse than sick. That's mostly right. The part you're wrong about is the slowly." She sat down on the edge of the bed, which creaked. "It isn't just slow. It's narrow. Think of a cask with one tap in the bottom and one small funnel in the top. That's every person in this room. One door, one tap. They open it, they use a bit, they close it, the funnel tops it up overnight."

"And me?"

"You opened three taps at once, in a cask with the same funnel as everybody else." The healer's voice did not change. "For about forty breaths. With the frame pressing back on all of them. You went down faster than anyone I've ever measured. You're lucky you were standing in the middle of the Compact with a healer twenty paces off."

Kiva swallowed.

"So you'll have ash tonight," said the healer. "A measured dose. It'll lift you off the floor so you can sleep without being sick. The funnel will do the rest in a few days."

She went away and came back with a tray. On it was a small brass balance, a lead-lidded jar, a spoon, and a printed chit. She set the balance on the windowsill and lifted the lid off the jar. Inside was a fine grey powder, a little darker than wood ash, a little lighter than soot.

"Do you know what this is?" she said.

"Riftspawn ash."

"Ash," said the healer. "Just ash. When a Riftspawn is broken, it comes apart into this, and this is nobody's. It doesn't belong to a soul, so any soul can take it. That's why it's worth more than silver." She measured a level spoonful onto the balance pan and watched the needle. Then she took a pinch back off the spoon with her thumbnail and put it in the jar. "It's also why there isn't enough. It doesn't grow. It doesn't make more of itself. Every grain in this jar came off a Riftspawn the Home Line or the Sag watch killed. When it's spent, it's spent."

She tipped the spoonful into Kiva's palm.

It was warm, faintly, the way a stone is warm after sun. Kiva closed her hand around it, as she had been taught in the lower school, and breathed.

At first nothing happened. Then something did, slowly, and from the inside: the cold hollow feeling under her ribs, which she had not even noticed until it began to change, eased and filled. It was like drinking water after a long walk without knowing how thirsty you had been. Her hands stopped shaking. She had not noticed that they were shaking.

When she opened her palm, it was empty and clean.

The healer was filling in the chit. Kiva could read it upside down. She had been reading Mara's cards upside down across the bench since she was six.

*Rift ash, clean grade. 1 spoon, less 1 pinch. Recipient: Rowan, Kiva. Placement: none. Charged to: Rowan household, Gauge Row.*

"Charged," said Kiva.

The healer glanced at her. "Students are charged to their school's allotment. You haven't got a school."

"How much is a spoon?"

The healer told her.

Kiva did the arithmetic without wanting to. It came to most of what Mara made in a week from the lamp regulators. It was less than a week of the Assay contract. She lay back on the pillow and looked at the small dark fish in the corner of the ceiling.

"It'll be less tomorrow," said the healer, more gently. "You'll only need a pinch. And you'll get a school." She stood up and took her tray. "Sleep, if you can. Don't open anything."

"I don't know how to open anything," said Kiva.

"That," said the healer, "is what frightens me."

---

Anwen came at the eighth hour, when the lamps were turned down. She sat on the edge of the next bed, which belonged to a girl who had gone down to the kitchens, and took an apple out of her coat.

"They let you out?" said Kiva.

"We're allowed the whole floor until the ninth bell. Orientation." Anwen bit the apple. "It means a woman in green read us the rules for two hours. Don't open your door indoors. Don't open it near a patient. Don't open it in the canal. I asked why not in the canal, and she said that was a separate lecture."

Kiva laughed, and the lines in her chest pulled, and she stopped laughing.

Anwen watched her. She did not ask if it hurt. Kiva was grateful.

"Your three," said Anwen, nodding at her collar. "Everyone's talking about them. The boy in the bed by the door says you've got a fourth one on your back."

"I haven't."

"I told him." Anwen took another bite. "I also told him that if he said it again, I'd open my door in the canal."

"What would that do?"

"No idea. It sounded bad, though."

They sat for a while without talking. Out of the window, the canal was going dark, and the lamp-men were walking the towpath with their long poles, lighting the lamps one by one. Kiva counted them without meaning to. Seven, eight. They were a long way apart tonight; one had been left dark, near the lock.

"You said field," said Kiva. "In the pen."

Anwen nodded.

"The north rotation."

"One a season. Any school. It goes out with the Home Line for six weeks, past the Sag, where the tears come." Anwen turned the apple core over in her fingers. "My family's had the Fourth Lock for a hundred and ten years. My mother, her father, his mother. They've seen the Home Line go past on the towpath every spring of their lives, with their packs and their bars. My mother says they're the only people in Meridian who aren't playing a game."

"And Wren House doesn't send anyone."

"Not for nine years. Healers are too expensive to lose." Anwen's voice made the words sound like something printed on a sign. "But Ward isn't healing. Ward's walls. And walls are exactly what you want past the Sag." She put the core in her pocket. "There's a petition. You need a sponsor's signature and a partner record."

"What's a partner record?"

"A Ward held under load, in a logged drill, with somebody else depending on it. Witnessed and signed." Anwen shrugged. "You can't get one until a school lets you into a drill with a partner, and Wren House doesn't put first-years into drills with partners until the half-season. So I'll put my petition in tomorrow, and they'll tell me to wait, and I'll wait."

"That sounds like a plan."

"It's half a plan." Anwen looked at her. "What's yours?"

Kiva opened her mouth and found that she did not know.

All day, standing in the pen, she had had a plan. Get a door. Get a school. Get a row on the Table with a placement in pale gold, the same as everybody else, and then go home and help Mara with the regulators and start in the morning. It had not been a large plan. She had not wanted a large one. She had wanted the ordinary one.

"I don't have a school," she said.

"Not yet."

"I don't have a row."

"You've got three rows. They just keep rubbing each other out." Anwen stood up and brushed off her coat. "Somebody'll work it out. That's what assessors are for." She paused at the foot of the bed. "That woman who talked you down. Dane."

"Orla Dane."

"Crooked Line. She's a lane-mistress, I asked." Anwen frowned slightly. "She's not on the platform. She doesn't rule on anything. But when she said *let go of her*, they let go."

"I noticed," said Kiva.

At the ninth bell a steward came through and turned the lamps lower. He stopped at Kiva's bed and handed her a folded scrap of paper without a word.

She unfolded it. It was written in pencil, in large round letters that leaned to the left.

*STILL ALIVE? — P. CADE (ROW 94, A TERRIBLE ROW)*

Kiva held it for a long time after the steward had gone. Then she put it under her pillow, beside the disk, which she had taken off so the healer could reach the lines. It was cold against her fingers in the dark.

She lay awake listening to thirty-five other first-years breathing. Every so often one of them would murmur in their sleep, or a door somewhere in the house would close. Each time, her chest went tight, and the three lines burned, and she waited for something to open by itself.

Nothing did. Somewhere after midnight, without deciding to, she slept.

---

On Gauge Row, Mara Rowan sat at the bench with the trace.

She had brought it home in her toolbag, rolled tight in oilcloth, along with the burnt left coil from the Assay frame. The repairer on call had the right to the trace for twenty-four hours, for diagnosis, and Mara intended to use all twenty-four of them. The original would go to the records office in the morning, logged and sealed. That was the rule. She would follow the rule.

But first she would read it.

She unrolled it under the bench lamp and weighed the ends down with two brass test weights. It was a long strip of paper as wide as her hand, ruled faintly in blue, and across it ran three ink lines from the three pens of the drum. All day they had drawn the same thing for every candidate: two flat lines and one that climbed, held, and fell. Three hundred and thirty-two times. She had wound most of the day's roll onto the spool and cut it at the last candidate before Kiva, and what she had in front of her now was the length she had not been able to stop thinking about.

It began like all the others. Three flat lines.

Then, at the place where the reader would have said *ten*, all three lifted.

Mara picked up her calipers.

She measured the gap between the first line's lift and the second's. Then the second's and the third's. The drum turned at a known rate; she had set it herself. The pen on the Ember wire had moved first, by a hair. Two-tenths of a breath, perhaps less. The pen on the glass wire had moved next. The pen on the slab plate had moved last, almost in the same instant. The lines did not just lift. They climbed and fell and climbed again, each in its own rhythm, crossing over one another as they went, for forty breaths, until the place where she had pulled the fuse and the pens had all dropped to nothing together.

She sat back.

She knew what the platform had seen. The platform had seen the gauges jerk and the Table write three bars and rub them out, and it had concluded that the frame was broken. It was not a stupid conclusion. The frame *had* been broken; the coil in front of her on the bench was black along half its windings, burnt through where all three signals had run into it at once. The gauges had been reading nonsense, and the Table had been showing nonsense, and Ysra Crest had been right not to sign it.

But the pens did not go through the coil.

Mara had rebuilt them herself, three winters ago, because the old design had run the pens off the gauge needles and every fault in a gauge had shown up on the trace. Now each pen had its own wire straight to its own door: Ember rest, glass case, slab plate. The pens were stupid in a different way from the gauges. They could not add or compare or project. They could only draw what touched them.

What had touched them was three doors, drawing at once, all of them real.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she got up and went to the shelf over the bench, where she kept the abstracts. Mara reconciled instrument records for Wren House as well as the municipal office, and over eleven years she had copied out, in her own hand, every Wren House injury entry that touched an instrument: every burnt gauge, every miscalibrated balance, every reading that had come out wrong and hurt someone. It was not her job to keep them. She kept them because a reading that hurt someone once could hurt someone again, and she liked to know.

She turned to the index and ran her finger down it. *Competing door.*

There were three entries in six hundred years.

The first was from 2311. *Two-door draw at Kindling, Ember and rift. Burns, chest and right arm. Recovered in a season. Placed Ember.* The second was from 2502. *Two-door draw under load, still and rift. Burns, chest, left arm, hand. Recovered. Fine touch lost in the first two fingers of the left hand. Not recovered.* Under that, in the remarks, someone at Wren House had written a single line two centuries ago. *Healing closed the burn. It could not give back the feeling.*

The third was from 2641, thirty-nine years ago, and it was only two lines long.

*Two-needle reading at Kindling. Ruled instrument fault.*

And in the remarks: *Trace withdrawn.*

Mara had read that entry many times over the years. She had always meant to ask someone at the records office what it meant. *Withdrawn* was not a word the records office used. Traces were filed, or sealed, or copied. They were not withdrawn.

She put the abstracts back on the shelf.

On the bench beside the trace was a slip of paper that had been pushed under the door while she was at Wren House. It was from the municipal instrument office, on the office's stamped stock, signed by the deputy clerk.

*Pending review of the Kindling Day frame fault, M. Rowan is recused from all service of the Assay frame. Return the left coil and the day's trace to the office by the second hour. The frame will be serviced by the district's second repairer until the review concludes.*

Mara read it twice. It was a correct notice. If she had been the deputy clerk, she would have written it herself. The repairer who had rebuilt the coil on Thursday was the aunt of the candidate who had burnt it out on Saturday, and anybody who looked at that would ask questions, and some of the questions would be fair.

It still meant that the Assay contract, which was more than half of what came into the house, had ended at the fourth hour of the afternoon.

She did the arithmetic. She did it twice, the way she did everything. The regulators. The canal-lock gauges. The lamp-line work she had turned down in the spring because the Assay frame paid better and needed her more. The tenancy on the house, which was a municipal tenancy, held on the strength of her municipal post. Kiva's ash, which would come on a chit tomorrow, charged to the house because Kiva had no school.

Then she put the pencil down and stopped doing arithmetic, because it would still be there in the morning.

She pulled a clean sheet of ruled paper toward her and uncapped her pen.

It took her an hour and forty minutes to copy the trace. She did it by hand, with the calipers, a tenth of a breath at a time, marking every lift and fall of every line onto the ruled sheet beside it. Her eyes ached. The lamp burned down and she trimmed it. Twice she had to stop because her hand was shaking, and the second time she realized it was not from tiredness.

When she was done, she rolled the original back into its oilcloth for the records office, tied it, and wrote the date on the tag.

Then she took the frame's card out of the brass box and turned it to the remarks column. Under *Left coil rebuilt. Watch.*, she wrote three more lines in her small flat hand.

*Left coil burnt, cross-feed. Gauges unreliable.*

*Pens honest. Ember lifted first, by 0.2 of a breath.*

*Three.*

She sat looking at the last word until the lamp began to gutter again. Then she put out the light and went up to bed. She did not sleep for a long time.

---

Mara came to Wren House in the morning with the dawn still grey on the canal, and Kiva knew from her face that she had not slept either.

"Can you walk?" said Mara.

"Yes."

"Then walk." Mara put a hand under her elbow anyway, and kept it there down the stairs and out of the green door and along the towpath, past the Fourth Lock, where a woman with Anwen's long face was turning the windlass and did not look up.

They went home the long way, by the Founders' Yard. Kiva did not ask why. She thought she knew.

The yard was half-cleared. The banners were down, and the stewards were stacking the pen ropes, and the Assay frame stood under its canvas with a man Kiva didn't know kneeling at its south leg with a toolbag that was not Mara's. On the south wall, the Table was lit for the morning, as it was every day for the first week, so families could come and read the rows. Somebody had reloaded the plates. Three hundred and forty-four rows in pale gold, and in among the R's, just below Kellan Renn's *Entered*, a single line that was shorter than all the rest.

*Rowan, Kiva. Unread. Held for review.*

A boy of about ten was standing in front of the wall with his mother, reading it. He turned around, saw Kiva, and looked straight at her collar. Then he tugged his mother's sleeve and pointed.

Mara steered Kiva away by the elbow. "Home," she said.

At home, Mara made porridge and Kiva ate it, and then Kiva stood up and went to the bench.

"Sit down," said Mara.

"I'm all right."

"You're grey. Sit down."

"I want to do the lock card." Kiva pulled the canal-lock gauge toward her, the one with its glass off, and found its card in the brass box. "It's been there since Thursday. You said the Fourth Lock wanted it by the anniversary."

Mara looked at her for a moment. Then she sat down on the other stool and passed her the calipers.

They worked without talking. It was the way they had always worked: Kiva read the instrument column aloud, and Mara read the paper column, and when the two did not agree, they stopped and found out why. The lock gauge was reading a little high, a hair's width on the dial at every mark. Kiva found the reason in the spring, which had been bent slightly at the coil's end, probably when someone at the lock had dropped it. Mara straightened it with the flat-nosed pliers. Kiva read it again. It agreed.

Kiva wrote *Spring bent at coil end, straightened. Agrees.* in the remarks, and felt, for the first time since the stone had gone cold under her boots, like herself.

"I was recused," said Mara.

Kiva put the pen down.

"From the frame. It's a correct notice." Mara was looking at the lock gauge, not at her. "The repairer who rebuilt the coil is the aunt of the girl who burnt it. They have to be able to say that nobody in the house touched the reading."

"Nobody did."

"I know. They know. They still have to be able to say it." Mara put the pliers down. "It means the Assay work's stopped. Until the review."

Kiva understood. She had read every card in the box, and she knew which ones paid for the house. "How long is a review?"

"As long as it takes."

"And the ash."

Mara did look at her then. "How do you know about the ash?"

"I read the chit." Kiva felt her face go hot. "Upside down."

Mara almost laughed. She did not quite manage it. "Of course you did." She reached across and turned the canal-lock card around so it faced the right way, and squared its edges against the bench. "Listen to me. The ash is mine to worry about. The house is mine to worry about. You're thirteen. You opened a door, which is what thirteen-year-olds do, and it opened wider than anyone's ever seen. That isn't a debt. It's just a fact."

"It's a debt," said Kiva. "It's on the chit."

"It's on the chit," Mara agreed. "And it's mine."

Kiva did not argue. She looked at the canal-lock card, at the two columns that agreed and the remarks that explained why. She thought about the cask with three taps and one funnel. She thought about forty breaths of falling.

Then she reached up and touched the cord at her collar.

"It was cold," she said. "The whole time. It didn't do anything."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Then she got up, went to the brass card box, and walked her fingers along the tabs to the back, past the lamp regulators and the canal gauges and the municipal clocks, to a section Kiva had never had reason to open. She pulled out a single card and laid it on the bench.

It was an ordinary instrument card, with the instrument column and the paper column and the narrow strip for remarks. But the instrument column was empty, and in the space at the top where the name of the instrument went, Mara had written, four years ago:

*Disk, near-black, two thumbs wide, nine grooves each face. Received from Home Line courier by M. Rowan for K. Rowan.*

In the paper column was the receipt number, and the date, and a stamp Kiva recognized: the plain ring-and-bar of the Home Line.

In the remarks, Mara had written: *Appointment sealed. Bearer: K. Rowan. Do not open seal.*

"You made a card for it," said Kiva.

"I make a card for everything that comes into this house." Mara tapped the empty instrument column. "That's where I'd write what it does. I've been waiting four years to write something there. It's still empty." She looked at Kiva. "It was cold this morning when I took it off your pillow. It was cold last night. It's been cold every day since it came. Whatever happened to you yesterday, it was you. Not it."

Kiva looked at the empty column for a long time.

"Who sealed it?"

"The person who released it. I don't know who. The courier didn't say, and I didn't ask, because the Home Line doesn't answer that kind of question." Mara put the card back in its place in the box. "When you're of age, you can go to them and ask to have the seal read. That's in the appointment. Until then, it's yours, and it's sealed, and that's all."

Somebody knocked at the door.

Mara opened it. A Compact runner stood on the step in a grey coat with a satchel, and he handed her two envelopes and went on to the next house without a word.

The first envelope was stiff and official, sealed with the Compact's red. Mara opened it standing up and read it, and Kiva watched her mouth go thin.

"Assay House," said Mara. "The quiet room. The second day, at the third hour. *Single-door isolation readings, under the senior assessor. Guardian to attend.*"

"What does that mean?"

"It means they're going to try to read one door at a time." Mara folded the letter. "By covering up the other two."

"Will that work?"

Mara did not answer. She handed Kiva the second envelope instead.

It was a plain folded sheet, not sealed, with Kiva's name on the outside in a narrow, upright hand. Inside was a single line.

*Eat breakfast before you come. Tired is worse than frightened. — O. Dane*

Kiva read it twice. Then she folded it and put it in her pocket, and went back to the bench, and picked up the next card in the pile, because there were eleven more instruments waiting and none of them would reconcile themselves.
