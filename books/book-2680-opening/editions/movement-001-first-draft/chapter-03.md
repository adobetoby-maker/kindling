# Chapter 3 — One at a Time

The quiet room in Assay House had no windows, and its walls were lined with grey felt from the floor to a little above Kiva's head.

"So nothing echoes," said the clerk who let them in. He was a thin young man with ink on his cuffs. "And so nothing breaks if it's thrown."

"Does a lot get thrown?" said Kiva.

He considered. "Not a lot."

The room held three bench gauges on iron stands, each one a smaller cousin of the great frame in the yard: one dial, one needle, one pen, one little drum of paper turning on a clock spring. Mara looked at them the moment she came through the door. Kiva watched her hands twitch toward her coat pocket, where her calipers were, and then go still. She sat down on the bench along the wall where guardians sat, and folded her hands in her lap, and did not look at the gauges again.

It was, Kiva thought, the hardest thing she had ever seen Mara do.

Ysra Crest came in at the third hour exactly. She was taller than she looked on the platform, with a narrow, pale, deeply tired face and a dark green-trimmed coat buttoned to the collar. She carried a ruling board under her arm with a sheet already clipped to it. Behind her came Orla Dane, who nodded once to Mara and sat at the end of the guardians' bench without being asked.

"Lane-Mistress Dane is here for the Crooked Line School, as an observer," said Ysra. "She may speak. She may not direct." She looked at Kiva. "Do you understand what we're doing today?"

"You're going to read one door at a time," said Kiva. "By taking away the other two."

"By taking away what each of them answers to," said Ysra. "The Ember door answers to an Ember. The rift door answers to tear-glass, at the Kindling. The still door answers to standing stone." She set the ruling board on a lectern. "If the door's interface isn't in the room, the door has nothing to open onto. In theory, that leaves the one door we want to read and nothing else to interfere with it."

"In theory," said Orla, mildly, from the bench.

Ysra did not look at her. "In theory," she agreed.

---

They started with the Ember.

Kiva stood on a square of plain pine boards in the middle of the room, nowhere near any stone. The clerk had taken the tear-glass out to the corridor in its lead case and closed the door on it. On the stand to her right was the issued Ember from the Kindling, the same dull ring of metal, its rest wired to the first bench gauge.

"Right fist through the ring," said Ysra. "Open the Ember door. Only the Ember door. When you have it, hold it, and count with me."

Kiva put her fist through the ring.

She had thought about this all night: how to open just one. She thought she knew what the Ember door felt like now. It was the coal against her breastbone that sat furthest to the right, the one that had moved first. If she could find it again, alone, and leave the others where they were—

She breathed out and reached for it.

The Ember moved. It lurched over her knuckles, stopped, lurched, and became the same ugly hooked bar as before, dark, as long as her hand. The bench gauge's needle climbed. Kiva felt a small fierce pleasure.

Then her left palm went hard.

It happened without her. The air an inch from her open left hand turned solid and slightly warm, the way it had in the yard, and pushed. A crease appeared in the felt on the wall four paces away, a dent the size of a dinner plate, as if somebody had leaned a fist into it.

"There's no glass," said the clerk, sounding offended.

"There was at the Kindling," said Orla.

Kiva's feet went heavy. Through the pine boards, through the floor beneath the boards, she felt the same deep dull weight of the still door taking hold, even though there was no stone, even though she was standing on wood. The bench gauge's needle, which had been sitting at two marks, jumped to four, fell to one, and climbed again.

"Count," said Ysra. "One. Two."

On *three*, the hooked bar on Kiva's fist shivered and sank back into the ring.

"Unheld," said Ysra, and wrote it down. "Reach between one and four. Close the doors, candidate."

"I'm trying," said Kiva through her teeth.

"Breathe out," said Orla. "All the way."

It took twenty breaths. When her left palm finally went soft and her feet came back into her boots, Kiva was shaking, and the three lines under her shirt were hot.

Ysra looked at the trace on the bench gauge's little drum for a long time. Then she said, "Again," and they did it again, and it went exactly the same way, except that this time the needle went to five before it fell.

---

The rift door was worse.

The clerk took the Ember out to the corridor and brought the tear-glass in. Kiva held her left palm over it. The glass rang almost at once, low and loud, and the Ward came up under her hand as hard as a table top. The second gauge woke.

But her right fist was empty, and it did not want to be.

It clenched by itself. The fingers curled in and locked, nails driving into her palm, and a cramp ran up her forearm to the elbow as if the hand were reaching for something that was not there. She heard herself gasp. The Ember door was open and straining, and there was nothing in the room for it to open onto.

"Her hand," said Mara from the bench, very quietly.

"I see it," said Ysra. She was watching the second gauge. Its needle was steady for a breath, and then it began to swing, slowly, in long arcs, from one mark to three and back again. "Reach one to three. The rift reading's changing in time with the hand." She looked up. "Candidate, can you open your right hand?"

Kiva tried. The fingers would not move. "No."

"Close the rift door."

That, at least, she half knew how to do now. She breathed out, all the way, and waited, and breathed again. After nine breaths the Ward under her palm softened and was gone, and the glass stopped ringing. Her right hand stayed locked for another twelve.

When it finally opened, there were four red crescents in her palm from her own nails.

The clerk brought her a cup of water. She drank it with her left hand.

---

They tried the still door last, on a slab of grey practice stone that the clerk wheeled in on a trolley, with both the Ember and the glass out in the corridor.

Kiva stepped onto the slab.

The still door opened as soon as her weight settled. She felt it go down through her heels like roots. The third gauge's needle climbed and stopped.

"Clerk," said Ysra. "Push."

The clerk came and put his hand on Kiva's shoulder, apologetically, and pushed. She did not move. He pushed harder, with his weight behind it, and his shoes slid on the floor, and she did not move. He looked at Ysra and shook his head.

"Hold steady at," said Ysra, counting.

Kiva held. She concentrated on her feet. She thought about nothing else. Her right hand was open. Her left was at her side, palm down. *Just this one*, she thought. *Just this.*

On the sixth count, her left palm went hard.

There was no glass in the room. There had been no glass in the room for an hour. The Ward came anyway, a slab of warm air standing out from her open hand, and the clerk, who was leaning on her shoulder, lost his balance against it and sat down on the floor.

"Sorry," said Kiva. "Sorry, sorry—"

"Don't speak. Close it."

She closed it. It took longer this time. When it was done and the clerk was on his feet again, brushing off his coat, Ysra stood at the lectern and did not write anything for a long while.

"The rift door doesn't need the glass," said Orla from the bench. Her voice was perfectly neutral. "Not after the Kindling. No rift-door student in Wren House carries a sliver around with them. The glass is how the door finds its way the first time. After that, the door is in the person."

"I know that," said Ysra.

"And the Ember door can't open fully without an Ember. But it can strain."

"I know that too." Ysra set down her pen. She turned to Kiva, and there was nothing unkind in her face, only a kind of weary precision. "Do you understand what we've learned?"

Kiva thought about it. "That you can't take the doors away," she said slowly. "Because they're not in the room. They're in me."

"Yes."

"And when you read one, the others pull on it. So the number changes."

"Yes." Ysra picked up the ruling sheet. "Reach is the measure of how much of you is lit through a door. At the Kindling, it's almost always one or two. It goes up as you grow. Over years, under real use and real pressure, that's what rank is: reach that you can show again, every time. Kindled, Ember, Flame, Fire, Torch." She turned the sheet so Kiva could see the column of figures. "Your reach for each door changes from one breath to the next, from one to five, depending on what the other two are doing. I can't sign a number that changes when you breathe."

"What about hold?" said Kiva.

"Hold is control. How long you keep what's lit steady against a load." Ysra tapped the sheet. "Unheld, unheld, short. Two, two, and six. Every time, the hold ends when another door interferes." She paused. "Your control of any one door is as bad as I've ever measured in a Kindled who wasn't ill."

Kiva heard Mara shift on the bench.

"That isn't a judgement," said Ysra. "It's a reading. Rank and control are different measures. A candidate can have very little reach and excellent hold. You saw one on Saturday. A candidate can have a great deal of reach and no hold at all." She looked at Kiva for a moment longer. "I don't know yet which you are. I don't know if you have a great deal of anything. That's what I'm trying to find out, and that's why I'm not going to guess."

She unclipped the sheet and wrote across the bottom of it, in a short decided hand, and turned it face down.

"Same time in three days," she said. "Guardian, thank you for coming. Lane-Mistress." She nodded to Orla. Then she picked up her ruling board and left, and the clerk wheeled the slab out after her.

---

"She's fair," said Orla, outside on the steps. "I want you to know that. Some of them aren't. She is."

Mara looked at her. "You knew she'd fail."

"I thought she might." Orla buttoned her coat. The wind came off the canal and blew grey dust across the square in front of Assay House. "Isolation works for one-door candidates who are confused. It doesn't work for a candidate who's not confused at all. Just very full." She looked at Kiva. "Are you hungry?"

"No," said Kiva.

"You're lying, but it's polite." Orla turned to Mara. "I'd like to take her to the Crooked Line shed for an hour. Not to train. To show her something. You can come."

"Why?" said Mara.

Orla seemed to think about the question seriously. "Because she's been told three times today what she can't do," she said. "And nobody's shown her what a door looks like when it works."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "One hour," and came with them.

---

The Crooked Line shed was long and plain, with a packed earth floor and racks along one wall full of practice bars, padded sacks, coils of rope, and old dented helmets. At the far end was a stove, and on the stove was a kettle, and by the stove was a table with three chairs. Orla put the kettle on before she did anything else.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you."

They sat.

Orla stood in the middle of the floor and lifted her right hand. On her fist was her Ember, a plain dark band like Rhea Sorn's, but older, scratched along one side.

The metal ran forward and became a blade.

It was the plainest shape Kiva had ever seen. A short straight blade as long as a hand, with a squared-off end. Not elegant. Not ugly either. It looked like something designed to be easy to hold.

Then a thread of light woke along its edge, and stayed.

It was not bright. It was a dim, steady, pale yellow, like a lamp turned all the way down, and it lay along the edge of the blade from root to tip without a flicker. Orla looked at it for a moment. Then, with her left hand, she reached over to the stove, took the kettle off, and poured three cups of tea.

She poured them carefully, one after the other, without looking at the blade. The light did not move.

"Milk?" she said to Mara.

"No," said Mara faintly.

"Kiva?"

"Yes. Please."

Orla added milk from a jug and stirred it with a spoon, still with her left hand, and brought the cups to the table, and sat down in the third chair with the blade still lit on her right fist, resting on her knee.

"I'm Torch," she said. "I have been for nineteen years. That's rank. It means that this much of me is lit"—she lifted the blade slightly—"and it's as much as one door will ever light. I'm not going to get any higher. Nobody does, through one door. That's not modesty. It's the ceiling."

Kiva looked at the light. It had not wavered once.

"This," said Orla, "is not rank. The light being steady while I pour tea and talk is control. I've had to work for every second of it. Most Torches I know can't pour tea." She considered. "Some of them can't pour tea with both hands and no blade."

Mara made a small sound that was nearly a laugh.

"Rank is how much," said Orla. "Control is how well. They don't come together. You can be a Torch with poor control, and you'll be dangerous, and everybody will be afraid of you, and they'll be right. You can be Kindled with superb control, and you'll be a Kindled who doesn't cut anyone by accident. Kellan Renn is the second kind. He's Kindled, like every child in that yard on Saturday. He's just a very well controlled one."

"And I'm neither," said Kiva.

"You're the first kind of problem, in three places." Orla took a sip of tea. "Do you know what the light costs?"

Kiva thought. "Reserve," she said. "From the soul. And attention."

"Concentration. It's a second thing to hold, on top of the shape." Orla lifted the blade a little. "Watch."

The blade became a spear-point. Before it did, her hand closed slightly on the band, and Kiva saw the half-breath pause, the same one she had seen on Rhea Sorn. The light went out during the change and came back along the new edge a breath later. Then two knives. Then the plain blade again.

"Every switch, I let go of the light first," said Orla. "Then the shape changes. Then I light it again. If I try to keep the light through the switch, I lose both." The blade sank back into the band, and the light went out, and she put her right hand around her teacup as if it had been an ordinary hand all along. "That's at Torch. With one door. Do you see what I'm telling you?"

"That you can't do two things at once," said Kiva.

"That you can't do two things at once *through one door* without paying for each." Orla set the cup down. "You have three doors. At the Kindling, every child's whole soul goes through the first door that opens, because there's only one way out. Your soul found three ways out at once. Each of them wants all of you." She looked at Kiva steadily. "That's why they fight. Not because something's wrong with you. Because there's only one of you, and you're being asked for three times."

Kiva looked into her tea. It had a skin on it, from the milk. She broke it with the spoon.

"Can it be fixed?" said Mara.

"I don't know," said Orla. "That's the honest answer. I don't know anyone who's had to fix it." She did not look at Mara as she said it. She looked at the stove. "But I'd like to see something, if Kiva's willing. There's a practice Ember on the rack. One door. No load, no frame. Just to see what happens when nobody's measuring."

Kiva looked at Mara.

Mara's jaw was tight. "No stone," she said. "Nothing on the floor she can root into."

"Packed earth over a wooden underfloor," said Orla. "She'll root anyway, if the still door wakes. But there's nothing for it to hold onto that matters."

Mara looked at the shed for a long moment, at the racks and the rope and the dented helmets. Then she nodded once.

---

The practice Ember was older than the issued one and smelled of oil. Kiva put her fist through it, standing in the middle of the earth floor, and Orla stood four paces in front of her with her hands at her sides.

"Don't reach for the door," said Orla. "That's what you did this morning. You went looking for it, and when you go looking for something you open everything on the way. Just let the Ember door know there's an Ember here. Feel the metal on your knuckles. That's all."

Kiva felt the metal on her knuckles. It was cool and slightly greasy.

"Now. When it comes, it'll come ugly. Let it. Don't try to make it better. Don't try to make it like mine, or Kellan's. The ugly shape is the one your door chooses when you don't interfere. Choosing your own comes later, if it comes at all."

The coal under her breastbone, the right-hand one, warmed.

The Ember lurched. The hooked bar came. It was exactly as ugly as before, short and heavy and hooked at the end like a drain lifter, and Kiva felt her fist tighten around the band to hold it there—

"Loosen your fingers," said a voice from the doorway. "Not much. Just a little."

Kiva did not look around. She loosened her fingers, a little.

The bar steadied. The heaviness at its hooked end, which had been dragging her wrist down, evened out along the metal. It was still ugly. But it was the same ugly all the way along, and it was not dragging.

"One," said Orla quietly. "Two. Three."

On *four*, Kiva's left palm grew warm.

She felt it coming. For the first time, she felt it before it happened, a slow pressure under the skin of her left hand like a door being leaned on from the other side. She did not try to stop it. She did not know how. But she knew it was coming, and she knew which door it was, and that was something she had not known an hour ago.

"Breathe out," said Orla.

She breathed out. The pressure in her left palm wavered. The bar on her right fist shivered, and sank back into the band.

"Four," said Orla. "Short, four." She looked at Kiva with an expression Kiva could not read. "That's not much. It's also the first time you've held anything at all for longer than the next door took to notice."

Kiva lowered her fist. Her hand was shaking, but not much. She turned around.

Kellan Renn stood in the doorway of the shed in his grey first-year coat, with a practice bar under his arm. He looked as if he had come to put it back on the rack and had stopped.

"You held your fist too tight," he said. "At the Kindling. That's why the end came heavy." He walked past her to the rack and hung the bar on its peg. "It's the same on everybody. Grip harder, the metal goes to the end of the grip."

"Thank you," said Kiva.

"I'm not helping." He straightened the bar on its peg, though it was already straight. "I just can't stand watching it done wrong."

Then he nodded to Orla, who nodded back without any expression at all, and went out.

---

On the third day, Kiva went to watch the yards.

Nobody told her not to, and she was not allowed to do anything else. Ysra's sheet had said *no unsupervised door work*, and Orla's supervision covered one hour in the shed every afternoon, and the rest of the day was empty. Mara had gone out at dawn with her toolbag to a lamp-line job on the far side of the canal, the kind she had stopped taking in the spring, and the house was very quiet without her.

So Kiva went to the Founders' Yard and stood at the rail.

The Crooked Line first-years were drilling in the straight lanes along the east wall. There were thirty of them in grey, in lines of three, and at the start of every drill they said the Declaration together, the way the lower school had said its morning words, flat and fast and all at once.

"Holding. Taking. Letting go. Yours. Mine."

Then the warden blew a whistle, and the first three in each lane walked toward the post at the end while a sack swung at them off a frame from the side, and they stopped it, or struck it, or stepped out of its way.

Kiva watched for an hour. She saw the same thing every time. The Declaration was said at the start, all five words, like a password at a gate, and then nobody said anything during the drill at all. The sack came, and each student did what they did, and the warden called out a number.

Kellan was in the first lane. He did not need the Declaration. He met the sack with the flat of his clean blade and turned it aside without seeming to try, and the warden called "Good," every time, which he did not call for anybody else.

At the Stonehand yard on the other side of the square, it was different.

There, the first-years in brown stood on a row of low flat stones, one to a stone, with their feet apart and their hands at their sides. Second-years walked down the row and pushed them, one at a time, hard, in the chest. Most of the first-years staggered and stepped off. One or two did not.

Perrin Cade went off his stone every time.

He went off it backward, forward, and once sideways into the boy next to him, who also went off his stone. Each time, he climbed back on, said something that made the second-year pushing him snort, and set his feet again. The warden at the end of the row was writing numbers on a slate. When she got to Perrin's she wrote something short.

At the noon bell, Perrin came over to the rail with his hair full of dust.

"Did you see?" he said.

"I saw," said Kiva.

"Four," said Perrin. "Every time. Four breaths. I've got the best four in Stonehand Hall. They're thinking of naming a stone after me." He leaned his arms on the rail beside her and looked at her collar, quite openly, which nobody else did. "Do they hurt?"

"Sometimes."

"Does anything happen when they hurt?"

"Sometimes a door opens," said Kiva. "Sometimes it's the other way round."

Perrin nodded slowly, as if this were reasonable. Then Anwen came across the square from the canal side, holding three hot rolls from the bread cart wrapped in a cloth, and gave one to each of them without asking.

"They told me to wait," she said.

"For the petition?" said Kiva.

"Half-season. Like I said." Anwen bit into her roll. "The rotation mistress was nice about it. She said she'd keep my name on the top of the list. She said that to everybody, I asked the girl after me."

They ate their rolls at the rail and watched the Crooked Line lanes empty for the noon break, and the grey first-years go past in their threes, talking. A few of them glanced at Kiva. Most didn't.

"I'd trade you," said Perrin suddenly.

Kiva looked at him.

"Three doors." He was not smiling. "I've got one door and it's mostly a wall I fall off. You've got three, and you've got Dane, and you've got Crest herself coming down to the quiet room for you. They've got Stonehand first-years queuing for a slate number." He tore a piece off his roll and didn't eat it. "I'd trade you."

"You wouldn't," said Kiva.

"No," said Perrin. "I know. I'd still say it." He ate the piece of roll. "That's the thing about me. I always say it."

Anwen looked from one of them to the other. "He's right," she said to Kiva. "He does always say it. He said it to me yesterday about my ribbon."

"Green's a good colour," said Perrin. "I look terrible in brown."

Kiva laughed, and the lines in her chest pulled, and she kept laughing anyway, a little, because it was the first time since Saturday that something had been funny without being frightening as well.

---

That night, she tried it alone.

She did not decide to. She was lying in bed with her fists clenched against the blanket, and she was thinking about the quiet room, and about Ysra Crest saying *I can't sign a number that changes when you breathe.* She was thinking that if she could only keep the other two doors shut, the number would stop changing, and somebody would sign it, and she would have a row and a school and a ribbon and a reason for the chit.

She got up and went down to the front room in her socks.

The bench was dark. Mara was asleep upstairs; she had come home after the eighth bell with her hands black from the lamp-line and gone straight to bed. The brass card box stood at the end of the bench, closed. Above it, on the shelf, were the abstracts and the spare gauge glasses in their rack.

Kiva stood in the middle of the floor on the bare boards.

*The still door*, she thought. *Just that one. It's the quietest. Nobody will hear it.*

She closed both fists as tight as they would go. She pressed her left hand flat against her thigh, hard, as if she could hold the rift door shut with it. She held her right fist against her ribs. Then she breathed out and let her weight settle into her heels.

The still door opened. She felt it go down through the boards, into the joists, into the ground under the house.

*Now just hold that.* She held it. She held her fists tight. She held her breath. She held everything she had, the way you hold a door shut in a wind—

Her left palm burned against her thigh.

The Ward came out of it sideways, because her hand was pressed flat to her leg, and there was nowhere else for it to go. It went out along the floor in a flat hard sheet like a shove from a giant hand. It hit the leg of the bench. The bench jumped. The rack of spare gauge glasses on the shelf above it rattled, tipped, and slid off the end, and the brass card box went with it.

The noise was enormous.

Glass broke. The card box hit the floor on its corner and burst open, and for a long moment, in the dark, Kiva heard the soft endless sound of eleven years of cards sliding out across the boards.

Then Mara was on the stairs with a lamp.

She stopped at the bottom. She looked at the floor: the glass, the box, the hundreds of cards fanned out in a long pale spill from the bench to the hearth. She looked at Kiva standing barefoot in the middle of it, with her fists still clenched.

"You opened a door," said Mara. "In the house."

"I was trying to shut them."

"You *opened a door in the house.*" Mara's voice was not loud. It was worse than loud. "Next to the glass. In your socks. With nobody here but me, asleep."

"I have to learn—"

"Not like this." Mara set the lamp down on the bench. Her hand was shaking. "Do you know what's in the Wren House books? I have them. I copied them. There are three cases like yours in six hundred years. Three. One of them burned her arm to the hand, and they healed the burn, and she never felt anything in her fingers again for the rest of her life. They healed it, Kiva. It was healed. It just never came back."

Kiva looked at her own left hand. The palm was still warm. The four red crescents from the quiet room had faded to pink.

"I have to learn to shut them," she said again, more quietly.

"Not alone." Mara knelt down on the floor among the cards. She picked one up, looked at it, and put it face down on the boards beside her, very carefully, as if it were made of glass too. "Not in here. Not where I can't get to you."

They picked up the cards together. It took until past the second hour of the morning. Kiva sorted them back into the box by district and instrument, and Mara swept the glass, and neither of them said anything until the last card was in its place and the lid was closed.

Then Mara went back up to bed, and Kiva sat on the bottom stair in the dark and looked at her hands.

She had held both fists as tight as they would go, and the door had burst out of the one she was holding hardest. She did not know what that meant yet. She only knew she had done exactly what she meant to do, as well as she could do it, and the house had nearly come down around her for it.
