# Chapter 4 — Nobody Can Sign It

The ruling room of the Meridian Compact was on the top floor of Assay House, under the slates, and in winter it was the coldest room in the building. Ysra Crest had always thought that was deliberate. Nobody stayed in it longer than they had to.

On the fifth evening after the anniversary, she laid two sheets of paper on the long table and waited while the four heads of the Compact read them.

The first sheet was her own: two sessions in the quiet room, six attempts, every figure she had taken. Reach between one and five on every door, changing from breath to breath. Hold two, two, six; then three, one, five. Every hold ending at the moment another door woke.

The second was a copy of the Kindling Day trace, certified by the records office that morning.

"The gauges were burnt," said the Warden of Stonehand Hall. He was a broad, slow man with a voice like a cart on gravel, and he had been pushing first-years off stones for thirty years. "Everyone saw. The fuse went."

"The gauges were burnt," said Ysra. "The pens weren't. The pens run on their own wires. The repairer rebuilt them that way three winters ago, and the records office has the order on file." She touched the second sheet. "Three doors drew at once. All three were real."

"The repairer," said the Mistress of Wren House. She was a sharp, small woman in green, perhaps sixty, with spectacles pushed up into her white hair. "Is the girl's aunt."

"The repairer filed the original trace by the second hour, sealed, as the rule requires. The records office copied it. I've compared their copy against her repair order. The wiring's what she says it is." Ysra did not raise her voice. She never did, in this room. "If anyone here wants to argue that the aunt altered a trace she had no way of knowing would matter, before her niece's Kindling, to produce a reading nobody's ever seen, I'll write it down. I'll also write down that I don't believe it."

Nobody wanted to argue that. She had not thought they would.

"So she's real," said the Head of the Crooked Line School. He was the youngest of the four, a lean grey-coated man with a lane warden's weathered hands. "Three doors. Fine. What's the ruling?"

"There isn't one," said Ysra. "That's why you're here."

---

She let them talk. That was the first rule of the ruling room, and she had learned it in her first year as assessor from the woman who had held the job before her: let them say everything, because whatever they don't say tonight they'll say in the yard tomorrow, where you can't answer it.

The Warden of Stonehand went first. "We can't put her on a load stone," he said. "Our floors are written for the still door. Every push, every count, every stop. If she roots and Wards at the same time on the push line, she'll break a second-year's ribs and won't know she's done it. I've got sixty first-years. I'm not risking sixty for one."

"Wren House can't take her," said the Mistress. "Our abort lines are by door. A healer standing beside a rift-door student can feel when the door is getting near its floor. It's a trained skill. It takes years. Nobody in my house can read three doors at once in one person. If she goes under in my house, under my healers, the house is liable." She took off her spectacles and folded them. "And I won't ask any of them to stand where they can't see."

"The Crooked Line can take her," said the Head. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged. "Can. Doesn't mean should. Our lanes are field method, which means anything that walks through the gate. But lane time is finite. A supervised student takes a lane-mistress away from twelve others, for every hour, for a season. Dane's the only one who's petitioned." He paused. "Dane's petition was on my desk before the fuse was cold."

Ysra had a copy of the petition. It was very short. *I request supervision of the candidate Rowan for the term of her review. — O. Dane, Lane-Mistress.* It gave no reasons. Orla Dane's petitions never did.

The fourth chair, the one in plain undyed wool, had said nothing yet. The Home Line's representative was an old man with a weathered face and a cough, and he sat with his hands folded on the head of a stick. Ysra had always found him the hardest of the four to read.

"The Home Line doesn't take first-years," he said now. "Never has. Not until they've had a season somewhere. You all know that." He coughed. "I'll say one thing. Whatever you do, put it down plainly. Put down what you knew and what you didn't."

"That's my job," said Ysra.

"It is," the old man agreed, and said nothing else.

Ysra looked down at her two sheets.

There was a way to do this that would be easy. She could rule an instrument fault. The burnt gauges would support it, and the Table had shown nonsense, and nobody in the yard on Saturday had understood what they were looking at. She could place the girl in whichever door the numbers liked best on a good morning, and write *fault, reread on retest*, and the Compact would go on as it was.

She would also be signing something she knew was not true.

Ysra had been the senior assessor for eleven years, and in that time she had refused to sign forty-one placements. She had the list. Every refusal had cost something: a family's anger, a school's displeasure, a season of argument. But it was the refusals that made the signatures worth anything. A signature from someone who signed everything was only ink.

"I sign what I can measure," she said. "I can't measure her at rest, because at rest she has nothing to hold against. Every door wakes when another does, and there's nothing to separate them." She turned the first sheet face down. "So I'll measure her under load."

The Warden frowned. "Under load is where she's dangerous."

"Under load is where a door has to commit," said Ysra. "A student on a still stone with nothing coming can let three doors drift. A student with a ram coming at her chest has to answer it with something. One door, declared, against a known weight, with the belt gauges on her and the frame recording and a healer on the bench." She looked at the Head of the Crooked Line. "In your old lane, because it's the only lane in the Compact with a full frame that isn't in daily use."

"And if she holds one door under load?" said the Mistress.

"Then I have a door," said Ysra. "And I can place her by it."

"And if she can't?"

Ysra was quiet for a moment.

"Then I'll know that too," she said. "And I'll write it down."

The vote took less time than the argument. Stonehand agreed, provided none of its second-years were in the lane. Wren House agreed, provided its own healer held the stop. The Crooked Line agreed, and assigned Dane to the test as supervising teacher, and added that Renn should walk the lane first as the standard, since he was Entered and the school would want its standard on the record. The Home Line abstained, as it always did when the matter was a placement, and coughed.

Ysra drafted the order on the spot.

At the bottom she added one more line, the one she added to every test of an unplaced candidate: *Supervised-test bond to be signed by the guardian.* The household would be liable for damage to Compact property and for any ash drawn beyond the school's emergency allotment. It was a standard clause. It existed because forty years ago an unplaced candidate had broken a frame and the Compact had spent two seasons arguing over who would pay.

She knew what the aunt's tenancy rested on. She had read the recusal notice. She signed the clause anyway, because it was the rule, and because a household that wanted a placement had to want it enough to sign.

Then she put out the lamp and went down the long cold stairs, and found, when she reached the street, that her hands were shaking slightly. It annoyed her. She put them in her pockets.

---

"Holding," said Orla. "Say it."

"Holding," said Kiva.

"Not like that. You're not reciting. You're telling me something." Orla stood beside the small frame in the corner of the Crooked Line shed, a waist-high timber rig with a padded sack hanging from a short arm on a rope. "When you say *holding*, you're telling everyone near you that you're going to stop whatever's coming, with what you have, and stay where you are. You say it before it arrives. Not after. Not while."

She let the sack go.

It swung across at Kiva's chest, slowly. It was only a small one, a practice weight, a quarter of what the ram in the old lane would be.

"Holding," said Kiva, and caught it in both arms, and staggered half a step.

"Good. You stayed where you were. Mostly." Orla hauled the sack back to the arm and held it there. "*Taking.*"

"What's taking?"

"Taking means you're going to put something into it. Strike it. Turn it. Change where it goes. That's for Edge, usually, or a hard Ward, or a body that knows what it's doing." Orla let the sack go again. "Don't open anything. Just your hands."

"Taking," said Kiva, and shoved the sack sideways with both palms as it came. It swung off its line and spun on the rope.

"*Letting go*," said Orla, catching it.

"Getting out of the way."

"Getting out of the way, or releasing whatever you're holding, or stepping back so somebody else can step in. All of those." Orla let the sack go. "Say it."

"Letting go," said Kiva, and stepped back out of the arc. The sack swung past where she had been and back.

"Better." Orla stopped the sack with one hand. "Now the other two."

"Yours and mine."

"Yours means *I'm giving this to you.* It means you're not going to deal with it, and the person you say it to is. Mine means *I've got it.* It means you're dealing with it, and everyone else can stop." Orla looked at her. "They're the only two words in the Declaration that need two people. The first three you can say alone in a field. These two, you need somebody standing next to you."

Kiva thought about the Crooked Line first-years in the yard, saying all five words at the start of the drill like a password, and then nothing.

"In the yard," she said, "they say them at the beginning. All together. And then nobody says anything."

"I know."

"Why?"

Orla was silent for a moment. She had a way of being silent, Kiva had noticed, that did not feel like refusing. It felt more like she was looking for the right shelf to take something down from.

"Because that's the way they learned it," she said finally. "And the way the people who taught them learned it. It's the oldest thing in the lane. Older than the frames. Older than the Compact. It's in the first pages of the oldest lane book we have, which is a copy of a copy, and it says *Say it out loud before you do it.* Somewhere along the line, people decided *before you do it* meant before the drill. It's tidier."

"And what does it mean?"

"Before you do it," said Orla. "Every time. So whoever's beside you knows."

"Knows what?"

"Whether to step in." Orla hung the sack up. "If you say *holding*, the person beside you knows you mean to stay. If you say *letting go*, they know there's about to be a gap, and they can fill it. If you say nothing, they have to guess. In a drill, guessing is fine. Nobody dies of a sack." She looked at Kiva. "Outside the wall, the Home Line still says it. Every time. That's how I know what it's for."

Kiva thought about this for a long while.

"In the test," she said, "it'll just be me in the lane."

"It'll be you in the lane," Orla agreed. "And whoever's standing at the edges of it."

---

Kiva told her about the cards on the fifth afternoon.

She had not meant to. She had been standing in the middle of the shed floor with the practice Ember on her fist, trying to hold the hooked bar for longer than four breaths, and on the third attempt her left palm had warmed exactly as before, and she had clenched it against her leg the way she had in the house, and Orla had said, very sharply, "Don't do that."

The bar had sunk back into the ring. Kiva stood with her fist pressed to her thigh.

"Do what?"

"Press it shut. You pressed your left hand into your leg. Why?"

So Kiva told her. The dark front room. The still door. Both fists, as tight as they would go. The Ward that came out sideways along the floor because there was nowhere else for it to go, and the rack of glasses, and the card box, and the long pale spill of eleven years of cards from the bench to the hearth.

Orla listened to all of it without moving.

"Which hand were you holding hardest?" she said, when it was finished.

Kiva thought. "The left."

"And which door came out?"

"The left." Kiva looked at her palm. "The rift door."

Orla said nothing. She waited.

"So I shouldn't press the left one," said Kiva slowly. "Because pressing makes it push back. I should leave the left one open. Flat. And I should keep my right hand open too, so the Ember doesn't have anything to close on." She looked up. "And just use the still door. Holding. With my feet."

Orla looked at her for a long moment, and Kiva had the feeling that she was being weighed, carefully, on a balance she could not see.

"That's a theory," said Orla at last. "Test it."

---

The letter came on the sixth morning, in the Compact's red seal, and Mara opened it at the bench and read it twice and put it down.

"Controlled test," she said. "The eighth day, the second hour. The old lane. Belt gauges, frame, a Wren House safety healer. One declared door, under load." She turned the second page over. "And a bond."

"What kind of bond?"

Mara read it aloud, flatly, the way she read the paper column on a card. "*The guardian of the candidate accepts liability for damage to Compact property arising from the test, and for any ash drawn beyond the school's emergency allotment. The guardian consents to the test being stopped on the call of the safety healer.*" She set it face down. "No."

Kiva had been expecting it, and it still felt like stepping onto a stair that wasn't there.

"Mara—"

"No." Mara stood up. "I've seen that frame. I've repaired it. It's the oldest frame in Meridian, older than the Assay, older than the house. The pawl on the counterweight channel wears every season and the Crooked Line never budgets for a new one because the old lane's only used once a year. The ram is sixty pounds. The counterweight's two hundred." She went to the shelf and took down a rolled sheet of ruled paper. "And I've seen this."

She unrolled it on the bench. It was her copy of the trace.

Kiva had not seen it before. Three lines, in Mara's careful pen, a tenth of a breath at a time: lifting together, climbing, falling, crossing over and under one another like three ropes twisted in a current. At the far end, where the fuse had been pulled, they all dropped at once.

"That's you," said Mara. "For forty breaths. That's what you looked like inside. And the healer said you went down faster than she'd ever measured." She put her finger on the place where the three lines crossed most tightly. "There. That's when the lines on your chest went hot. I checked the time against what the healer wrote."

Kiva looked at the place. It was just ink. It was also her.

"And this." Mara reached for the abstracts on the shelf. She did not open them. She did not need to. "Three cases. One of them lost the feeling in her fingers forever. She was under load, Kiva. Two doors at once, under load. That's what this test is."

"It's one door."

"It's one door *declared.*" Mara's voice cracked, very slightly, and steadied. "You declare one door, and the other two come anyway. You told me so. You showed me so, with the cards."

Kiva felt her face go hot. "I know what I did."

"Then why do you want to stand in a lane with two hundred pounds of iron over your head and do it again?"

"Because if I don't," said Kiva, "they put me nowhere."

Mara stopped.

"That's what happens," said Kiva. She had not known she was going to say it until it was out, and now that it was out it kept coming. "Ysra can't sign me. Stonehand won't take me. Wren House won't take me. The Crooked Line will only take me if somebody gives up a season for me, and Dane's the only one who will, and she's not allowed to place me, only teach me. So I stay unread. And unread means no school, no yard, no ash from any allotment, no door work at all except one hour in a shed." She was shaking. "And the doors don't stay shut. You know they don't. That's what the Kindling's for. So they can open somewhere safe the first time, and then be taught. If I'm not taught, they'll open anyway, at the bench, in the house, on the towpath. In the canal."

Mara said nothing.

"Everyone else got a test," said Kiva. "Everyone in that yard. They stood on the stone and they got a reading and a row and a ribbon. I want mine." Her voice dropped. "I want to earn it the ordinary way. Not have somebody decide for me that I'm too strange to try."

"Everyone else," said Mara quietly, "got a test that was built for them."

"I know."

"This one isn't built for you. Nobody knows how to build one for you." Mara looked down at the trace. "It's just the nearest thing they have."

They stood on either side of the bench for a long time without speaking. Outside, a cart went by on Gauge Row, and somebody's dog barked at it, and stopped.

"If the frame breaks," said Mara at last, "we pay for it. If you go under your floor, the ash is ours. And the house." She did not finish that. She did not need to. Kiva had read every card. The tenancy was a municipal tenancy, and Mara's municipal post was under review, and a household with an open liability on the Compact's books was not a household anyone was eager to keep on Gauge Row.

"I know," said Kiva.

"I know you know." Mara turned the bond over and read it again. Then she picked up her pen.

She did not sign it at once. She uncapped the pen and held it over the page for a while, and then she wrote, below the printed clauses, in the narrow space at the bottom of the sheet where nothing was meant to go, in her small flat hand.

*Remarks. The guardian consents on these conditions. The test stops on the safety healer's call. The test also stops if the candidate says stop. Nobody is to argue with her when she says it.*

Then she signed it, and blotted it, and folded it, and held it out.

Kiva took it. Her hand was not steady.

"I'm not doing it because you're right," said Mara. "I'm doing it because if you're right, the alternative is worse. And because I've looked at that trace every night for five nights, and every night I can see that you didn't do anything wrong at that Kindling. You stood on the stone and breathed." She capped the pen. "Say stop, if you need to. Say it loud. Don't be proud."

"I won't."

"You will," said Mara. "You always are. But say it anyway."

On the seventh evening, the assignments were posted on the board at the Crooked Line gate, and Kiva went with Anwen and Perrin to read them.

*Controlled test of the candidate Rowan. Old lane, second hour. Assessor: Y. Crest. Supervising teacher: O. Dane. Safety: Wren House senior healer on the bench, with one first-year Wren House assistant (A. Pryce). Rope crew: Stonehand Hall first-years (P. Cade and one other). Standard: K. Renn, Entered, to walk the lane first.*

"You're on it," said Kiva to Anwen.

"I asked." Anwen did not look at her. "The rotation mistress said first-years need bench hours. I said I'd take any bench hours. She didn't ask which ones."

"You're on the rope," said Kiva to Perrin.

"Stonehand puts first-years on rope," said Perrin. "Builds the arms. It's the only thing they're sure I can do." He grinned, but it didn't quite reach his eyes. "I'm going to haul your ram up so you can get hit by it. It's the closest I've come to being important all week."

They walked down to the old lane together in the dusk.

Kiva had seen it every year of her life, and she had never walked into it. It was a strip of packed earth twenty paces long and three paces wide, running north from the gate toward the yard wall, with low white-painted rails along both sides and the crooked post at the far end. At its midpoint, on the west side, stood the frame.

It was older than the frames in the straight lanes. Its timbers were black with age and its iron was hand-forged, and two of the brackets that held its swing arm did not match; one was square and one was rounded, as if someone had replaced half of it a long time ago with whatever they had. The arm reached out over the lane at the height of Kiva's shoulders. From its end hung a heavy chain, and on the chain, now, hung nothing, because the ram was kept in the shed.

On the frame's outer upright, the one furthest from the lane, an iron channel ran up the timber as high as two men. At the top of the channel, held in place by a toothed pawl, hung the counterweight: a stack of grey iron discs on a central rod, each disc as wide as a bucket lid.

"Two hundred pounds," said Perrin, looking up at it. "Give or take. When the ram swings and comes back, the crew hauls it up to the catch, and the counterweight drops a hand to reset the arm, and then you crank the counterweight back up to the pawl." He mimed hauling. "Haul, drop, crank. Haul, drop, crank. My arms are going to fall off."

"Where do you stand?" said Kiva.

Perrin showed her. The haul rope ran from the end of the swing arm back over a pulley and down the outside of the frame, beside the counterweight channel. The rope crew stood at the foot of it, outside the frame, on the far side from the lane, with their backs to the yard wall.

"Here," said Perrin. "Right under it."

Anwen frowned at the pawl. "Is that safe?"

"It's the old lane," said Perrin. "Nothing's safe. It's ceremonial."

Kiva walked the length of the lane by herself, slowly, on the packed earth. Twenty paces. At the midpoint she stopped and looked west at the frame, the arm reaching out above her, the counterweight hanging high in its channel beyond. Four paces, maybe five, from where she stood to the frame's outer upright. She counted them twice.

Then she walked on to the end and stood in front of the crooked post.

It was just a post, close up. Grey iron, pitted, as thick as her wrist, set into a collar of old dull lead. It leaned a hand's width toward the east. She remembered Rhea Sorn on the anniversary, laying her palm flat against it and bowing her head, and then stepping around it.

She did not touch it. She read the plate.

*Retained. Ceremonial. Custom.*

"My uncle says it's crooked because the founders couldn't afford a straight one," said Perrin, who had come up behind her.

"My mother says the Home Line touch it on the way out," said Anwen. "Every spring, when they go past the lock. One of them always comes down here first and puts a hand on it." She shrugged. "She doesn't know why. She says she's never asked."

Kiva looked at the plate for a long time. *Custom.* It was the shortest word anybody could have written there. It explained nothing, and it had been enough to keep the post standing while every other post in the yard was pulled up and set straight.

She thought about the remarks column on Mara's cards. *Spring bent at coil end, straightened. Agrees.* You wrote what you found and why, so that the next person who opened the instrument would know. You did not write *custom.* Mara would never have let her write *custom.*

"Do you think anybody knows?" she said.

"Knows what?" said Perrin.

"Why."

Perrin looked at the post. Anwen looked at the post. Neither of them said anything, and after a while the dusk came down over the lane, and the lamp-men started along the yard wall with their long poles, and the three of them walked back up toward the gate.

---

That night, Kiva lay in bed and went through it again.

*Holding. With the still door. My feet in the ground, and nothing else.*

She opened her right hand on the blanket. *Open, so the Ember has nothing to close on.*

She laid her left hand flat beside it. *Open. Don't press it. Pressing makes it push.*

She went through it six times, the way she went through a card when the two columns would not agree. Each time it came out the same. It was a good plan. It was simple, and it was based on something she had seen happen with her own eyes, and Orla had called it a theory and told her to test it.

The disk lay on the pillow beside her, where she had put it so the cord would not rub the lines. She touched it once in the dark. It was cold, as it always was.

*Holding*, she thought. *Holding. Holding.*

Somewhere around the twentieth time, she slept.
