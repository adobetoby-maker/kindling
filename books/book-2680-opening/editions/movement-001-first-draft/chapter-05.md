# Chapter 5 — Holding

The belt was heavier than it looked.

It was a band of old brown leather as wide as Kiva's hand, with three brass plates stitched into it: one at the small of her back, one over each hip. A braided cord ran from the back plate to a reel at the edge of the lane, and from the reel to the trace drum on Ysra Crest's table beyond the east rail. The Crooked Line clerk buckled it around her waist over her shirt and tugged it once to check the fit.

"Too tight?" he said.

"No."

"It has to touch. Through the cloth's fine. Just has to touch." He tugged it again. "It reads all three doors through the body. Not as well as the frame, but it doesn't care which one's which, so it doesn't get confused." He patted the back plate. "Don't land on this, if you fall."

"I'm not going to fall," said Kiva.

He looked at her with sympathy and did not answer.

It was the second hour of the eighth day, and the morning was cold and very clear. The old lane looked different with people in it. Ysra sat at her table on the east side, opposite the frame, with the trace drum and a small board of three dials in front of her and her ruling sheet clipped to its board. Orla stood at the east rail at the lane's midpoint, three paces from the painted line where Kiva would stop, with her hands at her sides. Behind her and to the south, nearer the gate, the Wren House bench had been set up on trestles: a jar of salve, a stack of cloths, a lead-lidded ash jar and its little brass balance. The senior healer sat at it, the same heavy-armed woman from the long room, and Anwen stood beside her in a green apron, very straight.

On the west side, beyond the frame, the rope crew stood at the foot of the outer upright. Perrin had his sleeves rolled to the elbow. The other Stonehand first-year was a tall, silent girl with cropped hair who had nodded to Kiva once and not looked at her since. Above them, high in its iron channel, hung the counterweight.

Mara was at the gate, behind the rail, where guardians stood. Kiva did not look at her. She knew that if she looked, she would see Mara's hands, and they would be still, and that would be worse than if they were shaking.

"The standard first," said Ysra.

---

Kellan Renn walked to the south end of the lane, turned, and faced the post.

He had his Ember on his right fist, a plain grey band, and he stood with his weight even on both feet. He did not look at the frame or the ram. He looked at the post twenty paces away, at the far end, leaning its hand's width to the east.

"Taking," he said.

He said it clearly, as if he were telling someone, and Kiva saw Orla's head turn very slightly toward him.

He walked.

At the eighth pace the metal moved on his fist and became the short straight blade, its edge dark. *Contact drill*, Kiva thought. *No light. The light would cut the leather.* At the tenth pace, as he reached the painted line, Perrin and the tall girl let the catch go, and the ram came off the frame.

It was a padded leather sack as long as Kiva's body, stuffed with sand, and it came out of the west on its heavy chain in a long low arc at the height of a chest. It made a sound as it came, a soft rushing creak of chain and leather. Kiva had heard sixty pounds described a dozen times that week. She had not thought about what it would look like moving.

Kellan did not stop it. He turned it.

He stepped into it with his blade held flat and angled down, and met the ram's leading face with the flat of the dark metal, and leaned. That was all. The ram struck the blade and slid along it and went past him, deflected upward and behind, its arc bent off true. Kellan's boots moved half a step and planted. The blade did not waver. Then the ram had gone by, swinging back west toward the frame, and the crew was already hauling on the rope to bring it up to the catch, and Kellan was walking on toward the post as if nothing had happened.

He stopped a pace from the post. The blade sank into the band.

"Ember door," said Ysra, reading her dials. "Hold through contact, long. Draw even." She wrote it down. "Thank you, Renn."

Kellan came back down the east side of the lane, outside the rail. As he passed Kiva at the midpoint, he paused, as he had at the rope on Kindling Day.

"Don't grip," he said quietly. Then he went on to the gate.

---

The issued Ember went on her right fist. Kiva had argued about that with Orla on the seventh afternoon, and Orla had won: if the Ember was not there, the Ember door would strain for it and cramp her hand shut, as it had in the quiet room, and that was worse than having it there and leaving it alone.

So she wore it, and held her right hand open, fingers spread, and walked to the south end of the lane.

She turned and looked at the post.

*Holding. The still door. Feet in the ground. Hands open. Nothing else.*

"Candidate," said Ysra. "Declare."

"Holding," said Kiva.

Her voice sounded thin in the cold air. She said it again, louder. "Holding." Then she walked.

The packed earth was hard under her boots. Five paces. Eight. The frame came up on her left, black timber and hand-forged iron, the swing arm reaching out over her head. Ten. The painted line. She stopped on it, and set her feet, and let her weight go down into her heels.

The still door opened.

It was easier than in the quiet room. Perhaps the packed earth was better than pine. She felt it go down through her boots and into the ground, deep and dull and heavy, and the belt's back plate warmed against her spine.

"Release," said Ysra.

The ram came.

She saw it come off the catch and drop, and swing, and grow. It was so much bigger close up. She brought both forearms up in front of her chest, the way Stonehand first-years were taught, crossed at the wrist, and braced.

It hit.

The impact went through her arms and into her chest and down through her body into the still door, and the still door took it. She did not move. Her feet did not slide. The ram stopped dead against her crossed forearms, sixty pounds of sand and leather, and hung there for a breath with the chain gone slack above it.

*Yes*, she thought. *Yes—*

Her right hand had closed.

She had not told it to. When the ram hit her right forearm, the fingers had clenched by themselves, the way any hand clenches when it is struck, and the instant they closed around the band of the Ember, the Ember door woke. She felt it lurch under her breastbone, the right-hand coal flaring, and on her fist the metal heaved forward and became the hooked bar. Its hooked end raked across the ram's leather with a sound like a boot scraped on stone, and caught, and tore a hand's width of seam.

Sand trickled out onto the earth.

"Two doors," said Ysra.

The ram swung away, back west, trailing sand. The crew caught it on the rope and hauled it up. Kiva stood on the line with her right fist shut around the ugly hooked bar, and her feet in the ground, and her face hot.

"Close them," said Orla. "Breathe out."

She breathed out. It took eleven breaths. The bar sank into the band. Her feet came back. She opened her right hand and looked at it.

"Still door, steady," Ysra was saying. "Ember door woke at contact. Reach unreadable through the overlap." She looked up. "Again, candidate. Second pass when ready."

Kiva walked back to the south end of the lane. As she passed Orla at the rail, Orla said, without moving, "What closed your hand?"

"The hit."

"Yes," said Orla. "So what are you going to do about it?"

---

The clerk restitched the torn seam of the ram with a sail needle while Kiva stood at the south end and thought.

The hit had closed her hand. Of course it had. Anyone's hand closed when something struck the arm above it. It wasn't the door's fault, or the Ember's. It was just a hand.

*So don't put that arm in front of the ram.*

She thought about the Stonehand first-years on their stones, the way they took a push on the chest with their arms down at their sides. She thought about Kellan, taking the ram on one dark blade, angled, with his whole body behind it. She thought about where her right hand could go so that nothing would make it close.

"Ready," she said.

"Declare," said Ysra.

"Holding."

She put her right hand behind her back, at the small of her spine, fingers open and spread, with the Ember resting against the belt's back plate where nothing could strike it. She held her left forearm up across her chest, alone, with the palm open and turned outward, not pressed against anything.

*Left arm takes it. Right hand stays out of it. Feet in the ground.*

She walked to the line. She set her feet. The still door opened, and it was easier still the second time, as if a path had been worn.

"Release."

The ram came off the catch.

It hit her left forearm square, and the still door held, and her right hand behind her back stayed open. She felt it stay open. Nothing struck it; nothing closed it. The Ember door did not wake. For one long breath there was only the still door and the weight of the ram against her arm, and the belt warm at her back, and her feet deep in the ground, and Kiva thought, with a fierce clean joy, *it worked.*

Then her left palm went hard.

It was not like the house. She was not pressing it. She had kept it open and still, exactly as she had planned, and the Ward came anyway, out of the open palm of the arm the ram had struck, as fast and as blind as a flinch.

It did not just hold the ram. It shoved it.

The ram went back the way it had come, west, and up. It went faster than it had arrived. Its chain went slack and then snapped taut at the top of the arc with a crack like a whip, and the whole swing arm jumped in its brackets, the square one and the round one, and the ram came swinging back down toward the lane on a wild crooked line, high and wide.

"Letting go!" Kiva shouted.

She did not think about it. The word was simply there in her mouth, the way *ow* is there when you stub your toe, and she let go of the still door all at once and stepped back off the line, two quick steps south. The ram swept through the space where she had been standing, a hand above where her head would have been, and swung on east and up and back.

On the frame, Perrin and the tall girl had the rope. They let it run, then took it, then let it run again, and on the third swing they caught it and hauled it up to the catch, and Perrin's face was white.

Kiva stood two paces south of the line with her left palm still hard and burning.

"Close it," said Orla.

It took fourteen breaths.

"Ember door quiet throughout," said Ysra. She was looking at the dials, and her voice was very even. "Still door steady to contact. Rift door woke at contact and drove the load. Hold, as a single declared door: unreadable." She wrote. Then she looked up. "That was correct use of the Declaration, candidate. The second one."

Kiva did not understand for a moment. Then she did. *Letting go.* She had said it before she stepped back, and Orla had heard it, and the crew had heard it, and nobody had been standing in the place she left.

It did not feel like a correct anything. It felt like being thrown.

---

The healer called her over to the bench before the third pass. Anwen held the cloths. The healer unbuttoned the top of Kiva's shirt and looked at the lines, and laid two fingers against the long one, and Kiva hissed.

"Hot," said the healer.

"Warm," said Kiva.

The healer looked at her. "Hot," she said again, flatly, as if correcting a reading on a dial.

"It's not blistered."

"No. Not yet." The healer took her fingers away. She glanced toward Ysra's table, and toward the gate, where Mara stood behind the rail, and back at Kiva. Kiva could see her weighing it: the order, the guardian's remarks on the bond, the candidate standing in front of her saying *warm.* "Can you hold your left arm out straight?"

Kiva held it out. It was steady. Mostly.

"Fingers," said the healer. "Spread them. Close them."

Kiva did. They spread and closed.

The healer was quiet for a long moment. "One more," she said at last, to Ysra, loud enough to carry. "If the lines blister, I call it. If she drops her arm, I call it."

"Agreed," said Ysra.

Anwen buttoned Kiva's collar for her. Her fingers were cold. "You don't have to," she said, very low.

"I know."

"Do you?" Anwen looked at her. "You said *warm.*"

Kiva did not answer. She walked back to Orla at the rail instead.

"The arm that's hit," she said, "that's the door that comes."

Orla said nothing. She was waiting.

"In the first pass, the hit closed my right hand, and the Ember came. In the second, it hit my left arm, and the rift door came. It didn't matter that I wasn't pressing. It didn't matter what I'd declared." Kiva frowned, working it through. "They answer where the load lands. Whichever part of me gets struck, the door in that part wakes up and tries to help."

"That's a better theory than the last one," said Orla. "What does it tell you to do?"

Kiva thought about it.

She could not keep the load from landing. That was the whole test. And every time she declared the still door, another door came anyway, and fought it, and won. The still door held, and then something else drove the ram wild.

"Declare the one that comes," she said slowly. "The rift door. If it's going to wake anyway, I should be the one who opens it. On purpose. And hold with that." She looked down at her feet. "And not open the still door at all. Stand light. So there's only one."

Orla looked at her for a long moment. Kiva could not tell what she was thinking.

"It's your test," said Orla. "It's your call. If you need to stop, say it."

"I don't need to stop."

"Then go."

---

At the south end of the lane, Kiva turned and faced the post for the third time.

She was tired. She could feel it now, under her ribs, the hollow cold feeling from the long room at Wren House, not as bad as that night but on its way there. Two passes. Two doors each time, fighting. Her soul had a funnel at the top and three taps at the bottom, and every pass had opened two of them.

*One more. One door, on purpose. Then they'll have something to sign.*

"Declare," said Ysra.

"Holding," said Kiva. "Rift door."

She heard a small sound from the Wren House bench. It might have been Anwen.

She walked. She did not set her feet hard. She walked lightly, on the balls of her feet, and at the line she stopped with her weight forward and her knees soft, the way you would stand if you wanted somebody to be able to push you over. She put her right hand behind her back again, open, against the plate. She held her left arm out in front of her, palm open, toward the west.

Then she opened the rift door.

She did it on purpose. It was the first time she had ever opened a door on purpose, and it was much easier than she had expected. She simply stopped not doing it. The left-hand coal under her breastbone warmed, and the warmth ran down her arm and out of her palm, and the air in front of her hand went hard.

It was a good Ward. She could feel its shape: a flat slab of warm, solid air, as wide as her shoulders, standing out a hand's length from her palm. It did not flicker.

"Release," said Ysra.

The ram came.

It hit the Ward.

Kiva had imagined it would stop. That was what holding meant: the thing came, and you stopped it, and stayed where you were. But she had not opened the still door, so there was nothing under her to stay with. And she had opened the rift door with all of herself at once, because she did not know any other way to open it, and it was far, far stronger than one small Ward ought to be.

The ram did not stop. It bounced.

It went back west as if it had been fired. Its chain cracked taut, and the ram did not swing up and over this time. It flew straight back on its chain into the frame and struck the inner upright, dead centre, with a sound like a door slammed in an empty house.

The whole frame shook.

Kiva saw it move. The black timbers swayed. The swing arm jumped clean off its rounded bracket and hung skewed on the square one. Dust came down off the crosspiece in a sheet. On the outer upright, high in its iron channel, the counterweight jerked, and Kiva heard the pawl that held it give a small bright *tick*, like a clock.

Then the counterweight fell.

It dropped the length of its channel in a single long scream of iron, two hundred pounds of grey discs on their rod, and struck the stop at the bottom. The stop was worn. It held for half a breath. Then it bent, and the bottom of the channel splayed outward, and the whole stack, rod and discs together, slid out of the foot of the channel and began, slowly, almost gently, to tip.

It was tipping west. Away from the lane. Toward the rope crew.

The tall Stonehand girl had already jumped clear. Perrin had not.

He had been crouched at the foot of the upright, coiling the slack of the haul rope the way the crew did after every catch, and when the frame shook he had lost his footing and gone down on one knee. The rope was around his boot. Kiva saw it in a single clear image, like something painted: Perrin on one knee at the foot of the channel, his left boot in a loop of rope, his face turned up toward the stack of iron discs leaning slowly out over him, and his mouth open.

She did not declare anything.

There was no time to think of a word. She was four paces away, five at the most, and the frame was between them. She went.

The Ember door woke under her as she moved. She had not asked for it; her right hand was behind her back, open, and it did not matter. The struts came. They ran down her legs from her hips in two jointed lines of dull grey metal, and her first step was not a step. It was a leap. It threw her sideways off the line and over the west rail and under the skewed swing arm, faster than she had ever moved in her life, and she hit the ground beyond the frame on both feet, too hard, and skidded, and was beside Perrin.

The stack was still tipping. It was over him now, leaning out past the foot of the channel at a steep angle. In another breath its top would be lower than its base, and it would come down all at once.

Kiva got her left shoulder under it.

It was the only thing she could reach. The bottom discs were too low and the rod was behind them, so she drove her shoulder and the top of her chest into the face of the discs halfway up the stack and set her feet on either side of Perrin's trapped boot. The iron was cold. It was so heavy that for one moment it did not seem to be a weight at all, but something larger and simpler, like being leaned on by a building.

The still door opened. She did not choose that either. Her feet went down into the ground and stayed there.

The rift door was still open from the pass, and it came too, out of her left palm, flat against the iron, pushing.

Three doors.

Kiva felt them all at once, the way she had on the stone at the Kindling: the right-hand coal, the left-hand coal, the deep coal under both, all three burning together. The struts held her legs locked. The still door held her in the ground. The Ward pressed back against the falling iron. And the three lines on her chest, the lines from her breastbone toward her left shoulder, went from hot to something there was no word for.

She smelled her own shirt scorching.

The stack stopped tipping. It did not rise. It stopped, leaning out over Perrin at its steep angle, with all of its weight on Kiva's left shoulder and her left palm and her rooted feet, and it stayed there.

*One*, she counted. She could not breathe. *Two.*

"Get your foot out," she said. She did not know if any sound came.

Perrin was pulling at the rope with both hands. It was wound twice around his boot, and the slack had gone tight when the counterweight fell, and he could not get his fingers under it. "I can't—it's caught, Kiva, it's—"

*Three.* The lines were burning down past her collarbone. She could feel them going, lengthening, a slow terrible heat tracing out toward the top of her shoulder, toward her arm. Her left hand, flat against the iron, began to shake. *Four.*

"Mine!"

The voice came from behind her, from the lane, high and hard.

Anwen came under the swing arm at a run in her green apron, and she did not stop. She went down on one knee on Kiva's left, right against her, and threw both hands up flat under the leaning face of the stack, below Kiva's shoulder.

The air went hard there. Kiva felt it happen. A second Ward slid in under the iron like a shelf into a cupboard, steady and narrow and absolutely sure of itself, and the weight on her shoulder changed. It did not vanish. It went somewhere else. Some of it, and then more of it, went down onto Anwen's shelf of hard air instead.

"I've got it," said Anwen through her teeth. "It's mine. Let go."

*Letting go.*

Kiva let go.

She did not know how she did it. She only knew that someone had said *mine*, and the weight had moved, and there was a space beside her where it could go. She stopped holding. The rift door, which she had opened on purpose, she let close; the struts she had not asked for, she let fold; the still door that had rooted her she let go of, and her feet came back into her boots, and she stepped back off the iron.

The stack sagged. It sagged onto Anwen's Ward and stopped, a hand lower than before.

"Six," said Anwen, counting her own hold. Her arms were trembling. "Seven."

Orla was there.

Kiva had not seen her come. She was simply there, on Perrin's other side, crouched, with her Ember on her fist. The plain short blade came up off her knuckles, and the dim yellow light woke along its edge, and she cut the haul rope where it crossed Perrin's boot in one short, careful stroke that did not touch the leather.

Perrin rolled clear.

"Down," said Orla. "Slowly. To your left, away from him."

"Eight," said Anwen. "Lowering."

She lowered it. It was not graceful. The Ward shook, and the stack slid off it at an angle and came down on the packed earth with a crash that Kiva felt through her boots, the discs shifting on their rod and one of them rolling free, and lay there on its side at the foot of the splayed channel, a hand's width from where Perrin's leg had been.

Anwen sat down hard on the ground.

Kellan Renn was on the other side of the frame, holding the tall Stonehand girl by the back of her jacket. Kiva did not know when he had got there, or why the girl had needed holding. She only saw that he had her, and that he was letting go now, and that his face was very pale.

Then she looked down at herself.

The front of her shirt was scorched along the left side of her collar in three thin brown lines, as if someone had drawn on it with a hot wire. Her left hand was hanging at her side, shaking.

She tried to close it and could not quite tell whether it had closed.

---

Ysra stopped the test.

She did not raise her voice. She stood up behind her table and said, "The test is ended," and wrote something on the sheet, and then she came around the end of the table and through the gap in the east rail and across the lane, which Kiva had never seen an assessor do.

The healer was already there. She knelt beside Kiva on the cold earth and unbuttoned her collar and looked.

The three lines no longer stopped short of Kiva's collarbone. They crossed it now. They ran up over the bone and on, onto the top of her shoulder, and the longest of the three, the one that curved toward her arm, went furthest of all, almost to the place where her shoulder became her arm. They were darker than before. Along the longest, the skin had risen in a thin clear blister, like a line of rain on glass.

"Oh," said the healer. It was the same small professional voice as on Kindling Day.

"It's all right," said Kiva.

"It isn't. Be quiet." The healer turned Kiva's left hand over, palm up, and pressed the pad of her thumb against each fingertip in turn. "Tell me when you feel it."

"That one." The little finger. "That one." The ring finger.

The healer pressed the middle finger.

Kiva waited. She could see the healer's thumb on her fingertip. She could see the skin dimple under the pressure. She knew she was being touched. It was only that the touch seemed to arrive from a long way off, through something thick, like being touched through a glove.

"I can feel it," she said. "It's just. Thick."

The healer pressed the first finger, and Kiva felt it through the same thick distance. Then the thumb, which was ordinary and close and clear. The healer went back and pressed the first two fingers again, one and then the other, and her face did not change at all.

"Anwen," said the healer. "The ash jar. A spoon and a half."

Anwen got up from the ground and went. Her hands were shaking so badly that Kiva heard the brass balance rattle on the bench.

---

Mara did not come into the lane. Guardians did not. She stood at the gate with both hands on the rail, and when Kiva looked at her at last, over the healer's shoulder, Mara was looking back, and her face was perfectly still.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. *Say stop*, she had said. *Say it loud. Don't be proud.*

Kiva had said *warm.*

The ash came. Kiva held it in her right palm, because the healer would not let her use her left, and breathed, and felt it settle into the hollow under her ribs the way it had in the long room. The shaking in her arms eased. The shaking in her left hand did not.

Perrin sat against the outer upright of the frame with his boot off, holding his ankle. There was a red weal around it where the rope had been, and another across the palm of his right hand where he had tried to pull it free. He was looking at Kiva. He did not say anything either, which was the strangest thing of all.

Orla was standing over the fallen counterweight with her Ember dark on her fist again, looking at the bent stop at the foot of the channel.

"The stop's worn," she said to nobody in particular. "It's been worn for years."

"It's on my card," said Mara from the gate, very quietly. "I wrote it on my card last spring. The Crooked Line didn't budget for it."

Orla looked at her across the lane and nodded once, and said nothing else.

Ysra Crest stood in the middle of the old lane with her ruling sheet in her hand. She looked at the frame, skewed on its one bracket. She looked at the counterweight on its side, and the splayed channel, and the cut rope. She looked at the torn seam of the ram, swinging gently from its chain over the lane, still leaking a thin thread of sand. Then she looked at Kiva, sitting on the ground in her scorched shirt with her left hand in her lap.

"Belt gauges," she said. "First pass: two doors. Second pass: two doors, Ember quiet. Third pass: one declared door, then all three, under load, to the top of the scale." She paused. "The reading is complete. It's also useless. I can't place a candidate by a door that only stays alone until something touches her."

"I know," said Kiva.

"I know you know." Ysra folded the sheet in half. "I want you to understand something. The Table shows what happened. It doesn't show what you are. Those aren't the same thing, and I won't pretend they are." She looked at the scorched lines on Kiva's collar for a moment longer. "Wren House will take her again tonight. Guardian, I'll send the account."

She walked back across the lane to her table. Halfway there, she stopped beside Anwen, who was standing at the bench with the empty ash spoon in her hand, and looked at her.

"You left the bench," said Ysra. "Without the healer's word."

Anwen went red to the ears. "Yes."

"I'll be noting that," said Ysra. Then, after a moment, in exactly the same tone: "I'll also be noting the hold. Eight breaths, under two hundred pounds, with your partner still under it." She went on to the table.

Anwen stood very still with the spoon in her hand.

Kiva closed her eyes.

Somewhere far off, beyond the yard wall, the noon bell was ringing at the Compact tower. She sat on the cold ground in the old lane and listened to it and counted without meaning to, one, two, three, and tried again to close her left hand, and could not tell, for a long time, whether it had closed.
