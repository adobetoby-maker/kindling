# Chapter 8 — The Lowest Line

On the sixteenth evening, Kiva could not find the lentil.

She sat at the bench after supper with her left hand flat on the wood and pressed the tip of her first finger with her right thumb, the way she had been pressing it every hour for four days. She pressed the middle of the pad, and the edge, and the very tip. She pressed the place where it had been. She felt the press each time, close and clear and ordinary, the way a finger is supposed to feel.

"Stop that," said Mara, without looking up from the lamp regulator.

"It's gone."

Mara looked up.

"The thick bit. On the first finger." Kiva pressed it again, to be sure. "I can't find it."

Mara put down her screwdriver. She came round the end of the bench and took Kiva's left hand in both of hers and turned it over, palm up, under the lamp. She pressed each fingertip in turn with her own thumb, slowly, little finger to first, the way the healer did.

"Tell me," she said.

"Yes," said Kiva. "Yes. Yes. Yes." On the first finger, she waited, and paid attention, and there was nothing to wait for. "Yes."

Mara held the hand a moment longer. Then she let it go and went back to her stool and picked up the screwdriver, and Kiva saw that she did not start working again at once. She sat with the screwdriver in her hand and looked at the regulator for a while.

"Write it on the card," she said at last.

Kiva took out *Rowan, K. — doors* and wrote in the instrument column, under the readings she had copied from the Table on the fifteenth: *Day 16. First two fingers, left hand, full touch. Thick patch gone.*

She looked at it. Then, in the remarks, she added: *Healer says don't do that again.*

---

The reply to the account came on the seventeenth morning, with a Compact runner who looked as if he had been told to wait for it to be read.

Mara read it standing at the door.

*Re: supervised-test bond, candidate Rowan. The guardian's submission of the old lane frame card is accepted. The counterweight stop and channel foot are recorded as pre-existing wear, reported to the school in writing before the test, and are struck from the account. Charge for the stop and channel foot transferred to the Crooked Line School's maintenance book. All other charges stand.*

Below that was a new total. Below that was Ysra Crest's signature, and beside it, in a smaller and slightly different hand, a line that was not part of the form.

*Reconciled by K. Rowan noted.*

Mara gave the runner a coin and shut the door. She stood looking at the paper for a long time.

"How much?" said Kiva.

"Less." Mara folded it. "Still a lot. Not a season anymore. Two-thirds of one." She put it in the brass box, in the back, behind the disk's card. "The housing review's still at the half-season. My review's still open. The records office has asked for my wiring order from three winters ago, and the second repairer has the Assay contract until they're done with me, which won't be soon." She said all of it flatly, like the paper column of a card, and then she put her hand flat on the lid of the box. "But it's less. And it's less because you read the card."

Kiva did not know what to say to that, so she did not say anything.

Perrin came at the sixth bell with two loaves of bread and Anwen behind him, and Mara, who had not invited them, made a pot of soup without asking anyone and set it on the bench between the lamp regulators.

"Five," said Perrin, before he had even sat down.

"Five what?" said Mara.

"Five breaths. My hold. At dawn load this morning. The second-year pushed me and I stood there and counted and I got to five before I went off." He spread his hands. "A whole extra breath. In six days. At this rate I'll be at Torch by the time I'm a hundred and nine."

"That's very good," said Mara, and seemed to mean it.

"It's terrible," said Perrin happily. "It's the best terrible I've ever had."

Anwen had brought her notebook. She showed Kiva the page from the fifteenth: the three doors, the three holds, the three waits, in her neat square writing. At the bottom she had written *Bench hours logged under Assessor: 2.* and underlined it.

"The rotation mistress put a mark next to my name," she said. "On the list. A little tick. I asked what it meant and she said it meant she'd seen something." Anwen shrugged. "I still don't have a partner record. I still need a logged drill with a Ward under load and somebody depending on it. But she's seen something."

"You'll get it," said Kiva.

"I know I'll get it," said Anwen. "I'm just not sure I'll get it this season." She looked at the soup. "And I'm not going to get it by standing next to you forever, either. You know that."

"I know," said Kiva.

"Good." Anwen ate a spoonful. "Because I'm not support staff. I'm the north rotation. I just haven't been told yet."

Perrin laughed so hard that he nearly knocked the lamp regulator off the bench, and Mara caught it without looking, and for a while the front room of the house on Gauge Row was very warm and very loud, and nobody talked about the ruling at all.

---

Ysra Crest wrote the ruling on the seventeenth night, alone, in the ruling room under the slates.

She had brought everything up with her. It covered half the long table: the Kindling Day trace in its certified copy; her own two sheets from the quiet room; the belt-gauge trace from the eighth day, with its three lines climbing together to the top of the scale; the trace from the fifteenth, with its three separate humps. Dane's petition. Dane's protocol. The guardian's bond, with the narrow lines written in the space at the bottom where nothing was meant to go. The Wren House injury notes. The account, and the guardian's submission, and the old lane frame card with its two lines from last spring that the school had never answered.

And Renn's objection, with its one extra line on the back.

She read all of it twice. Then she set out a fresh ruling sheet and sat looking at it for a long time with her pen capped.

She knew what the fifteenth day showed. Three doors, separately, each at Kindled reach, each with an ordinary hold. Short seven. Steady eight. Steady eleven. On their own, every one of them was a reading she might have signed on Kindling Day without a second look.

She also knew what the eighth day showed. Under load, the doors did not stay separate. They answered wherever the load fell, and they fought, and they drove the candidate past her floor and burned her down the arm. The sequence that worked on the fifteenth had taken nearly two hundred breaths from start to finish, most of it spent standing still and waiting. It could not be done with a ram coming. It could not be done in the field. It could not be done in the Circuit, where nobody waited three hundred breaths for anything.

So the candidate had three doors and could use them one at a time, slowly, in a closed yard, with a Ward at the bench and a teacher at the ring and nothing coming at her. That was true. It was all that was true. Ysra could defend every word of it.

She could not defend anything more.

She uncapped her pen. She wrote the heading, and the name, and the date. Under *Door of record*, she paused.

There could only be one. The form had never had room for more. Six centuries of Kindlings had poured through one door at a time, and the sheet had been built for them, column by column, until there was no space left on it for anything else.

She looked at the Kindling Day trace again, at the guardian's annotation in the margin of the certified copy, and at the repair order that proved the pens had been honest. *Ember lifted first, by 0.2 of a breath.*

She wrote: *Ember (provisional).*

Then she went down the sheet and filled in the rest, plainly, the way the old man from the Home Line had asked in this room twelve days ago. *Put down what you knew and what you didn't.*

When she reached the bottom, she stopped.

The ruling sheet had no remarks column. It never had. It had been set by the Compact's printers two centuries ago, and somewhere in the setting someone had decided that a ruling should say what was decided and nothing more, for clarity. Ysra had signed eleven years of them like that. Thousands.

She picked up her ruler.

She ruled a single line down the right-hand edge of the sheet, a finger's width from the margin, from the heading to the foot. It was not straight. Her hand was tired, and the paper was cold, and the line wandered very slightly toward the end.

At the top of the narrow column, she wrote: *Remarks.*

And under it, in a small hand, everything the form had no room for.

*Additional doors: rift, still. Recorded. Not ruled.*

*Door of record chosen on the Kindling trace, not on demonstrated control. The candidate's control of the Ember door is the weakest of the three.*

*Placement is the lowest the assessor can defend. Not a judgement of the candidate. A limit of the assessor.*

She read it over. Then she signed the sheet at the bottom, with a short decided stroke, and blotted it, and sat for a long time in the cold with her hands in her lap, looking at the crooked line she had drawn down the edge of the page.

---

The ruling was read on the eighteenth day, at the second hour, in the lower hall of Assay House.

It was not a large room, and it was not full. The four heads of the Compact did not come; they sent their clerks, who sat in a row along the side wall with pens. Mara sat on the guardians' bench. Orla stood beside it, with her hands at her sides. Kellan Renn sat at the end of the clerks' row, alone, in his grey coat, because he had objected, and an objector had the right to hear the ruling on his objection.

Kiva stood in front of the lectern where Ysra had told her to stand. Her arm was out of the sling. The linen on her shoulder had come off the day before, and the long line was pink and new and itched under her shirt. The disk lay against her breastbone, cold.

Ysra read it aloud. She did not hurry.

"*Rowan, Kiva,*" she read. "*Kindled at the six-hundredth anniversary. Door of record: Ember, provisional. Rank: Kindled.*" She turned the sheet slightly toward the light. "*Hold, in sequence only, under the protocol of the fifteenth day: Ember door short, seven. Rift door steady, eight. Still door steady, eleven. Hold under load: unheld.*"

The clerks wrote.

"*Placement: the Crooked Line School. Standing: Supervised. Supervising teacher: Lane-Mistress O. Dane. Not entered for the Meridian Circuit.*" Ysra paused. "*Door work only under supervision. Simultaneous access to more than one door is forbidden. Sequence work only under the protocol of the fifteenth day, with a Ward at the bench. Review at the first Circuit window. The guardian's bond continues.*"

She lowered the sheet.

"That's the ruling," she said. Then she looked at Kiva directly, over the lectern, the way she had looked at Kellan on Kindling Day. "I want you to understand it. Supervised is the lowest standing the Compact has. It's for students who can't be trusted with their own doors yet. I've put you there not because I think you're the least of anyone in that yard. I don't know what you are. I've put you there because it's the lowest line I can defend." She folded the sheet once. "I can defend *Kindled, supervised.* I can't defend anything higher, because I can't measure it. And I can't defend anything lower, because there isn't anything lower."

"I understand," said Kiva.

"Do you accept it?"

Kiva thought about it.

She thought about the pen on Kindling Day, the Table writing three rows and rubbing them out. She thought about the quiet room and the felt walls and *I can't sign a number that changes when you breathe.* She thought about the old lane, and the stack tipping, and *mine*. And the fifteenth day: the three gold rows on the south wall, one after another, ordinary, the kind nobody would look at twice.

She had wanted a place. She had wanted it the ordinary way, on the same stone as everybody else. She had not got it the ordinary way. She had got it slowly, and painfully, and three times over, and it was the lowest line on the sheet.

But it was a line. And she had stood on the stone for it.

"Yes," she said.

"Then sign under me," said Ysra.

She turned the sheet around on the lectern and held out the pen. Kiva took it. She held the sheet flat with her left hand, because her left hand could feel the paper now, close and clear under her fingertips, and she wanted to feel it. She signed her name below Ysra's.

Then she saw the column.

It ran down the right-hand edge of the sheet, a thin ruled line not quite straight, with *Remarks* at the top in Ysra's hand, and three short lines of writing under it. Kiva read them upside down, the way she read everything.

*Placement is the lowest the assessor can defend. Not a judgement of the candidate. A limit of the assessor.*

She looked up at Ysra. Ysra was looking at the sheet, not at her, and her face was exactly as tired and precise as always.

"You ruled a column," said Kiva.

"The sheet didn't have one," said Ysra. "It needed one." She took the pen back. "The objection."

Kellan stood up.

"*Objection of K. Renn, Entered,*" Ysra read from a second sheet. "*That the school's lane hours not be reassigned for the supervision of the candidate.*" She looked at him. "The objection is sound. The school's lane hours are not reassigned. Lane-Mistress Dane's supervision is drawn from her own hours, which she has volunteered, and from no one else's." She turned the sheet over. "*The objector's second request: to walk the lane against the candidate, when placed.*" She paused. "Noted. Referred to the school for when the candidate's standing permits it. Which it does not, yet."

"Yes, Assessor," said Kellan.

He sat down. He did not look at Kiva. But when Ysra had gone and the clerks were gathering up their pens and the room was emptying, he came past the lectern on his way to the door and stopped beside her, the way he always seemed to stop.

"Supervised," he said.

"The lowest line," said Kiva.

"It's a line." He looked at her for a moment with his flat, careful, measuring face. "I meant what I wrote. Both parts."

"I know."

"When they let you walk," said Kellan, "I'll be at the other end."

"I'll be the one saying what I'm doing," said Kiva.

Something happened at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile. "Good," he said. "Somebody should." And he went out.

---

There was a ribbon after all.

A Crooked Line steward brought it to the bench where Mara sat, in a flat paper envelope: grey, like Kellan's, like every Crooked Line ribbon in the yard. But down its centre ran a single black thread, woven in, so that anyone who looked would see it.

"Supervised," said the steward apologetically. "It's the—"

"I know what it is," said Kiva.

She took it. Mara pinned it to her collar herself, on the right side, away from the lines, with fingers that were very steady.

Then Mara took out the bond.

It was the continuing bond, a new sheet, with the same printed clauses as before and Ysra's signature at the bottom already. Mara read it twice, as she read everything. Then she uncapped her pen and, in the space at the bottom where nothing was meant to go, she wrote the same remarks she had written on the first one, word for word: *The test stops on the safety healer's call. The test also stops if the candidate says stop. Nobody is to argue with her when she says it.* And below that, a new line.

*The candidate has agreed to say hot.*

She signed it. She turned to Kiva.

"I'm afraid for good reasons," she said, quite quietly, so that only Kiva and Orla could hear. "Every one of them is on a card, or in the abstracts, or on that trace. I'll tell you every one of them, whenever you ask. I won't hide any of them to make you feel better." She capped the pen. "And you'll tell me hot."

"Yes," said Kiva.

"Even when it's the house."

Kiva swallowed. "Even when it's the house."

Mara nodded, once. She handed the bond to the clerk. Then she put her hand on the back of Kiva's neck, briefly, the way she used to when Kiva was small and they were crossing the canal bridge in a wind, and took it away again.

At noon, the Table on the south wall of the Founders' Yard was relit for the day, and the three of them went to read it: Kiva, and Anwen, and Perrin, who had come from dawn load with dust still in his hair.

It was easy to find. The row was in among the R's, where it had always been, just below Kellan Renn's *Entered.* For eighteen days it had said *Unread. Held for review.* Now somebody had changed the plate.

*Rowan, Kiva. Ember door (provisional). Kindled. Crooked Line School. Supervised.*

It was the shortest row on the wall, and the lowest.

"That's you," said Perrin.

"That's me," said Kiva.

"It's a terrible row," said Perrin. "It's nearly as bad as mine." He put his arm round her shoulders, carefully, on the right side. "Congratulations."

Anwen said nothing. She stood beside Kiva and read the row twice, as Mara would have, and then she reached over and straightened the black-threaded ribbon on Kiva's collar where it had twisted, and left her hand there for a moment, and took it away.

Kiva touched the disk through her shirt. It was cold.

---

Orla Dane went to the Crooked Line records room at the ninth bell, when the school was empty, and unlocked the bottom drawer of the desk that had been her teacher's, and had been hers for twenty-six years.

There were two things in it. She had not taken either of them out in a very long time.

The first was a sheet of thin paper, folded in four, soft with age. She unfolded it on the desk under the lamp. It was a rubbing, done in charcoal, of a disk two thumbs wide: nine fine grooves, running across its face in a pattern she had looked at so often in her twenties that she could have drawn it from memory. In one corner, in faded pencil, in the hand of the old lane-mistress who had taught her, there was a single line.

*If you ever see one of these warm, tell the Home Line before you tell the Compact.*

That was all her teacher had ever said about it. Orla had asked, of course. She had been twenty-two and certain that every question had an answer somebody was keeping from her. The old woman had looked at her for a long time and said, *I don't know what it is. I know who it goes to. That's all anybody's supposed to know.* And then she had folded the rubbing and put it in this drawer and told Orla where the key was, and a year later she had died.

Orla had kept the key for thirty-five years. She had never seen one warm. She had seen one once, cold, on a cord around the neck of a Home Line field woman walking past the Fourth Lock, and had not dared to ask.

Then, on the fifteenth day, she had watched a thirteen-year-old girl step off the Kindling stone with three doors quiet inside her, and put her hand flat to her chest, and gasp.

Orla folded the rubbing and set it to one side.

The second thing in the drawer was a long, flat oilcloth packet, tied with tape. She did not untie it. She knew what was in it. She had taken it herself, thirty-nine years ago, when she was nineteen and a junior clerk to the senior assessor of the day, and the assessor had called her into the ruling room late one night after a Kindling.

*Two-needle reading. Ember and still. Ruled instrument fault.* The assessor had been a good woman, careful and tired, and she had looked at the trace for an hour before she said it. *The frame was overdue for service. The coil was suspect. It's a fault.* And then: *Withdraw the trace, Dane. We don't file faults. It only confuses the next assessor.*

Orla had withdrawn it. She had signed the ruling sheet as witness, *instrument fault*, in her own careful nineteen-year-old hand. And then, instead of burning the trace as she had been told, she had put it in her coat and carried it home, and kept it in a box under her bed for years, and then, when the desk became hers, moved it into this drawer beside her teacher's rubbing, because she could not have said why, except that the two needles on it had not looked like a fault to her at all.

The boy had been placed Ember. He had never held anything for longer than three breaths. Two years later he had left the Compact on a southbound transit, and nobody had followed him, and nobody had asked.

She had not thought of his face in a long time. She thought of it now.

Orla sat back in her chair.

She could take the packet to Ysra Crest in the morning. She could lay it on the long table in the ruling room and say, *I withdrew a trace thirty-nine years ago on an assessor's order, and here it is.* Crest would read it. Crest would write it down. That was what Crest did. And the next morning, every head of the Compact would know that the Rowan girl was not the first, and that the last one had been ruled a fault, and somebody would ask what had happened to him, and somebody else would ask what the Compact had done about it. And somebody else, inevitably, would start asking what the Rowan girl was, instead of what she could do.

She was thirteen. She had a black thread in her ribbon and a line of new skin down her shoulder, and she had just, that morning, learned to say *hot.*

*Not yet*, Orla thought. *Not until I know what it will cost her.*

She knew exactly what that thought was. She had had it once before, at nineteen, in the ruling room. It had sounded just as reasonable then.

She sat with that for a long while.

Then she untied the tape on the oilcloth packet, just enough to slide in a single folded sheet of her own, and wrote on the outside of that sheet, in her narrow upright hand: *Day 15, 2680. K. Rowan. Three doors, in sequence, separate, released. Disk warm after. Not during. Seen.* She retied the tape. She put the packet back in the drawer, and the rubbing beside it, and locked it.

Then she took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote a letter.

It was short. It was addressed to the field office of the Home Line, on the canal side, past the Fourth Lock, and it said only that the lane-mistress O. Dane of the Crooked Line School wished to speak in person with whoever kept the bearer appointments, concerning an appointment made four years ago, and that the matter was not urgent but should not wait long. She did not mention the girl's name. She did not mention warmth. She signed it, and sealed it, and put it in her coat to carry down herself in the morning.

She had done what her teacher told her. She had told the Home Line before she told the Compact.

It was not enough. She knew it was not enough. But it was one thing done in the right order, and it was more than she had done at nineteen.

On her way home she went down to the old lane. She had not meant to. The frame was still propped on its timbers, and the counterweight still lay roped off at the foot of the channel, and the lane itself was dark between its white rails. She walked the twenty paces to the end in the dark and stood in front of the post.

She laid her left hand flat against it, the way the field people did on their way out every spring, the way Rhea Sorn had done on the anniversary, the way Orla's own teacher had done every morning for forty years without ever saying why.

The iron was cold. It leaned its hand's width to the east, as it always had, listening for something.

"I don't know either," said Orla to nobody, and went home.

---

The first supervised hour was at dawn on the nineteenth day.

Kiva was up before the bells. She came down the stairs in the dark and found Mara already at the bench, with the lamp lit and two cups of tea poured and a heel of Perrin's mother's bread on a plate.

"Eat," said Mara.

Kiva ate. Mara watched her do it. When she had finished, Mara pushed the brass box toward her across the bench, and Kiva took out *Rowan, K. — doors* and read it over: the readings from the fifteenth, the fingers, the order and its reasons, Orla's line about *hot.* In the paper column, Mara had copied the ruling from the Table, word for word. *Ember door (provisional). Kindled. Crooked Line School. Supervised.*

The remarks column was nearly full. There was room for one more line, at the very bottom.

Kiva uncapped the pen. She thought for a long time.

Then she wrote: *Lowest line. It's mine.*

She filed the card. Mara looked at her, and did not ask what she had written, and Kiva understood that she would read it later, when Kiva had gone, twice, the way she read everything.

The ribbon with the black thread was on her collar. The disk was under her shirt, cold. The new skin on her shoulder itched. She put on her coat and went out into Gauge Row, where the ribbons for the six hundredth year were coming down from the doors one by one, and walked north in the grey light toward the Founders' Yard.

The yard was empty. The Table on the south wall was dark. In the straight lanes along the east wall, nobody was drilling yet; the Crooked Line first-years would not come for another hour, to say their five words all together at the start and then nothing more. The posts stood at the ends of the lanes, plumb, to the line, like soldiers.

Orla Dane was waiting at the head of the old lane.

She stood beside the gate with her hands at her sides and her Ember on her right fist, dark. Behind her, the lane ran north between its white rails, twenty paces of packed earth, past the propped black frame with its arm laid down along the rail, to the far end.

To the post.

"Supervised hour," said Orla. "Your first. Do you know what we're going to do?"

"No," said Kiva.

"Good." Orla turned and looked down the lane at the crooked post, leaning its hand's width east in the dawn, and the brass plate at its foot that said *custom.* "Why is it crooked?"

Kiva looked at it.

She thought about the plate, and Rhea Sorn's hand on the iron, and the Home Line coming past the Fourth Lock every spring and one of them always stepping down to touch it first. She thought about whoever had kept this one post, a long time ago, and fixed a plate to its foot, and written one word on it and nothing else.

"I don't know," she said.

"Neither do I," said Orla. "Not properly. Not yet." She stepped aside from the gate, so that the lane lay open in front of Kiva, all the way to the end. "That's where we start."
