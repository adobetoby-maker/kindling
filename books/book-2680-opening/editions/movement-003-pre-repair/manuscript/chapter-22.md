# Chapter 22 — Kiva: Rung One

Kellan Renn came down out of the air dark.

Kiva heard him land, six paces behind the grey column, light and flat, and heard the metal run home. He did not ask her why. She had said *not yet,* and he had spent a bound on nothing and come down again with his blade unlit. A month ago, he could not have done it.

"The head's over the hem," she said. She kept her eyes on the canvas. "All six feet are on, but it leans. The top knot's hanging over the north edge. It'd fall on the lead. Anwen. Two more."

"Step," said Anwen. "Step."

The wall went back two paces. The six legs followed, and the dents in the waxed canvas moved south, and the shadow of the thing slid across Kiva's hands and on.

She dropped a pinch. It fell straight, and slid.

"Now."

She did not see him go up. She had promised herself the sheet. But she heard the struts bite the cinder, once, twice, and for the space of a held breath, there was nothing at all.

Then the cinder in front of her knees turned blue-white, and was grey again.

It was so short that she doubted it. Four-tenths, he had said. It was the time it took to shut a book.

Something above her let go.

She looked then. She could not help it; it was hers now. The frosted face stood out against the sky by itself, a shallow bright curve like the lid of a pot, with nothing behind it. At the weir it had dropped and broken on the sheet. On the Kennel floor it had dropped and broken.

It did not drop.

It tipped. It slid down the front of the column, quite slowly, as a plate slides off a tilted shelf, and she lost it against the grey of the next knot.

There was no time to think about a plate. Sixty drams were coming down.

They came like sand out of a split sack, a long soft roaring rush down the face of the string, and they struck the middle of the canvas and heaped and spread. She felt the weight of it arrive through her knee. It was warm. She could feel the warmth on her face from the hem.

"*Dark!* Whole floor! One. Two."

The four wards went out together.

She heard it go round the Pit, the same word from a dozen mouths, *dark, dark,* and then a silence from the cinder that she had never heard on a floor before. Sixteen people had put out every worked thing they had. There was not a blade lit from the Kiln to the sump, nor a wall, nor a stance. Up on the terraces, eleven hundred people had noticed it and were not sure what they had noticed.

"Three. Corners!"

Anwen was at the south hem before the word was finished. Perrin had the west. Kellan came in from the north on his own two feet, with his hands open, and took his fistful of lead without being told how.

"Up. Together. Over the top. Seven. Eight."

It was a great deal heavier than a Handful. The four hems rose like the sides of a tent, and the heap slid inward and lay in the hollow, and lifted, and settled.

It was listening.

She had expected that. She had not expected how much of it there would be. On the Sag Line there had been a few bent grains in every panful. This was the knot that had worn the skin, and all of it was bent. The whole heap leaned one way under her hands, smooth and round, like the inside of a bowl.

And under the curve, there was something else.

It was very small. She nearly took it for her own pulse. It was a beat, slow and even, a little tap and then a space and then a little tap. It did not belong in loose ash. Loose ash listened. It did not keep anything.

She knew what kept time like that. She had grown up over a shop full of it.

*That's a drum,* she thought. *That's one pen on one drum.*

"Twenty," she said aloud. She had a number. She was not going to lose sixty drams to an idea. "Twenty-one. Haul."

She hauled the cord. The neck came in, hand over hand, stiff as a hawser. Her left arm did its share from the elbow. She took the first turn, and the second.

"Anwen. The third's yours. On three. One. Two. Three."

"Mine." Anwen's hand came in, quick and neat, and crossed it over. "Yours."

Kiva pulled the end through. She pressed the wax down with the heel of her right hand, and felt it take.

"Sealed. Forty-seven."

Somebody on the east terrace cheered, one voice, and was hushed.

She should have felt it then. It was thirteen counts inside the mark, on a full rung, with a skin, in front of the Placement Chair. But the little tap was still in her knees, fading as the bag settled, and she had learned on a plank bridge what came of feeling a thing through a cloth and saying nothing.

"Foreman!"

Hollis Vane was ten paces off, at the foot of the sheet, with his chalk.

"It's curved. All of it, not a trace. And there's something else in it, under the curve. I felt it till the seal." She made herself say the stupid part. "It's keeping time. Like a mast pen."

He did not chalk that. He looked at her for one long breath, and she saw him decide that he did not know what it meant either.

Then he looked up, past her, at the east wall.

Kiva turned her head. Under the great board, in its glass-fronted case, twelve needles stood where they had stood all morning. The old keeper sat beneath them on his stool with his hands on his knees.

They were steady. Every one.

Vane wrote something short on the slate. "Get it to the table," he said. "I've heard you."

---

Perrin took the pole across his shoulders like a yoke.

The bag hung from the middle of it, a fat canvas pudding with a hem full of lead, and he said *ow* once, quietly, in the tone of a boy keeping a promise to a healer. Kiva limped beside him with one hand on the neck of the canvas. A catcher stayed with her ash. The warden had told her that on her second day.

It was a long way to the south end. She counted it, because she was counting everything. Thirty paces. Forty.

"Forty-seven," said Perrin, between breaths. "With a skin on. Kiva. That's a pass. That's four of us with a second step."

"Subject to the bag."

"Subject to the bag. You sound like him." He shifted the pole. "I'm going to enjoy it anyhow. Somebody ought."

The tinning table stood on the cinder in the south-east, five paces short of the sump. It was a plain deal trestle with a scale on it and a row of empty tins, and a young Circuit clerk in a grey apron who looked as though he had never touched ash that was not in a jar. Perrin let the bag down onto the boards with great care.

"Don't pan it without me," he said. "I'm to go and mind a string. I'd like to see what fifty-odd looks like when it's ours." He straightened, with a hand on his side, and grinned at her, and went back up the floor at a trot.

Kiva put both hands on the table. The knee was beating.

*You'd best watch it for them,* the warden had said, on a brick floor. *And not your own bag going off to the table.*

She had not looked round once in sixty paces.

Somebody laughed, high up on the west terrace. Then a good many people did.

"It's found the drain!"

She turned.

The string had not gone to the Kiln.

She saw that first, and for a moment it was only odd. It stood in the middle of the floor, forty-odd paces from her, seven knots high. It had walked. It had left her chalk mark while she was hauling a cord, blind, with nothing lit anywhere to turn it, and it had gone ten paces west.

It was standing on the race.

Its six thick legs were planted on either side of the line of gratings, three and three, like a horse straddling a ditch. It was not leaning on anything. There was nothing there to lean on. It simply stood over the iron and swayed a little.

The lowest of its knots hung down between the legs. It hung slack. It brushed the bars.

*Like the hem of a coat that wants taking up.*

She had written that on a river bank, a fortnight ago, with a question mark after it. She had read it aloud at a kitchen table and been told it was half a reading.

"That's ten paces," said Kiva. Nobody was near enough to hear but the clerk. "The Kiln's twenty-two. I set it twenty-two from the Kiln."

Loudest, they had all said. Vane had said it. She had said it herself, on a terrace in the sun, to fifteen people who had put up their hands.

Nobody had said *nearest.*

Along the floor, the fifth company stood exactly where they had been standing when she called it. Their hands were open. Their walls were down. Every one of them was as dark as she had asked them to be, and they were watching a Flask stand on a drain, and some of them were smiling.

---

"Wall," said the stern girl in green.

It was Old Four's rung. Their sheet lay squared on its own mark, twelve paces south of where Kiva's had been, with the quiet boy kneeling at its hem. The four wards came up in a row with Merrin in the middle. They were bright. They were five paces from the thing's north flank.

The count was past ninety. The second knot ought to be waking.

It was awake. Kiva could see that from the table. There was frost on it.

She understood about the plate then. The skin had not broken. It had slid down one rung and sat itself on the next knot like a hat on a different head, and it lay there, a faint bloom, turned toward nothing.

It did not turn toward the wall.

"Brighter," said Lowen Brask. He was standing at the corner of his team's sheet, with his broad hands empty. "Merrin. All of you. Lean on it."

The silver flared. Four students put everything they had into a wall, and at five paces a reading knot ought to have swung to it like a compass needle.

It swayed. It stayed.

"It won't look," said Merrin. Her voice was high and thin. "Lowen, it won't *look* at me."

"Tolliver," said Brask.

The lanky third-year went up the back of it in two bounds. Kiva saw his blade light at the top, a yellow-white line, and hang there. He had lit it to be seen. It was the brightest worked thing in the Pit, and it was an arm's length from the frost.

The knot did not so much as tilt.

He came down. "Catcher?" he said, and it was not a question about a fall.

"It's not on my sheet," said the quiet boy. He sounded bewildered. "It's nowhere near my sheet. I can't call a fall onto a drain."

"Hold there." That was Vane. He had not raised his voice. Everybody heard him. "Nobody cuts. Blades dark. Wall, stay as you are."

The frost was moving.

It was the only thing on the floor that was. It crept. It spread down from the second knot onto the third, and round the sides of the column to the back, where the grey had been only grey. It went as breath goes across a cold window, all the way to the edges. The whole string began to shine, faintly, from the top to the legs.

There was no back to it now. There was no place behind the edge of the skin, because the skin had no edge.

And Kiva felt it.

It came up through the cinder and the soles of her boots, forty paces off. A little tap. A space. A little tap.

It was the thing that had been in the bag. It was in the floor.

She looked at the gratings. She was too far away to see through them. But the nearest person to the string was a Team Twelve ward-holder, and he was looking straight down between his own feet with his mouth open.

"There's something gone down," he said. "Through the bars. It's let something down. It's got hold of the—"

"*Vane!*"

It was the old keeper, and he had knocked over his stool.

"I've a load on the race that isn't on my book! All twelve! They've gone into step, man, look at them, they're ticking like a row of clocks!"

Kiva looked at the glass case.

She could not read a dial at that distance. She did not need to. Twelve needles caught the light together, and dropped it together, and caught it again. Above them, the lamps of the great board had begun to pulse. *FIFTH COMPANY,* it said, brighter and dimmer, brighter and dimmer, in time with a thing standing on a drain.

Nobody on the terraces was laughing now.

"Event's struck," said Hollis Vane.

He said it flatly, for the slate, and chalked as he spoke.

"Ten twenty-two. This is a response and it's mine. Students dark and off the race. Back to the east wall, all of you, and stand where I can count you. Graywater. You're in."

---

They came through the west gate at a run.

It was the first time Kiva had seen anyone in slate blue hurry. Four ward-holders spread across the floor in a line with their hands coming up as they went, and Doran Mace was behind them, and the silver that rose in front of the string was nothing like the students' wall. It had no seams. It was as bright as a shop window at night.

"Give it a gate," said Mace. "Five paces. Hold."

They held.

It was four Torch wards at five paces. Kiva had seen that pull a Flask the length of a meadow as a magnet pulls a nail.

The shining column swayed over the gratings. It did not turn.

"It's not reading," said one of the four. She had a broken nose. "Mace, there's nothing coming off it. It's like holding a lamp up to a wall."

The tall woman with the white streak had not run.

She walked in through the gate after the others with her hands out of her pockets. She went round the thing, once, at a long pace's distance, all the way round, looking up. Her right fist was dark. She stopped where she had begun.

"There's no door in it," said Rhea Sorn. Her voice was low and rough, and it carried. "It's skinned all round. I could light on it till supper." She looked down at the bars under its legs. "It's on your current, Hollis. It's got a tune under it. It isn't listening to us because it's found something better."

"Can you cut what's gone through the grating?"

"Not from the top. The iron's in my way." She frowned. "A ward might. Slid in flat, under the belly, across the bars. If the cords are only ash, a wall will shear them off the copper."

"Stroud," said Mace.

The woman with the broken nose stepped out of the line.

She did it well. Even from the table Kiva could see that. She went down on one knee at the edge of the race, a pace from a leg as thick as a bollard, and brought her two hands down low, palms to the cinder. Her ward came out of them thin and flat, like a baker's peel. She slid it in under the hanging knot, along the top of the gratings, toward the grey ropes.

It touched them.

Kiva had seen open ash take a pattern. She had seen it drink a lit blade in a brick cold room, and a pump tune in a hut. She had never seen it done backward.

The silver did not shear anything. It went *in.* The cords took the edge of that ward as a wick takes oil, and the brightness ran along it, the wrong way, out of the wall and up the woman's arms.

Greta Stroud screamed once. It was short. Then she was lying on her side on the cinder, curled round her own two hands, and there was smoke coming off them.

"*Down!*" said Mace. "Close the line. Nobody touches that with a pattern. Nobody!"

"Renn." Vane had not moved from where he stood. "You're a Carry. Get her out."

Kiva saw Kellan come off the east wall.

He did not light anything. He did not Stride. He ran, on his own feet, with his hands open, straight in under the flank of a seven-knot Flask to where a woman lay a pace from its leg. He went down on one knee and got her across his shoulders as a sack is got, and stood, and walked.

"Carry," he said. She heard it. He had declared it, to nobody, because that was what one did.

He took her north and west, toward the Graywater gate, and did not look round.

---

The string moved.

It was not much. One thick leg lifted, and came down a pace to the south. Another followed it. It was the slow rolling tread that Kiva had watched four still-doors use in a brick lane, the tread of something that has nowhere to hurry to. It walked straddling the race, three legs to a side, and it went south.

"It's going down the line," said the keeper. He had got to his feet, and his voice had cracked. "It's following the copper. Vane! It's going for my tunnel!"

"What's in your tunnel?"

"The trunk, man! The bank! Four-and-twenty cells with that tick in every one of them, and the pumps hung off them!" Dunmore's keys were jangling. "If it gets a cord past my link, I can't cut it off from this side. I'll have to quench the lot. The whole bank. And the Ferren's a yard over the weir this week."

Kiva knew what that sentence meant. She had read it out, a line at a time, at a kitchen table.

*Was it closed. Who was hurt. What still works in the morning.*

"Then open your link," said Vane.

"I'm going! Four minutes!" The old man was already at his gate, with his head thrust forward, at something that was meant to be a run.

"Rhea. Can you slow it?"

"With what? I've nothing it wants."

"Then it's the lid first." Vane's voice went up at last, a hard flat bark that struck the terraces and came back. "Every still-door on this floor to the capstan. Now. Brask. Cade. You, and you. If that branch goes dark with the Kiln standing open, I'd like there to be iron in the doorway. *Run.*"

They ran, as still-doors run. It was like watching furniture leave a room. Kiva saw Perrin go, heavy and fast, with a hand clamped to his ribs, and the shaved head of Lowen Brask beside him. Two more came off the east wall after them.

And she was looking at the gratings.

She had not stopped. The line of iron ran straight down the floor from under those six legs to the lip of the sump, fourteen paces west of the table where she stood, and through the nearest bars she could see into the channel.

There was grey in it.

It was moving. It came along the copper in the bottom of the race like water finding its way down a gutter, two ropes of it, one to each bar. It was well ahead of the legs. It had reached out before the body had taken its first step.

She watched it pass under a grating. She counted.

*One. Two. Three. Four.*

It passed under the next.

A grating was a pace. A pace was four counts. She looked up the line to where the grey began, and down it to the sump, and counted iron.

Fifteen.

*Sixty counts.*

She had done this sum before. She had done it on a mezzanine with her palm on a drum, and a needle going up a tenth at a time, and three people who would not be out of a pump room when it got to the top. She had been right then. It had not helped.

Sixty counts to the link. Four minutes to the keeper.

And the link was a sprung iron lever on a shelf across three paces of open sump. It wanted four hundredweight to bring it level. *One good still-door with his boot on the shelf.* There was no way onto that shelf but a door in a wall that an old man had not reached yet.

Every still-door in the Pit was running north.

It would not have mattered if they had been running south. They could not have crossed. A still-door could no more jump that trench than a wardrobe could. An Ember could have been over it in a breath, any Ember, Tolliver or Dray or that First Division girl. An Ember could have hung on the lever with all sixty kilos of her and not shifted it the width of a thumb.

The thing wanted somebody who could cross like the one and stand like the other.

Kiva stood at a deal table with a sealed bag and a whole dram in her that she had not touched.

*No,* she thought. It was quite clear. *It's not on my paper. I've a knee I'm not to land on till the second of next month. Fourteen tries. I'd sign none of them, she said.*

*Fifty-two.*

The grey slid under another grating.

She was not going to do it. She saw that with relief. She was going to do the thing she was certified for, which was to tell somebody who was in charge.

"*Foreman!*"

Her voice cracked on it. The Pit took it and threw it up the terraces. Vane's head came round.

"It's ahead of itself! The cords! They're in the race, fifteen gratings from the sump, a grating every four counts!" She pointed. Her hand was not steady. "That's sixty. Fifty now. The keeper's four minutes!"

He looked. He looked down the line of iron as she had, and she watched him do the same sum, and get the same answer, and not like it.

His pale eyes swept the south end of the floor. They went over the sump, and the sheer yellow wall, and the iron shelf. They went over a trestle table, and a clerk in an apron with his hands up to his mouth.

They stopped.

"Who's south of it?" said Hollis Vane.

She could have said *the clerk.*

"Rowan," said Kiva. "Seven. Catcher."
