# Chapter 24 — Kiva: What Was Tinned

Healer Aske had the coldest thumbs in Meridian, and on Wednesday morning they were in the back of a cart.

It was the Wren House cart that stood at the west gate on every Kiln day, a covered wagon with two cots and a locked chest. On one cot lay Greta Stroud, grey in the face, with both her hands wrapped to the wrists in wet cloth. Kiva was put on the other.

"Left hand," said Aske. "Palm up. Don't help me."

The cold thumbs went along her first two fingers, joint by joint. Kiva watched them press. She felt the third finger and the fourth. She felt the first two some while afterward, faintly, as one hears a neighbor through a wall.

"When?"

"On the lever. Forty minutes ago. A little more."

"Then you're inside the hour, and somebody remembered what was said." The grey eyes went briefly to Anwen, who was standing at the tailboard with the team book open in both hands. "Is that page signed?"

"Four names. One dram, ward-trace, sealed at the ash office. To Healer Aske, for K. Rowan." Anwen held it out. "She signed with her right."

"That's what the page is for. I ease now, and I draw against it tomorrow." Aske unlocked the chest. "Sleeve."

The ash came out of a jar no bigger than an egg cup. It was the pale silver-grey that Kiva had seen once before, in a grading cup, and Aske did not settle it. The healer laid it along the inside of Kiva's forearm in a thin line from the elbow to the wrist, and then set both hands over it, and shut those grey eyes.

It was like having a cold, wet string drawn slowly through the arm.

It did not hurt. It went down from the elbow, through the wrist, and out along the two dull fingers to their tips. Where it passed, the deep ache went quiet. The trembling in her hand slowed, and stopped.

"There," said Aske, after a long time. The ash on her arm had gone the color of chalk. "Now. I told you a month ago that this nerve had been burned once and wouldn't forgive a second. I've been looking for the second. It isn't there."

Kiva let her breath go.

"It isn't burned. It's starved. Something went through that arm this morning a great deal faster than it has ever been asked to carry anything, and it took what there was. I don't know what you did. I'm told half the Compact watched and nobody else knows either." The lid of the jar went on. "They'll sulk for a week. Then they'll be where they were on Tuesday, which is slow, and late, and not finished mending. No better. I want that understood. I've eased it. I haven't cured anything."

"And if I'd come tomorrow?"

"Then I'd be saying something else, and you'd not have liked it." Aske turned to the other cot. "A fortnight. No lanes, no floors, nothing lit. The knee was already owed that. Tell your captain."

Greta Stroud had turned her head on the pillow. Her broken nose was very white.

"You're the one on the shelf," she said hoarsely.

"Yes."

"I'd my eyes shut for most of it. They tell me it turned round and walked off." She looked down at her wrapped hands. "I slid a wall under a Flask because a woman I'd follow anywhere said it might do. It was a fair notion. I'd have had it myself." Her mouth twisted. "Your girl stood my place in the line for ten. Mace says he never heard the join."

"She's not my girl," said Kiva. "She's her own."

"Good," said Stroud, and shut her eyes.

---

They were waiting at the foot of the cart.

Mara had the canvas-backed book under her arm. Orla had both hands on the stick. Neither had tried to climb the step. Kiva got down it by herself, slowly, and stood between them in the thin sun with her sleeve still rolled.

She told them.

She had meant to choose her words. There were not enough of them to choose among. She said that the disk had been warm. She said when, and for how long, and what her fist had been doing. She said that it was cold again by the keeper's gate.

Then she told them about the shelf going away.

Mara's face did not change. It had not changed in a hallway at a quarter to three in the morning either. Only her hand moved, and came to rest flat on the cover of the book, as it rested on a case she was reading.

"Standing up," she said. "In daylight. Over an open sump."

"Dray had me by the belt."

"Then I owe that girl a thing I don't know how to pay." She breathed. "The same as before?"

"There was sound. I'll tell you all of it tonight. At the table. That's the rule."

"It is." Mara looked at her for a moment longer. "I was in the pump house. I had my hand on cell nine. The bank went into step at twenty-two minutes past ten, all four-and-twenty, like a room full of clocks. It came out of it at twenty-four." She tapped the book. "Not one fouled. The pumps never dropped a stroke. I wrote that down before I knew who'd done it."

Orla had said nothing at all.

She was looking at the place where the cord crossed Kiva's collarbone. Her crooked face was perfectly still.

"Is it warm now?"

"No."

"Did you ask it for anything?"

"No. I'd nothing to ask."

"Did it tell you anything?"

"No." Kiva thought. "It was only warm. Like a hand."

Orla nodded, very slowly. She did not put out her own hand to feel. Kiva noticed that.

"Then you'll write it tonight, before you sleep," she said. "The hour. How long. What you'd just finished doing, in what order. Not one word of what you think it means. I'll want to read that page, and I'll want it to be only what happened."

"You know something."

"I know there's something to know. That isn't the same, and I've been wrong before by treating it as though it were." Orla shifted her weight on the stick. "I saw your shins from the rail. Bare. I saw your foot come down. I saw that lever start."

"Will you sign it?"

"No."

It was so prompt that Kiva nearly laughed. It set her nose off again.

"I've seen it once," said Orla. "Once is an accident with good manners. You told a Home Line officer you'd missed fourteen, in front of every assessor in the district, and then you made the fifteenth. I'm prouder of the first half than I can easily say. But my name goes on things I can ask for twice." She turned toward the gate. "Show me six."

---

The fever came after dark.

It was not like the last. That had been hot sand in the lines. This was a slow heat everywhere, a banked stove, and a headache that sat behind her eyes. She lay on top of the blanket with a wet cloth across her chest and the disk cold on the cloth.

There was no dream. She waited for one, and it did not come.

There was only the note.

She could not hum it. She tried, quietly, with her teeth shut, and what came out was not it. But it was there, the way the after-ring of a bell is there. It was one note that had been a great many.

She got up at ten and went down the passage to the kitchen. The rule said the same night.

Mara had the lamp trimmed and a clean page.

Kiva told it. She told the height, and the roads, and the lights that were all unlike. She told the notes. She told about trying to count, and losing four. She told the lights touching, and the one note, and looking up.

"And under it," she said. "At the very end. Something small. It ticked. Once."

Mara's pencil stopped.

"Like the bag."

"Like the bag. Like a drum pen." Kiva pressed the cloth to her chest. "I don't know that it's the same. I only know it's the word I'd use for both."

"Then that's what I'll write. *The word she'd use for both.*" Mara wrote it. She sat back. "I looked at the hall panel when we came in. Did you see me?"

"Yes."

"It's quenched. It's been quenched since the thirteenth. There's nothing in it to touch. I looked anyway." She was quiet. "I shall probably go on looking."

The stove ticked. Kiva drank the water that had been set out for her.

"Orla told me what you said to him," said Mara. "The officer. Before you went."

"That I'd missed."

"Fourteen times. Out loud. With the Chair in the front row." Mara turned the pencil end over end on the table. "I signed a paper on an embankment six weeks ago. I've wondered every night since whether I'd signed it because I believed you or because I couldn't bear to be the one who said no." She set the pencil down. "I heard a girl tell eleven hundred people exactly what she couldn't do, and then be let do it by somebody whose business it was to decide. That's a meter, and a shutdown path, and a book. I couldn't have asked for more if I'd drawn it up myself."

"I was frightened."

"I should hope so. So was I. I was across the road with my hand on a cell." She pushed the water jug nearer. "Drink that. And then you'll help me with something, if your head will stand it. I've the keeper's book for Wednesday. I'm to reconcile it."

It lay under the brass weight, a smaller book than the trunk's, black with handling.

"Not tonight," said Mara. "Saturday. I want to read it twice by myself first. There's a line in it I'd like to be sure of before I show it to anyone."

Grader Halloran cut the first cord on Thursday morning and looked at what was in the tin for rather a long time.

The Wren House grading room was as it had been a month ago, with no lamp or cell within thirty paces, and seven cups, and the long shelf of jars. Team Seven stood in a row before the bench. Perrin had been strapped again from the armpits to the hips, and stood like a pillar.

"Fifty-one and two-tenths," said Halloran. "Agreed with the table. Now the cup."

A pinch went into the test cell. The grader laid a slow, plain tune on it and began to count.

Kiva counted too. Sixty. That was Second. Their Handful had held eighty-one, with a cinder in it. Eighty. Ninety.

At a hundred, Kellan's hand closed on the edge of the bench.

"A hundred and eighteen," said Halloran, and lifted the hand away. "That's First. By a clear margin. I've not graded a student rung First since the year my daughter was born." A dry look went along the row. "What took the last one off First, Mr. Renn? You asked me that in this room."

"Cinder. From the path."

"And this was caught on waxed canvas and bagged in forty-seven. So it would seem that somebody learned." The grader took up the comb.

It was the brass frame with nine reeds. Halloran held it over the open tin, as before, and Kiva watched the third reed from the left begin to shiver.

It did not hum faintly, as it had for a trace. It sang. It was a clear thin steady note that filled the bench.

"Ward," said Halloran. "Full. Not a trace. The whole rung's bent that way. I've an infirmary upstairs that would take this tin out of my hands before I'd finished writing the card. There's nothing kinder to a nerve or a burn." The grader's eyes went to Kiva's left sleeve and away. "As I gather somebody's found."

Then Halloran frowned.

The comb had not been taken away. Under the third reed's song, there was another sound. It was low and dry and very small. Kiva looked along the brass.

The seventh reed was moving.

It was one of the four that had no name. It did not sing. It tapped. It touched its neighbor and came away, and touched, and came away.

"Now that," said Halloran softly, "I've heard twice in nineteen years."

Nobody breathed.

"Both times it was ash swept out of a cell that had been salted late. A tune leaves that. Something that's been made to keep step." The comb turned a little in those careful fingers. "The shelf's cards call it *I.* For induction. I'd never thought to hold it over anything that came out of a rift." A pause. "This was cut and bagged before the string went near your race?"

"Forty-seven counts after the cut," said Kiva. "It was still on our sheet. It hadn't moved."

"Then it brought that with it."

"I felt it. Inside the count. I told the foreman it was keeping time."

"Did you." Halloran wrote on a fresh card, in a small upright hand, and read it aloud while writing. "*Flask, rung one, skinned. Yield 51.2. Grade First. Resonance: W, full. I, trace. Catcher reports 'keeping time' inside the count.*" The card was propped against the tin. "I'm not going to tell you what that means. I grade ash. I don't grade springs. But there's been a man in black and gold in my passage since eight o'clock, asking very civilly what weight I'd put on a seventh reed. I thought you ought to hear it from me before you hear it from him."

Ysra Crest read the board's findings on Friday as she read everything, from the first word to the last.

They were all in her office. There were not chairs enough. Team Seven stood, and Orla sat by the window, and Hollis Vane leaned on the wall beside the door with his slate turned inward against his coat.

"*Was it closed,*" said Ysra. "The Kiln is on its lid. The branch was restored at four that afternoon under a Home Line wall, and the lattice took back what was behind it. The Pit is shut for a fortnight." She turned the page. "*Who was hurt.* A chartered ward-holder, both hands, six weeks. Two students. *What still works.* The Ferren pumps. That is underlined, and not by me. *What was tinned.* Four hundred and nineteen drams, of which fifty-one and a fifth were bagged by this team before anything went wrong."

She laid the page down.

"The board finds as follows. The fifth company left no gate lit. That was inside the Circuit's book and outside the Home Line's custom. The foreman said he would leave one, and recorded that he had said it. Neither the book nor the custom foresaw a string that could take a bus." Her level eyes went to the door. "Officer Vane asked that his own name stand first in that paragraph. It does."

Vane said nothing. He inclined his head.

"The book has been changed. As of last night there is a rule. *A gate shall be left lit.* It has Miss Rowan's floor to thank for its existence, and I should not, in her place, take that as a compliment."

"I don't," said Kiva.

"No. I see that you don't." Ysra took up the next sheet. "The second step. Your rung passed its mark, clean, at First. Your company failed its third column until three professionals and a keeper retrieved it. The board will not grant a full step on that, and it will not withhold one from a team that bagged fifty-one. So it has invented something, which is the board's habit." She read. "*Second step, attached.* Each team of the fifth company may answer a Flask-yield call only as an attached recovery and support team, under a chartered crew, until it has stood one whole Flask under a foreman without remark."

"Attached to whom?" said Kellan.

"Old Four goes to the Home Line. Officer Vane asked for them." Ysra turned the sheet over. "You were asked for as well. By name, by the senior Edge of the Graywater Company, on a draft card, with the words *I'll take the shift myself.* I have rarely seen that officer write a sentence she did not have to."

Anwen made a small sound. Kiva did not look at her.

*She looked at you twice,* Perrin had said, on a river bank.

"Now the rest, and it is less agreeable. Eleven teams were to have qualified on Wednesday afternoon and Thursday. They will wait for a held tear. Several have written to me, and they have not written kindly. Miss Dray has renewed her objection. She has it on evidence this time, as I told her she must." Ysra folded her hands. "She also pinned a link, and says in her second line that the one has no bearing on the other. I am inclined to agree with her on that point. I have not ruled on the rest. I shall hear it when I have the Pit's tape in front of me."

She looked at Kiva last.

"Your conditions. Officer Vane struck the event at twenty-two minutes past ten. He gave you a field direction a minute later, with a reason, and your sponsor took the consequence upon herself in the hearing of the whole ground. Nothing that followed occurred in a sanctioned event. There is therefore no incident. I looked for one." A pause. "Your place is as it was. Your certificate is as it was. Your sponsor has added nothing to it, and has told me why, and I thought the reason sound."

"Six," said Orla, from the window.

"So I understand." Ysra put the papers into the folder. "You are still provisional Third Division, Miss Rowan. You are certified to kneel. On Wednesday you did something that nobody in this building can classify, and it cost more ash for its length than any act on that mast's drum. Mr. Bexley has shown me. I should like you to leave this room hating both of those facts equally."

"I'll try."

"I have every confidence," said Ysra Crest.

The ash office had a new line on its posted list. *Purse sheet and poles: 3.0.* Kiva saw it on the way in, and did not think that anybody had put it there for them.

The clerk did the sum on a slate. It came out as evenly as the first had.

*Yield, 51.2. Civic, half: 25.6. Casualty reserve, a quarter of the remainder: 6.4. Schools, half of what is left: 9.6. Team: 9.6.*

Below it was the line that the board had drawn across the standings.

*T7. Total yield, 72.0. Ninth of thirty-one. (att.)*

"Ninth," said Perrin. "With brackets."

"I like the brackets," said Kellan. "They're accurate."

They took the team book home to the Rowan kitchen on Saturday evening, because that was where the first share had been divided, and because Perrin could no longer get down the laundry's back steps without language.

Mara was at the table when Kiva came in ahead of the others. She had the keeper's black book open, and two sheets of copying paper beside it.

"Sit," she said. "I've read it three times. I'm sure of it."

She turned the book round.

It was the Pit's branch book for Wednesday the twenty-sixth, in a shaky, old-fashioned hand. *Kiln opened 10:12. All circuits in step, 10:22. Link opened from the floor, 10:24. All dark.* And then a line by itself.

*10:24, later by about forty seconds. Lamp ring. Every lamp, once. Branch isolated. Link pinned. No current. No fault found. T-0. A.D.*

Kiva read it upside down, and then the right way.

"Dray said they jumped."

"Dunmore saw it from the road stair. He wrote *T-0* because he's kept that book for twenty years and didn't know what else a man writes." Mara's finger rested beside the line. "I've copied three of those for you, out of registers older than this street. I never thought I'd be handed one with the ink hardly dry." She drew one of the loose sheets across. "And this came from Hanley Street on Friday. The clerk's finished."

It was the entry for 2446. Kiva had waited six weeks for it.

It was three lines. *Night. Every lamp, every district. No fault found.* She had known that much. But the clerk had copied the margin as well, faithfully, because he was that sort of clerk. In the margin there was a note in a different hand, older and cramped. He had not been able to read it. He had drawn it.

It was a row of marks, like a line of bent pins.

"He says it's a record hand that went out two hundred years ago," said Mara. "He says there's a woman at the Compact Library who can read it, in the superseded stacks, if anyone asks her nicely." She squared the two sheets, one on the other. "I don't know what I've got here, Kiva. I know that I'd not put either of these down as nothing."

There were feet on the stair. Kiva put both papers inside the field book, and shut it.

---

"Nine and six-tenths," said Kellan. "Graded First. I've a list."

"So have I," said Anwen. She set hers beside his. For once she looked at it. "Mine's short. Two drams sealed. Before anything. The same as last time, only twice, because we've seen what one's worth." Her eyes went to Kiva's left hand, lying on the table. "It stays ward. Nobody breathes on it."

Nobody argued. Perrin wrote it down.

"One back to the Wren House," said Kiva. "To the infirmary. At par." She had thought about it in the cart. "There's a woman upstairs with both hands burned. Her company will pay for her easing; I know that. I'd still like it to be ours. We called the floor dark."

"*You* called it," said Perrin. "We put our hands up. I've been wanting to say that since Wednesday. I'd like it written that I voted."

"It's written. I've your hand in the book."

"Good. One to the infirmary. That's three."

"A sheet," said Kellan. "Our own, with poles. Three. I'm not bagging my next rung in a canvas I've had to sign for at a gate."

"Six."

Then there was a silence. They had all known that it was coming.

"Two," said Kellan. He said it to the list. "For the yard. For her."

Kiva had her mouth open already.

"It's a third of a dram a go. For nine seconds. Noa's shown me the tape. It's the worst Draw anybody's ever put on a sanctioned mast, and it went to open a lever. I can't ask you—"

"You haven't asked. I've put it on my list." He looked up. His face was as it had been in a cold room, saying *me.* "It's also the only thing anybody at this table can do that nobody else in the Compact can. I stood on that floor with a Flask in front of me and nothing to cut, and I carried a woman to a gate. I was glad to. It was what there was." He frowned. "You were the one thing on that ground that fitted. I'd like to know whether it was luck. Two drams is six goes, at that price. I'm told your sponsor wants six."

"She does."

"Then it comes out exact."

"I'd like it said," said Perrin slowly, "that I mind."

They looked at him.

"I do. I held a capstan on two ribs that had nearly mended. I'm strapped like a parcel. Nobody's voting me a dram to practice walking in a circle." He drew a careful breath. "And I vote yes. I've decided I'm allowed both; she taught me. It's two drams to find out what one of us is. I'd pay more than that to know what I am."

"Anwen?" said Kiva.

Anwen had been turning her pencil.

"I'm going," she said. "I told Mace this morning. Six weeks from the tenth. I wanted to say it here first and I couldn't find the place." She set the pencil down. "So I'm voting to spend ash on a yard I shan't be standing in, and a sheet I shan't hold a corner of till the autumn. I want that said. And I want it said that I'd vote the same if I were staying."

"It's said."

"Then yes. On three conditions, and you'll write them, Kiva, in your own hand. A sanctioned mast, every go. Orla on the crate. And Noa reads it after and never during." She almost smiled. "You taught me to write conditions."

"That's eight," said Perrin. "One and six-tenths over."

"Held," said Kiva. "On the book. Four-tenths a head, for the next time one of us is dry in a lane." She had run dry at the Terrace, on two hundredths, because she had asked for nothing. "I was wrong about that in the spring."

Kellan wrote the last line. He turned the book.

*Sealed, medical, W: 2.0. Returned, Wren House infirmary, at par: 1.0. Purse sheet and poles: 3.0. Sanctioned practice, two-door, K. Rowan, on conditions: 2.0. Held, 0.4 a head: 1.6. Total 9.6.*

They signed. It took four names, as it always did.

"The Halden man found me," said Kellan, when the pencil had come back to him. He did not look up. "On Thursday, outside the grading room. A license team of theirs, for the Regional. A stipend. He named the figure."

"What did you say?"

"That I'd compare." He folded his paper. "I've not finished. I thought you should all know that I'm doing the sum."

Perrin laughed, and held his side.

"Brask's been offered a summer on a Home Line hold crew," he said. "Vane asked him at the capstan, with the lid still warm. And the Hall's master has my name down for the second team in the autumn, on Brask's word. *The only one who didn't have to be told twice.* I'm to have another go at the plate when I can breathe." He shook his head. "A month ago, we'd one share and nowhere to go. Now there's four of us and I can't find two going the same way."

Nobody said anything to that. Outside, the lamp at the corner of Coper Street came on.

Noa Bexley had the tape pinned along the edge of a drawing board, and he had been looking at it for two days.

He was in the mast room at the Pit, a brick cupboard under the eastern rim. The keeper had let him in with a grunt. The Chair's letter said *sanctioned instrument,* and *copy to this desk,* and Noa had made the copy on Thursday, and then he had gone on sitting there.

Sixteen pens. He had looked at fifteen of them once.

The sixteenth lay dead flat along its line from the gate until nearly twenty-four minutes past ten. He had pointed at it on Wednesday morning. *Yours. Doing nothing.* A whole dram, unspent, for ninety minutes.

Then it moved.

There was a small neat step. That was the bound. He had measured a great many bounds, his own among them, and that one was two hundredths, as it ought to be.

After the step the pen should have run level. She had shut the Ember at the top. Everyone said so. She said so. Shut meant nothing drawing.

It did not run level.

It went straight up the paper.

He had put a glass on it. The line climbed twenty-six hundredths in a fifth of a second. There was no slope to it. It was a cliff. The pen had gone up so hard that it had left a tiny blot at the top, where the nib had jarred.

And then, from the top of the cliff, a gentle slope. Nine seconds and a little, three hundredths, a tired stance on a heavy lever. That was all.

"Two, and twenty-six, and three," said Noa aloud. "Thirty-one."

He had written it into her book on Thursday, in the cart shed, and into his own. He had worked the Draw because he could not stop himself, and it had come out near twelve. The bench had frightened a Placement Chair with three point one.

Twelve. For the only thing she had ever done that worked.

It was the cliff that he could not leave alone. The mast drew a pen for a fighter, not a door. It could not say what had been open. But he had the keeper's word, and the foreman's slate, and a Graywater Edge's draft card that the Chair had let him read, and they all said the same. Her shins had been bare. Her foot had not yet come down.

By every account, there had been nothing open at all when twenty-six hundredths went out of her.

*The work is in me in the air,* she had written. He had seen it. She let him read the *why* column now. *Where is it when it isn't in either one?*

He did not know. He knew what it cost to get it across.

He turned to the notebook's other page. *Things that are probably nothing.* It had grown. He read down it, as he did most nights. The tears. The skins. The class. Her still door. A plate in a meadow. A seventh reed.

He wrote at the bottom, in pencil.

*The Pit, 26th. Every lamp on a dead ring, once. Keeper's book says T-0. She has three more of those in hers.*

"Mr. Bexley?"

He had not heard the door.

The man was about forty, and pleasant, and wore a black coat with a narrow gold cord at the cuff. He stood with his hat in his hand as if the mast room had been somebody's parlor.

"I'm told you're the one who reads for the Chair. I'm with the Halden technical office. We've an interest in efficient patterning, as you'd expect." He smiled. "I understand that there's a tape."

"There's a sanctioned tape," said Noa. His mouth was dry. "It goes to the Placement Chair. I can't show it."

"Of course not. And your own notes? I'm told you've a paper in hand. We fund a good many papers. It can make a difference to a young man in Third Division." The smile did not alter. "Your name would be first on it, naturally."

Noa looked at the drawing board. He looked at the cliff, with its tiny blot.

He had wanted a paper since he was eleven. He had wanted it in the roster corridor, among the drawers, so badly that he had had to sit down. *Second,* he had told her, at the foot of a stone porch. *After yours.*

"They're her numbers," he said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Every one of them. I measure. I don't own." It came out steadier than he had expected. "She sees the sheet first, and her sponsor does. If she says stop, I stop. You'd have to ask her. I don't think you'd like what her aunt would say."

"I see." The pleasant man put his hat on. "The Circuit's tapes are open to chartered parties thirty days after the standings post. You'll know that. So I shall be reading this one on the twenty-eighth of next month, in any case." He nodded at the board. "I only thought it might be agreeable to read it in company. Good evening."

The door shut.

Noa sat for a while with his hands flat on his knees.

Thirty days. Anybody with a charter could walk in here in thirty days and put a glass on that cliff. They would not need him. They would not need her leave.

He took the pencil, and under the last line on the page that was probably nothing, he wrote one more.

*They can look now. So I'd better find out first.*

The Old Yard was the same on Sunday night as it had always been.

Kiva had come on the tram with the book under her arm and nothing settled in her. She was not to light so much as a lamp for a fortnight. Orla had sent word anyway. *Bring the page.*

She gave it over, open at Wednesday.

Orla read it by the lamp on its hook. She read it twice. Kiva sat on the other crate with her leg out and watched the crooked face, and could tell nothing.

*Disk: warm. Began after I shut the still door, with the lever on its pin. Not before. Lasted from the shelf to the top of the road stair. Cold by the keeper's gate.*

*Did not ask it anything. It did not do anything.*

*Then the other thing, about three counts. See Mara's page. Dray says the lamps jumped. Keeper's book says T-0, 10:24 and forty seconds.*

"You've kept to it," said Orla at last. "There's not a guess on here."

"You told me not to."

"I did. I wasn't sure you could." She closed the book and held it on her knee. "I'll tell you what I've got, then, since you've been honest. It isn't much. There's a book at the Home Line hall. A bearer's book. I've known for thirty years that it existed, and I have never once been on the list of people who may ask to see it."

Kiva sat very still.

"Ilse Corran was on that list. She wrote in it, I think. I don't know what." The stick turned slowly in Orla's hands. "Hollis Vane is on it. He's watch officer; they all are. I took that man down his first culvert and fell in it, and he's owed me a favor for twenty-five years. I'd been saving it. I asked him for it on Thursday."

"What did he say?"

"He said that he'd been about to come and ask me." Orla nearly smiled. "It seems that an officer who gives a field direction to an unclassified candidate has to write a reason in a ledger older than this school. He went to write his. He found that somebody had been there before him."

"When?"

"I don't know. He wouldn't tell me in a corridor." She handed the book back. "I'm not promising you an answer, Rowan. I've read things in old books before and been no wiser. I'm telling you that there's a page somewhere, and that for the first time I've a reason good enough to ask for it."

Kiva opened the field book at the back. She took out the two sheets and held them to the light.

A keeper's line, four days old. A clerk's copy of a line two hundred and thirty-four years old, with a margin full of bent pins.

"There's a woman at the Compact Library who can read this hand," she said. "In the superseded stacks. Mara says so."

Orla looked at the marks for a long moment.

"I know those stacks," she said. Her voice was odd. "I've been in them. I read a thing there once that I've told nobody, because I'd no right to think it had to do with you." She gave the sheet back, with care. "I think I may have to stop being so sure of that. Not tonight. Tonight you'll fill in your columns."

The lamp hissed. Down the lane, the last post leaned out of its row into the dark.

Kiva opened the book on her good knee.

*Exercise:* Flask qualifier, the Pit. Fifth company. Rung one.

*What went wrong:* I called the whole floor dark. The foreman said he'd leave a gate and I didn't. I set the mark for the loudest thing and never asked what was nearest. Walked off with my own bag and didn't watch the string.

*Who was hurt:* Greta Stroud, both hands. Perrin, ribs again. Me.

*What it cost:* A branch, a fortnight. Eleven teams their day. 0.31 of a dram in nine seconds. The sealed dram, for my hand.

*How long to come back:* Fingers, a week, to where they were. Knee, the second. The rest I don't know.

*Second go:* Told him what I couldn't do. Was let. Shut the first at the top. Set the second like a pan. Counted through it. Gave it to Dray when she said *mine.*

She stopped at the last column. She thought about a brick lane, and a line of chalk on a slate, and a reason that had won on Saturday and fouled a grid on Wednesday.

*Why:* A man with nothing in front of him waits. A husk goes looking. I'd been fighting a man.

That was true. It was not all of it.

*The work goes across. It costs more than both doors. I don't know what carries it.*

She looked at that. Then, smaller, at the very foot of the page, where she had once written *kneeling:*

*Six. And find out who wrote it down.*

She shut the book, and sat with it on her knee in the yellow light.

The disk was cold. She did not take it out to see. She knew what it weighed, and what it had felt like for the length of a road stair, and that there had been people before her who had known the same and put it on a page.

She did not think that any of them had been told why, either.

But somebody had kept the page.
