# Chapter 17 — Kiva: Twenty Handfuls

Orla Dane drew a line across the Old Yard lane with the end of her stick.

It was Tuesday night, and the lamp on its hook made the usual yellow room in the dark. The chalk had run out years ago, so the line was only a scrape in the dirt, four paces short of the crooked post. She drew a second mark at the post's foot, a cross, and leaned on the stick to look at the two of them.

"There," she said. "That's the whole of it. You stand on the line. You go from the line to the cross in one bound, on the Ember. You come down on the cross with the still door open and nothing else. And the moment your right foot is on the ground, I push."

She showed Kiva the end of the stick. It had an iron ferrule, worn bright.

"Where?"

"Left shoulder. Hard enough to move a girl. Not hard enough to move a stance." Orla lowered herself onto her crate with her bound leg out. "If I can't move you, and your line stays cold, and there's no metal on your legs when I look, then the speed went across. That's a sequence. One bound of one. It's the smallest one I could think of."

Kiva looked at the four paces. She had crossed forty in the Cellhouse with a husk behind her. They did not look like much.

"One bound," she said. "I can do three."

"You can do three and land on a knee that's been strapped for a month. One. Low. You come down on the right." Orla's crooked face did not change. "And you'll want the words for how it goes wrong, because it will, and I'd like us to mean the same thing. If you shut the first door and stand there a while and then open the second, the speed's gone into your boots and the dirt. You've put the work down. That's a drop. If you open the second while the first is still in the doorway, they'll fight, and your line will tell you so. That's a jam. You've had one of those. It was on a run plate."

"I remember it."

"A drop gets you pushed over. A jam gets you burned. So you've a word, and it's the same word the warden gave you. *Off.* Yours to say, not mine. The line warms, you say it, and you shut everything you've got and fall down like a sack if that's what it takes." She tapped the ferrule on the crate. "I'd rather pick you up than quench you. Is that understood?"

"Yes."

"Then settle your ash, and let's see how bad it is."

The ration came in a wax-stoppered glass no bigger than a thimble. Third Division drew half a dram a week from the Low Lane store, on Mondays, signed for, and it was meant to last through five afternoons of ordinary practice. Kiva had spent a few hundredths of this week's on Monday's drill. She tipped the rest into her left palm now and closed her fingers, and felt the small warmth go up her wrist and settle.

She stood on the line.

*Ember.* The metal came off the cuff on her left forearm and ran, cool and quick, down her sides and her thighs, and closed round both calves in two dull copper struts. She bent her knees. She did not think about the knee.

She went.

It was a good bound. It was low and flat, and the cross came up under her exactly where she had meant it to. She shut the Ember as her right boot touched. She felt the struts let go and the metal start for home, and she counted one, as she had counted it four hundred times in this lane, and reached down for the weight of the ground.

The stick took her in the shoulder.

She sat down in the dirt.

"Drop," said Orla.

"I was planted."

"You were planted a breath after I hit you. I felt you arrive." Orla had not moved from the crate. She had only straightened her arm. "You came down, and you stopped, and then you asked the ground for its weight. That's your drill. It's a very clean drill. Up."

Kiva got up. The seat of her trousers was damp, and her knee had noticed the landing and was thinking it over.

"The stop's the thing I'm meant to carry," she said. "Isn't it? The speed. So I can't stop first."

"I'd have thought not."

"So the still door's got to be open when I land."

"I didn't say that. You said that." Orla looked at her steadily. "Go on, then. Try it your way. You know your word."

She stood on the line again. *Ember.* The struts closed. She went, and this time, in the last arm's length of the bound, with the cross rushing at her, she reached for the stance before she had let the Ember go.

It was like putting a foot down on a stair in the dark and finding another foot already there.

The two of them met in her. The weight came up out of the ground, and the speed had not finished going forward, and each of them wanted the whole of her legs. The struts shivered on her calves. Something tore, lightly, along her breastbone, a thread of heat laid on the skin from the inside.

"One," said Kiva. "*Off.*"

She shut them both. She did it badly, and all at once, and for the length of a stumble she was a girl with no doors at all, going forward at the speed of a Stride into an old black post. She got her right hand up. The post took the rest. It was two hundred years old and banded with iron, and it did not give her anything back.

She stood with her forehead against the wood and breathed.

"Jam," said Orla. "And a called one. You said it inside the breath. That's the only part of that I liked, and I liked it a good deal." A pause. "Is the line still warm?"

Kiva put two fingers of her right hand to her breastbone, through the shirt. The bottom thread was hot, and it was already cooling.

"Going."

"Then sit down till it's gone."

She sat on the other crate. Her hand had gone on up from the breastbone without her leave, to the cord at her collar, and she felt the disk through the cloth. She did not take it out. It was the shape and the cold of a coin left on a windowsill.

"It doesn't know either," said Orla.

Kiva let go of it.

"I wasn't asking it."

"No. You were only seeing whether it had an opinion." Orla's voice was dry, and it was not unkind. "I've sat on this crate for three weeks watching you not do that. Don't start again because the work's got hard. I can tell you a drop from a jam, because I've a stick and two eyes. I can't tell you what the right one feels like. I've never seen one. Neither has that." She nodded at Kiva's collar. "So there are three of us in this yard who don't know, and you're the only one who can find out."

They did it twice more that night.

The third was a drop, because she was frightened of the jam, and she let the Ember go so early that she came down as nothing but herself and had to catch her whole weight on her right leg. The fourth was a jam, because she was ashamed of the drop. She called *off* on the fourth before the line had properly warmed, and fell over the cross on her hands, and lay there for a moment with her cheek in the dirt of the Old Yard, thinking about nothing at all.

"That's enough," said Orla. "How's the glass?"

Kiva did not need to look at the glass. The ash was in her, and she could feel how much. It was the same sense that told her when a bench cell wanted filling.

"More than half gone."

"In four goes."

"The drill takes hardly any." She sat up. "I've done the drill two hundred times on a ration. I didn't know the other way cost."

"Nor did I. I told you. I've never seen it." Orla reached for her stick and got herself onto her feet, slowly, with the grunt she allowed herself after dark. "So now we know one new thing, and it's a dull one. You can afford to be wrong about eight times a week. I'd spend them carefully."

Warden Druce had borrowed a hat stand.

It stood on the cast-room table on Wednesday afternoon, an ugly thing of black iron with a ring of hooks at the top, and it had clearly come from the porter's lodge, because the porter's own umbrella was still hanging on it. To the upright the warden had tied eight flour bags, one above the other, like buttons up the front of a coat. Each bulged. A length of red cord ran through all eight.

He had drawn a face on the top one, in charcoal. It was not a good face.

The benches were full. There were thirty-one teams on the list, and every one of them had sent its blade and its catcher at the least. Kiva sat between Kellan and Anwen with the field book open on her knee. Perrin sat on the end of the row, very upright, in the manner of a boy whose ribs had been strapped that morning by a healer with no sense of humor.

Anwen had come straight from the tram. She had been back from the Ferren culverts for a day, after two nights of carrying a lamp behind Graywater's ward line, and she had the grey, polished look of somebody who has slept in the afternoon and not enjoyed it.

"A Handful," said Druce. He held up one grey-stained finger. "One knot. Four lent legs. You've all cut one or caught one, or you'd not be in this room. Now. A Flask is twenty Handfuls in the yield book. Who wants to tell me what it looks like?"

Nobody did.

"A big dog," said somebody at the back, at last.

"Hm. That's what I'd have said at your age, and that's what's printed on the cover of the penny books, and it's wrong." The warden laid his hand flat on the top bag. "A pattern that holds up a dog won't hold up twenty dogs' worth of ash. It hasn't the strength. So when a tear's wide enough to let twenty Handfuls through at once, the ash doesn't make one large knot. It makes eight. One above the other, on one cord. The Home Line calls it a string. Each knot's about sixty drams by the book, and each lends its own limbs, and the whole affair stands about as high as the roof of a tram."

He let them look at the hat stand.

"The top one reads. That's what I've drawn the face for. It turns to the brightest worked thing it can feel, the same as a Handful does, only it feels a good deal further. Twenty paces. Five-and-twenty. The other seven don't read at all. They walk where the top one's pointed."

Kellan had stopped writing. He was looking at the red cord.

"So you cut the top," he said.

"So you cut the top. Stand up, Mr. Renn, since you've a voice. What happens then?"

"Sixty drams come down. The count opens."

"And the other seven?"

Kellan was silent for a moment. Kiva watched him work it through. She had seen him do the same thing over a load board in the Cellhouse.

"They're still standing," he said slowly. "They've no reader. So it's blind."

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"That's the right answer, and I'm obliged to you for it. The book says a slow hundred. I've seen eighty. After that the next knot down wakes, and it starts to read, and you've a Flask again that's one rung shorter and has just watched you kneel on the floor with your hands full." Druce took a penknife from his apron. "The yield book calls a knot a rung, because a string's a ladder when you come to climb down it. You go from the top. One rung, and you catch it. Then the next. You can't light a blade inside three paces of an open count. So you can't cut the second until the first is sealed or you've walked the thing off somewhere else."

He cut the cord above the top bag.

The charcoal face slumped. The bag split along a seam he must have unpicked beforehand, and sixty drams of dead ash came down the front of the hat stand in a soft grey sheet and heaped on the table, and spilled off it, and went on spilling.

Kiva had never seen sixty drams fall. It was ten of her tins, all at once, and it looked like more.

"Sixty counts," said the warden mildly. "Who's got a pan?"

There was a small unhappy laugh along the benches.

"No. You'd be there till supper. A Flask's caught on a purse sheet. Six paces square, waxed, lead in the hem, and a cord run through the hem like the string of a bag. The catcher sets it and calls it. Three more take the corners. You bring it up round the fall and you haul the cord, three turns and a seal, and that's a bag. Bagged inside the count is tinned, for the book. You carry it off dark and pan it at a table in your own time." He wiped the knife. "Four people to a bag. Which means that while your rung's on the floor, your blade's dark and your ward's dark and your Carry's got a corner in his fist. Your whole team is on its knees. And seven knots are standing behind you with nobody minding them."

He looked along the rows.

"So a Flask isn't done by a team. It's done by a company. Four teams, sixteen hands, and a foreman off a chartered crew who answers for the lot. Your team takes one rung, hold and cut and bag and carry, the whole of it. The other three mind the string while you do. Then you get up and mind it for them. The foreman's own people take the bottom four, because the last two rungs carry the legs, and when the seventh goes, it sits down wherever it happens to be. You'll not be asked to stand under that this year."

"What's the mark?" That was a Stonehand voice.

"Fifty of your sixty, bagged inside the count, at Second or better. Nothing printed. Nothing fouled. Nobody hurt. And then the foreman's four columns, for the company. Closed, hurt, what still works, what was tinned." Druce folded the knife. "It isn't a fight. I want that understood before any of you goes near the Pit. A Handful's a fight, a short one. A Flask is a schedule. The hands that keep it are the catcher's, and the best blade in this room is going to spend most of that morning standing in the dark being told *not yet*."

Kellan sat down.

"I've had practice," he said, to nobody.

The companies were posted on the cast-room door.

There was a crush for it. Kiva let the crush go by, because of the knee, and so she read the fifth sheet over the shoulder of somebody who had got there first and was reading it very carefully.

*Fifth company. Wednesday the 26th, forenoon. The Pit, Brickfields. Foreman: H. Vane, Home Line.*

*T7 (17th). T4 of '78 (18th). T33 (19th). T12 (20th).*

The shoulder was broad, and the head above it was shaved.

"It seems we're a company," said Lowen Brask.

He turned round. He wore a plain grey coat instead of a Hall tunic, and there was a notebook under his arm. He looked exactly as calm as he had looked on his knees in the sand with a line judge counting over him.

"You're on a license team," said Kiva. It was a stupid thing to say. It was what came out.

"Old Four. The year puts a number after it so we don't get your post." He nodded at the sheet. "We tinned twenty and six-tenths two springs ago. Last year the board took nine teams to a Flask. The year before, it took six. We've been eighteenth on somebody's list for a long while." He considered her, mildly. "I'm the Carry. I'd not have picked a company with you in it, if I'd been asked. I don't mean that rudely. I mean I haven't finished thinking about you, and now I'll have to do it quicker."

"We lost to you."

"By two. I've the lane card pinned over my bed." He said it without a smile, and then there was one, slow and a little rueful. "There's a return on the eighteenth. Your court, your book. I've been reading your book. It's a strange one."

"I haven't read it," said Kiva.

"No. Home sides never do." Brask tucked the notebook more firmly under his arm. "I'd read rule nine, Miss Rowan. I mean that kindly as well. Good afternoon."

He went away down the passage at his slow rolling walk. Kiva found that Perrin was beside her, holding his side.

"He taught me to fall down," said Perrin. "When I was twelve. It took him a month. He said I was the only boy he'd ever met who argued with the floor." He looked after the grey coat. "He's told you where to look. He does that. It's maddening. You go and look, and it's true, and you still lose."

"What's rule nine?"

"I'm Stonehand. I can't read your book. It'd be disloyal." He shifted his ribs. "Ask your captain."

Someone laughed behind them. It was short and had no pleasure in it.

Selka Dray was leaning on the passage wall with her arms folded. Her cropped red hair was damp, as if she had come from a lane. She held a slip of yellow card between two fingers.

"Reserve blade," she said, when she saw Kiva look at it. "In case somebody's Ember breaks an ankle. That's what a four-second kill's worth this spring." Her eyes went to the fifth sheet. "The Pit. Did any of you look at where it gets its power? I did. I'd nothing else to do on Sunday. There's a pump bank across the road that keeps half the Ferren streets out of the river." She pushed herself off the wall. "Fifth company. I wonder who thought of that."

She walked off the other way.

Nobody had answered her on the Sag Line either. Kiva looked at the yellow card going away down the passage, and wished very much that she had a thing to say that was not *I know*.

Mara Rowan had brought eleven years of books to the Works in a handcart.

She had not needed the cart. The boy from the bakery had offered to carry them, and Tobin had offered the van, and she had said no to both and pushed the cart through the Thursday morning streets herself. She wanted her hands occupied, and she wanted to arrive knowing how heavy it was.

Examiner Rusk had the books in three stacks by ten o'clock. He was a broad, tidy man of about sixty, with a clerk's linen guards over his cuffs and an operator's thick, scarred thumbs. He had been through every one of them. She could see his slips of paper sticking out of the pages like tongues.

"Operator Rowan," he said. "Sit down. I'm going to say what I've found, in the order I found it, and then what I've decided. If I've a fact wrong, stop me. If you only dislike it, let me finish."

Mara sat.

"The household filing of the thirteenth. A resident minor woke with her hand on your flat panel. The panel took a three-door pattern. The shared core tripped at sixteen minutes to three. You quenched the panel by hand, in the dark, and you were at the records office when it opened to file against yourself." He turned a slip. "I've had thirty years of filings. Most arrive a week late with a lawyer's letter. That goes in your favor, and so I've written it."

He turned another.

"The condition of 2677 persists. You say so in your own hand. The minor has a sponsor's certificate, which I've read. It keeps her off cells. That's a help. It isn't a cure."

"No," said Mara.

"Mast six." Rusk laid a half sheet on the desk between them, covered in a quick slanting hand that she knew. "You declined to recalibrate it. You wrote down why. On the twenty-eighth it rang four minutes ahead of its neighbors, and a Home Line officer has taken the trouble to tell me what he did with the four minutes." He looked at her over the paper. "I spent a morning trying to decide whether that was skill or luck. Then I read your reason again. You hadn't thought of the bell. You'd only refused to hide a line you didn't understand. I've concluded it was neither. It was a habit, and a good one."

Mara said nothing. She had learned on a great many benches that the moment after praise was when the meter fell.

"There's a bench cell behind you," said Rusk. "A plain lamp tune. Read it for me, please."

She got up and went to it. It was a small municipal cell in an oak cradle, humming to itself. She laid her hand on the case, and felt the tune come up slow and even through her fingers, with a little drag in it where somebody had been impatient.

"Lamp tune. Steady. It was laid in a hurry and smoothed afterward. I'd give it eight months before it wants doing again."

"Seven, I'd have said. Near enough." He had not looked at the cell. He was looking at her arm. "That's your left hand, Operator Rowan."

The room was quiet. Down in the yard a meter van was being loaded, and somebody dropped a crate.

She could have said that she favored it. He would have had no way to know.

"The middle of my right palm has had no feeling since the Linden Row cell," said Mara. "Three years. I can hold a wrench with it. I can't read with it. I taught the left." She turned round. "I didn't declare it. I told myself that a reading hand was a reading hand, and the license didn't say which. I knew better than that when I told it to myself."

Rusk wrote for some time.

"Thank you," he said. "I'd guessed in the third book. Your figures don't change. Your handwriting on the cell tags does." He blotted the page. "Here's what I've decided, then. I don't average, Operator Rowan. People ask me to. They say she did one thing well and one thing ill, call it even. A cell doesn't call it even, and I'm not paid to."

He set his thick hands on the desk.

"Your Grade Two is suspended to the autumn sitting. It is not revoked. You'll re-sit the practical in the ninth month, and the left will be entered as your reading hand, and after that nobody will have cause to wonder. Until then you tune nothing, refill nothing, and seal nothing. The flat panel stays quenched and tagged. For three years of an undeclared hand, I'm obliged to fine you a week's fees. I've gone through your books for the smallest week I could find. It was the one with the flood."

Mara breathed in, and out.

A third of the money, until autumn. She had expected to lose it for good. She found that she was not grateful, and not angry either. It was the right size. That was all she had ever asked of a number.

"Repair and reconciliation continue," said Rusk. "And there's this, which is not a kindness, so don't thank me. The Home Line has written to the board about what they're calling a dirty spring. The board wants the baseline books gone through, every mast on the Sag Line and every drum on the Brickfields trunk, by somebody who won't flatten a line to make it sit. I've forty operators who can read a drum. I had exactly one who wrote *recal. would mask trend.*" He pushed a thick canvas-backed book across the desk. "Records rate. It pays about half of what the tuning did. That's the trunk's own book. Start there."

She took it. It was heavier than it looked.

"The Brickfields trunk," said Mara. "That's the Ferren pump bank."

"Twenty-four cells, three pumps, and a branch off to that clay hole the Circuit plays its games in. One link between them. I'd like to know whether its baseline's been creeping as well, and since when." He was already drawing the next file toward him. "That's all. Take your cart round to the side door. The front steps are murder on the wheels."

---

"Suspended," said Perrin. "Not revoked. I'd like to shake that man's hand. I'd also like to put him in a sack."

"She says it's the right size."

"She would. She's a Rowan. You'd all thank a person for hitting you if he'd measured first."

They were in the back room of the Pryce laundry, among the hampers, with the team book open on an ironing board. It was Thursday evening. Through the wall the big machines were turning over, and the air smelled of starch and hot linen. Kiva had come down from the flat with the taste of a quiet supper still in her mouth. Mara had eaten with the canvas-backed book open beside her plate. On the first page there was a diagram of a bus, a long black line with twenty-four small squares on it, and one thin branch going off through a mark like a hinge.

*S. link. Pinned and locked, 2661.*

Kiva had read it upside down. She read most things upside down.

"Two floors," said Kellan. He had his own paper beside the book. "Seven-tenths each, paid. We've nothing else on the page but the reserve, and that's sealed. So it's these two, and whatever the school gives us for nothing." He looked up. "I'd like the first tomorrow. A skinned one, if the Kennel's still throwing them, and I'd be surprised if it's stopped. I've cut that skin once in my life. I'd like not to be early the second time."

"And the sheet," said Anwen. "The warden will lend us one. I asked. If we're to bag sixty at the Pit, I'd like to have bagged twenty-four somewhere first. All four of us. I've never held a corner."

"I've held four hundred kilos," said Perrin. "I expect a corner's within me."

"Your ribs are within you. That's all the healer wants to hear about for three weeks." She had her list, and she wrote on it. "The second floor we keep. Mace says the Home Line means to let every catcher and every blade on the list watch a real one, if a tear obliges. There've been ten on the district board now. He thinks one will." She looked at Kiva. "I'd sooner spend the second floor after we know what we're looking at."

It was agreed in about a minute. Kiva noticed that. Three weeks ago, it had taken them an evening and half a pot of tea to agree on a catch kit.

Kellan signed. Perrin signed, with a flourish. Anwen signed, and held the pencil out.

"How's the other thing?" she said. "The yard."

"Four tries. Two of each."

"Each what?"

Kiva told them. She told it plainly, the line and the cross and the stick, the drop and the jam. Perrin winced at the post. Kellan asked how long the bound was, and wrote it down.

Anwen did not write. She sat with the pencil across her knees and a small frown, as she sat when a thing reminded her of another.

"The line changes over at two in the morning," she said. "On the culverts. Graywater doesn't let a ward-holder stand more than four hours. So at two there's a relief, and I thought I knew how that would go. One drops, the other raises. I'd have done it that way." She shook her head. "They don't. The new one comes up behind the old one's shoulder and brings her wall up *inside* the first. And the first one leans on it. I watched her do it. She leaned until hers wasn't holding anything, and then she let it go, and there was never a moment with no wall and never a moment with two. Nobody said *now*. Mace says if you can hear the join, it's a bad one."

"Lean into it," said Kiva.

"Till yours isn't doing anything. Yes." Anwen's eyes were on her. "That's two people. I don't know that it's the same."

"It's the nearest thing anyone's told me."

She signed the book with her right hand, and went to find out.

---

The Old Yard was cold, and Orla had the lamp lit.

Kiva stood on the line. She had thought about it all the way up from Eastbank on the tram. She would not shut the Ember and then open the still door, and she would not grab for the one while holding the other. She would bring the stance up inside the Stride, as a relief brings up a wall, and lean the speed over into it a little at a time.

*Ember.* The struts closed. She went.

In the air she opened the still door, a crack, gently, and began to lean.

For about as long as it takes to blink, it was lovely. The two of them lay alongside each other in her legs like two hands on one rope. She had time to think that Anwen had been right.

Then the bottom line lit like a fuse.

"One. *Off!*"

She came down on her right foot with nothing open and nothing in her head, and Orla's stick, which had been on its way, stopped a finger's width from her shoulder and stayed there.

"That was a jam," said Orla, "with manners. Sit."

"I didn't grab. I leaned it across. Slowly."

"You held two doors and were polite about it. It's still two doors." Orla lowered the stick. "Who told you to lean?"

"Anwen. It's how Graywater relieves a ward."

"It is. I've stood in that line. And it works because the two walls belong to two different women, and neither of them has to hold both." She was quiet for a moment. "It's the right notion in the wrong place. The work goes across. The doors don't overlap. I don't know how you get the one without the other, Rowan. I know that was a stack. Your line knew it before I did."

The thread along her breastbone was cooling. It took longer than it had on Tuesday.

"Again," said Kiva.

"When it's cold."

When it was cold, she stood on the line a sixth time. She had decided nothing. She had only an idea that the Ember must be shut and the stance must be open, and that there was a place between them no wider than a hair where neither was true and the speed had to live anyhow.

*Ember.*

The metal came off the cuff. It ran to her elbow, and to her ribs, and slowed. It reached her hips like the last of the water coming out of a jug.

It stopped.

She had already bent her knees. She had already gone. She left the line with one strut closed on her left calf and the other a grey smear on her right knee, and the bound died under her halfway to the cross. She came down flat on both feet, with a jolt that went from her heels to her teeth, two paces short.

The metal ran home on its own.

Kiva stood very still in the dirt of the lane. She was waiting to find out about the knee.

It hurt. It held.

"What was that?" said Orla sharply.

"The glass." Her voice came out small. "It's empty. I thought I'd one more in it."

It was Thursday. The store opened on Monday.

Orla looked at her for a while across the yellow light. Then she bent, with a hand on her bad leg, and picked up the stick, and laid it across her knees.

"Six goes," she said. "Two drops, three jams, and one that never left the ground. And four days now with nothing to spend." She did not say it harshly. She said it as she would have read a tally off a slate. "Get your book. Write it while it stings. I'll want the *why* column filled in, and I'll want it true, and *I ran out* isn't a reason. It's only what happened."

Kiva sat on the crate and opened the book on her good knee.

*Exercise:* Stride into stance. One bound. Line to the cross.

*What went wrong:* Everything, six times. Shut the first and stopped, and then opened the second: a drop. Opened the second with the first still open: a jam. Leaned one into the other: still a jam. Ran dry in the air.

*Who was hurt:* Me. The post. Knee jarred on the sixth. Bottom line, three times.

*What it cost:* The whole week's glass by Thursday. The drill never cost a tenth of it.

*How long to come back:* Line cold in about a hundred counts. Longer each time.

*Second go:* None till Monday.

She held the pencil over the last column. Across the yard, the crooked post leaned out of its row, as it had leaned for longer than anyone had kept the reason.

*Why:* I keep trying to do it with the doors. Orla says the work goes across and the doors don't. I don't know where the work is when it isn't in either one.

She looked at that for some time.

It was the most honest thing on the page, and it was a question, and there was nobody in Meridian she could take it to.
