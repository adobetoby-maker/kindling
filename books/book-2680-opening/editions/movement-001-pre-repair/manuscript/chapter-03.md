# Chapter 3 — Kiva: Sequence, Not Stack

The Old Yard was not supposed to be used after dark.

There was a sign on the gate that said so, in brass letters gone green. *CROOKED LINE SCHOOL. OLD YARD. CEREMONIAL USE ONLY. CLOSED AT SUNSET.* Orla Dane had a key to the gate, and a hand lamp, and a very loose idea of what *ceremonial* meant.

"Every time I teach something," she said, unlocking the padlock, "it's a ceremony. Ask anyone."

She swung the gate open with her stick and went in. Kiva followed with Anwen and Perrin behind her.

It was Friday, the day after the north round. Seven days to the Cellhouse. The yard was dark and cold and smelled of old earth. Orla hung her lamp on a hook on the first shed, and its yellow light spread down the long strip of packed ground, over the line of black practice posts, to the far end where the last post leaned out of the line and into the shadow.

Kiva had been here on Thursday nights for four years. She had been here on Mondays and Wednesdays too, when it was just her and Orla. She knew every rut in the ground. She knew which post had a nail sticking out of it at knee height and which shed roof dripped when it rained.

Tonight it felt different. Tonight there was a form folded in Mara's apron pocket with three words on the back of it.

"Right," said Orla. She lowered herself onto an upturned crate by the shed and stretched her bad leg out in front of her. "Your aunt is a better teacher than I am. Did you know that?"

"She doesn't teach," said Kiva.

"She just did. She told you exactly what you need to learn in seven days, in three words, on the back of a form." Orla held up one finger. "Meter. You need a way to see what your doors are doing before they do it." A second finger. "Shutdown path. You need to be able to close a door on purpose, cleanly, when it opens without being asked." A third. "Book. You need to write down what happened, every time, so the next attempt starts from what you know and not what you hope." She lowered her hand. "Every serious cell in this city has those three things. Your aunt wants you to be at least as safe as a street lamp. That's not too much to ask."

"It's in seven days," said Perrin.

"It is in seven days," agreed Orla. "So we'd better start."

---

She started with the thing Kiva already knew. She always did.

"Rank," said Orla. "Tell me what it is."

"How much of you is lit."

"And Hold?"

"Control. How well you can manage what's lit."

"Those are the board's words. Give me yours."

Kiva thought about it. She thought about the shop, and the cells on their racks, and the flat above it with its six rooms.

"Rank is how many rooms in the house have the lamps on," she said. "Hold is whether you can carry a lamp from one room to another without dropping it."

Orla smiled. It made her crooked nose more crooked. "Good. And you?"

"I've got three rooms lit," said Kiva slowly. "And every time I try to carry one lamp, I try to carry all three. And I drop them."

"That's it. That's your whole problem, in one sentence." Orla leaned forward on her stick. "Your rank isn't small, Kiva. The board can't read it properly, but it isn't small. Three doors open at thirteen is three doors. That's more light than most people in this city will ever have. But the board doesn't post you on light. It posts you on what you can carry. And you can't carry anything yet without the other two lamps jumping into your hands."

"I know."

"Then here's what you don't know. Or what you know and won't do." Orla put her stick across her knees. "You keep trying to use them together. Ward and Edge at once. Stride and stance at once. You think if you could just stack them, you'd be strong. You'd be faster than Kellan Renn and steadier than Perrin and you'd ward like Anwen while you did it." She shook her head. "That's not a skill you have. It might be one day. It isn't now. Right now, every time you stack, they fight."

"So what do I do?"

"Sequence," said Orla. "Not stack. One door. Close it. Next door. Close it. Next." She tapped the ground with her stick three times, slowly. "It'll be slower. It'll look stupid. People will laugh. It's still better than a cell screaming in your hands."

Kiva looked at the ground where the stick had tapped. Three marks in the dirt.

She had heard this before. Orla had been saying some version of it for two years. *One at a time. Slower. Clean before fast.* And Kiva had tried, and it had been slow, and it had looked stupid, and in every sparring bout she had ever fought, somebody faster with one good door had walked straight through the gap while she was still closing the first.

So she had gone back to trying to stack. Because stacking, when it worked for half a second, felt like flying.

"You're thinking about the gap," said Orla.

Kiva looked up.

"You're thinking that the gap between one door and the next is where everybody hits you." Orla nodded. "It is. It's going to be. That's the price. But you can't make the gap smaller by pretending it isn't there. You make it smaller by closing cleaner. And you can't close clean until you can see what's opening." She leaned back. "Which brings us to your aunt's first word."

---

"You've had a meter all along," said Orla. "You just don't read it."

She pointed her stick at Kiva's chest.

Kiva put her hand there without thinking. Under her shirt, under the disk, the three heat-lines lay quiet.

"They light up when the doors compete," said Orla. "Yes? Every time. In the frame. At the draw bench. That night in the lane when your slab discharged and took the mast out."

"Yes."

"Do they all light at once?"

Kiva opened her mouth to say yes. Then she stopped.

She thought about the draw bench yesterday. The lamp lit orange. The hand door waking up to look. And then the heat. But it had not been all three at once. There had been a moment, very short, when it was only one. The bottom line, the one nearest her breastbone. It had gone warm first. Then the second. Then the third, at her shoulder, and then the lamp had flared.

"No," she said slowly. "The bottom one goes first."

"How long before the next?"

"I don't know. Not long. Half a breath?"

"Half a breath," said Orla, "is a long time. You can do a lot in half a breath. You can close a door in half a breath, if you see it coming." She pointed at Anwen. "Anwen. Stand there, please. About a pace off her left side. Have a ward ready, but don't raise it unless I tell you."

Anwen went and stood where she was told. She held her hands loosely in front of her, palms in, not quite touching.

"Perrin. Behind her. Hand flat between her shoulders. If her stance goes, I want you to feel it before she does."

Perrin moved behind Kiva and put his big warm palm on her back. "I'm feeling it," he said. "I'm very present."

"Kiva," said Orla. "Edge. Just Edge. Hold it. Don't light it. And the moment you feel that bottom line go warm, before anything else happens, you say *one*. Out loud. That's all. You don't have to stop it. You just have to say it."

Kiva raised her right fist and asked for Edge.

The metal came off the copper slab on her left forearm and flowed across to her right hand the way it always did, as if it were going downhill. It ran over her knuckles and closed into the bar. Short, flat, square-ended. Ugly. She held it out in front of her, dark.

Nothing happened. She held it. The yard was quiet. Somewhere out in the city a tram bell rang.

Anwen shifted her weight beside her.

And the hand door woke up.

Kiva felt it lift, cool and curious, toward Anwen. Toward the faint shimmer of the ward Anwen was holding ready between her palms. The hand door liked wards the way it liked cells. It wanted to see.

And the bottom line went warm. Then the second.

"One," said Kiva. Too late. The second line was already hot, and the third was starting, and the bar on her fist jerked as if someone had bumped it.

"That was two," said Orla. "Again."

They did it again. Kiva held the dark bar. Anwen stood with her ward ready. Perrin breathed slowly behind her. This time Kiva did not look at the bar. She did not look at anything. She listened to her chest the way Mara listened to a cell.

The hand door lifted.

The bottom line warmed.

"One," said Kiva.

The second line warmed a moment later. But she had said it first. Before. She had felt the first wire go hot and named it before the second.

"That's one," said Orla. "Good. Again."

They did it eleven times. She got it early on the second try, and late on the third, and late on the fourth, and early on the fifth and sixth. By the tenth her chest ached the way it did after a long day at the shop, a dull, used ache. By the eleventh she could feel the bottom line move before the hand door had quite finished waking up. It was like hearing the click in a tram's brakes before the car begins to slow.

"That's your meter," said Orla. "It's always been there. You've just been reading it after the needle's already swung."

Kiva let the bar fall back into the slab. Her right hand was shaking a little.

"Is it always going to hurt?"

"I don't know," said Orla honestly. "Probably, while they're fighting. Maybe less when they stop." She shifted her leg. "Rest five minutes. Then we'll learn how to close."

---

Closing was harder.

Each door shut differently, and Orla could only teach one of them from the inside. She was Ember-door. She had been Ember-door for forty years, Torch rank, which was as far as one door went, and she had used it on a hundred rifts with the Home Line before her knee gave out. She could feel Edge. She could not feel a ward or a stance any more than she could feel someone else's toothache.

So she made the others teach.

"Ember first," she said. "I know that one. Form the bar."

Kiva formed it.

"Now let it go."

Kiva let it go. The metal ran back over her fist and into the slab.

"No," said Orla. "You dropped it. Do it again, and this time put it down."

"What's the difference?"

"When you drop it, it goes back to the slab, but you're still holding the door open behind it. The door's still awake. It's just got nothing in its hands. So it looks around for something else to hold." Orla held up her own right fist. Her Ember was a dark iron-grey slab on a worn steel cuff. The metal ran from it over her knuckles and closed into a short, broad, leaf-shaped blade, old-fashioned and very plain. She held it. Then the metal ran back. It did not hurry. It went back into the cuff the way water goes back into a bucket when you tip it gently, and then, after it was gone, Orla let out a slow breath through her nose.

"There," she said. "Did you see that?"

"You breathed out."

"After. After it was home. That's when I shut the door behind it. Not while it's going. After." She looked at Kiva. "You've been closing the door on the metal while it's still in the doorway. That's why it catches."

Kiva formed the bar. She let the metal run back. She waited until it was all the way home in the slab, the copper warm and heavy on her forearm. Then she breathed out and imagined a door closing.

It closed. She felt it go quiet, like a cell powering down.

"Better," said Orla. "Anwen. Your turn."

Anwen came and stood in front of Kiva. She did not like teaching. She had told Kiva so many times. She said it was like being asked to explain how you walk. But she did it the way she did everything, plainly and thoroughly, as if she were writing it down.

"You make a ward like a tray," she said.

"I make a ward like a ward."

"You make it like a tray. Flat. Out in front of you. With your arms stiff." Anwen raised her own hands, and a ward came up between them, faintly silver, the size of a dinner plate. It was not flat. It curved a little, like the side of a bowl. "A ward's not a thing you hold. It's a door you hold shut. You lean on it. And when you want to put it down, you don't drop it. You fold it." She brought her palms slowly together. The silver curve folded in on itself like a closing hand and was gone. "If you drop it, it splashes."

"Splashes?"

"Everything you were holding back comes through at once. Like taking your thumb off a hose." Anwen looked at her. "You splash every time. That's why the hand door doesn't close after. It's still got water all over it."

Kiva raised a ward. It came up between her palms, tray-sized and flat.

"Curve it," said Anwen.

She tried. It wobbled. It curved, a little.

"Now fold."

She brought her palms together. The ward folded halfway, and then the hand door, startled, let go of the rest of it all at once. There was a soft pop, and a puff of cool air in her face.

"Splash," said Anwen. "Again."

The fourth time, it folded. Not neatly. But it folded, and the hand door closed after it without reaching for anything else, and Kiva felt it go quiet.

"Perrin," said Orla.

Perrin rubbed his hands together. "Right. Stance." He came around in front of Kiva. "You're good at stance. You're better at stance than you should be. But you never come out of it. I've watched you. You sink down into it like a bath, and then you just sort of stay there, and the still door's still open under you, holding the ground."

"So how do I get out?"

"You give the ground its weight back." He planted his feet. Kiva felt him do it. It was not visible, exactly. He did not move. But something about the way he stood changed, the way a bench changes when someone heavy sits on it. "You took it, when you went into stance. You borrowed it. So when you come out, you give it back. Lift your heels a tiny bit. Breathe in, not out. And sort of—" He made a face. "Say thank you."

"Say thank you?"

"To the ground. Not out loud. In your knees." He shrugged. "That's what my first teacher at the Hall told me. I thought it was stupid. Works, though."

She went into stance. She felt the heaviness come up through her feet and settle in her knees. Then she lifted her heels very slightly, and breathed in, and thought *thank you* at the packed earth of the Old Yard.

The still door eased. It did not close all the way. But it eased.

"Good," said Perrin. "Weird, isn't it?"

"Very."

"Now," said Orla, from her crate. "All together. Sequence. Edge. Close it properly. Then ward. Fold it. Then stance. Give it back." She tapped the ground three times with her stick. "Slowly. I don't care how long it takes."

---

The first time, it went wrong in the first gap.

Kiva formed the bar. She held it. She let the metal run back toward the slab, and she waited for it to get home, and she waited, and it was taking so long, and the ward was the next thing, and she could feel Anwen standing there, and she reached for the ward.

The metal was not home yet.

The bottom heat-line flashed. Then all three. The hand door opened straight into the Ember door's doorway. The last of the metal, still running back over her wrist, jerked and spat a hot orange spark from its edge, though she had never lit it. Her slab went hot on her arm, hot enough to hurt. Somewhere down the yard, on the shed at the far end, the lane's little sensing mast gave a sharp electric ping.

Anwen's ward came up between them before Kiva could blink. The spark hit it and went out.

Kiva stood very still, breathing hard. Her chest burned.

"What happened?" said Orla.

"I didn't wait."

"What else?"

"I was thinking about the next one. While I was closing the first one." She thought of the tag on the Tallis Street regulator. *Runs fast. Hot on load.* "I was in a hurry."

Orla said nothing. She did not need to.

"Again," said Kiva.

The second time, she waited. She let the metal run all the way home. She felt it settle in the copper. She breathed out and closed the door behind it, and felt it go quiet. And then she counted, deliberately, *one, two*, before she reached for anything else.

Then she raised the ward.

It came up flat. Tray-shaped. But it came up into an empty doorway, and nothing pulled against it. The bottom heat-line stayed cool. She curved the ward a little. She held it.

Then she folded it. It splashed a little at the end. But it folded.

She stood for a moment with her palms together. Two breaths. Then she went into stance.

The weight came up through her feet. She held it. She gave it back.

Then she stood in the dark yard with her hands at her sides, and her chest did not hurt at all.

"Eleven seconds," said Orla. "From Edge to the end of stance."

Perrin whistled. "That's a long time."

"It's a very long time," said Orla. "Kellan Renn would cut you in half three times in eleven seconds. Any Riftspawn bigger than a Handful would walk through you. You'd fail every timed lane in the Circuit." She looked at Kiva across the lamp-lit yard, and her face was very still. "And it's the first time in four years I've seen you go through all three doors without a single line lighting."

Kiva had not realized it until Orla said it. She put her hand to her chest. The lines were there, under her shirt. Quiet. Cool.

"So that's a sequence," said Perrin.

"No," said Orla. "That's a drill. It's three doors one after another, with nothing passing between them. Each one starts from standing still." She tapped the three marks in the dirt again. "A real sequence is when the first door hands its work to the next one without dropping it. The speed of a Stride going straight into a stance, so you stop where you mean to and nobody moves you. The stop of a ward going straight into a strike. The work carries across. Nobody I know can do that across doors. I've seen it written about. I've never seen it done." She looked at Kiva. "This is the drill that gets you there. If anything does."

She understood something then that Orla had been telling her for two years, and that she had not been able to hear. The gap was not the enemy. The gap was where the closing happened. If she rushed the gap, the doors fought. If she let the gap be as long as it needed to be, they did not. It was slow because it was clean. It would get faster only by getting cleaner first.

She had always thought it was the other way around.

"Again," she said.

---

At ten o'clock, Orla called a stop. She reached into the canvas bag at her feet and took out three small books.

They were plain field books, the kind the Home Line still issued to its officers, bound in waxed grey card with a pencil tied to the spine on a string. She handed one to Kiva. When Kiva opened it, she saw that every page had been ruled into seven columns.

*Exercise. What went wrong. Who was hurt. What it cost. How long to come back. Second go. Why.*

"Your aunt's third word," said Orla. "Book."

Kiva turned the pages. They were all blank. All ruled.

"You ruled these yourself?"

"Last night. My eyes aren't what they were. Some of the lines aren't straight."

"Where's this from? These columns?"

"The Home Line case files. Field officers have kept them like this since before there was a Compact." Orla shrugged. "Some of the other schools use six columns now. The standard manual dropped the last one before I was born. The Home Line never did."

Kiva looked at the last column. *Why.*

Perrin was looking at the book in Kiva's hands. Then at the one Orla was holding out to Anwen. Kiva saw his face do something complicated.

"Can I have one?" he said.

It came out more sharply than he probably meant. Orla looked at him.

"You can have one," she said. "You could have had one last year. You didn't ask."

"I didn't know they were for asking."

"Everything's for asking." She took the third book out of the bag and held it out to him. "I made three. I thought you might."

Perrin took it. He turned it over in his big hands. For a moment he did not say anything at all, which was rare enough that Anwen looked up from her own book.

"Thanks," he said. And then, because he was Perrin and could not leave anything alone, "It's not a disk, but it's something."

"Perrin," said Anwen.

"I'm joking. Mostly." He put the book in his coat. Then he looked at Kiva, and his face was honest and a little ashamed. "I'm jealous. You know that. I've been jealous since I came here. You get the private nights and the old Home Line teacher and the mysterious necklace and Ysra Crest in a little room, and I get to stand behind you with my hand on your back." He shrugged. "It's not your fault. I just wanted it said. Like Anwen does."

"It's said," said Kiva.

"Good." He bumped her shoulder. Harder than usual. "Now write in your stupid book."

Kiva sat on the crate beside Orla and opened the book on her knee. The hand lamp threw its yellow light over the ruled page. She took the pencil off its string and wrote, slowly, in the first row.

*Exercise:* Sequence. Edge, close, ward, close, stance, close.

*What went wrong:* First try, rushed the gap. Reached for ward before metal home. All 3 lines lit. Spark. Mast pinged.

*Who was hurt:* Nobody. Anwen caught the spark.

*What it cost:* Slab burned my arm a bit. Lines ached ten min.

*How long to come back:* Ten min.

*Second go:* Waited. Counted two in each gap. No lines. 11 sec total. Too slow.

She stopped at the last column. *Why.*

She did not know why. She did not know why waiting worked. She did not know why the doors fought in the first place, or why there were three of them, or why a stance she had never trained was better than an Edge she had trained for four years. She sat with the pencil over the column for a long time.

"Leave it blank," said Orla quietly, beside her. "If you don't know. Blank's better than a lie. Somebody will fill it in later. Maybe you."

Kiva left it blank.

---

They walked the lane before they left. Orla always made them. It was a habit from the Home Line, she said. You walked the ground at the end of the night so that your feet would remember it.

The lane ran down the middle of the Old Yard between the two rows of practice posts. It was rutted and narrow and not quite level. They walked it in single file, Kiva first, then Perrin, then Anwen, with Orla bringing up the rear on her stick and the lamp swinging from her hand.

At the far end, the last post leaned.

Kiva stopped beside it. It was the same post. The same black, split wood, the same two iron bands. Four years ago she had put her hand on it after the lamps went out. She put her hand on it now.

"Why is it crooked?" she said.

She had asked Orla before. She had asked everyone. She asked the way you press on a bruise.

"The standard manual says *retained for ceremonial continuity*," said Orla.

"That's not a reason."

"No," said Orla. "It isn't."

Kiva looked at her. Orla was looking at the post, and there was something in her face that Kiva had not seen there before. Not quite sadness. Something more like a person looking at a page with a line torn out of it.

"There used to be one," said Orla. "A reason. It's not in the manual anymore."

"Do you know what it was?"

Orla was quiet for a moment. "Not for certain," she said. "And I won't guess at it in the dark." She lifted the lamp. "Go home. Get some sleep. Monday we'll do it again, and you'll do it in ten seconds, and you'll hate every one of them."

---

When they had gone, Orla Dane sat on the crate in the empty yard and let her knee hurt.

It hurt every evening now. Twenty years on the Home Line had done it. Twenty years of Stride, of landing hard on broken ground with the struts locked and the whole weight of a fall going through one joint. The healers at the Wren House had done what they could. She had another five years of teaching in it, maybe, before she needed the stick for everything.

She did not mind. Mostly.

She sat and looked down the dark lane at the crooked post.

She had told Mara Rowan the truth. She had put her name beside a student's once. Twenty-one years ago, when she was newly off the Home Line and full of herself, she had sponsored a twin-kindled boy from the Brickfields named Jessamy Hart. Ember and hand, both at thirteen. Not three doors. Two. It had seemed like enough of a miracle at the time.

She had thought she understood him. She had thought she could teach him the way she taught everyone, with patience and the old lane and her own field sense. She had put him into a Circuit ashcraft trial in his second year, because he had asked, and because he was ready, and because she wanted the board to see what she saw.

His doors had argued over a live cell in front of four thousand people.

The cell had run away. The quench had worked, but not before the backwash went up his arms. He had kept one hand. Two other students had been burned trying to pull him clear. The Compact board had closed his file and sealed the incident, and Jessamy Hart had left Meridian at nineteen with one hand and no category. Orla had heard, years later, that he was fixing pumps somewhere on the south coast. She had never written to him. She had not known what to say.

They had suspended her sponsoring standing for ten years. It had been restored for eleven. She had never used it.

She thought about that, and about the girl who had stood in this yard tonight and gone through all three doors in eleven seconds without a single line lighting.

Eleven seconds was nothing. Eleven seconds was a beginner's number, a number to be ashamed of in any sanctioned lane. And Orla had sat on this crate and watched it and felt something rise in her chest that she had not felt in twenty-one years.

She knew what it was. It was the same thing she had felt about Jessamy.

That was what frightened her.

She thought, too, about the disk. She had seen it before. Not on Kiva. On Ilse Corran, twenty-five years ago, at a field camp on the Ferren levee, the night after a bad rift. Ilse had taken it out from under her shirt and turned it over in her fingers by the fire and said nothing about it at all. Orla had known Ilse for six years by then, and had never asked. You did not ask a bearer. That was the oldest Home Line rule there was. The disk went by appointment, sealed, from one bearer to the next. It was not owned. It was carried.

Ilse had carried it for thirty years. And then, without a word to anyone, she had appointed a six-year-old girl whose mother had just died.

Orla did not know why. She had wondered about it for eleven years.

She knew one other thing, and she had told no one. Not Kiva. Not Mara. Not Ysra Crest.

Years ago, in the Compact Library, looking for something else, she had found a catalog card in the disputed drawer. *Annick, H. Personal notebook. Includes fever account ("the sky unroofed"). Status: disputed. Retained.* She had read the notebook in the reading room, out of curiosity, because Hesper Annick had been a famous name once. It was a Glory master's fever dream. People walking from every road at once. A sky with no roof. Every lamp in Meridian answering.

She had put it back and not thought about it again until four years ago, when every lamp in the Old Yard went out and a thirteen-year-old girl lay on the ground with her eyes open, looking at something very far away.

Kiva had never told her what she saw. Orla had never asked. She did not know if there was any connection at all. A fever was a fever. A Glory master's nightmares were not a map. She had no proof of anything, and she had been on the Home Line too long to mistake a feeling for a fact.

But she had not forgotten the card.

Orla looked at the post for a while longer. Then she got up, stiffly, and took the lamp off its hook, and locked the gate behind her.

In the bag over her shoulder, where it had been for four years, was a sponsorship application from the Compact office. She had filled in the first page the week after the Old Yard. She had never filled in the second.

She did not take it out. She walked home with it.
