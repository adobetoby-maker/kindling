# Chapter 7 — Kiva: Fever

Orla took her home on the tram.

She did not ask. She came down from the gallery when the hall was being cleared and stood at the Gate with her stick and waited, and when Kiva limped out, grey with ash and white with salve, Orla fell in beside her and said nothing at all. They walked to the stop. They waited. They got on.

Kiva held the rail this time. She did not think she could stand without it.

The tram went over the river in the late light. Below, the water was brown and fast. Above, the evening lift craft moved along the flight lanes with their rings lit. Kiva watched them without seeing them. Her left arm hung at her side in a sling the medic had tied. Under it, the heat-lines throbbed slowly from her breastbone to her elbow, the way a burn throbs a few hours after, deep and steady. The first two fingers of her left hand still felt like they belonged to somebody else.

"You're going to ask me what I thought," said Orla at last.

"No."

"You're going to want to."

Kiva looked out the window. "What did you think?"

Orla was quiet for a while. The tram rocked on the rails.

"I thought you tuned a live cell with one door on it," she said. "In front of three thousand people. I've watched you try for four years to keep the hand door off a cell for ten seconds. You held it there for thirty, and you pressed a planted fault out of a municipal tune by feel, and the needle stopped." She shifted her bad leg. "I thought that was the best single-door work I've ever seen you do."

"And then."

"And then you were frightened, and the Ember came in behind it, and you'd taught the cell to call every husk in the building." Orla did not soften it. "And you'd told your team you wouldn't touch it. And you went over the rail without a word. And you did the thing in the landing that I said nobody could do, and you did it wrong, and it cost you that." She nodded at the sling. "And you held. And you called the quench. And the boy pulled it because he believed your numbers."

Kiva closed her eyes.

"That's what I thought," said Orla. "All of it. At once. I'm not going to pick one."

---

Mara was standing in the shop doorway.

She had closed early. The sign said *Closed* and the bench lamp was off. She was standing on the step in her apron with her arms folded, and she had been standing there, Kiva thought, for some time.

She looked at Kiva coming down Coper Street. At the ash in her hair. At the sling. At the way she was walking.

She did not run. She did not say anything. She unfolded her arms and came down the step and met them on the pavement, and looked at Kiva's face for a long moment, and then at Orla's.

"Is it broken?" she said.

"No," said Orla. "Knee's strained again. Forearm's scraped. Backwash down the heat-lines, past the shoulder. Two fingers numb. The Wren House medic thinks they'll come back."

"*Thinks.*"

"Thinks."

Mara looked at Kiva again. Then she put her hand under Kiva's good elbow, gently, the way she would lift a meter with a cracked face.

"Did you say it out loud?" she said.

Kiva had known she would ask. She had been dreading it the whole way across the river.

"Once," she said. "I said it when I changed to tuning. I didn't say it the second time. When I went over the rail." She swallowed. "I didn't say anything. I just went."

Mara's face did not change.

"Why?"

"Because the husk was going for Perrin and he couldn't move, and there wasn't time."

"Was there time to say two words?"

Kiva thought about it. Honestly. The rail under her hand. The husk on its line across the floor. The bar running down her arm into struts.

"Yes," she said. "There was time. I didn't think of it."

Mara nodded slowly. She did not say anything else about it. She turned to Orla.

"Thank you for bringing her."

"I'll go," said Orla.

"You'll come in," said Mara. "And sit on my stool. And have a cup of tea, and tell me what happened. All of it. In order." She looked at Kiva. "And you'll go up and get in the bath. I'll be up to do your hair. You've got half the Kennel in it."

---

Anwen Pryce did not go home after the Cellhouse. She went to the Graywater table.

It was under its slate-blue awning at the foot of the Cellhouse steps, where it had been all week. The afternoon runs were finished. The crowd was thinning. Most of the recruiters were packing their boards. But the Graywater field officer was still sitting at the table with a ledger, because Anwen had written to him on Wednesday asking for ten minutes after her run, and he was the kind of man who answered letters.

His name was Doran Mace. He was about fifty, bald and brown and soft-spoken, a hand-door field medic who had run Graywater's ward line for twelve years before he took the placement desk. Anwen had read everything she could find about him. She had a list.

"Miss Pryce," he said, and pointed at the chair. "Sit."

She sat. She held her list in her lap and did not look at it.

"You stayed in the pump room," he said. "When the cell was going. When your teammate on the mezzanine called for you to get out."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She had thought about how to answer this for an hour. She had written three versions on the back of her list. She did not use any of them.

"Because the legs were still pinned," she said. "And the housing was a hand off them, and Perrin had it. If I'd come out, he'd have had to drop it to come out too. The casualty would have been back under it. And we'd have had to start again with a dead winch." She paused. "And because the casualty was my objective. It was on the card. It's what you score."

Doran Mace wrote something in his ledger. "Is that the order? The legs first? Or the score first?"

Anwen looked at him.

"The score first," she said. "Then the legs. I'd like to say it the other way round. I can't, honestly."

He looked up. For a moment she thought she had ruined it. Then he smiled, very slightly.

"Thank you," he said. "Most people say the other way round. It's very hard to train someone who's lying about why they do things." He wrote again. "You held your Sustain read on the casualty all run. You didn't drop it when the breaker went. You didn't drop it when the shutter came down. You knew exactly where the legs were under that housing the whole time. That's what I'll score you on." He closed the ledger. "And your teammate on the floor?"

Anwen was quiet for a moment.

"She chose too," she said. "She chose Perrin. Over the cell. Over the card. Over the run." She looked down at her list. "I'm not going to pretend I'd have done the same. I wouldn't. I'd have stayed on the cell and quenched and let the shutter come down and trusted Perrin to hold it. That was the right call by the book."

"Would he have held it?"

"He did hold it. When the breaker went."

"With a husk on his back?"

Anwen did not answer that.

Doran Mace nodded as if she had. "Graywater will post placements on the twentieth," he said. "Four, this year. I'll be honest with you, since you were honest with me. You're on my list. You're not at the top of it. Today moved you up."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. You did it." He began to pack his ledger into a satchel. Then he paused. "One more thing. You know the girl. Rowan. Well?"

"Since we were nine."

"Is she always like that?"

Anwen thought about it. About Kiva at nine, on the shop step, taking apart a broken kitchen meter with a butter knife to see where the spring went. Kiva at thirteen, on the ground in the Old Yard. Kiva at fourteen, white-faced at the laundry door, saying *Mrs. Pryce, please bring the burn salve, Mara says walk don't run.*

"She's always trying to fix the thing in front of her," said Anwen. "Even when she shouldn't. Even when it isn't hers."

Doran Mace looked at her for a moment longer. Then he nodded and stood up.

As he did, a woman came down the Cellhouse steps behind him. She was tall, in a long slate-blue coat, with a white streak through her black hair from the left temple. She walked straight to the table without looking at Anwen.

"Doran," she said. "The booth keeps the run record. Can you get me Team Seven? From three-thirty to the end."

"Tonight?"

"Tonight." She glanced at Anwen. Her eyes were very pale grey and very steady, and they rested on Anwen's face for exactly as long as it took to decide who she was. Then they went away again. "Not the placement scoring. The floor."

"I'll ask," said Doran Mace.

The woman nodded and walked away across the plaza.

Anwen sat at the empty table and looked after her. She knew who it was. Everybody in the Compact knew who it was. She had a poster of her on the back of her bedroom door when she was eleven.

She took out her list, and on the back of it, under the three answers she had not used, she wrote: *Rhea Sorn asked for our run. Not scoring. The floor.*

Then she sat there for a while longer with the pencil in her hand, feeling something she did not like.

It was not quite envy. She had done well today. She had done exactly what she meant to do, and the field officer had said so, and she was on his list. That was hers. Nobody had given it to her.

But nobody was going to ask for the run record to watch Anwen Pryce kneeling in the dark with her hands on a sandbag.

She wrote one more line. *I want it anyway. On my own.*

Then she put the list away and went home.

---

Perrin came up the back stairs at eight o'clock with a covered dish in each bandaged hand.

He could not knock. He kicked the door instead, gently, until Mara opened it.

"My mother made dumplings," he said. "For the hero. And also for Kiva."

Mara looked at his hands. They were wrapped in white cloth from the knuckles to the wrists, and the fingers that stuck out of the cloth were purple and swollen. She took both dishes from him without a word and stood aside.

Kiva was on the couch in the front room with her hair still wet. The bath had got most of the ash out. Mara had got the rest, sitting on the edge of the tub with a jug and a comb, working through it strand by strand without saying anything, the way she worked through a clogged meter. Kiva had sat with her eyes closed and let her. Neither of them had mentioned that the last time Mara had washed Kiva's hair, Kiva had been seven.

Perrin sat down on the floor in front of the couch, because his legs would not do anything else, and leaned his back against it.

"I can't use a fork," he said. "Somebody's going to have to feed me. I've decided it's you."

"My arm's in a sling."

"You've got another arm." He tipped his head back against the cushion and looked at the ceiling. "They posted the individual notes. On the gate. After you left."

"And?"

"*Cade, P. Still. Load hold under failure: exceptional. Sustained approx. 400 kg on hands, 9 sec, after winch loss. Controlled release.*" He said it in a flat, careful voice, as if he had read it many times. He probably had. "Exceptional. They wrote exceptional. The bursary board meets Monday. My Hall master came and found me and shook my hand. Very carefully. Because of the hands."

"Perrin, that's—"

"I know what it is." He was still looking at the ceiling. "Everyone saw. That's what I keep thinking. Three thousand people. My father was in the gallery. He's never come to anything. He came to this. He saw me hold it." He was quiet for a moment. "Nobody's ever seen me do anything, Kiva."

Kiva did not say anything. She put her good hand on his shoulder.

"And I wouldn't have held it," said Perrin, "if you hadn't been standing in front of me. With that thing on you. I'd have been holding the shutter with a husk chewing on my ear. I wouldn't have lasted nine seconds. I wouldn't have lasted two." He turned his head and looked at her. "So thank you. Properly. I mean it."

"You're welcome."

"And I'm still jealous." He said it in the same voice. "I want that said too. I'm still jealous. Of all of it. And I'm grateful. Both. At once. It's very uncomfortable and I'd like it to stop."

Kiva laughed. It hurt her chest. She did it anyway.

"And one more thing," said Perrin. "Next time you go over a rail to rescue me, say so first. I'd like to know I'm being rescued. I'd like to enjoy it."

"I'll try."

"*Try.*" He snorted. "Now feed me a dumpling. The big one. I held four hundred kilos. I've earned the big one."

---

The fever came after midnight.

It started in the heat-lines. Kiva woke in the dark in her narrow bed with her chest on fire, and for a moment she did not know where she was. The lines were burning. Not the way they burned when the doors fought. Deeper. Hotter. Like the whole path of them, from her breastbone to her elbow, had been filled with hot sand.

She was sweating. The sheet was wet under her. Her head ached. Her mouth was dry.

*Backwash fever,* she thought. The medic had said it might happen. *If the lines run hot tonight, cold cloths, water, and the Wren House in the morning.* She thought she should get up and get water. She thought she should call Mara.

She did not get up. She lay and burned.

The disk lay on her chest in the middle of it all, cold as a coin in snow.

And the room went away.

---

She was high up. Very high. And they were coming.

From every road. From every direction. She could not see their faces. Only their light, and the lights were all different, and they were walking toward her and toward each other, and they were very old and very bright and she could not look at any of them for long.

The lights touched.

She looked up. There was no sky.

And what was above came down. Full. Pressing. Into the air and the ground and her skin and her chest, into the three hot lines, into the cold disk, into everything.

Below her, every lamp answered.

---

She woke up standing.

She was in the narrow hallway outside her bedroom door. She did not remember getting there. Her bare feet were on the cold boards. Her left arm was out of the sling, stretched out in front of her, and her left hand was flat against the wall.

Against the flat's cell panel.

It was a small steel box set into the hallway wall, with a meter on its face and a red handle on its side. Every flat in the building had one. It held the flat's share of the building's reserve cell, so that if the core in the cellar ever shut down, the lights and the cold-cupboard would run for a few days more. Kiva had walked past it ten thousand times. She had never touched it. Mara's rule.

Her palm was flat on the steel. Her numb fingers were spread across the meter's glass.

Under her hand, the cell was screaming.

The needle on the meter was pinned past red. The panel was hot. It was ringing. She could feel it through her palm, the same way she had felt the drum in the Cellhouse, and it was the same thing. Three shapes. Fighting. Twisting round inside the cell. And something else, something bigger, pressing down on top of all of them like a hand.

Her heat-lines were blazing. All three. She had not opened any door. She had been asleep. They had opened on their own.

She tore her hand off the panel.

The ringing did not stop.

From somewhere below, deep in the building, there was a heavy *clunk* that she felt through the soles of her feet. Then every light in the flat went out. The cold-cupboard in the kitchen stopped humming. Through the floor, she heard the big night machines in the Pryce laundry downstairs grind to a halt, one after another.

The building's core had tripped.

In the dark, the panel under her hand was still ringing.

"*Kiva.*"

Mara was in the hallway behind her. Kiva did not know when she had got there. She came past Kiva in her nightshirt with one hand out, not to Kiva, to the panel. She broke the wire seal on the red handle with her thumb, the way she had done ten thousand times on other people's cells, and pulled it.

There was a clank and a long hiss. The ringing stopped.

It was very dark. It was very quiet. Kiva could hear herself breathing. She could hear Mara breathing. Through the floor, she could hear voices on the stairs, doors opening, somebody calling up *What's happened? Is it the core?*

Mara put her hand on Kiva's face.

"You're burning up," she said.

"I didn't open anything." Kiva's voice came out thin and strange. "I was asleep. I didn't—I woke up and my hand was on it."

"I know."

"I didn't *open* anything."

"I know. I know you didn't." Mara's hand moved to the back of Kiva's neck and held it. "Sit down. Sit down, here, on the floor. Your nose is bleeding."

Kiva sat. She had not known her nose was bleeding. She put her good hand to it and it came away wet and dark. Her heat-lines were still burning, but less. Her left hand, the one that had been on the panel, was throbbing. The first two fingers buzzed now, like a limb waking up, prickling and hot.

Mara went to the kitchen and came back with a candle and a wet cloth. She knelt in the hallway and held the cloth to Kiva's nose and the candle up to her face and looked at her.

"What did you see?" she said.

It was the question she had stopped asking four years ago. She asked it now very quietly, as if she already knew the answer would be hard to carry.

And Kiva told her.

Not pieces. Not *people* and *the sky*. All of it, as much as she could hold onto before it went. She told her about the height, and the people coming from every road with their different lights. About the lights touching. About looking up and finding no sky at all. About what came down, and how it was not heavy but full, and how it was enormous and paying attention and could not have been held back by anybody. About the city answering. Every lamp.

"Every lamp," said Mara.

"Every lamp. And every cell. And the cores. And the doors. Everybody's doors." Kiva held the cloth against her face. "Some went out. Some went white. It's the same every time. It's been the same since the Old Yard."

"Every time?"

"It doesn't come often. A few times. When I've been sick." She looked down. "I never told you because I didn't know what it was. I still don't know. I thought if I said it out loud, it would sound—"

"Like a fever."

"Like a fever. Or like something worse."

Mara sat back on her heels in the hallway. The candle shook a little in her hand. She was not looking at Kiva. She was looking at the panel on the wall with its broken seal, and her face had gone very still, the way it went at the bench when a meter did something she had not expected and she was working out why.

"The lamps went out," she said. "In the Old Yard. When you went down."

"Somebody said it was the rig."

"It wasn't the rig." Mara stood up slowly. "Stay there."

She went down the hall to the front room and opened the window. Kiva heard her lean out. Below, Coper Street was full of voices. The laundry was dark. So was the bakery. So, Kiva realized, listening, was every building on their side of the street.

No. Not every building.

"It's us," said Mara from the window. "Our building, and the laundry, and the bakery. We share the core. Across the street's still lit. The street lamps are lit." She was quiet. Then she pulled the window shut. "It's just us."

---

The building's core reset itself at a quarter to three. The lights came back on one flat at a time. The laundry machines started again downstairs. The neighbors went back to bed.

The flat's own cell stayed dead, with its quench box full of wet salt and ruined ash. Mara wrapped the panel's broken seal in a twist of paper and put it in her apron pocket.

Then she sat on the edge of Kiva's bed with a bowl of cold water and a cloth, and she did not sleep.

Kiva drifted. The fever came and went. Every time she opened her eyes, Mara was there, wringing out the cloth, laying it on her forehead, on the hot welts along her arm. Once she woke and Mara was holding her left hand, pressing the tips of the first two fingers one at a time with her thumb.

"Feel that?"

"A bit. It's coming back."

"Good." Mara did not let go.

At some point near dawn, the fever broke. Kiva knew it because she was suddenly cold, and wet through, and very tired, and her head was clear. The heat-lines still ached. But it was an ordinary ache now. A burn, healing.

She looked at Mara. Mara looked grey. She looked ten years older than she had at supper.

"The core reports trips," said Kiva. "Doesn't it. On the municipal wire."

Mara did not answer at once.

"Every licensed core in the city reports a trip," she said at last. "Automatically. To the Works. With the flat that caused it, if the core can tell. This one could." She wrung out the cloth. "It'll have gone at a quarter to three. It'll be on a clerk's desk by eight."

"An incident filing."

"An incident filing. Household."

Kiva lay still. She thought about the letter Ysra Crest had read from in the little office. *Your aunt's shop filed an incident report three years ago.* She thought about the slab discharging in the lane last spring. That had been at the school. Not the household.

"How many is that?" she said. "For you. For your license."

Mara's hands went still on the cloth.

"Kiva."

"How many?"

Mara put the cloth in the bowl. She sat for a moment with her hands in her lap, the scarred one over the other. Then she got up and went out of the room. Kiva heard her go down the stairs into the dark shop. She heard a drawer open. Then Mara came back up, and sat on the edge of the bed again, and held out a letter.

It was on heavy paper with a red stripe across the top. Kiva read it in the grey dawn light from the window.

*Grade Two Tuning License, M. Rowan. Household condition noted, 2677. One further incident filing within the license household will trigger automatic review.*

She read it twice. Then she looked up.

"This came in the winter," she said.

"It came in the winter."

"You didn't tell me."

"No."

"You signed the form for the Cellhouse. With this in the drawer."

"The Cellhouse isn't the household. A trial incident goes on the Compact's book, not mine." Mara's voice was very steady and very tired. "I checked. Before I signed."

"But this is the household."

"This is the household."

Kiva held the letter. Her hand was shaking. She did not know if it was the fever or something else.

"What happens? In review?"

"They suspend the license while they look at my books. A season, usually. Sometimes two." Mara looked at the window. "No license, no municipal contract. The van stops coming. The cell books stop coming. The reconciling stops." She shrugged, a small, tired movement. "The shop's still mine. I can still fix kitchen meters for the neighbors. It just won't pay the rent."

"Because of me."

"Because of a cell." Mara turned and looked at her. "I want you to hear me. Because of a cell, and a fever, and something we don't understand. Not because you did anything wrong tonight. You were asleep."

"You should have told me," said Kiva. "In the winter. When it came."

Mara was quiet for a long time.

"Yes," she said. "I should have. I didn't want you carrying it. You were carrying enough." She looked down at her hands. "That was wrong of me. It was the same thing I told you not to do. Holding something that's going to come out anyway, and not saying it out loud." She let out a breath. "I'm saying it now. I'm sorry."

Kiva folded the letter and held it.

Outside, the sky was getting light over the Eastbank roofs. Somewhere down the street, a lift-truck started up. The bakery's ovens would be on by now. The core in the cellar was humming again, the way it always did, the sound Kiva had stopped hearing years ago and could hear now very clearly.

"I've got to go to the Compact at nine," she said. "Ysra Crest. With my book."

"I know."

"What do I tell her?"

Mara stood up. She took the bowl and the cloth and stood by the door with them for a moment, her back to Kiva.

"The truth," she said. "All of it. Same as you'd write it down." She turned. "And I'm going to the district records office at eight to file this before the Works files it for me. I'd rather they read it in my hand than a clerk's."

"Will that help?"

"Probably not." Mara almost smiled. "But it's mine to write."

She went out. Kiva lay back on the damp pillow.

The disk lay on her chest. She put her good hand over it.

It was cold.

It had been cold all night. Cold through the fever. Cold through the dream. Cold when the panel screamed and the core tripped and her nose bled into Mara's cloth. Four years, and it had never once been anything else.

She did not know if it had done this. She did not know if it had carried the dream to her, or if the dream had come on its own and the disk had only lain there in the middle of it, the way a stone lies in a river. She did not know whether the bright people had come to hold the sky shut or tear it open. She did not know why every lamp answered.

She knew her hand had been on the panel. She knew the panel had held what was in her. She knew it had cost Mara her license, or would.

She took the field book off the chair beside her bed, and the pencil on its string, and turned to a clean page.

*Exercise:* None. Asleep.

*What went wrong:* Fever from the backwash. Dream again. Woke standing in the hall with my hand on the flat cell. Lines open by themselves. Cell held it. Core tripped. Whole building dark.

*Who was hurt:* Me (nose). The laundry lost a night's wash. Mara's license.

*What it cost:* The flat cell (quenched). A household incident filing.

*How long to come back:* Don't know.

*Second go:* There isn't one.

She stopped at the last column for a long time.

*Why:* Don't know. Writing it down so somebody can.
