# Chapter Thirteen

Doctor Keel came at the tenth hour and she was shorter and worse, exactly as advertised.

She was a tall woman of about forty-five with her sleeves rolled past the elbow and forearms like a person who rowed, and she came down the ward, looked at the leg for eleven seconds, put two fingers on the boy's foot, watched the toes, and then turned round and said to Jab:

"Not this afternoon. Tomorrow, ninth hour."

Jab stood up out of the chair.

"He's—"

"I know what he is. I've read it and I've looked at it and I'm going to look at a picture of the inside of it in about an hour." She said it fast and flat and without any apology in it whatsoever. "Here's the reason, and it's the only one you're getting, and it's a good one. If I open that leg today I'm opening it on a child who has had one bag of fluid and eight hours of medicine after seven weeks of being dried out on a road. If I open it tomorrow morning I'm opening it on a child who's had a day of both, and he'll be on the table for two hours either way, and the second one comes off the table better."

"And if he turns tonight?"

"Then somebody wakes me and I do it tonight," said Doctor Keel. "That's why I sleep where I sleep." She put her hands in her pockets. "Anything else?"

"How many of these have you done?"

"In a child that age, with the bone dead in the middle like that? Nine."

"How many were all right after?"

"Six were straightforwardly all right. Two took a second go and were all right after that. One I took the leg off at the knee." Doctor Keel did not look away while she said it. "He's twelve now and he's a perfectly ordinary boy and he is extremely rude about my handwriting, and I'd have him in here tomorrow to talk to you if this were the kind of station where that was allowed, and it isn't, so you'll have to take my word for him."

Jab found he had nothing to say.

"Good," said Doctor Keel. "Now go and eat something, because you are grey, and I do not operate on children whose people fall over in my corridor."

---

He went to the hatch in the reception yard at the middle of the day and got his things back, and they were clean.

The boots had been washed and dried and stuffed with paper. The pack had been hosed and the hide cover had been washed, and both of them smelled of the sharp thing they washed everything with. The water skins were there and the food bag was there with five smoked fish in it that had been through hot water and were not going to be worth eating, and the birch paper with three twists of bark folded in it, which the young man at the hatch handed over with a raised eyebrow and no comment at all.

And there was a small bin with a paper tag tied to the handle, and inside it, on a square of cloth, a thumb-length of river-worn metal on a new cord.

They had washed it. It was the first time in four years that anybody had.

He had not known it was that colour. It had been grey going green with four years of skin and smoke and river on it, and what came out of the bin was pale and clean and dull, smooth as an egg on one face and dimpled on the other, and it caught the flat white light off the ceiling in a way it had never once caught firelight. It looked like a thing off a bar. It looked exactly like the thing it had been before anybody picked it up.

"Sign there," said the young man.

Jab signed there. He put the cord over his head and dropped the metal down inside his shirt and felt it land cold in the hollow of his throat and start, slowly, to get warm off him.

He kept the tag. He did not know why. He put it in his coat pocket with his thumb over the number.

---

The picture was taken in a shed at the north end that was not a shed.

They took Tonk down on a rolling bed and Jab walked beside it, along the ward and out through a door and down a corridor that sloped, under the building, where the air got cooler and the walls got thicker and grey and there were no windows at all. At the bottom of the slope there was a door wider than the gate in the reception yard, and on the floor in front of it there was a line painted across, bright yellow, as wide as his hand.

On the wall beside it there was a sign, and Jab could read, because his mother had taught him out of the only two books in the valley.

*NO METAL PAST THIS LINE.*

"It's the machine," said the man in white pushing the bed. "It doesn't like metal. It pulls. Anything iron that goes in that room it'll have off you and across the floor at it, and it won't ask first." He looked at Tonk. "Anything on him?"

"No."

"Anything on you?"

Jab stood at the yellow line.

He had been wearing it for four years. He had handed it over once already, twelve hours ago, in a road, to a man in a glove, and that had been a thing done to him, and this was not.

"Yes," he said, and put his hand up under his collar, and lifted the cord over his head for the second time in a day, and stood there holding it.

"There's a tray," said the man in white.

"It's not iron."

"Then it's the other thing, and I've been told about the other thing, and there's a line and a tray and I'm not arguing with a boy about a machine I don't understand." The man was not unfriendly. "Tray, please."

Jab put it in the tray.

"Will they mend my leg," said Tonk, from the bed, out of nowhere, in a small clear voice.

It was the second time he had ever asked it.

"They're going to look at it first," said Jab. "With a machine. It takes a picture of the inside."

"Of the *inside*?"

"Of the inside."

"Can I see it?"

"I'll ask." Jab got hold of his brother's hand, the one without the needle. "Listen. This is a door I don't go through. It's about twenty steps and then there's a room, and the machine's loud, and you have to lie very still, and you're already very good at lying still because you've been doing it for seven weeks."

Tonk's hand went tight.

"You said they'd have to carry you out."

"I know what I said."

"You *said* —"

"I know," said Jab, and made himself keep his voice level and keep looking at him. "I'm not going to tell you they made me. I'm standing here on my own feet and I'm going to let go of your hand, because that machine would take the metal off my neck and fire it across the room, and there's a four-year-old going into that room."

Tonk looked at the yellow line on the floor.

"Is it a *rule*?"

"It's a rule."

"Do *they* keep it?"

"Everybody keeps it," said Jab. "That's the only reason it's any good."

His brother let go of his hand, which Jab had not asked him to do, and which he thought about for a long time afterward.

---

There was a bench against the wall by the line.

He sat on it with a paper tray in his lap holding a thumb-length of river metal on a cord, and the heavy door shut with a sound like a lid, and then, from behind a wall as thick as a bank, the knocking started.

It was a loud sound and a strange one. It came through in long runs, fast and steady and mechanical, like somebody hammering a pipe in another building. It stopped. It started again, faster. It went on and on, and it did not sound like a thing that could look at the inside of a person.

Jab sat on the bench and could not see his brother, and could not hear him, and could not get to him, and had, for the first time since a bridge went into a gorge in the autumn, absolutely nothing to do.

He put his hand flat on his own chest, over the place where the metal was not.

And he asked.

He did it carefully, the way he had learned, flat, not wanting anything, not reaching. He sat on a bench in a cold corridor under a building and put the question down in front of the thing under his breastbone and took his hand off it.

Nothing answered.

Nothing at all. Not up, not down, not a slope in the dark, not the smallest lean in any direction. There was the concrete and the knocking and the cold, and there was a boy sitting on a bench with an empty space behind his ribs where something used to be.

He had known it was twenty feet and a wall. He had known it was probably more than that, and that the wall had metal in it and the door was a foot thick, and he had known at the top of it all that the thing had never once come when it was called, not on a bad night and not in daylight and not for anything.

He sat with his hands open on his knees and found that he had wanted it to, very badly, and that he was going to have to be careful about that for the rest of his life.

"He's about twenty feet in," said a voice.

---

The girl with the bad leg was standing at the top of the slope.

She came down it carefully, putting her weight on one side, and sat down on the other end of the bench and stretched the bad leg out straight in front of her and looked at the heavy door, with her head a little on one side.

"On his back," she said. "He hasn't moved. There's two of them in the room with him and they're at the far side, and one of them is sitting down." She paused. "He hasn't moved at all. He's very good at it."

Jab stared at her.

"How do you know that?"

"I know where people are," said the girl, flatly, in the voice of somebody who has said it four hundred times and is tired of having to. "About thirty feet, all round, without looking, in the dark, all the time. It isn't a trick. It doesn't cost me anything. It's just there."

"All the time?"

"All the time."

"Does it ever go away?"

"No," she said. "That's the part people never ask about." She looked at him. "Dessa."

"Jab."

"I know," said Dessa. "Your brother's been saying it since the second hour."

They sat on the bench in the cold and listened to the knocking, which had got faster.

"He's still not moving," said Dessa. "Somebody's stood up."

Jab put his hands over his face.

"You can do it through the wall," he said.

"Through the wall, through the road, through the ground. It doesn't care what's in the way." Dessa was quiet for a moment. "Why?"

"Because I've got something and it doesn't reach past my own arm and it comes when it likes and it went out like a candle last night and I can't find it at all." He heard his own voice come out of him and could not stop it. "I've had it twelve days. I don't know what it is. Nobody I've ever met has one. And I have been sitting on this bench asking it whether my brother is all right through a foot of concrete like an absolute fool."

Dessa Cole looked at the door.

"I was Kindled at thirteen and nothing happened," she said. "Nothing at all, for two years. They've got a phrase for it where I come from, and the phrase is *Kindled to nothing*, and people say it in front of you." She shifted the leg. "And then one night a man told me a thing about an empty mind and an open soul, and something came on in me, and it has been on ever since and it does not go off, and I know where every person in this corridor is right now including two behind that door, and I could not tell you how, and if you asked me to make it bigger I couldn't.

"So no. I don't think you're a fool. I think you've got one that only comes when it's needed, which everybody would tell you is worse, and I'd point out that it came twice in a fortnight and both times it was needed, and I'd like to know what that adds up to." She paused. "I count things. I can't help it."

"So do I," said Jab.

"I know," said Dessa. "You counted the paces last night. Out loud. All three hundred and forty of them."

---

They brought Tonk out at the end of it with his eyes shut and his hair every which way, and he opened them as they crossed the yellow line and found Jab standing there with a paper tray in his hands.

"It *knocked*," he said.

"I heard."

"It knocked like a *man*," said Tonk, with the outrage of a person who has been personally imposed upon, "mending a roof, inside my *leg*," and Jab laughed, and put the cord back over his head, and walked beside the rolling bed all the way up the slope.

---

The afternoon was the part he had not planned for, because he had not, in sixteen days, planned for any afternoon at all.

They came in twos and threes and nobody made an occasion of it.

The big boy came first. He came in at the start of visiting with his right arm strapped across his chest inside his coat and a piece of squared steel bar about eighteen inches long held in his left hand, and he stood at the foot of the bed and looked at Tonk without any expression and said:

"You're the one that shouted."

"You're the one that didn't *move*," said Tonk.

The big boy considered this.

"No," he agreed.

"It ran at you. It ran at you and you went like *this* — " Tonk did a thing with his shoulders that cost him and that he did anyway, four inches of shoulder movement and then nothing " — and it went in the *wall*."

"That's about what happened."

"*Why* didn't you move?"

"I don't fall over," said the big boy.

There was a pause in which Jab watched his brother take this in and file it, permanently, in whatever place a four-year-old keeps the eleven or twelve facts that will turn out to have built him.

"Wyck," said the big boy, to Jab, as an afterthought, and pulled the chair round with his foot and sat down on it backward with the bar across his knees, and stayed for an hour and a half.

---

He was not good with children. That was the thing Jab watched and did not entirely understand. He did not soften his voice or ask questions in the bright way people asked children questions, and twice he said things that would have made an aunt at home put her hands on her hips.

"That's going to hurt worse tomorrow than it does now."

"*Wyck*," said Jab.

"He knows," said Wyck, without turning round. "He's had it seven weeks. He knows what it does. He doesn't know what *tomorrow's* going to do, and if I tell him it'll be fine he'll find out at the second hour in the dark that I'm a liar, and then he'll have had it and he won't have anybody."

And Tonk, in the bed, said: "Does it *stop*?"

"Yes," said Wyck.

"When?"

"Ages. And then it doesn't stop all at once, it stops a bit at a time, and one day you notice you've gone a whole morning and not thought about it, and that's the day it starts being ordinary." He held up his right hand, out of the strapping, and moved three of the fingers, and did not move the other two. "These don't work. They haven't worked since the spring. I dropped everything I picked up for two months and I was extremely unpleasant about it."

Tonk's eyes went very round.

"Can I *touch* them?"

"If you like."

Tonk pinched them, hard, with great concentration, watching Wyck's face for a reaction. Wyck looked back at him with an entirely level expression.

"Nothing," he said.

"*Nothing?*"

"Nothing at all."

"Do it *again*," said Tonk, delighted and appalled, in exactly the voice he had used about a squirrel, and Jab put his head down on the edge of the bed and laughed until his ribs hurt.

---

The small woman came at the end of the afternoon and did not sit down.

She stood at the end of the bed with her cap in her hands and looked at Tonk asleep for a while, and then she looked at the chart hanging on the rail, and then she looked at Jab, who had a paper cup of cold tea in his hands and had been holding it for about an hour.

"You've had how much sleep?"

"Some."

"That isn't a number."

"Two hours," said Jab. "On the bench."

"Since when?"

Jab worked it out and found that the answer was a long way back.

Katori put her cap on.

"Right," she said. "There's a hut in the second row with a bed going spare in it and a stove that works, and the window's got a blanket over it because of the lamp. Otto's in it. He'll wake you at the eighth hour and he'll wake you properly, because he does everything properly and it's very annoying."

"I'm not leaving him."

"No," said Katori, "you're not. That's why I said the eighth hour and not tomorrow afternoon." She stood there. "I'm going to say one thing and then I'll go. The nurse at the desk told me what you had in your coat when you came through the gate. Three twists of bark, in paper, folded in eights."

Jab did not say anything.

"Forty is what you'd count out for a road like that. Forty, at two a day. And you came in on the sixteenth day with three left, which means you've been halving them for a week, which means somebody sat down at a fire every night for a week and did a sum about his own brother's pain, and then did the sum again in the morning." She was looking out of the window over the bed. "I'm not going to say anything else about it. I only wanted you to know that somebody in this station has done the sum backwards and knows what's in it."

"Anybody would have," said Jab.

"No," said Katori. "They wouldn't."

She went to the door and stopped.

"Eat the thing Otto gives you," she said. "He'll hover. Let him."

---

The girl who read ground came at dusk and sat on the end of the bed, which nobody else had done.

"Did they tell you how long you're staying?" she said.

"Months. Years, probably. He's got to come back every year till he's grown."

"Mm," said Callie. "Mine's for good."

Jab looked at her.

"There's a thing in my mother's head they can't take out," she said, in the flat way a person says a thing they have decided to say the same way every time. "They can make her days better and they can't make more of them, and she takes pills at the eighth hour every morning in this building, and if she's not within a mile of it she doesn't get them. So we're here." She turned the tag on her own wrist over. "Four hundred miles, on a wagon, to get her to a door. And then we got to the door and everything on the other side of it turned out to be a different problem."

"Yes," said Jab.

"That's the bit nobody warns you about. The road's the hard part right up until you finish it." She looked at him sideways. "I was quite good at the road."

"So was I."

"I know," said Callie. "I read it. The frame's in a bin at the yard office and I went and looked at it this morning, and you've mended that shoulder band on the road with a strip cut off your own cover, wet, so it'd shrink on, and you took the corner off the rail and capped it instead of cutting the rail off, which is the harder answer and it's the right one." She said it the way Pell read a distance. "And your hip belt's on the fifth hole and the leather's got a memory in it at the third, so you've been getting thinner since about halfway."

Jab sat in the chair by his brother's bed and found he could not speak for a moment.

"Nobody's ever done that to me before," he said.

"It's all I do," said Callie. "People find it rude. It isn't meant to be." She stood up off the bed and pulled her coat straight. "I'll tell you the thing I wanted to tell you and then I'll go, because you're asleep sitting up.

"There's a man in eleven who signed four seasons for an address, and there's a man in a long coat who's going to come to your door with a folded paper in about a day, because he came to mine. He'll ask you nicely. He'll tell you that you can say no." She was looking at the door. "You can say no. That's all. It's true and nobody says it to you and I had to be told."

"By who?"

"By the man in eleven," said Callie. "Who signed."

---

Jab slept four hours in a hut in the second row with a stove ticking and a blanket over the window, and Otto woke him at the eighth hour by standing in the middle of the room holding a bowl of something and radiating anxiety until he opened his eyes.

He was back in the ward by the ninth.

They had taken the bag off the hook and there was a nurse doing something with a chart and Doctor Keel was standing at the foot of the bed with her sleeves already rolled, saying something to Tonk that made him look extremely suspicious.

"— and then you'll wake up somewhere else and you'll be cross, and I want you to be as cross as you like, and I want you to tell the nurse about it and not save it up for your brother."

"I *will*," said Tonk.

"Good," said Doctor Keel. "That's the whole of it, then."

"No it isn't," said Tonk.

Doctor Keel stopped.

"There's the fish," said Tonk.

---

The fish went with him. It was wood, and wood did not matter to anything, and Doctor Keel looked at it and said it could go as far as the door and would be waiting at the other end, and then looked at the boy's face and changed her mind out loud, in front of everybody, and said that it could go the whole way and she would put it in his hand herself when he was asleep.

"Sing the one," said Tonk.

"Which one?"

"The one about the boat."

So Jab sang the four verses he had left, badly, standing in a long white room at the ninth hour of the morning with about nine other people in it who were not pretending to be busy, and Tonk corrected him twice on words he was fairly sure he had right, and he let him have both.

Then they wheeled the bed away down the ward, and Jab walked beside it with his brother's hand in his until the corridor, and at the corridor there was a place where the floor changed colour, and a man in white put his hand out.

"You can come to here," he said.

"I know," said Jab.

He let go of the hand in one movement and not in stages.

"Jab," said Tonk, from the bed, going away.

"Mm."

"You have to come after."

"I'll come after," said Jab.
