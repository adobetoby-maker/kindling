# Chapter Fourteen

He sat on the floor of the corridor with his back against the wall, because there was no bench on that side and he was not going to go and find one.

The doors were eleven feet away. They were double doors with a round window in each and a strip of rubber down the join, and every so often somebody went through them and the air moved, and Jab's whole body came up off the wall and then went back down again.

Dessa Cole arrived at about the quarter hour with a wooden chair, dragged it the last twenty feet because carrying it was not available to her, set it down against the opposite wall, and then did not sit in it. She lowered herself onto the floor beside him with the bad leg out straight, put her back against the concrete, and looked at the doors.

"You're going to want to know," she said. "So I'm going to tell you, and I'll tell you the dull bits as well as the rest, because if I only say something when something happens you'll go mad between them."

Jab did not trust his voice, so he nodded.

"Six people in there," said Dessa. "He's on the far side, about twenty-two feet, and he's the only one lying down. Four of them are standing round him and two of them are close in. One of them's moving about — that'll be somebody fetching. Nobody's fast."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know what's good," said Dessa. "I know what's fast. I've been in a room where people got fast." She shifted the leg. "Nobody's fast."

---

They were there for two hours and ten minutes, and Dessa talked for most of it, in a flat voice, about very small things.

Nobody moved for a long time. Then somebody stepped back from the table and stood still for about forty seconds and stepped in again. Then two people swapped places, which took a while, and Dessa said that this was the sort of thing people did when they were being careful about where their hands went, and Jab decided to believe her.

At the hour and a quarter, somebody went out through a door at the far side and came back with a fifth person, and Jab's stomach went over.

"Don't," said Dessa. "Everything in a place like this is somebody fetching somebody. If it were bad they'd be fetching them fast and they'd be fetching them from this side, because this side's where the rest of the building is."

"How do you know that?"

"Because my grandmother has been in this building a month and I have spent that month sitting in corridors," said Dessa, "and I count everything, and I am the only person here who knows which way the bad news walks."

At the hour and a half, Wyck came and sat down on Jab's other side without saying anything at all, and turned a piece of squared steel bar over and over in his left hand, and dropped it twice on the concrete, and picked it up.

At the hour and three quarters, Otto came with a paper cup of tea and gave it to Jab and went away again, going red.

At two hours, Katori came and stood against the far wall with her cap in her hands and did not sit down and did not speak.

At two hours and ten minutes, the doors opened.

---

Doctor Keel came out with her sleeves down and her arms scrubbed pink to the elbow and something folded over her forearm, and Jab was on his feet with no memory whatever of standing up.

"He's alive and he's asleep and he's got both legs," she said.

Then she waited about four seconds while that went in, and Jab understood, distantly, that she had said those nine words first on purpose, and that she did that every time, and that somebody had taught her to.

"Now the rest of it, and it's long, and there's a chair behind you. Sit down or don't, but don't stand there swaying at me.

"It was worse inside than it looked on the picture, which is usual. The dead piece was longer than I'd hoped — about as long as my thumb — and it was loose, which is the one piece of luck in the whole business, because loose means it came out whole and I didn't have to go chasing bits of it. I took it out, I took out a good deal of the muck round it, and I've washed that leg out with more fluid than you'd think a leg could hold.

"I've taken the bad join apart and set it straight. It's straight now. It's the straightest that leg has been since the day it broke, and it's held by four pins through the bone and a bar on the outside, and it is the ugliest object you will ever see on a child and it is going to stay on him until the spring.

"That's the good half. Here's the rest, in order.

"There's a gap in the bone where the dead piece was. That's expected and there's a way of dealing with it and it is slow and it involves him being in that frame for a long time and it involves me. I'm not going to explain the whole of it to you standing in a corridor with your hands shaking.

"The infection. I've taken out what it was living in and he's on the medicine, and in two weeks we'll know whether it's gone out of him or whether I'm going in again. I told you four in five. It's still four in five. It does not become five in five because I've had a good morning."

"And the growing," said Jab.

Doctor Keel looked at him.

"You've got a memory on you," she said. "Yes. And the growing. I could see it and I'll tell you plainly: the place that leg grows from has been in the weather for two months and it is damaged. Not gone. Damaged. So it will grow and it will not grow as fast as the other one, and I can't tell you today by how much, and anybody who does tell you is guessing.

"What that means in practice is that he will walk with a limp, and he will have one boot built up, and I will measure him every year until he stops growing, and somewhere in the middle of that I will very likely have him back on a table.

"That's it. That's the whole of the bill. He'll be asleep another two hours and then he'll wake up and he'll be sick and he'll be extremely angry, and the anger is the best sign in the world and I want you to enjoy it."

She took the folded thing off her arm. It was a cloth, and there was something in it.

"He asked me for this at the door," she said, "and it went in with him, and I put it in his hand myself like I said I would, and it's been on his chest for two hours, and I've had to wash it, and I'm told by the nurse that if I don't hand it to you personally there'll be a row."

The wooden fish was as long as Jab's thumb, smoked black by a lifetime of hanging near a fire, with one fin broken off before Tonk was born.

Jab took it, and turned round, and put his forehead against the corridor wall, and stood there with his back to all of them for about a minute, and nobody in that corridor said one word to him or to each other until he had finished.

---

Tonk woke at the middle of the afternoon and was sick, and was extremely angry.

He was angry about the sick. He was angry about the bar on his leg, which he could see, and which he described in detail to everybody who came near the bed. He was angry about the tube in his hand. He was angry that Jab had not been there when he woke, which Jab had, and which he had been asleep for, and which did not matter in the slightest.

"You *weren't*."

"I was."

"You *weren't*, and it was ages, and nobody *told* me —"

"All right," said Jab. "I wasn't."

Tonk lay back on the pillow, mollified and exhausted.

"It's got a *bar*," he said.

"I saw."

"It's got a bar and it's got *nails in my leg*."

"Four of them."

"*Four?*"

"Four," said Jab. "I counted them."

His brother lay and looked at the ceiling of a long white room and thought about it for a while, and Jab sat in the chair and watched the whole machinery of it turning behind a four-year-old's face.

"It's the rail," Tonk said at last.

"What is?"

"The bar." Tonk moved one hand, vaguely, at the leg in its cradle. "It's the rail. Like on the frame. So it doesn't *hang*."

Jab looked at the thing bolted through his brother's shin and did not say anything for a moment, because his throat had shut.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly what it is."

"They did it *inside*," said Tonk, with enormous scorn, and went to sleep.

---

The cord was in the pack, because they had washed the pack and everything in it and given it all back with a tag on.

Jab took it out that evening and tied one end round his own left wrist with a knot that would not draw, and looked for somewhere to put the other, and there was a rail on the side of the bed, painted white.

He tied it to the rail and then untied it, and put the end into his brother's hand instead, and closed the fingers over it, and Tonk's fist shut on it in his sleep without waking up at all.

"He won't need that in a week," said the young woman in white, going past with a tray. "There's a bell on a string."

"I know," said Jab.

She stopped.

"I'm not telling you to take it off," she said.

"I know," said Jab.

---

The man in the long coat came on the second morning.

He was tall and thin and about fifty, with a close-cut grey beard and a quiet face, and he came to the door of the hut in the second row where Jab was not living so much as keeping a bed, and asked for him by name, which he had got off a card.

"I'm from the yard office," he said. He did not give a name. "I've read your declaration."

"All of it?"

"Both sides." He had a folded paper in one hand and he did not offer it. "I'll tell you what I came to say and then I'll say the other things. What I came to say is that there's a Flask of ash on the board in your name."

Jab stood in the doorway.

"The hound at the culvert head," said the man. "It was lifted the same night by the crew that was working the line, and it's been weighed, and there was a disagreement at the yard office about whose name it went against, which I am told lasted some time and was settled by a small woman in a cap who does not raise her voice." He almost smiled. "It's yours. Less station, as everything is. It's not a great deal of money. It's more than nothing and it'll be on the paper at your review, and I'd rather you heard about it from a person than found it on a wall."

"I didn't kill it for money."

"No," said the man. "Nobody's ever killed one for money in the whole history of this station. They kill it and then there's money, and that's a different sentence and it took me about nine years to learn to say it the right way round." He put his hands behind his back. "Now the rest.

"Your household's reviewed in twelve days. That's you and a child in a bed in the long building, and on the paper at the moment there's a Flask of ash and a red thread and nothing else, and I'm not going to pretend to you that that's a strong sheet.

"Your brother's care doesn't depend on it. You've been told that, probably four times, and it is true, and I'll say it a fifth if you like. What depends on it is whether you go on sleeping inside this berm, within four hundred yards of him, or whether you're a transient, outside, coming in on a pass on visiting days."

"And what puts something on the sheet?" said Jab.

"Work," said the man in the long coat. "And there's all kinds of it, and most of it is ordinary and pays ordinary and counts ordinary." He paused for exactly as long as it takes a person to change the subject without appearing to. "And there's the north yard."

---

He explained it standing on the step, without any dressing on it at all, which Jab came to understand afterward was itself a kind of technique.

There was a stretch of road at the edge of the north yard that had never settled. Things came through there. The station kept a line on it, and a man to hold that line shut, and a crew behind him to lift what was left of whatever came through, because what was left was reserve-ash and reserve-ash was fuel and money and the reason anybody went near the place at all.

Holding the line shut was a narrow trade. Almost nobody at Meridian could do it, and the ones who could were worth whatever the station had to pay them, which was why the station did not pay them in money.

"You can't hold a road," said the man. "Your card says so and I believe it.

"What you can do is the other job, which is that when the line goes down — and it goes down; it went down four times last winter and twice this autumn — the people sacking the ash are on their knees with their backs to whatever's just arrived, and somebody has to be standing over them with something sharp until the line comes back up. At the moment there isn't a somebody. There's a man with a hole in his back who holds the road and then has to try and do both.

"It is the worst work in this station. It pays like a joke, and everyone involved knows that it pays like a joke, and the reason it pays like a joke is that the people who take it have got nothing else to put on a review." He held up the folded paper, briefly, and put it back in his coat without giving it to anybody. "Four seasons. It's a thing you sign.

"And it would count. I'll be honest with you about that, because if I'm vague you'll find out in twelve days and you'll think I was clever. It would count heavier than anything else you could put on that sheet, and it would very likely settle the question of where you sleep for the next four years, which by my arithmetic is about a third of the time your brother has to keep coming through those doors."

Jab stood in the doorway of a hut in the second row with a paper band on his wrist.

"You've done that arithmetic," he said.

"I do it every time," said the man. "I'd be no use here if I didn't." He looked at Jab levelly. "And I'll tell you the thing I'm supposed to tell you, which is that this is a request, and that you can say no."

"Do people say no?"

"Most people don't," said the man in the long coat.

---

"There's one more thing," he said, at the end of the row, having turned round.

"The second thing on your card. The one you haven't got a word for."

Jab said nothing.

"There's a column for it," said the man. "It's a wide column and there's very little in it. Nobody's going to come and take you anywhere, and nobody's going to ask you to do it in a yard for an audience, and if anybody does you may tell them I said they were not to." He paused. "But you wrote it down, and you didn't have to, and I'd like you to know that a person read it. That's all. We'd like to understand what's on the register. That is genuinely the whole of the sentence and there isn't a second half to it."

"It doesn't come when I call it," said Jab.

"No," said the man. "It says that on the card. In three separate places, actually, which I noticed."

Then he went away down the row, past the door of hut eleven, where a man was sitting on the step with his coat over his knees, turning something small and flat over and over in his fingers, and did not look up.

---

Rook was still there an hour later.

Jab had not planned to go and stand in front of him. He had walked the length of the row twice and then a third time, and on the third time the man on the step said, without raising his voice:

"You've been up and down four times. Sit down or go in, but don't wear the path out, it's the only one I've got."

Jab sat down on the step of hut twelve, which was empty, two doors along.

They did not say anything for a while. Down at the end of the row the lamp on the pole was not lit yet.

"He came to you as well, then," said Rook.

"This morning."

"Long coat. Grey beard. Doesn't give a name and it isn't a trick, he just genuinely doesn't think it's relevant." Rook turned the flat grey stone over in his fingers. "He's not lying to you. I want to start there, because a lot of people would rather he was. Every single thing he told you is true and he told it to you in the right order and he didn't leave the worst of it out."

"He said most people don't say no."

"He says that to everybody," said Rook. "He said it to me on this step in the rain about five weeks ago. And then I signed, four days later, standing up, in a room, and I have not once decided it was the wrong thing and I have not once felt clean about it either."

"Why did you?"

"Because there's a woman in that long building who has to be within a mile of a machine in a shed every eleven days for the rest of her life," said Rook. "And her medicine was never the station's to sell, and they never tried to sell it, and they didn't have to, because they own the ground the shed stands on and a bed is a different window." He was looking at the unlit lamp. "It's an extremely good arrangement. It's the best one I've ever seen. Nobody in it is even doing anything wrong."

"That's the part I can't get round," said Jab.

"No," Rook agreed. "Nor me. I've been at it five weeks."

---

"How old are you?" Rook said, after a while.

"Seventeen."

"And the boy's four."

"Four."

"And there's nobody else."

"There's nobody else," said Jab.

Rook nodded slowly, and did not say any of the eleven things that people had been saying to Jab since the gate.

"Right," he said. "Then I'm going to tell you one thing and it's the only thing I've got that's worth anything, and somebody had to say it to me twice before it went in.

"You can say no.

"That's all. I'm not telling you to. I'm not telling you not to, either, and if you came to me in a week and said you'd signed I'd not say one word about it, because I'd have no standing to and because I'd understand exactly why." He put the stone away in his coat. "I'm telling you it's allowed. People get that muddled with being told what to do, and they're different things, and nobody ever says the first one out loud in this place because everybody's busy being reasonable at each other."

"What happens if I say no?"

"Then you're reviewed in twelve days on a Flask of ash and a red thread," said Rook, "and I don't sit on the review, and neither does he, and I'm not going to lie to you about which way it goes."

"Then it isn't a choice."

"No," said Rook. "It's a choice with a price on it. That's what a choice is. The other kind's a menu." He got his breath. "And I'll say the last of it and then I'm going in, because I'm not supposed to sit out here in the cold and there's a girl of fifteen who counts how long I do it.

"Don't decide tonight. There's twelve days. Everything in this station is designed so that you decide it at three in the morning on the second night, when you've just come off a road and you're frightened and you'd sign anything to stop the feeling. That's not anybody's plan. It's just what the shape of the place does to people, and the only defence against it is to know it's happening."

---

Tonk asked about the squirrel on the fourth day.

He asked it out of nowhere, in the afternoon, lying flat with the bar on his leg and the ceiling to look at, in the middle of a conversation about something else entirely.

"When we go back," he said, "I'm putting the thing on the heap."

Jab, who had been folding a blanket, went on folding it.

"You said," Tonk went on, with the absolute confidence of a man quoting a signed agreement. "You said when my leg's mended I walk up there with a pocket full of something, and I do it *myself*, and you'd show me the tree. You *said*."

"I did say that."

"So when's my leg mended?"

Jab put the blanket down and sat on the edge of the chair and looked at his brother, and made himself do the thing he had made a rule about six weeks ago and had kept, every single time, even when it cost him a night.

"Your leg's going to mend," he said. "It's going to take until the spring before you stand on it, and a while after that before you walk properly, and it's never going to be quite the same as the other one. That's all true and you'd have got it out of somebody in this building by the end of the week anyway.

"And we're not going back."

Tonk's face did not do anything. That was what it did.

"Not ever?"

"Not to live. It's sixteen days of road and you've got to come to this building every year until you're grown, and I'm not going to tell you a story about it. That's what it is."

"But the *heap*."

"I know."

"You *said*." His voice went up and broke. "You said I could *put it back* — "

"I know what I said," said Jab, "and I meant it when I said it, and I can't do it, and I'm not going to pretend I can." He got his brother's hand, the one without the needle in it. "So here's what I've got instead, and you can tell me it's not as good, because it isn't.

"That squirrel got robbed by me. Not by you. You never took anything off anybody in your life. So it was always going to be me who owed it, and what I owe him I can't pay, because he's four hundred miles north and he's had his winter and he's either got through it or he hasn't."

"Then it doesn't *count*," said Tonk, furiously.

"It counts," said Jab. "That's the whole trouble with it. It counts and I can't pay it where I took it." He looked out of the window over the bed, at a turf roof and a grey sky. "So I'll have to put it back here."

"Here?"

"Here," said Jab. "Somewhere in this place. To somebody. I don't know who yet and I don't know what with." He heard himself say it and heard what it meant about four minutes after his mouth had finished. "That's what it is when you can't get back to the heap. You don't get out of it. You just have to find a different heap."

Tonk lay and thought about this for a long time, with his brows down, doing the arithmetic of a four-year-old, which was not the same arithmetic but was not nothing either.

"All right," he said at last. "But I'm doing it. Not you."

"You're doing it."

"*Me.*"

"You," said Jab.

---

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour that night, all at once, the way it did.

Jab was on the step of the hut in the second row with his back against the door frame, because the ward turned the visitors out at the tenth hour and he had discovered that he could not lie down yet. His brother was four hundred yards away in a white bed with a bar bolted through his leg and a wooden fish on the pillow and a twelve-foot cord in his fist, and somebody would come and get him if the cord was pulled, because he had stood at a desk and asked who he talked to until they wrote it down.

Three days ago he had been on a road with everything he owned on his back.

He went through it now, the way he had gone through the ledger every night for sixteen days, because his head was not going to let him do anything else.

He had a bed in a hut that was his for twelve more days. He had a paper band with a red thread in it. He had a Flask of ash on a board with his name beside it, less station. He had a piece of river metal at his throat, washed clean by strangers for the first time in four years, and a shape on his fist that four people in a valley four hundred miles north would have recognized across a river. He had a second thing that had come twice, both times without being asked, and had not come since, and would not come now if he sat here all night and begged it.

He had a brother who would keep his leg, probably, and who would limp, certainly, and who would be coming back through those doors every year for thirteen years.

And he had a folded paper that a man had shown him and not handed over, which would answer the whole of it, for four seasons, on the worst work there was.

He had not said yes. He had not said no. He had gone four days without saying either, which was the longest he had put off a decision since the accident, and he was not sure yet whether that was cowardice or the only sensible thing he had done since he came through the gate.

The road had taken sixteen days and he had been good at it.

He sat on a concrete step in the dark in a place with a written reason for everything, and thought: *I don't know how to do this one at all.*

---

"Jab."

It was the next afternoon, and the light through the window over the bed had that thin winter quality that made everybody in the ward look like they were underwater, and his brother had been quiet for a while.

"Mm."

"You know when we were on the hill."

"Which hill?"

"The *last* one." Tonk's hand moved on the blanket. "With the smoke. And you showed me and I said what's it called and you said Meridian and I said no, *it*."

Jab put the cup down.

"I remember," he said.

"And you said I had to see it up close first."

"I did."

"I've seen it," said Tonk.

He had. He had seen a gate come open in an earth bank at night with a lamp burning over it. He had seen a room with a light in the ceiling that did not move. He had been inside a machine that knocked like a man mending a roof. He had had a piece of his own bone taken out of him by a woman with her sleeves rolled up, and a rail bolted on the outside of his leg so it did not hang, and a wooden fish put into his hand by somebody who had not had to.

"Well, then," said Jab. "Has it got a name?"

Tonk looked at the ceiling for a long moment.

"Not yet," he said.
