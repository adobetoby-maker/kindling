# Chapter Five

He worked on the frame until the middle of the night, which was how he found out what the crossing had actually cost him.

The fire was good by then. He had got it out of a standing dead alder on the bank, split down to the dry pith and shaved into a pile of curls with the blade dark, and he had built it long rather than round so that a body lying beside it took heat along its whole length instead of on one side. Tonk lay against the pack inside the arc of it with the hide over him and the wooden fish on his chest, asleep in the shallow way that was not rest.

Jab sat cross-legged with the frame across his knees and the fire at his shoulder and made himself look at the whole of the damage before he touched any of it.

The left shoulder band had not simply come unlashed. The hide had torn through at the fold, where it turned under to meet the lattice — the wet had got into it over nine days, and the fold had been working against itself every step of every one of those days, and the boot-lace splint had held the cord together for exactly as long as the hide beneath it was prepared to hold anything at all. The torn end was frayed into strings for two fingers' width.

He had no spare hide. He had a hide cover, a pack, a pair of boots, a coat, and a brother.

He went through it properly, out loud, because thinking it in his head at this hour would have got him a worse answer.

"Pack sling's the right hide and the right width," he said. "And then I'm carrying the food in my arms for six days."

Not that.

"Boot tops."

Thin, and cut small, and he had already taken one lace and the ground ahead of him was going to be frozen or snowed and his boots were the only thing between him and being a man who could not walk.

"Hide cover."

He looked at it, lying over his brother, big enough to make a shelter and a bed and a roof for a child in the rain.

It was four feet by six, and he needed a piece three fingers wide and as long as his forearm.

"That's what it is, then," he said, and got up and slid it gently off Tonk and cut a strip off the short end with the blade lit, drawing it slow to keep the line straight, and put the cover back over him before he had time to get cold. The cover was now four feet by five and a half. In a week he would not remember it had been anything else.

The repair took him three hours and he did every step of it the way it was done at home.

He cut the ruined fold off the band entirely, back into sound hide, which shortened the band by two fingers and could not be helped. He punched the new holes with the point of his own belt knife rather than the Edge, because the Edge cut too clean and a hole with a burr in it held a lashing better than a hole without. He soaked the new strip in the creek until it went soft and dark, and lashed it wet — over, under, back through, a turn at each crossing, and a frapping turn at the end pulled down into the gap, the way his mother had taught him and had complained about the whole time, *tight and short, tight and short, you leave your cords long and then you cut them and then where are they.* Then he set the whole frame near enough to the fire that the wet lashing would dry slowly, because hide dried fast went brittle and hide dried slow shrank down onto the thing it was holding and became part of it.

Then he sat back, and drank water, and considered the rail.

The rail was what had nearly killed them both. He turned that over for a while, honestly, without any pride in it. If the stub had gone in a hand further along, he would not have been able to reach the cut. If it had caught while he was upright instead of down, it would have turned him off the log before he knew it was there.

He could cut the rail off. That was one answer. Then Tonk's leg would hang.

He could shorten it. He measured with his hand: the rail ran out past the frame's line by nearly a forearm's length, to take the foot as well as the shin. He could take three fingers off the end and lose the foot support, and the foot would swing, and swinging was pain.

He took two fingers off the end — the smallest cut that did anything — and then spent an hour on the part that mattered more, which was the shape of what was left. He rounded the end back like a spoon handle, cut a shallow groove behind it, and lashed a cap of folded hide over the whole thing, smooth and blunt, so that anything it met would slide off rather than take hold.

"That's the answer," he said to it. "The trouble wasn't the length. The trouble was it had a corner."

He fell asleep sitting up against the pack with his hand on his brother's chest and woke an hour later with his neck locked, and the fire down, and the sky gone hard and clear and full of stars, and every wet thing in the camp going stiff.

---

The cold came down that night like a lid.

By morning the creek had dropped a foot and a half and grown a grey shelf of ice along both banks, and the spruce log over it wore a white rime that would have thrown a man off it in half a second. He stood on the bank and looked at that for a moment, and did the small ugly arithmetic of the near miss — twelve hours later and the crossing simply would not have been available — and then put it down and went to see about food.

He had nine fish and the bottom of the root sack, and the root sack was mostly dust and three pieces the size of his thumb.

Nine fish was three days if he fed them both properly, and five if he fed Tonk properly and himself the way he had been feeding himself since the crossing, which was not properly. The road had five or six days in it. He had known this was coming since the fourth day. He had been putting off doing anything about it because doing something about it cost hours, and hours were the other currency, and a man could go broke in either one.

The cold had just made the decision for him. Everything that lived up here had known the freeze was coming for a day before he did, and everything that lived up here had spent yesterday moving.

He found the run in twenty minutes.

It was a good one — a hare highway along the bottom of a willow thicket where the alders gave out, the trails beaten down to bare frozen ground, droppings everywhere, the willow whips cut off clean at the height of a sitting animal's reach. There were four gaps in the brush where the trail necked down between stems, and three of them were the real thing and one was a false one that just looked like a gap, and he could tell the difference because the true gaps had hair on the stems.

He set four snares with the light going, which was the wrong time to set them, but the alternative was to set them in the dark and he had been doing enough things in the dark.

He came back to the camp pleased with himself and cooked the last of the roots into the fish water and slept in ninety-minute pieces with his brother's head on his thigh, and dreamed once, badly, about the bar at home with nobody on it.

In the morning all four snares were empty and every one of them had been run through.

---

He crouched over the second snare for a long time with his hands in his armpits and the cold coming up out of the ground into his knees.

The loop was still open. It had been pushed sideways and one of the stems it was tied to had been rubbed clean. There were tracks going through — going through *fast*, the long pairs of the back feet landing ahead of the front, which was an animal moving, not an animal browsing — and a tuft of grey-brown hair on the willow at a height Jab could put his finger on.

He put his finger on it. Then he put his other hand on the bottom of the loop.

The loop was a thumb's width above the hair. The whole snare, all four snares, sat a thumb too high.

"There it is," he said.

He knew exactly how it had happened, and it had not happened because he did not know how to set a snare. He had been setting snares since he was six. It had happened because he had set them standing up, in the last of the light, tired to the bone, gauging the height off his own crouch instead of getting his own eye down onto the ground where the hare's eye was — which was what his aunt on the smoking side had made him do for one entire summer, lying on his belly in the willow like an idiot, looking down a trail from a hare's height until he could not unsee it.

Four snares. One mistake, repeated four times, because he had made it once and then copied himself.

He sat down on the frozen ground in the willow and put the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Then he got up and made the decision, which was the actual cost of the morning.

He could move now. Half a day's walk, hungry, on nine fish going to eight. Or he could reset these four snares properly, put in two more at the false gap where he had not bothered, and lose the morning — four hours, at least, because a snare set at dawn and lifted at noon was a snare with a poor chance, and he would be trading real miles for a poor chance at one animal.

He looked back through the alders at the smoke of his own fire and thought about his brother's face at the crossing, and about what four hours of lying still by a fire was actually worth to a boy who had spent yesterday being carried across a flood.

"Morning," he said. "And then we go hard till dark."

He reset them from his belly.

He lay down in the frozen willow with his cheek on the ground and looked along each trail from a hare's height, and set each loop so the bottom of it was four fingers off the ground and the top of it was where a running head would be and not a hand above it, and he checked every one by lying down again afterward and looking. He rubbed each wire loop and each anchor stem with a handful of crushed willow leaf and rotten wood from the bank, because his hands smelled like smoke and fish and a hare could read that off a stem a day later. He set two more at the false gap, which on his belly turned out not to be false at all — it was a bolt-hole, and the ground under it was polished.

It took an hour and a half. Then he went back and did the other thing the morning was for.

---

"You're *stealing*," Tonk said.

"I am."

"That's stealing. That's *his*."

"It is."

Jab was up to his elbow in the squirrel's midden, which was a heap of shredded cone scales the size of a bed at the foot of a big spruce, built by generations of red squirrels and added to by the current one, who was six feet above Jab's head running up and down the trunk in a state no words could properly describe.

Down in the middle of the heap, where the cold got in last, were mushrooms. Forty or fifty of them, picked in the autumn by a squirrel and wedged into the cone scales to dry, brown and light as paper. There were cones down there too, green ones, packed damp and whole, and the seeds in a green cone were fat and oily and worth the hour it took to get them out.

He took about a third and left the rest, which was the rule, and did not pretend to himself that the rule made it fair.

"He's *shouting*," Tonk said, from the pack, delighted and appalled.

"He is."

"He's saying it's *his*."

"He's saying a lot more than that."

"You're the *jay*," Tonk said, and sat up as far as he could, with his whole face lit up at having got there. "You're doing the jay. You watched where he put his things and you *took* them."

Jab stopped with a handful of dried mushrooms and looked at his brother for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "I am the jay."

"That's *bad.*"

"It's very bad. Do you want to know the difference between me and the jay?"

"What?"

"The jay's going to do it again tomorrow to a different squirrel." He came back to the fire and tipped the mushrooms into the fish pot. "And next autumn, when we're not hungry, someone's going to put something back on that heap. That's what we do at home with a thing we've taken out of turn. It's not a fair trade and everybody knows it isn't, and you do it anyway, because a person who takes and doesn't put back turns into a different kind of person very quickly and doesn't notice it happening."

Tonk thought about this with great seriousness.

"Can I put it back?"

"You can put it back."

"*Me*?"

"You," said Jab. "When your leg's mended, you're going to walk back up here with a pocket full of something and you're going to do it yourself, and I'll show you the tree."

It was not a lie. He had made himself a rule about lies six weeks ago and he had kept it, and this was not one, because he had every intention of it being true and no ability to make it true, and those were two different things.

Tonk lay back, satisfied, with the arrangement filed somewhere permanent.

"He'll be cross," he said happily.

"He'll be furious. He's going to tell every squirrel on this creek."

---

There was a hare in the fifth snare.

Not the fourth, not the reset originals — the fifth, at the bolt-hole he had nearly not bothered with, which he thought about the whole way back with the weight of it swinging against his leg.

He butchered it on a flat stone away from the camp and did it fast, and cleanly, and put everything that was not meat into the creek downstream, because he was going to be sleeping here for one more night and there was no sense advertising. The liver went into the pot at once. The rest he cut thin and hung on a rack of green sticks in the smoke of the fire all afternoon, turning it, feeding the fire green alder to keep it sour and cool — not proper smoking, which took three days and a shed and a season's practice, but enough to make five days of meat into eight.

Tonk helped.

He had one job and it was a real one: keeping the green alder coming. Jab set the cut pieces in a pile within the reach of his good arm, and Tonk fed them onto the edge of the fire one at a time when the smoke thinned, and got told twice to stop putting on three, and did the job for two solid hours with the concentration of a man building a house.

"Is it enough smoke?"

"It's good smoke."

"Is it *enough*?"

"It's better smoke than I'd have got. You've a better eye for it than me."

"I have," Tonk agreed, without a flicker.

By the time the light went there was a hare's worth of dark strips hanging on the rack, nine fish in the bag, two hands of dried mushrooms, a folded piece of hide holding about a cupful of spruce seed, and seven twists of bark.

Seven.

Jab did that count sitting on the far side of the fire with his back to his brother, and did it twice, and then sat looking at the birch paper folded in his palm.

Two a day was the schedule. The schedule came from a woman who had counted forty of them into his hand at a table and told him how the road went, and forty had been exactly right for a road with no bad nights in it.

Seven, at two a day, was three days and a half.

The road had five in it. Six, if the weather did what the sky had been threatening to do since the freeze.

He sat with that for a while and did not let himself reach the obvious conclusion at speed, because it was the kind of conclusion that would be easier to live with if he had walked all the way around it first. Then he reached it anyway. From tomorrow the morning dose would be half, and the night dose would stay whole, because the nights were where it lived. Half a twist would not hold a morning. He knew that. He had watched what a whole one did to a bad morning and it was not much.

"Five days," he said, under his breath. "Five and a half."

Behind him Tonk said, sleepily, "What?"

"Nothing. Counting fish."

"How many?"

"Nine."

"That's a lot."

"It's not so many," Jab said.

---

They went on at first light and it snowed on them at noon.

It was not a real snow. It came in on a wind out of the north and went sideways through the alders for an hour and did not lie, and it stopped, and the sky behind it was the flat white of a thing arranging itself. Jab walked with his head down and the hide over Tonk and his own hands in his sleeves between rests, and made eleven miles by his own reckoning, which was the best day since the seventh.

At the third rest he got his brother out and looked at the leg, and did not like what he saw, and then did the other thing — the thing he did four or five times a day now without telling anybody, because there was nobody to tell.

He put his hands either side of the leg without touching and made his mind as flat as he could and did not care what the answer was.

Up, it said.

He checked. It was up.

He sat back on his heels in the snow-wet moss and looked at his brother, who was busy explaining to the wooden fish about the squirrel, and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do for two days, which was not about bark or fish or miles at all.

Six weeks ago the fever had gone up at night and come all the way down by morning.

Two weeks ago it had gone up at night and come down by the middle of the morning.

For the last four days it had gone up at night, and come down, and stopped coming down as far — a hand's breadth higher each time, so that the low place his brother rested in between the bad hours was not the low place it had been. Yesterday's low had been the same as last week's high.

He had been watching it happen for four days and calling it the weather, and the crossing, and a bad patch.

"Right," Jab said.

Tonk looked up. "What?"

"Nothing." He got his hands under him and stood, and turned his face north, into the wind, where the country climbed one more time before it let go toward the flats and the river and whatever was on the other side of that. "We're going to do a long one tomorrow. Longer than you'll like."

"How long?"

"Longer than you'll like," Jab said again, and bent to lift him.
