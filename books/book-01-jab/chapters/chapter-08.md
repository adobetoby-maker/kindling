# Chapter Eight

The road went down out of the snow at about the middle of the morning, and by noon there was no snow at all, and Jab found that he did not trust it.

It was the same road. That was the strange part. It had the same raised bed and the same ditch either side and the same hard grey surface under his boots, and somewhere in the last two miles it had simply stopped being white. There was frost in the shadow of the ditch banks and ice standing in the wheel ruts of the field tracks, and the fields themselves were brown and bare and enormous, going away east to a river he could see in pieces. Behind him the hills had their winter on. Down here the country was only cold.

He kept catching himself looking back at it, the way you look back at a house you have left a door open in.

"Are we there?"

"Not yet."

"You *said*."

"I said today. It's today. I didn't say now."

Tonk made a sound that meant the distinction was noted and rejected. He had been awake since first light, which was more than he had managed in four days, and Jab had spent the first hour of it so glad that he had not looked at why.

He knew why. He had known since he unwrapped the leg in the grey before dawn and the smell had come up at him and been different. Not worse in the way it had been getting worse for a week — different, the way milk is different.

He had put the wrapping back on and had not said anything about it, and had got them both fed, and had given the morning dose whole instead of half because there was no longer any point in stretching anything.

Three twists.

---

At the second hour there were people on the road.

He heard them before he saw them, which told him how long it had been: a cart coming up behind at the pace of a walking animal, and two voices, and the particular flat clop of a shod hoof on made stone. Jab stopped on the crown of the road and stood there with his hands on the belt and did not know what he was supposed to do.

Six weeks. He had not spoken to a person who was not four years old in six weeks. He had built an entire body of law in his head for how to arrive at a gate, and had said it out loud to himself on three separate nights, and none of it covered a cart coming up behind him on an ordinary road in the middle of an ordinary morning.

He stepped off onto the shoulder and turned round to face it, because being looked at from the back seemed worse.

It was a flat cart with two wheels as high as his chest, pulled by a heavy brown pony with its winter coat coming in, and there was a man on the box and a woman sitting on the load with her feet hanging off. The load was cut poles, roped in two bundles, the ends squared off the way a person squares them when they are going to be paid by somebody who counts.

They came up level. The pony did not care about him. The man on the box looked at him, and then looked at the frame, and then looked at the place on the frame where a small white face was looking back with a wooden fish in its fist, and his own face did a thing that Jab watched all the way through: interest, then the arithmetic, then the decision.

He did not stop.

"Gate's open till dark," he said, over his shoulder, already past. "They'll not shut it early on you."

And that was all, and the cart went on down the road with the woman on the back looking at them until the road bent.

Jab stood on the shoulder for a while after they had gone.

"He didn't say hello," said Tonk.

"No."

"That's *rude*."

"It might not be, here." Jab got himself moving again and found his legs had gone stupid in the two minutes of standing. "At home a person who went past you on a path without saying anything would be a person with something wrong with him. It's not a path. It's a road with a hundred people on it. You'd never get anywhere."

Tonk considered the enormity of that.

"A *hundred*," he said.

"More than that."

"Say hello to all of them."

"I'm not going to say hello to all of them."

"Say hello to *one*," Tonk said, with the air of a man making a fair offer, and Jab, who had been dreading the next person on the road for the last quarter of an hour, laughed and found that he had stopped dreading them quite so much.

---

He said hello to eleven people between the bend and the middle of the afternoon, and got six answers, and learned things off every one of them.

It was a trick he had from the smoke house. When his father's aunt wanted to know something she never asked it. She said a thing she already knew, slightly wrong, and stood there while somebody corrected her, and by the end of the correcting she had the whole of it and had never once been a person who did not know.

So Jab said, *long way in yet, is it*, and a man with a hand-barrow said no, three miles, four at his pace with that on his back. He said, *I'll want the big gate*, and a woman leading two goats said there was only the one on this side and it was the northwest and it had a queue in the mornings and none at all after the tenth hour because nobody came in in the winter. He said, *I'll be wanting a doctor for him*, and watched three separate people go carefully blank, and a fourth — an old man sitting on a milestone doing nothing whatsoever, who had the look of somebody who had been the person on that milestone for twenty years — say: "Then you want the long building at the north end, and you want to say *clinical* at the gate, and you want to say it before they've got your boots off. They'll take everything else off you first if you let them. They're not unkind. They're just in an order."

"Clinical," said Jab.

"Say it at the gate. Say it to the first grey coat you see and don't be polite about it." The old man looked at the frame and then away at the fields, which Jab had already noticed was what people here did instead of asking. "How long's he been like that?"

"Seven weeks."

"Then say it twice," said the old man.

---

They stopped at the third hour in the lee of a stone sheep-fold twenty paces off the road, because Tonk had gone from talking to not talking in about a mile, and Jab had learned to stop at the change rather than at the bottom.

He got him out and laid him on the hide in the dry grass and did the whole of it properly: wrappings off, the leg looked at in daylight, boiled water gone cold, the wrapping on again, not tight. He did it with his hands and kept his face out of it entirely, and Tonk lay and looked at the sky and did not ask.

The open place low on the outside had widened. There was less to see than there had been, and more to smell, and the skin all round it had gone a dull colour that was not the angry colour it had been for a fortnight. He put the back of his hand on the shin above it and then on the boy's forehead and got the same heat off both.

Then he sat back on his heels, and put his hands on his knees, and made his mind flat.

He had got good at the flatness. It was a trick like the other one and it worked the same way: you did not hold your breath and you did not hope and you did not decide what you wanted before you looked. You put the question down in front of the thing under your breastbone the way you put a bowl down in front of a dog, and then you took your hand off it.

*Up or down.*

Up. Not the shape of the ordinary night climb, either, which had a lean in it, a curve, a thing that would come over the top somewhere. This one went up like a road.

He checked it against the back of his hand. The hand agreed. It had been agreeing for four days.

"Right," Jab said.

"What?"

"Nothing. Counting fish."

"There's *five*," said Tonk, from the hide, without opening his eyes. "You counted them at the fire. You always say nothing and it's always the fish and there's *five*."

Jab sat in the dry grass beside a stone wall with his brother's whole chest going up and down too fast under a hide, and laughed until his eyes ran, and then wiped his face with his sleeve before he turned round.

---

The carter came up on them at the fourth hour, going the same way, and this one stopped.

He was a squat man of about fifty with a beard in two colours and a cart no bigger than a door with a single pony in the shafts, and the cart had four sacks in it and a coil of rope and a child's chair with a broken arm, which was the part Jab could not stop looking at. He pulled up on the road above them and sat there and looked down at the sheep-fold for long enough to be rude, and then said:

"That a carrying frame?"

"It is."

"Whose work?"

"Mine."

The carter got down off the cart. He came over the grass with the unhurried flat-footed walk of a man who has got down off a cart eleven thousand times, and he went round the frame where it stood propped against the wall, and looked at the hip belt, and put two fingers under the left shoulder band where the new strip was lashed in wet and had dried down onto it.

"That's been mended on the road."

"Ten days ago."

"With what?"

"The cover."

The carter grunted. He tipped the frame a little to see the rail, and the rounded hide-capped end of it, and the groove cut behind the cap.

"And that?"

"It caught on a branch stub," said Jab. "On a log over a creek. It wouldn't let go and I couldn't stand up with it. So I took the corner off it."

"Hm," said the carter, in a way that made the hair go up on Jab's arms, because it was his father's noise exactly, made by a stranger on a road four hundred miles from anywhere his father had ever stood.

Then he straightened up and looked at Tonk on the hide, and his face did the arithmetic the same way the first man's had, and came out somewhere else.

"I'm going to the north yard," he said. "I go in the morning. I've a shed at the turn, about a mile on, and a stove in it, and I'll be at that gate at the second hour after light with the boy on the cart and you walking beside him, and it'll cost you nothing because there's nothing you've got."

Jab opened his mouth.

"Before you answer," said the carter, "I'm going to say the other half, because I'd rather you had it. I'm not going up that last stretch tonight. Not for you and not for money, and I'd want you to know it's not you."

"Why not?"

The carter looked at him for a moment. Then he did a thing Jab had been watching people do all day and had not understood: he glanced north along the road, at the place where it ran on flat and straight toward the smoke, and then away at the fields.

"How far've you come?" he said.

"Upriver. Sixteen days."

"On your own."

"Yes."

"Met anything?"

"Weather," said Jab.

"That's not what I asked and you know it isn't." The carter said it without any heat at all. "You've come down off the top, then. They don't get up there. They get here. There's a stretch of that road about a quarter mile out of the north gate that's never sat right since the old days, and things come through it, and they come through it more than anywhere else round here, and everybody in this valley knows it and works round it and doesn't talk about it more than they have to. The station keeps a crew on it to lift what's left when something's been killed, because the stuff that's left is worth money, which is the single most Meridian thing about the whole business." He scratched under his beard. "They work it in the daylight. They come off it before the light goes. And so do I."

"What comes through?"

"Whatever's coming through this month." The carter shrugged. "Hounds, mostly, this time of year. They lie up in the ditches and they count you. If you're lucky they count you and go."

"And if you're not?"

"Then you'll wish you were somewhere with walls," said the carter, and turned and looked at Tonk again. "How bad's the boy?"

Jab found that his mouth had gone dry.

"Bad," he said.

"Bad by tomorrow, or bad by next week?"

And there it was, laid out flat on the grass between them by a man who had known him for four minutes, which was a thing Jab had not been able to do for himself in four days.

---

He went and stood at the wall for a while with his back to both of them.

The shed was the right answer. He knew it the way he had known the fallen spruce was the right answer while he was still walking the bank looking for another one. A stove and a dry floor and a man who had mended a hundred carrying frames. Fourteen hours of lying still and warm. Tonk carried the last mile in a cart instead of on a frame that pressed a seat board into the backs of his thighs at every step. A gate in daylight, with a queue, and a man on a box beside him who knew how to say things to the people at it.

Everything about it was right, and he could see it all, and it had exactly one thing wrong with it, which was that it was tomorrow.

He crouched down out of the wind with his forearms on his knees and made his mind flat again. He was not going to get away with pretending he did not care about the answer. He could not remember the last time he had not cared about an answer. But he could do the other half of it, which he had learned in the snow with his arms round his brother: he could stop *asking*, and let the thing lean where it wanted to lean, and then look at where it had leaned.

It leaned up. It went on leaning up, the way a slope goes on being a slope.

And under that, not from the thing in his chest at all but from the ordinary part of him that had been counting fish and twists and rests for sixteen days, came the rest of the arithmetic. Three twists. A fever with no top on it. A smell that had changed this morning and not last week. The Elder at a table with her hands flat on it, saying *six weeks* to a room, in front of people who had argued with her and lost.

Seven weeks ago tomorrow.

"I'm not going to take it," he said, out loud, to the wall.

Then he stood up and went and said it to the man, which was harder.

---

The carter heard him out without interrupting, which Jab had not expected, and then stood looking at the pony's ears for a while.

"You've done the sum," he said.

"Twice."

"Is it a real sum, or is it a sum you did because you've carried him four hundred miles and you can't bear to put him down a mile short?"

Jab thought about that honestly, because it deserved it.

"It's a real sum," he said. "The other one's in there. I know it's in there. But if I sit in your shed tonight and he turns, I'll have the whole of the rest of my life to think about the mile."

The carter nodded slowly.

"Right," he said, and turned round and started untying one of his sacks, which was not what Jab had braced for at all. "Then you'll take this, and you'll not argue with me about it, because I've stood here and failed to talk you into a stove and I'm going to be allowed to do something."

It was bread. Half a loaf of it, grey and even and heavy, in a paper wrapper with a number printed on the paper.

"And this." A screw-top tin of something. "Fat and salt. Put it in hot water, it's soup, and it's got more in it than you have."

"I can't pay you."

"No," the carter agreed. "You can't. You'll do me a favour instead and it's this: when you get to that gate you'll say the word *clinical* before they've got your boots off, and then you'll say it again, and then — this is the part I want — when they tell you you can't come in, you'll ask them who you talk to, and you'll keep asking that same question at every person they hand you to. Not *let me in*. *Who do I talk to*. They've got an answer to that one and they haven't got an answer to the other one, and the difference is about a day."

Jab took the bread.

"Why?" he said.

The carter got up onto the box and gathered the reins and did not look at him.

"Because I had a girl with a bad hip," he said, "and I've had the chair on this cart for nine years and I've never once got round to mending the arm of it." He clicked his tongue at the pony. "Go on, then. Go fast and don't stop in the sag, and if something stands still and looks at you, don't you stand still and look back."

---

The last of the light went out of the sky at the fifth hour, and it went fast, the way it did in winter in a flat country, and Jab was still a mile and a half out.

He had made better time than he had any right to. The road ran dead straight now, up on its bed, with the berm coming into view ahead as a long low bank blacker than the field behind it, and the smoke standing up over it in a wood, and here and there a light. He could see one of the lamps on a pole a long way off. It was small and flat and white and it did not move at all, and looking at it for more than a moment made the rest of the world go darker, so he stopped looking at it.

Tonk was awake. That was the thing that had begun to frighten him. He had been awake since noon, and he was not talking, and every so often he made a small sound that had nothing behind it.

"Two miles," Jab said. "Less."

Nothing.

"Tonk."

"Mm."

"Say something. Anything you like."

"Little's cold," Tonk said, from a long way down, and Jab put his hand back over his own shoulder and felt for the small fist and found the wooden fish still in it, held so hard that the fingers had gone stiff around it.

"Put him under the blanket."

"He wants to see," said Tonk, and that was all he said for a quarter of an hour.

The dark came down over the fields. The road went on being under his boots and then, quite suddenly, stopped being reliably under his boots: the made surface ended and started again in patches, and there were slumps at the edges where the shoulder had gone down into the ditch, and twice he put a foot into a dip he had not seen and felt the whole frame swing.

The second time, the rail hit something and the load turned him half round, and his brother cried out.

Jab stood still in the middle of the road with his heart going and did the thing he had promised himself for sixteen days that he would not do on this last stretch.

He lit the blade.

---

He brought the metal out first, dark, because he needed the shape before he needed the light. It came off the cord and ran down his sleeve and thickened on his fist, that fat-bellied ugly working knife, and the weight of it in his hand at the end of the worst day of the worst week was so familiar that something in his chest unclenched a quarter of a turn.

Then he kindled it.

The light woke at the grip and ran outward along the working edge, a narrow amber line no thicker than a hair, the colour of a coal with the ash still on it. Two-thirds of the way along it hit the swell of the belly and stuck, and guttered, one whole breath of guttering at that one curve — the same fault it had had on a gravel bar in front of sixty people when he was thirteen, the same fault it had had every single time since — and then it caught and ran on and lit the hook at the end.

It did not light the road. He had known it would not. It put a dull amber wash on the stone for about four feet round his own boots, and it showed him the edge of the crown and the slump of the shoulder, and that was all he had wanted.

"That's better," said Tonk, behind his ear, in a small clear voice with more in it than there had been all afternoon.

"Is it?"

"I can see your *hand*."

Jab held it out a little to the side, where his brother could see the whole of it, and walked on down the middle of a straight road with an amber line burning on his fist and a boy watching it over his shoulder.

It cost him. He had known that too. Holding the shape was nothing after four years, but holding the light was a small steady drip out of a bucket that had almost nothing in it, and by the time he had gone four hundred paces he could feel the drip.

He went on anyway, because the road was bad, and because for the first time in six hours his brother was talking.

"Jab."

"Mm."

"It's the boat one."

"You want the boat one?"

"No," said Tonk. "*You.* You're doing the boat one. With your mouth shut."

He had been. He had not noticed.

So he sang it out loud instead, all four verses he had left, on an empty road at the front edge of a winter night, and his voice went out flat across the black fields either side and came back off nothing at all.

And somewhere in the third verse, out in the ditch on the left, at the edge of what the amber line could show him, something that had been keeping station with him stopped.
