# Chapter Nineteen

The road forked at the crossroads on the thirty-third day and they took the left-hand one, north, and Rook walked behind the cart.

Toren did not work out for most of the morning why the road felt wrong. It was a perfectly ordinary road. It ran up out of the bottom between two hedges with the hawthorn going over, and the ruts were good, and the country opened out on both sides into wet grazing with willows in the drains, and there was nothing in it to dislike.

Then Pitch put his ears back at a pheasant and Toren looked up the road to see what Rook thought about it, the way he had looked up that road about four hundred times in a month, and there was nobody up the road.

He was behind them. He had been behind them since the crossroads, walking in the cart's dust with his hands in his coat pockets, at the mule's pace, which was a pace Toren had never in his life seen the man keep for more than a hundred yards.

"He's not going out front," Toren said.

Dessa had the mule's head. She did not turn round.

"He hasn't been out front since Tuesday," she said. "You've been watching the road. That's fine, somebody's got to. I've been watching him."

"Is he—"

"He's doing exactly what he told us he'd be doing," said Dessa, "which is being worth about what Milo's worth, and he told us that to our faces four nights ago, in advance, in daylight, and everybody nodded and then went straight back to expecting him to be up the road."

Behind them Milo said, cheerfully, "I'm worth a great deal."

"You're worth more than he is at the minute," said Dessa, "and that's not a joke, and I'd like you to stop making it into one before somebody needs it not to be."

---

The halt was at the bridge over a drain at midday, in the sun, with the mule off and the wheel greased, and Milo did the bread.

Milo had done the bread since the road cut. Nobody had ever appointed him to it. He cut it too thick and handed it round in a fixed order that had grown up by itself over a month and that had become, without anybody agreeing to it, a thing the day contained: Senna first, and Senna always got the end crust because she had said once, in the first week, that the end crust was the only part worth eating and had been given it every day since whether she wanted it or not. Then Dessa. Then Wyck, who took it without looking up. Then Rook, who took it and said nothing. Then Toren. Then Milo sat down on whatever was nearest and ate his own standing up half the time, and talked.

"Is there a wall between here and Meridian?" he said, with his mouth full. "An actual one, with a gate and a weighmaster and a person who sells things."

"There's Ferrow," said Rook. "Six days. It's not a wall, it's a bank and a ditch and a hundred and twenty people who grow flax."

"Do they have—"

"They have flax."

"What do they *do* with it?"

"They sell it to people who want flax," said Rook, "which as far as I can tell is nobody, which is why it's still there, because nobody's ever wanted anything they have enough to come and take it. Ferrow is the safest settlement in this country and it is the dullest place on the face of the earth and every single person in it is ninety."

Milo laughed, and Senna laughed, which she did not often do because it cost her, and Rook did not smile, but he had told it to make them do that and everybody at the halt knew it, and Toren thought: *he's paying for the morning.* He did not know when he had learned to see that.

Then Rook put his bread down on the cart shaft half eaten and put two fingers on the inside of his own wrist.

Nobody said anything. He had done it four times in three days and he had started doing it in front of them instead of up a bank, and Toren had begun to be frightened of it in a low steady way that did not have anywhere to go.

"Right," said Rook, to all of them. "Before the afternoon. Because the rule is you say it out loud before a bad piece of road and not after it, and I made the rule.

"A tenth."

Dessa's chin came up.

"That's lower than yesterday."

"It's lower than yesterday."

"You said a sixth yesterday."

"I did," said Rook. "And last night I sat in a cart bed for three quarters of an hour with my hands on an old woman's chest, and that's where the difference went, and it will go there again tonight and tomorrow night and every night after, because that's what it's for." He screwed the cap of the empty flask down, which he did about nine times a day now, a thing his hands did without him. "I'd like everybody to understand the shape of it rather than the number, because the number's going to keep doing that. I fill fast. I fill faster than any of you and it's not a skill and I've explained why. What I haven't got is anything put by. The flask was nine days of me in a tin and I poured it into a cup, and what that means is that from now until I stop, I am carrying exactly as much as I have made since the morning, and every night I give it to her.

"So: a tenth. Which is a man who can hold the stone, put an edge on it, and walk about. It is not a man who can put up a shape that'll stop anything, and it is not a man who can cross forty feet of road faster than you can look at it, and if any of you are carrying a picture of me doing either of those things this week, take it out and burn it now, this afternoon, while it's cheap."

"And if it stops being cheap?" said Wyck.

"Then you'll have burnt it already," said Rook, "and you'll thank me, and I won't hear you, because I'll be dead in a ditch with a knife in my hand looking extremely ordinary."

---

They met the two men at the fourth hour, on a straight stretch with willows down one side, coming south.

There was nothing wrong with them. Toren went over it afterward about a hundred times and could not find one thing that had been wrong, which was the beginning of his education in a subject he would have preferred not to take.

They had a hand-cart. It was a good one, better than Ott's, with an iron-shod wheel, and it had sacking over the top of it roped down at four corners. The older one was about forty, grey at the temple, with a countryman's face and a countryman's unhurried way of stopping in a road, and the younger one was seventeen at the outside and had the kind of open, slightly stupid friendliness that Toren had spent his entire life in a village surrounded by.

"Afternoon," said the older one.

"Afternoon," said Dessa, from the mule's head.

"You've got a wheel going."

"We know."

"You've got about four days in it." He came round and looked at it without asking, and crouched, and put his thumb on the felloe where the crack was, and Toren watched the whole party fail to object to a stranger touching the cart because the stranger was talking the entire time. "See that? That's not the wheel, that's the tyre gone slack and the wheel's working inside it. You want to stand it in a drain overnight. Swell the timber up. It'll hold you to a wheelwright."

"There isn't a wheelwright," said Dessa.

"There's one at Ferrow."

"Is there."

"Old boy. Ninety, if he's a day." The man straightened up and dusted his hands, pleased with himself, and Toren felt something in his chest go loose with relief at how ordinary it all was. "Where're you for?"

"North."

"Everybody's north." He looked along the cart, at Senna propped up in the bed with the blanket round her, and touched two fingers to his brow at her, and Senna inclined her head half an inch. "Ma'am. You've picked a poor month for it."

"I've picked a poor decade for it," said Senna.

The man laughed. The young one laughed too, a beat late, and looked at Milo, and Milo — who could not help it, who had never once been able to help it — offered him the last of the bread out of the cloth, and the boy took it and said "ta," and ate it in three bites like somebody who had walked a long way, and Milo asked him if the road south was any good, and the boy said it was a road, and Milo said that was the trouble with roads, and the two of them stood there in the middle of the afternoon for a minute and a half grinning at each other about nothing.

"You've not seen a man on this road," the older one said. "On his own. Buys ash. Pays over the odds for it and asks after a company."

"No," said Rook.

He had come up the near side of the cart while it was happening. He had one hand on the rail and he was looking at the man with an expression of mild, bored inconvenience, and he was a man in a worn coat at a mule's shoulder with a mule's pace in him.

"Mm," said the man. "Worth a bit, if you did. There's a fellow at a wall east of here'll pay for word of him."

"Then I'm sorry for both of us."

"Aye, well." The man took up the cart handles. "Stand that wheel in a drain."

They went on south. The young one turned round once, walking backward for a few paces, and lifted a hand at Milo, and Milo lifted one back.

---

Rook did not say anything for a quarter of a mile.

Then he said, "Stop the cart."

Dessa stopped the cart.

"That was them."

Nobody said anything at all for about three seconds, and Toren discovered that his hands had gone cold in a way they did not go cold for husks.

"No," said Milo.

"Milo—"

"No, but that was a *man with a wheel*—"

"He knew wheels," Rook agreed. "He'd have to. Nobody sends a fool out in front. Now listen, because I'm going to take that apart in front of you once and then we're going to move quickly.

"There was sacking roped down over that cart at four corners and there was nothing under it. It came up over the ruts behind him and it didn't sound and it didn't shift. A hand-cart full of goods goes over a rut like a box of plates. That one went over like a drum.

"He talked for a minute and a half about a wheel so that all of you would look at a wheel. That's not a trick, it's a trade, and he's good at it.

"He came round the cart to the crack, which meant he came down the length of it, which meant he saw the bed. He counted you. He counted the old woman, and the leg, and the arm" — a nod at Wyck — "and he saw a stone in one pocket and worked out there'd be more, and then he asked the question he'd come to ask while he was walking away from it, standing side-on, so nobody would be looking at his face when it landed."

"And then he *told* us he was looking for you," said Dessa.

"Yes."

"Why? Why in God's name would he—"

"Because a man who tells you he's hunting somebody isn't hunting you," said Rook. "That's the whole of it. It costs him nothing, it makes him a chancer after a bounty instead of a scout, and it means that if any of you *had* seen me, one of you would have said so, and the one who'd have said so is thirteen and has a face like a window." He looked at Toren. "You did well. You said nothing. You said nothing because you'd gone cold and couldn't think of anything, and I'll take it."

Milo had gone a colour Toren did not like.

"He ate the bread," Milo said.

"He did."

"I gave him the bread and he stood there and *laughed* at—"

"Yes," said Rook. "And he's seventeen and he's had a joining fee in brass in his pocket for about eleven days and he has not yet worked out what he's joined, and when he does it'll be in the dark with somebody shouting at him. That one's not a liar. He's a boy who's been put next to a liar and told to walk south looking pleased." He took his hand off the rail. "Milo. Look at me. You did not do anything wrong and you are not going to start being careful about handing people bread, because if that ever goes out of you the rest of us are finished. That's not comfort, it's the arithmetic. Now go and sit up with your grandmother, because the next bit is going to be unpleasant and I want you where she can get hold of you."

---

The unpleasant part took about ten minutes and Rook did it standing in the road with the stick again, and Toren realized halfway through that it was the second time in five days that the man had done his thinking out loud in front of all of them, and that he was never going back to the other way, and that Toren had watched a thing change in a person and had not noticed it happening.

"They walked south past us," said Rook, "which means the rest of them are north."

"North," said Wyck.

"North. Two of them came down past us in daylight so they could count us and go back and say what we were, and they'll be back on this road behind us before dark, because a scout walks past you and then turns round and follows. Which means whatever's in front of us on this road has been in front of us since this morning and is waiting, and whatever's behind us is two men who know exactly how many of us there are and how fast the cart goes.

"Second thing. They're not at Barrow Gate any more. Ott gave me a company sitting in a yard hiring until the new moon, and that was straight, and I'd bet my life she had it right on the day she left. They've come out ahead of the moon and left the yard hired behind them, which costs a paymaster money, and a paymaster does not lose money for a road job."

"Why?" said Toren.

"Because a woman walked into that yard with better ash than anybody's seen out of that country in a year and sold it well," said Rook, "and somebody at that table asked her where a salvager gets road-ash of that grade, and she told a story that didn't have me in it, and it had me in it anyway, because there is one man on this road who pours out half a flask to pay for apples and I have been doing it in ninety settlements for six years." He put the stick down. "That's not Ott's fault and nobody is going to say one word against Ott at any fire on this road. That's mine. I have spent six years making a shape in this country that could be recognized, and it got recognized, and the first time it has ever cost anybody anything it's going to cost all of you."

"How many?" said Wyck.

"Ott saw forty at a table. They won't have brought forty. They've a yard hired to a date and a weighmaster counting hours and a paymaster who'll want the men he's paying for, and a section that comes out early for a private piece of work comes out with the ones who can march." Rook rubbed his eye with the heel of his hand. "Twenty. Twenty-two. Say twenty-two and be pleased if it's less."

Dessa said, "Then we go west."

"There isn't a west."

"There's *country*—"

"There's eleven miles of it and then there's water," said Rook. "This is the bottom of the old catchment. Everything from that hedge to the hills is drain and reed and it has been for sixty years, and there is one hard line across it, and it's this road, and it goes up onto an embankment about four miles ahead and stays up there for eleven miles with water on both sides. They call it the Level."

He let that sit.

"And I'd like everybody to hear the next part properly," he said, "because it's the thing I have been dreading for a fortnight and it's arrived and I'd rather say it than have you all watch me not say it.

"I have spent six years being the man who picks the ground. That is the entire trade. That is what one man alone can do against a company, and it is the only thing one man alone can do against a company: he chooses where, and he chooses when, and if he can't have both he takes where and goes home.

"I don't get to pick it. They picked it. They picked it this morning while I was walking behind a cart in the dust, and what they've picked is a raised road with water on both sides and a cart on it that makes ten miles a day with a wheel that's going and a woman in the back of it who can't walk.

"So that's where we're going. Up onto it, tonight, because the alternative is to sit in a wet field in the open with two of them behind us counting, and I will take a bad road I can only be come at from two ends over good country I can be come at from everywhere."

"That's not a choice," said Wyck.

"No," said Rook. "It's the last one on the shelf. I've had my pick of the shelf for six years and I've got very used to it, and it's off me now, and I want it said out loud in front of all of you that I don't like it, because in about a day somebody is going to ask me why we didn't do something cleverer and I'd like the answer to already be in the air."

---

They got onto the Level at dusk.

It came up out of the grazing slowly — the road lifting, the hedges dropping away and stopping altogether, and then suddenly there was nothing on either side of the ruts but a long dark grass slope going down twelve or fourteen feet to water, and the water going out flat and pale and enormous in both directions under the evening with the reed beds standing black in it in long combed streaks.

It was the widest sky Toren had ever stood under. It had not one single thing in it to stand behind.

"Don't look at it like that," said Rook, behind him. "You're reading it as open. It isn't open. Open is a field where a man can come at you from any point on a circle. This is a corridor with water for walls, and there are exactly two ends to it, and that's the only good news there's going to be for about a day, so hold on to it."

The engine house came up on their left when the light was nearly gone: a squat brick block sitting out on a spur of the bank with a fallen chimney beside it and a doorway with no door, and the whole of the top storey gone so that the last of the sky showed through the upper windows. Below it the bank was cut clean through by a sluice — a stone channel forty feet long with four gates in it, three of them rotted down to their frames and one still standing, and the water going through the gaps with a low continuous sucking noise that Toren's ears never once stopped hearing for the next two days. The road crossed the sluice on a brick arch about twelve feet wide with no parapet on the water side, because the parapet had gone into the channel a long time ago and was still down there, humped and green.

Rook stopped the cart on the near side of the arch, and stood on the crown of it and looked north along the road, and then south along the road, and then down into the channel for a long moment.

"Right," he said. "We're not going over that tonight."

"We could—"

"We could get the cart over that arch in the dark with a cracked wheel," Rook agreed, "and if the wheel picked the arch to go on, we'd have a cart in a sluice and an old woman in the water and no road either way. We'll do it in daylight with everybody's hands on it. Camp on this side. Mule off, hobbled short. No fire."

"No fire?" said Milo.

"No fire."

---

The man came up the road at full dark, alone, with his hands out from his sides, and stopped forty paces off and called out, in an ordinary voice, that he was going to come closer.

Dessa had him before he called. She had been sitting on the cart step with her hands open on her knees for an hour and she said, without turning her head, "One. On the road. Thirty feet, and he's stopped," and Wyck was up off the ground with the bar and Toren had the spike out of his pocket and lit before the man's voice ever came out of the dark.

"That's a nice bit of work," the man said. "Somebody's got ears."

"Stay where you are," said Rook.

"I'd rather come to where you can see me, and I'll tell you why: because you can't, and a man you can't see is a man somebody gets nervous about, and I'd like to do this without anybody being nervous." A pause. "I've nothing in my hands. I'll come to ten paces and stop there. Say no and I'll stand here and shout and we'll all be very undignified."

"Come to ten."

He came to ten and stopped, and Milo, without being told, put his hands together and opened them and set the light down on the road a good way off to the right, and stepped back from it into the dark, which was a thing they had practised eleven times in a morning on a flat by a gate and had never once done for a reason.

The green glow came up off the road bed and lit the man from underneath, and he stood in it with his empty hands turned out and let himself be looked at.

He was fifty-odd and heavy through the shoulders, with a short grey beard and a good coat and boots that had been made for him and not found, and he had the air of a man who had been sent to do a job he had done many times and did not particularly mind. He looked at the light on the road, and then to the right of it, at the dark where they were all standing.

"That's neat," he said. "Who taught you that?"

Nobody answered.

"Hoyle," he said. "I'm the one they send. My name's not worth anything and I'll give it you anyway, because you'll hear it later and I'd rather you'd had it from me." He turned his palms a little further out. "Four terms, and I'll say them once and slowly, and then I'll go away and you can talk about it all night, which is what you're going to do anyway.

"One. We want the man. That's the whole of it — one man, walking, on his own two feet, out to us in the morning. Nobody's hands tied. He walks.

"Two. The rest of you go north. The road's yours. You'll have it to yourselves as far as Ferrow and I'll put that in a man's mouth at both ends so nobody gets clever in the night after.

"Three. I'll put four days of good ash in your cart for the old woman, off our own store, and a wheel-hoop, and a man to set it, because I can see that wheel from here and you'll not make Ferrow on it.

"Four." He let his hands fall to his sides. "If he doesn't come out, then in about a day and a half we'll do it the other way, and the other way is twenty-odd men on eleven miles of raised road with a cart on it, and I have got nowhere else to be until it's finished. I'm not saying that to frighten the children. I'm saying it because you'll do the sum yourselves in an hour and I'd rather you had my numbers than your own."

Nobody moved. Out in the reeds something went into the water with a small flat sound.

"You've not asked what he's done," Hoyle said, at last.

"No," said Wyck.

"Most people ask."

"You'd tell us a thing," said Wyck. "And then we'd have to stand here and decide whether it was true, and I don't fancy it."

Hoyle looked toward the voice for a second, and something in his face shifted — not respect exactly, but the small adjustment of a man adding a column he had not expected to have to add.

"Fair," he said. "Then I'll tell you the true one for nothing, since we're being plain. He's been in the same country as us for six years buying our chits and asking after us at every wall between here and the coast, and a man does not do that for six years over a debt. Whatever's between him and my section is old and it's deep and none of you were born for it. That's all I know and it's all I want to know, and I've been in this trade twenty-two years, and I'd say the same about him if he was standing here and I was in the cart."

"He is standing here," said Rook.

"I know," said Hoyle. "I've known since I came up the road. You're the only one who hasn't moved."

---

He went back down the road and the dark took him and Dessa tracked him out to thirty feet and said, "Gone," and then said, a moment later, in a much smaller voice, "There's another one at the edge. There's another one at the edge and I only got him because he moved."

"There'll be two," said Rook. "There always are."

Milo picked his light up off the road and it went out in his hands, and the dark came down over all of them properly.

Nobody suggested a fire.

Toren went and sat on the tailgate near his grandmother because it was the only place on eleven miles of raised road that had a wall to it, and after a while Senna put her good hand flat on his back between the shoulder blades and left it there, and did not say one word about any of it, which he was grateful for in a way he could not have said out loud.

Later, Milo came and sat on the other end of the tailgate with his knees up.

"He said he'd give her four days," Milo said.

"Don't."

"I'm not — I know. I'm not saying we should." He picked at the rag on his thumb. "I'm saying he knew to say it. That's what I can't get past. He came up that road and the first thing he had in his mouth was the exact thing we most want in the world and he'd got it *ready*, and he'd got it ready because somebody stood in the road this afternoon and looked in the cart bed."

Out on the water a bird went up, invisible, clattering.

Wyck said, from the dark by the wheel, where he had been standing without moving for half an hour, "Milo. Count the fires."

Milo turned round and looked south, and Toren turned with him.

They were down there on the road, a long way back, where the embankment came up out of the grazing: small orange points in a loose line across the width of the bank and a little way down the slope on both sides. Toren counted them twice and got nine both times.

"Nine," said Milo.

"Nine," Toren agreed.

Rook was standing on the crown of the arch with his back to them, looking the other way, north, up the empty road they had not gone up.

"Count them again in an hour," he said.
