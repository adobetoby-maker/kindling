# Chapter Eighteen

In the morning, he ran the drill.

Toren had spent a good part of the four hours he had actually slept preparing for a different day. He had worked out, lying on his back watching the sparks go, that things were going to be gentler now, and that there would be a period of some days in which everybody was careful with everybody, and that the man would be — he had not been able to finish the thought, but the word hanging about at the end of it had been *warmer*.

What happened was that Rook got up at grey light, put the fire out, said "Up. Line. On the flat by the gate," and ran them for fifty minutes, and it was the worst fifty minutes in a fortnight.

He took Toren's footwork apart twice. He made Milo do the light-on-the-ground thing eleven times, until Milo could set it down and step off it without looking. He told Wyck that his left hand was a liability and that he had four months to make it a merely poor hand and that he was going to be unpleasant about it every single morning until then, and Wyck said "Right," and did it again.

Milo, at the end of it, sitting on the gate with his knees up, said, "I thought it'd be different."

"Why," said Rook.

"I don't know. I thought — " Milo waved a hand. "After all that."

"After all that," Rook agreed, "there are still four of you on a road with a cart, and this country is worse than the country behind, and nothing I said at that fire puts one thing between any of you and what's out here." He slung his coat. "Knowing a thing and doing it while frightened are two separate skills. That hasn't stopped being true because I've had a bad night."

He went to get the mule.

Milo looked after him with an expression of enormous betrayal.

"He's exactly the same," he said.

"No, he isn't," said Dessa.

"He's — "

"He's exactly the same, and it's completely different," said Dessa. "Milo. He's not going to start being *nice*. He was never going to start being nice, and if he had, I'd be frightened. The difference isn't in him, it's that when he says something horrible now, we know where it comes from." She got herself up off the gate. "He'll be reserved and difficult for the rest of his life. That's fine. I'd just rather be difficult *with* somebody than at them."

---

She did it that evening, at the halt, and she did it entirely on her own, and nobody saw it start.

Toren was at the tailgate with his grandmother and heard Dessa say, from six feet away in the grass, in a voice with nothing in it at all:

"Rook."

He was up in about a second and a half.

She was sitting in the grass with her bad leg out and her hands open on her knees, exactly as she had sat on nineteen banks in twenty-eight days, and she had her eyes open, and she was looking at nothing, and her face had gone the way it had gone at the field wall — the face of somebody who has said a word in a language she does not speak and been understood.

"Don't get up," said Rook, who was already crouching.

"I'm not going to get up."

"What have you got?"

"Milo's behind the cart," said Dessa. "He's at the off-side wheel and he's crouched down doing the strap and he's got the mug in his other hand. Wyck's at eleven paces, straight up the slope, standing still. The mule's — the mule's further than I thought, the mule's at about twenty-six and it's eating. Senna's in the cart and she's sat up. Tor's at the tailgate and he's turned round now, he's turned round because I said your name."

Nobody moved.

"I can't see any of them," said Dessa. "I've got my back to the whole camp and I have not turned round once and I can't see a single one of them and I know exactly where they all are."

"How far does it go."

"Thirty feet," she said, "about. It stops. It doesn't stop like a wall, it stops like — it goes thin, and then there's nothing, and the mule's right at the edge of it, and I nearly missed him."

"How long have you had it."

"Since about four o'clock this afternoon," said Dessa, "and I didn't say anything, because I thought if I said it out loud, I'd check, and if I checked, it'd go out."

"That's exactly right, and it's the last time you'll ever have to do that on your own," said Rook. "Now stop talking about it for a minute. Sit there and think about the corn."

"The corn."

"The corn, the weather, anything. Something with no light in it."

She sat and thought about the corn.

After about forty seconds she said, "Toren's moved."

"Where."

"Four paces to his left. He's at the wheel. And Milo's stood up."

"Yes," said Rook. "You've got it."

---

"Right," he said. "Hand."

Dessa put her hand out, and Rook took her wrist and put two fingers on the inside of it.

Toren had seen him do that four times: to Senna, at a road cut, on the first night; to Senna again in the cart in the dark; to himself on a towpath after a sack of corn; to himself again at a tailgate with his arms not working. Four times, in twenty-eight days, always the same two fingers, always for about three seconds, always without a word of explanation.

This time he held it for a long while.

"Now," said Rook. "Last night I gave you a word and said I'd come back to it. This is it. This is a Reading, and this is what it is: you sit still, and you take honest stock of what's in you, from the inside, and you say it as a fraction, out loud, in a number a person can use.

"It isn't a mystery. Everybody at that station could do it by the second year. You do it when the plain word isn't enough — before a bad piece of road, after a bad night, when two people have to decide which of them is fit and neither of them wants to be the one who says. You do not do it in the middle of a fight. You do not do it to show off. And you never, ever do one on somebody else without asking, and I'm asking you now, eleven seconds after I started, which is the wrong order, and I'd like it noted that I know it."

"Do it," said Dessa.

"Say what you've got first. You go first, always. Mine's for checking, not for telling you what's true about you."

Dessa shut her eyes.

She sat with it for perhaps a minute and a half, which Toren, standing at the wheel, found almost unbearable.

"A fifth," she said. "Maybe a bit under. And it's — " She frowned. "It's not going down. That's the thing I can't say properly. It's been on since four o'clock, and it's been on the whole time, and it hasn't cost me anything, and I keep waiting for the bottom to come out of it the way it came out of Tor in the orchard, and it doesn't."

Rook let go of her wrist.

"A fifth," he said. "About that. Maybe a shade over, and the difference is you undercounting yourself, which everybody does for the first year, and which I'd rather have than the other way round.

"And it isn't going down because it's lit, not spent. That's the whole difference, and it's the one everybody has to be shown twice." He sat back on his heels. "Dessa Cole. That's Ember. That's the rank, and that's the word people will use about you now, and they'll be right, and I'd like you to hear the rest of it before anybody says anything else.

"Kindled is a door standing open. Ember is the first thing that stays lit when you stop looking at it. He got there on a towpath with a sack of corn on his chest" — a nod at Toren — "and that one got there on a press-house step without noticing he'd done it, and you have got there sitting in wet grass on nineteen separate evenings getting nothing, which is the hardest of the three by a distance, and the other two should think about that."

"It's not a light," said Dessa.

"No."

"It's not a wall and it's not a shape and it's not a — it's just *knowing where things are*." Her voice went slightly. "That's not anything. That's not a thing you can show anybody. I've been doing that from a bank and a press-house step for a fortnight, and everybody thought I was being useful."

"You were being useful."

"I want to know if it's real—"

"It's real, and it will be the most valuable thing in any party you ever stand in, and you will spend the whole of your life being told it isn't anything," said Rook, "by people whose stones make a noise. I told the one with the bar something like this on a press-house step eleven days ago, and I'll tell you the same, and then I'll stop.

"A man who can put a shape on the end of his arm is worth having. A person who knows where all six of you are in the dark, all the time, without looking, is worth the whole of the rest of the party, and everybody finds that out at about four in the morning on the worst night of their lives."

Dessa put the heels of her hands into her eyes.

"Fifteen days," she said.

"Twenty-eight," said Rook. "You started on a bank at nine minutes."

---

"Why now?" said Toren, later, at the fire. "Why today and not the day after, or a month?"

Rook did not answer straight away.

Dessa did.

"Because I stopped trying to prove it was mine," she said.

She had her hands round a mug, and she said it to the fire.

"That's all it was. I've been sitting down every night for a fortnight trying to find out whether the four seconds belonged to me or to his stone or to the weather, and every single night the entire exercise was a girl auditing herself. And then last night a man told me I was the third person he's ever seen do it and that one of the other two is dead and that I am, personally, on my own, sitting on a stone with a bad leg, the proof of the thing she died holding on to." She turned the mug round. "And this morning I got up, and it was just — settled. It's mine. Somebody who'd know said so. And then there was nothing left to check, and I sat down at four o'clock to do the nothing, and I did the nothing properly for the first time in my life, and it took about a minute."

Rook was looking into the fire and did not say anything for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "That's how it goes. It went like that for me, and it went like that for her, and nobody's ever been able to shorten it for anybody, and I've watched three people do it now, and it's the same every time.

"You can't reach it while you're still asking whether you're allowed to."

---

They met Ott on the thirtieth day, four miles short of a crossroads, coming the other way.

She had a different hand-cart and a great deal less in it, and she saw them from three hundred paces and stopped in the middle of the road and waited, which was, for Ott, an effusive greeting.

"Well," she said. "You're all still upright."

"Two Ember," said Milo, "and one more since Tuesday," and Dessa hit him in the arm.

Ott looked at Dessa for a second longer than she looked at any of the rest of them, and something in her went down about half a notch, the way it had in the yard at the Drum.

"Is that right," she said.

She had come from Barrow Gate. She had sold what Rook had poured into her cup, and she had sold it well, and she had spent four days doing the thing that salvagers do at a wall, which is drink one cup and listen to everybody.

And she said, forty minutes into it, with the kettle on and the cart off the road and Senna propped up in the back:

"Your lot are at Barrow Gate."

---

Rook did not move.

"Say it properly," he said.

"There's a company sitting in the north yard at Barrow Gate, and they've been there eleven days, and they're hiring." Ott poured. "They're not hiding. That's the first thing. They've got a table out and a man behind it with a box, and they're taking anybody who can stand up. Which means whatever they're going to do, they're going to do it with numbers, and they're doing it soon, because nobody feeds forty men at a wall for the fun of it."

"Cinder."

"Cinder," said Ott. "Chit and all. That's how I know — they pay the joining fee in brass on the table so a man can see it, and I watched four of them get handed out."

Rook took the piece of brass out of his coat.

He did it slowly. He turned it over once in his fingers and held it out across the fire, flat on his palm, and Toren realised that he was going to have to watch this happen and that there was nothing whatsoever he could do about it.

Ott took it. She got the lamp round and turned it to the light.

"Four letters and a six," she said. "TULL. That's a section — they're named off whoever runs them, it's how the pay's kept straight. Tull's sixth."

"Is Tull's sixth at Barrow Gate."

"Tull's sixth is *what's* at Barrow Gate," said Ott. "That's the name on the table. That's the name the man with the box says forty times a day."

She handed it back.

"And they break on the new moon," she said. "Everybody at that wall knows it because the yard's been hired to that date, and the weighmaster's counting the hours. They break, and they go, and nobody at Barrow Gate will know where, because they never do, and they've never once told anybody anything."

"When's the new moon?"

"Five days," said Ott.

---

Toren did the arithmetic in his head before anybody said anything, and got it wrong twice, and then got it, and wished he hadn't.

Rook did it out loud.

That was the thing Toren never forgot about the next ten minutes: he did it out loud, in front of all five of them and Ott, sitting on his heels in the road with a stick, and he did not go and stand at the top of a bank and do it with his hand over his mouth.

"Barrow Gate's east and a bit south," he said. "How far?"

"Three days at your pace. Two if you're daft and don't sleep."

"Two," said Rook. "I can do it in two.

"That's two days out. Then whatever happens at the other end takes as long as it takes, and I'd give it a day, and then it's three days back at a walk if I come back the same way, and I might not be able to come back the same way. Call it six days minimum and eight if it goes poorly, and it will go poorly, because it's forty men in a walled yard, and I'm one."

"You said you've been six years arranging this," said Wyck.

"I have."

"And that's the first time it's ever been a *place with a name in it*."

"Yes," said Rook. "It is."

He drew a line in the dust with the stick and put a mark on it.

"And here's the other column, and I'd like everybody to watch me do it, because this is the bit I'd usually keep.

"The cart makes ten miles on a good day. We are a hundred and fifty miles from Meridian on this road, and there is not a shorter one, and that is fifteen days if nothing goes wrong, and it always goes wrong, so it's eighteen.

"Six days of me at Barrow Gate is six days the cart stands still, because I am not sending four Kindled children and a sick woman up this road on their own, and none of you are going to pretend otherwise." He looked round at them. "So it's eighteen days, or it's twenty-four.

"And she had a bad night four days ago. She'll have another one. I can hold a gate for a night, and I have got about" — he did not open the flask; he did not need to — "about two of those left in me and in what I'm carrying, and after that there's nothing between her and the road but the road."

Senna said, from the cart, "Then go."

---

"I've listened to all of it," she said, "and I'd like my go, and I'll be brief because I get out of breath.

"Six days. I've had a great many six-dayses. You go and do your thing at this wall and we sit in a field and Wyck and the clever one keep the line and Milo counts, and if I die in that field, I will have died in a field, which — Toren, don't — which was always the likeliest thing that was going to happen to me from the morning we left Sallow Creek, and everybody in this family has known it for forty days and been extremely polite about not saying so.

"You have been waiting eleven years. You have got a name and a wall and a date, and you will not get another one, because you told us so yourself last night — you've been in the same country as them twice in six years, and both times you found a cold camp.

"So go. And I'd like it minuted that the old woman said so first, and without anybody asking her, and that she was not being brave, she was doing sums."

---

Rook stood up.

He stood in the road with the stick in his hand for about four seconds and then threw it into the hedge.

"No," he said.

"Now listen—"

"I've listened. I'm going to say why, and then it's finished, because if we have this every evening for a fortnight, it'll take something out of all of us we won't get back.

"It isn't about whether you'd survive six days in a field. You might. You'd probably die, and you might not, and if that were the whole of it, I'd weigh it, and you'd all hate me for weighing it, and I'd weigh it anyway.

"It's that six days is the number *if I come back*."

Nobody said anything.

"Forty men in a walled yard," said Rook. "I've been honest with you about everything else, so: I don't know what happens when I walk into that. I'd back myself at ten. I don't know about forty, and neither does anybody else, because nobody's ever tried it, because it's an idiotic thing to do.

"So the actual choice isn't six days against eighteen. It's this: she gets to a gate at Meridian, or there's a fair chance that in about four days there is nobody on this road who can hold a gate shut for one night, and four children push a cart a hundred and fifty miles with an old woman in it and nothing at all.

"I'm not doing it." He picked his coat up off the cart shaft. "And I want one thing understood, because Milo's face is doing something, and I'd rather kill it now. I'm not being noble. There isn't a noble version of this. What I am doing is choosing which of two things I want more, and I've chosen, and I'm going to be poor company about it for a while."

---

They made three miles that afternoon and camped early, and Senna had her bad night a day sooner than anybody expected, which Toren afterward thought was the cruellest possible arrangement of the facts.

Rook got up into the cart bed without being called and did what he did, and it took longer than it had taken four days ago.

Afterward he sat on the tailgate with the flat tin flask in his hand and unscrewed it and looked into it in the lamplight for a while.

Then he drank about half of what was in it, and poured the rest into Senna's cup, which is a thing Toren had never seen anybody do in his life and had not known was possible.

"Don't," said Senna.

"It's done."

"That's yours—"

"It's ash out of a road seam, and there's nine days of me in that flask, and I've just spent the lot," said Rook. "It buys her about four days of walking she wouldn't otherwise have, and it is the best use anything in my possession has ever been put to."

He screwed the empty cap down and put the empty flask back in his coat, and then he turned round and said, to the four of them standing in the dark by the wheel:

"Now the practical part, and I want it heard properly because it's about your lives and not about my feelings.

"For the next four or five days, I am worth roughly what Milo is worth. I can hold the stone. I can't do the running thing, I can't put up a shape that'll stop anything, and if something comes out of a hedge at us, I will be a man with a knife who is quite good with a knife.

"So the line is the line. Not me stepping through it. Dessa calls at thirty feet, and she calls first now, before Milo, and Milo calls what he can see, and Toren says it after him. Wyck's on whichever end the bad side is, and if I tell him to come off it, he comes off it. Nobody waits for me.

"You've had a month. This is the week you find out what it was for."

---

Toren went up the bank at about the fourth hour because he could not sleep and because he had known for six hours that he was going to.

Rook was sitting where he sat.

He was not facing north, up the road they were going. He was not facing south, back down the road they had come by. He was sitting turned about a quarter round from both, looking east across country in the dark, at nothing at all, at a wall three days away with a table in the yard and a man behind it with a box.

The brass chit was not in his fingers. He had it flat on his knee under the palm of his hand, the way you hold something down.

Toren sat down about eight feet away and did not say anything, which was a technique he had learned from Dessa, and which he had been practising for twenty-nine days.

After a long time, Rook said, "Five days."

"I know."

"Four, by the time we've done two more of these."

"I know."

"They'll go, and nobody will know where," said Rook. "That's the real cost, and I'd rather say it once out loud than have all of you watching me not say it for a fortnight. It isn't that I'm six days late. It's that Tull's sixth walks out of that yard on the new moon, and there is no wall between here and the coast where a man with a chit and a cup of ash can buy that name again, and the last time I got this close, it was six years ago, and the last time before that, it was never."

He turned his hand over on his knee, and turned it back.

"And I'd do it the same way tomorrow," he said, "and that's the part I can't get to sit down flat."

Toren sat with a cold disk against his breastbone and a warm stone in his coat pocket and thought about a man on a gravel bar who had known what the disk was in under a second and had not said, and about how there was still one thing in this camp that had not been put on the ground in front of everybody, and about how it was his, and how he was going to have to do it, and how he was not going to do it tonight.

"You'll get another one," he said.

"Probably."

"You'll get another one, and this time you'll have four people who can hold a line."

Rook did not answer that for a while.

"Go to sleep," he said, at last — and it was the same two words he had used on a bridge and at a field wall and on a gravel bar at midnight, and this time he moved over about a foot to make room on the bank, and did not say anything about having done it.

They sat there together in the dark facing east, the man and the boy, until the grey came up behind them on the road they were going to walk instead.
