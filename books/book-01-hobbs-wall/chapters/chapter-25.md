# Chapter Twenty-Five

The revetments at Hallam Cross were eleven concrete blocks, each about the size of a hay wain, set in a broken row along the foot of the embankment with grass grown up between them.

Rook had them put the cart in the gap between the fourth and the fifth, which was the narrowest, and then had them pull it through and put it in the gap between the fifth and the sixth instead, which was wider and which he liked better for a reason he did not explain until Dessa asked.

"Fifth and sixth has got the pier behind it," he said. "Fourth and fifth has got eleven feet of grass and then the open. If you're going to make a hole, make one with a back on it."

They made the hole. It took an hour and a half. Wyck got the mule out of the traces and walked him round behind the pier and tied him short to a bolt in the concrete, and stood there a moment with his good hand flat on Pitch's neck, because Pitch had been shaking since the flyover and had not stopped.

"He's been wrong since the culvert," Wyck said.

"He's been right since the culvert," said Rook. "We've been wrong. Leave him tied and leave him where he can't see out."

Senna they took out of the cart, which she hated, and set on a blanket in the angle where the sixth block met the embankment with the cart drawn across in front of her, and she made four separate remarks about it and then stopped, because she had run out of breath, and that was worse than the remarks.

"You'll want me quiet," she said.

"I'd want you eight miles north," said Rook. "Quiet's what's available."

"Elias."

He stopped.

"You've a hole through your back," said Senna Voss, "and I can hear it in your voice at forty feet, and I am going to say this once because I'm not going to get another clean run at it. Whatever it is you're about to do out there, do the version of it that has you in the road afterward, because these four have had one funeral and they are not — " she took a breath she had to go some distance for " — they are not good at them yet."

Rook stood with his hand on the cart rail.

"No," he said. "They're not."

Then he went out past the fifth block and put his back against it and slid down it onto the grass and sat there facing south, which was the direction Dessa had counted ninety-one paces in, and did not answer her, and that was the only answer anybody got.

---

It came at the eleventh hour.

The warning was Pitch. The mule went absolutely still behind the pier — not startled, not braying, still, the way a thing goes still when it has finished having opinions — and Dessa said, "Rook," from the top of the embankment where she was lying on her front in the grass with her chin on her forearms.

"Where."

"I don't know where. It's inside thirty feet."

"It can't be inside thirty—"

"It's *inside thirty feet*," said Dessa, "and I can see four hundred yards in every direction and there is nothing on any of it, and I want somebody to tell me which of those two things is the lie."

Toren got the spike out.

"Put it away," said Rook.

He put it away. It was the hardest thing anybody had asked him to do in a month.

They waited. The wrong light was doing its thing along the top of the embankment, and the iron of the cart tyre was warm at Toren's shoulder, and the grass between the blocks was moving in an ordinary way in an ordinary wind, and Toren counted to about forty.

Then the broken span of the flyover made a sound like a church bell being hit with a hand, once, and eighty feet of it came down.

It went down in a single piece, and it did not fall so much as arrive at the ground, and the noise was enormous and went on a long time in the flat country afterward, and the dust came out from under it in a grey wall and rolled over the revetments and over the cart and over all of them, and inside that dust Toren could not see his own hands.

He heard Wyck say something. He heard Dessa's voice above him on the bank going flat and clear and fast — "*north, north, forty feet, north*" — and then it changed, in the middle of a word, to "no — nine feet — nine *feet* —"

And then it was there.

---

Later, when he had to describe it to people who wanted a description, Toren found that the only honest thing he could say was that it was the size of the cart and Pitch together, and that it was the colour of ash before it is ash, and that it was put together by somebody who had never been told what the parts were for.

It came out of the dust at the fifth block low and fast with its head down. It had four limbs and they did not match. Three of them were legs — heavy, hooked, set wide, made for pushing. The fourth was a foreleg on the near side, and it was too long, and it was carried up and folded, and as it came it unfolded, and the whole length of it went flat and thin and hard and took an edge, and the edge came round at the height of a man's chest with the sound of a hand going through water.

It was not a blade. That was the thing Toren could not get people to understand afterward. A blade is a decision somebody made. This was a limb that had been put under pressure until it had nothing left to be except an edge, and you could see the places where it had not quite managed it, the flat spots, the places where it was still a leg.

It took the corner off the fifth concrete block.

Rook had got there in the two seconds of the dust the only way left to him, which was on his hands and one knee across nine feet of grass, and he put his left hand flat down on the concrete apron at the foot of the sixth block and said something under his breath, and the air in front of the cart went hard.

It was not a wall and it never had been. It was a piece of nothing standing across eleven feet of gap with light going through it in a way light does not, and Toren felt it open above him in his back teeth, and the thing hit it, and the thing stopped.

Everything on that ground came off the floor an inch. The cart went back six feet with the brake on and its wheels locked and gouged two trenches. Toren was flat on his back with the breath gone out of him and grass in his mouth and no idea how he had got there.

The thing stood in front of eleven feet of held air and put its head against it, and pushed, the way a bullock pushes a gate, with its three legs driving.

Rook was down on one knee behind the cart with his hand flat on the concrete and his whole arm shaking.

"NOW," he said.

---

They had drilled this. They had drilled it for four months, on towpaths and in orchards and on the crown of a road with a man shouting at them, and Toren was extremely surprised to discover that his body knew what *now* meant while the rest of him was still lying in the grass.

He was up. Wyck was already moving on the far side with the bar in his left hand, low, which was new and which was worse than it had been, and Dessa was calling from the bank — "Wyck, its off hind, Wyck, the *off hind*" — and Wyck put the bar into the back of its off hind leg where the hook of it folded, and the bar stopped dead, and the shock of it went up through a broken wrist and Wyck made a noise.

Toren came in at the near shoulder with the spike out, at the flat place behind the blade-limb, where a thing that size has a seam.

It had no seam. The spike went in about half an inch and turned, the way a knife turns on wet bone, and Toren's whole arm rang to the elbow, and he was looking straight up the length of that limb from four feet away when the thing decided.

He saw it. He saw it perfectly clearly and he had all the time in the world, and he read it exactly the way he had been taught for four months to read a thing standing still, and he was completely and catastrophically wrong.

The blade-limb went slack.

It dropped — the whole long flat weight of it came down off the horizontal and went loose at the shoulder, and it went quiet, and the edge came off it, and the end of it trailed in the grass.

*That's the wind-up*, thought Toren, with absolute confidence, because a Verge-hound feints stillness before it commits and everybody who had ever survived one knew it, because Rook had told them so on a bridge in the fog in the first week and had made them say it back. A thing that goes still is a thing that is about to go.

He stepped inside it to be under the cut when it came.

The cut did not come. The back end came.

There was no gather. That was the whole of it and it was over before he understood any of it — the three driving legs did not crouch and did not load and did not do any of the things that a body does before a body moves; they were standing and then they were at full speed, and the thing went past him so close that the wind of it turned him round on his heel, and it was not in front of him any more, it was thirty feet behind him, at the gap between the fourth and the fifth block, which was the narrow one, and which had nothing but eleven feet of grass and the open behind it.

And Wyck Tanner was standing in it.

---

Wyck planted.

Toren had seen him do it at the tailgate in the cut on the second day and at Coldwater and on the north side of the arch, and every single time, for four months, the world had been wrong about Wyck and Wyck had been right about the world. Two of them at full run and he was standing where he was standing. That was what he was for. That was the one thing he had, the fact that was not a job, the thing nobody had ever had to teach him.

He set his feet, and turned his hip, and got the bar across in both hands — in *both* hands, with a broken wrist and three fingers that could not feel a bar, because there was no other way to hold it — and he did not move, and he did not flinch, and he took it.

It threw him thirty feet.

He went back through the gap and hit the grass on his shoulder and rolled twice and lay in a heap against the foot of the embankment, and the bar went end over end into the dark under the flyover and rang on concrete somewhere out of sight.

Dessa screamed his name. It was the only time in four months that Dessa Cole made a noise she had not decided to make.

The thing came round in the gap. It did not turn like an animal turns. It stopped — and it did stop, it went from full speed to standing with nothing in between, the way it had gone from standing to full speed — and it stood over Wyck with its head down, and its blade-limb came up off the grass and went flat and hard and quiet and took the edge again.

And Rook shut the road.

He did it the second time from where he was, forty feet off, on one knee, with his hand still down on the concrete and his head down between his shoulders, and Toren felt it open the way you feel a window go up in another room, and eleven feet of nothing stood up across that gap between the thing and the boy on the ground, and the edge came down on it and stopped, and the stopping put a crack six feet long through the fourth concrete block.

Rook went over onto his hands.

There was blood coming through the back of his coat in a patch the size of a plate, and it had not been there an hour ago.

---

"*It drops one to pick up the other.*"

Dessa's voice came off the top of the embankment absolutely flat, the way it came when she was reading Milo's feet on a bank in the spring, and Toren, dragging Wyck by the coat collar with both hands and his heels skidding in the grass, heard every word of it.

"Say it again," said Rook, from his hands and knees.

"It drops one to pick up the other. It can't carry both." She was still on her front with her chin on her forearms and she had not moved once. "Watch the arm. Every time that arm goes slack and drops, the *legs* fire. Every single time. It's done it four times and it's done it four times the same. And when it's going to cut, the back half stops pushing — it settles, it goes soft behind, it quits. It went soft behind just now before it cut at Wyck and I didn't say it out loud because I wasn't *sure*, and I'm sure now, and I'm sorry."

"Don't," said Rook. "Say the rule."

"The thing that goes quiet is the thing that isn't coming."

There was about a second and a half in which nobody on that ground said anything, and Toren lay on his side in the grass with Wyck's collar in his fist and turned it over and felt the whole of the last four months slide about two inches sideways and settle somewhere else.

Because it was backwards. That was what made it so bad.

*Stillness is not disengagement. Stillness is the wind-up.* A man had said that to five children on a bridge in the fog in the first week and made them say it back to him, and then: *the side that goes quiet is the side that is coming. Always. Every time. It is the quiet flank, not the loud one.* And a Stilt stops in the open because it has to stop to choose, and the stop is the only window anybody will ever get out of one. Every single thing in this country that had ever tried to kill them had taught the same lesson three different ways: *watch the still part. The still part is where the next second comes from.*

And this one put down whatever it was not going to use.

"Toren."

"I've got it."

"Say it back to me."

"The thing that goes quiet is the thing that isn't coming," said Toren, "and the loud half is the half that's coming, and I stepped into a cut that was never going to happen because I've been taught by hounds."

"Yes," said Rook. "Now go and be right about it, because I can't hold that a second time and I'd rather you found out now than in nine minutes."

---

Toren went out into the gap.

He did it poorly and he did it anyway. He went out past the cracked fourth block with the spike out in his right hand and his left hand empty and nothing at all in his head except a girl's voice saying *the thing that goes quiet is the thing that isn't coming*, and he stood in eleven feet of grass in front of a thing the size of a cart and a mule and made himself look at the wrong half.

It came round.

The blade-limb was up and flat and hard and singing very faintly, and every instinct Toren Voss had ever been given said *that is the danger and it is about to move*, and he made himself look past it at the back end instead, which was standing loose with its three hooks down in the turf and no weight on any of them.

Loose. Not loaded. Quiet.

*The legs are the quiet ones. The legs aren't coming.*

He stood absolutely still, which was the hardest physical act of his life, and the blade came.

He did not step back and he did not step inside. He stepped *along* it, one pace to his own left, the way you step off a road to let a cart go by, and the edge went past his chest close enough to take the button off his coat and put eight inches of itself into the grass where he had been standing, and its own weight carried it through and over, and for about a quarter of a second the whole length of that limb was extended and rigid and out in front of it and the shoulder of it was open at the root.

Toren put the spike in.

It went in five inches this time, into the place where the leg stopped being able to decide what it was, and something in there came apart, and the thing screamed — it was not a scream, it was an entire flyover being hit with a hand again, at close range, in his face — and it wrenched back and took the spike with it and tore it out of his hand and threw him flat.

He rolled. He got up. His hand was empty and his palm was opened across the base of the fingers where the spike had gone out of it sideways and he did not notice that for another quarter of an hour.

But it was wrong now. Its near foreleg came down on the turn and it came down *flat*, on the flat of the limb, unable to fold, and it dragged, and it went round in a half circle in the gap with its head swinging, and it made no sound at all.

"Dessa," said Rook.

"It's still got the back half," said Dessa. "It's still got all of the back half. It hasn't lost anything that matters."

"No," said Rook. "It hasn't."

---

It broke off at the twelfth hour.

There was no reason anybody on that ground could see. It stood in the gap for a while with its ruined limb down and its head going from side to side, and Toren stood eleven feet from it with nothing in his hands and did not move, and it looked at him or at something behind him or at nothing.

Then the back end fired and it was four hundred yards away out on the flat, and then it was out of sight, and the grass where it had been standing was flattened in a circle and steaming slightly.

Nobody moved for a long time.

Then Dessa came down off the embankment on her backside because it was faster than the stick, and got to Wyck, and Wyck was conscious and swearing very quietly and continuously in a way Toren had never heard from him, and had a collarbone that was plainly the wrong shape under his coat, and had the use of exactly one arm now, which was the arm with the broken wrist in it.

Rook was still on his hands and knees. He had got about nine feet and then stopped.

Toren got him sitting up against the sixth block. Getting the coat off him was a four-minute job and at the end of it Toren had to put his hands on his own knees and look at the grass for a while, and then he packed the hole through Rook's back with the last clean linen in the cart while Senna, propped on a blanket eighteen inches away, told him exactly how to do it in a voice that kept having to stop.

"That's not four days," Rook said, at one point, to nobody. "That's not four days of walking. That's four days of lying down."

"Then you'll lie down," said Senna.

"Then we'll all lie down," said Rook, "in a concrete pen, four miles from a wall, with a Barrel on the flat that has learned this morning that we can hurt it. Have you ever sold ash?"

"No."

"A Barrel's what they call one of those. There isn't a weighmaster in this country who'd put a price on one, because it's never been anybody's problem to price — you'd want a wall to weigh it and a wall's got no use for it." He let his head back against the concrete. "There's four or five hundred pounds of good ash walking about out there on three legs. Ott would cry."

Nobody laughed, and it had not quite been a joke, and it had been about a quarter of one.

---

They took stock at dusk, because Rook made them, sitting in a row against the sixth block in the last of the wrong light.

Wyck had a broken collarbone, a broken wrist, three fingers he could not feel, and no bar, because the bar was somewhere under eighty feet of fallen flyover and nobody was going in there. He could walk. He could not carry, lift, push, or hold. Dessa's leg was what it had been. Toren's right palm was opened across the base of the fingers and strapped, and he had got the spike back out of the grass and could hold it and could not grip it hard, and it did not matter, because he had never been meant to grip it hard.

Rook could not stand for more than about two minutes and could not shut a road again in less than three days, and said so, out loud, without being asked, which was the second time he had done that since the Level and which frightened Toren more than the morning had.

Senna could not be moved at above a walk.

Pitch was alive and had chewed halfway through a tether rope that was made of hemp and would not chew through, and had done it in about forty minutes.

"Right," said Rook. "Here's the arithmetic and I'm going to do all of it out loud, because I have spent eleven years doing it in my head and handing people the answer, and you're none of you children and the answer's bad.

"It's four miles to Meridian's outer line. Call it four and a half to the south gate, and the last three of it is flat open pasture with a fence line and nothing else, because that's what a wall wants in front of it and Meridian's had sixty years to arrange it. There's no cover between here and there. Not a wall, not a cut, not a culvert, nothing. That's not bad luck, that's a defensive glacis and it's doing exactly what it was built to do, and it was built to do it to men."

"We go in the dark," said Wyck.

"It doesn't hunt by light and neither do we. We'd be four hours on that pasture instead of two."

"Then we go now—"

"We go at first light," said Rook, "at a walk, because the old woman sets the pace and I'm not going to sit here and invent a version of this where she doesn't. And I'll tell you the rest of it now so nobody has to have a clever idea at the third hour.

"It comes at worked power. All of them do. They come at it the way a dog comes at a whistle, and there is nothing in this world brighter to one of those than a man with his hand down on a road holding twelve feet of nothing shut, which is why it came through eleven feet of dust straight at my end of this pen this morning and not at the mule." He stopped and breathed for a while. "And I can walk four miles or I can hold once. I can't do both. So one of those is going to happen out on that pasture tomorrow morning, and the moment it does, it'll know where we are from three miles out, and it'll cross three miles in about the time it takes to say so."

Toren sat with his back against the concrete and his strapped hand open on his knee.

"I can read it now," he said.

"You can."

"I read it and I got inside it and I put the spike in the root of that arm and it *worked*, and I can do it again—"

"You can," said Rook. "Once. Standing still, in an eleven-foot gap, with a block behind you and eleven feet of grass in front, and me forty feet away with my hand on the concrete in case you got it wrong. Out on that pasture, in the open, with a cart, you'll get one cut read correctly and then it'll put the back half down and go, and you'll want to be holding the other thing, and you'll be one and a half seconds behind it." He turned his head. "That's not a failure. That's the number. That's what you've got and I built it with you and I'm not going to insult you by pretending it's bigger."

"Then what do I do?"

Rook looked out at the flat dark country where four or five hundred pounds of ash was walking about on three legs.

"You get some sleep," he said, "and in the morning I'm going to tell you the other thing."
