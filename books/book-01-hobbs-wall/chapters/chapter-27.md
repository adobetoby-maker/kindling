# Chapter Twenty-Seven

The gate took twenty-six minutes, and Toren could not decide whether that was cruelty or just what happened to a place above a certain size — whether it stopped having the sort of insides where the difference could still be found.

They counted it because Dessa counted it. She sat down on the wet road at the ninety-foot mark with her leg out and her boots full of ash and said, "Right, I'm timing this," in a voice with something badly wrong in it, and then she timed it, out loud, in minutes, the whole way.

At eleven minutes a second lamp came on over the gate.

At nineteen a man came up onto the berm who had not been there before, in a long coat, with nothing in his hands, and looked at them for some time and then went away again.

At twenty-six the gate opened, and what came out of it was not help.

Four people came out in sealed grey coats with the hoods up and the faces behind flat panels, moving at a fast walk in a line abreast, and they went straight past the tailgate with Senna Voss on it and straight past a boy sitting in the road who could not stand up, and they went to Dessa's cooking pot.

"Ma'am, I need you to step away from that."

Dessa looked up at a panel with her own face in it.

"It's *ash*," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"It's eleven pounds of lifted ash, it's — there's four hundred pounds more of it in a road three miles back, there's a *year* of it out there, it's—"

"Yes, ma'am, and I need you to step away from it, and then I need your boots."

---

They took her boots. They took the pot and Marta Faust's flask and Rook's flask, and put all of it into a lidded bin with a clamp on it, and hosed the six of them down where they stood, in the rain, in the road, including the old woman on the board, who bore it without one word and afterward remarked that it was at least warm, which was true and which nobody else had noticed.

Then, and only then, two of them went back through the gate and came out again with a stretcher, and did the thing that Toren had been walking three hundred miles toward for four months, which was that a woman in a grey coat knelt down in a road beside Senna Voss and put a hand on her wrist and said, "All right, my love. What year did it happen?"

And Senna said, "2130."

"Where were you?"

"Inside the fence line. Eleven days."

"Which fence line?"

"The old plant at Caul Hill. I was — " she took the breath she had to go some distance for " — I was fifty-three and I went in for the copper, and I knew what was in there, and so did every other soul on that crew, and we went in anyway because it was copper."

The woman in the grey coat wrote it down. Then she said, "You've been carried a long way, haven't you," and Senna said, "By a great many people who could have done something else," and that was the last thing Toren heard her say for two days.

They took her through the gate at a fast walk with the stretcher level and one of them holding a bag of something up above her in the rain, and the gate shut behind them, and Toren sat down in the road in the shadow of a green berm and put his forehead on his one good arm and made a sound he was not proud of.

"That's it," said Rook, from where he was sitting. "That's the whole of it. That's all of it done."

"They took her—"

"They took her *in*, and there were proper instruments in that woman's hands, and she asked the right question first and she asked it in the right order, and I have been frightened for four months that this place wouldn't be what it said it was." Rook had his head back against the berm and his eyes shut and the rain going onto his face. "It's what it said it was. Everything that happens from here is a different kind of problem and I'll take every single one of them."

---

The reception yard was a pen of poured concrete forty feet by twenty with a drain in the middle and a bench along one wall and a hatch in the far end with a window in it.

They were in it for two hours and it was the warmest two hours any of them had had in a fortnight, because there were pipes running along the wall behind the bench, and the pipes were hot, and nobody had told them the pipes would be hot. Wyck put his back against them and shut his eyes and said, "Oh," in a completely undefended voice, and did not speak again for twenty minutes.

There was a light on in that yard. A real one, in a fitting, behind a cage, on a wire — a small flat white light that did not move and did not flicker and had no flame in it at all, and Toren looked at it for a long time with his arm in a sling somebody had given him, and thought about a road eleven miles south of Sallow Creek four months and a lifetime ago, and two boys arguing about whether the lights at Meridian were in the streets or only in the buildings.

"Wyck."

"I've seen it."

"You said only in the buildings."

"I'm aware of what I said," said Wyck Tanner, with his eyes shut and his back against a hot pipe. "I'd like to point out that this *is* a building."

"It's a yard."

"It's got a roof at that end."

At the far end there was a hatch, and behind the hatch was a room with more light in it, and in the room was a man at a desk with a book.

His name was Ivor Hallet. He gave it at once, unprompted, the way you give a thing that is going to be on a document anyway, and he was about forty-five, and he had a grey cardigan on under an oilskin and a pen on a string round his neck, and he was not unkind at any point in the next two hours, and Toren disliked him with a clean and total intensity for approximately eleven years.

"Right," said Ivor Hallet, through the window. "I'm going to do this in the order it's got to be done in, and I'd ask you to let me, because if I do it out of order I have to start it again and that's another hour for all of us. Persons in party."

Nobody said anything.

"Persons in party," said Ivor Hallet, pleasantly. "How many of you are there."

"Five," said Toren.

"Five."

"Five," said Toren again, and heard his own voice do something, and Dessa put her hand flat on his back between the shoulder blades the way Senna did, and left it there.

Hallet's pen stopped for about a second and a half. He did not ask. He wrote *five*, and went on, and Toren understood dimly that the man had seen that particular pause on that particular question a very great number of times.

"Names and ages, starting at your left. Names as you'd want them written."

"Toren Voss. Thirteen."

"Dessa Cole. Fifteen."

"Wyck Tanner. Fifteen."

Rook said, "Rook."

"That's not a name, sir."

"It's the one I've used for eleven years."

"It's the one you'll be called," said Hallet. "It isn't the one I can write. This is not a trick and I'm not asking you for a reason. I've got a book and the book has got a column and there's nothing in it I can put *Rook* in."

There was a silence in the reception yard that went on a little too long.

"Elias Marsh," said Rook.

Ivor Hallet wrote it down. Then he stopped writing. Then he turned back a very large number of pages in a very large book, and went down a column with the pen held above the paper the way a man does when he already knows, and found the place, and looked at it.

"Elias Marsh," he said.

"Yes."

"Out of Lowry Bend."

"Yes."

"You were at this gate on the fourth of the twelfth month." He gave the year, and it was eleven years ago. "You were nineteen and you had no fixed settlement and you gave a statement over two days concerning a research station and a company of men, and you were issued a transient card and a docket number, and the file says *referred*." He looked up. "Do you want to know what it was referred to?"

"No," said Rook.

"That's your right."

"I found out at the time," said Rook. "I stood at a window at the top of a stair for four hours on the second day and a very polite young woman told me the word means it's gone to the right place. And it had. That's the part nobody ever believes. It had gone to precisely the right place and it is still there." He had not moved and his voice had not changed at all. "Write the name. I've no quarrel with you, Mr Hallet, and I'd like that in the book as well if you keep a column for it."

"I don't," said Ivor Hallet, "and I would if I could."

---

Then they did the other thing, and the other thing took an hour.

"Declaration of capability," said Hallet, and laid four buff cards on the ledge inside the window, and did not push them through. "This is where I'm required to tell you exactly what this is before you fill one in, and I'm required to tell you that you may decline. So. Meridian keeps a register of Kindled persons inside the line. It's not a secret register, you can go and look at your own entry any Tuesday, and there's about eleven hundred names in it. If you're Kindled and you're inside this berm, you're expected to be on it.

"What goes on the card is your name, your rank if you've a word for it, and a plain description of what you can actually do. Not what you'd like to do. What you'd do at four in the morning if a wall came down.

"What you get for it is a residence band with a red thread in it, which gets you the same as everybody else's band and also gets you through the north yard, which nobody else can use, and which is where the work is.

"What it costs you is that you notify. If you go up a rank you tell this window within seven days. If you leave the line you tell this window. If you're going to do anything on a public street that a person who isn't Kindled would find remarkable, you tell this window first, and I'll tell you honestly that nobody does, and I'll tell you equally honestly that the two times in nine years that somebody really needed to have done, it went extremely poorly for them at a hearing."

"And if we decline," said Dessa.

"Then you're transient, and transients get fourteen days conditional and a grey band and no north yard, and there's a review, and I don't sit on the review and I've never been told how it goes."

"That's not a choice," said Dessa. "That's the same thing said twice with a bad face on one of them."

"Yes, miss," said Ivor Hallet. "It is. I'd write that down too if there were a column for it."

Wyck said, "Does she get treated either way?"

Hallet looked at him.

"The old woman," said Wyck. "On the stretcher. Does she get treated either way, or does she get treated if we sign these."

"She gets treated either way," said Ivor Hallet, and something came into his voice for the first time. "Her admission's a clinical matter and it went through at the gate and it is nothing to do with this window and nothing to do with any of you, and if anybody in this station ever tells you different, you will come back down here and you will say that to me, and I will make it the worst fortnight of that person's life. That's not procedure. That's mine."

"Right," said Wyck.

He took a card.

---

They filled them in on the bench with the pipes hot behind them and a paper cup of tea each, which was the first tea any of them had had that had not been made in a road.

Wyck wrote for a long time and then crossed most of it out.

"What do I put," he said.

"What you do."

"I don't *do* anything, that's the whole of the — " He stopped. He looked at his own strapped hand, and flexed the three fingers that could not feel anything, which he had done four hundred times a day for four months. Then he wrote one line and turned the card over so nobody could read it, and only years afterward, in a different room, did Toren find out that it had said *I don't fall over*, in Wyck Tanner's flat square hand, with nothing else on the card at all.

Dessa wrote hers in about nine seconds. *Dessa Cole. Ember. I know where people are, to about thirty feet, without looking, in the dark, all the time. It does not cost me anything.* She read it back once and added, at the bottom, *I limp,* which nobody had asked for and which she declined afterward to discuss.

Rook wrote four words and stopped.

Toren, sitting next to him with a sling on and a card on his knee, watched a man who had answered every question put to him for a month look at a buff card for the better part of a minute.

"Say it," said Toren.

Rook made a sound.

"You made me do it about nine hundred times," said Toren. "Say the whole question."

"The whole question," said Rook, "is whether I put down what I can do, which is the true answer and which will put a man in a coat in this yard within the hour, or whether I put down less, which is a lie in a book, and I have been telling that particular lie since I was nineteen years old and I have never once decided it was the wrong thing and I have never once felt clean about it either." He turned the pen over. "And the reason I can't do it this time is that I've spent a month telling four people to say the thing out loud even when it's awful, and one of them died still doing it, and I'm not going to sit in a concrete pen and be the only one who didn't."

He wrote it.

Toren did not see the card. He saw the length of time it took and he saw Ivor Hallet read it through the window and put it down and read it again, and then look up, over the top of it, through the glass, at a man sitting on a bench in a wet coat with a hole through his back.

"Mr Marsh," said Ivor Hallet.

"Yes."

"I'm going to have to have somebody else look at this."

"I know," said Rook. "Take your time. I'm not going anywhere at speed."

---

Toren's card said: *Toren Voss. Ember. One shape. It's out at present and I don't know when it's coming back.*

He looked at that for a while and then folded his hands over it, and Hallet, who missed nothing, said, "That's a true card and I'd rather have it than a good one," and took it, and did not ask.

Then Hallet turned to the last page of the day's sheet, and the tone of the room changed by about half a degree, and Toren — who had spent four months learning to read weather off people — felt it and did not understand it.

"Two more and then you can go and lie down," said Ivor Hallet. "These aren't from me. These came down from the yard office while you were on the bench, and I'm going to read them as they're written, because that's fairer than me putting them in my own words.

"One. *Has the party any knowledge of the Riftspawn presently ashed at grid marker south nine?*"

"We killed it," said Dessa.

Hallet wrote it. "*Two. Who did.*"

Nobody spoke.

Rook, on the bench, did not move and did not look at anybody, and it occurred to Toren afterward that the man had given them a rule in a road and had then sat in a concrete yard and let it run without touching it, because a rule you touch is not a rule.

"The question's got a name on it," said Ivor Hallet, after a moment. "I'll read that as well, because you'd have it out of me sooner or later. *The party reports a boy of thirteen. Confirm.*"

Toren put his hand flat on his knee.

"I did," he said.

The pen did not move.

"Say again?"

"I killed it," said Toren Voss. "I put an Ember into the root of its arm in a ditch beside the road at about the third hour this morning and it came apart. Wyck held the mule. Dessa called it from the tailgate with her eyes shut. He shut twelve feet of road across my grandmother twice and that's the only reason she's on your stretcher and not in that pasture." He found that his voice was perfectly level, which surprised him. "It's four hundred pounds of ash out there and you can go and weigh it and it's yours, and I'd like somebody to go and get the mule off the road, because he was nine hundred pounds of extremely disagreeable animal and he pulled that cart for three hundred miles and he has a name."

Ivor Hallet wrote all of it. It took him some time. He did not ask a single follow-up question, which Toren noticed, and which he did not understand for another four days, when it was explained to him that a man at an intake window does not ask follow-up questions about a thing that is going to be asked about again by somebody else.

Then Hallet put four paper bands on the ledge and pushed them through, and three of them had a red thread run through the paper.

"Conditional admittance," he said. "Fourteen days, reviewable, and the review's a formality in about nine cases out of ten. Yard C, second row, hut eleven — there's four beds in it and a stove and the stove works. The bathhouse is open till the tenth hour and there's no queue at this time of year because nobody comes in in the autumn.

"And there's three lights on the main street and they're on all night, and I'm told people find that hard to sleep next to at first, so put the blanket over the window and don't be proud about it."

---

Wyck stood in the middle of the main street of Meridian at about the eighth hour that night in a borrowed coat with his arm strapped across his chest and looked up at a light on a pole.

It was not much of a street. It ran two hundred yards between long low buildings with their roofs turfed over and their windows small and deep, and there was a drain down the middle of it, and there were three lamps on poles, and the light they put down was flat and white and steady and did not move at all, and under each of them a perfect circle of wet ground was lit up the colour of bone, and between them it was dark.

"It's three," said Wyck.

"It's three," Toren agreed.

"I said only in the buildings."

"You did."

"There's three of them," said Wyck, "in the street, in two hundred yards, and between them you can't see your hand, and that is not what either of us meant and I want that on the record."

"Nobody's keeping a record."

"Everybody in this place is keeping a record," said Wyck Tanner, "and I've been here four hours."

---

Toren went and stood outside the long building at the north end at about the eleventh hour, because Dessa had found out where it was and told him without being asked, which was the kindest thing anybody did for him that week.

He could not go in. There was a woman at a desk inside the doors who said, without any malice whatever, that visiting was in the afternoons, and that his grandmother had come in at the second hour with an exposure history of twenty years and was under Doctor Sowerby, and that Doctor Sowerby would speak to the family in the morning, and that no, she could not say more than that, and that yes, he could stand outside if he wanted to, and there was a bench.

So he stood outside, and then he sat on the bench, and the rain stopped at about the twelfth hour.

Rook came along the wall at some point after that. It took him a while to get there and he did not make anything of it, and he lowered himself down onto the bench in three separate movements and sat with his hands on his knees.

They were quiet for a bit.

"I answered it," said Toren.

"You did."

"You told us not to say anything."

"I told you not to say anything until somebody asked a question with a name attached to it," said Rook, "and a man in a window read your name off a sheet and asked you directly, and I sat nine feet away and let it happen, and I'd do it again." He rubbed his thumb once across his knee. "I'd have lied. I want you to know that, because you'll hear a version one day where I'm wiser than I am. I had the lie ready. I'd have shaved it — *there was a man on the road, we came on it after* — and it would have held for about a fortnight and then it would have come apart in front of somebody who mattered, and the difference between what they think of us now and what they'd have thought of us then is a year of everybody's life."

"Then it was right."

"It was right and it was expensive," said Rook. "Both. It's nearly always both, and people who tell you otherwise are selling something.

"Because here's what's in that book now. There is an old woman in that building being treated, and there's four of us on a register, and somewhere behind us there's four hundred pounds of the best ash anybody at this wall has seen in ten years lying in a road that their own people won't go out to in the dark. And on a sheet in a drawer it says that the thing that made it was put down by a boy of thirteen who has been Ember since the spring." He looked up at the long dark roofline. "They're not frightened of what came out of the Sag, Toren. They've got a procedure for what comes out of the Sag. They've had one for sixty years."

The door of the long building opened and shut somewhere down the wall and somebody crossed the yard with a lamp and went in again.

"They'll be frightened of us," said Toren.

"They'll be *careful* about us," said Rook, "which is a colder thing and it lasts longer, and it'll be in writing, and it'll still be in writing in ten years when everybody who wrote it is dead." He got his breath. "I've been trying since the gate to think of something to tell you that isn't that, and I can't, so we'll have the last one instead, and then I'm going to go and lie down for four days.

"You got her here. That's done. Nobody can take it off you and it's not conditional on anything and it's not reviewable in a fortnight, and whatever it turns out to have cost — and it's going to cost, and I don't know the number yet either — it is the only thing any of us set out to do, and it is done, and you did it."

Toren sat on a bench outside a long low building with electric light coming out from under the door in a flat white line, with his left arm broken and his right hand strapped and the back of his leg stitched by a person who had used a needle made for it, and a dead stone in his coat pocket, and a cold disk on a boot-lace against his breastbone, and he could not think of one single thing to say.

So he did not say anything, and Rook did not say anything either, and the two of them sat there until the last lamp on the main street went off at the first hour, which Toren learned the next day it did every night, because Meridian turned its lights off at the first hour, and always had, and had a written reason for it.
