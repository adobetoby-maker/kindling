# Chapter Three

His father held the bend for two minutes.

Toren knew afterward that it had been about two minutes, because he had been counting, and he never stopped being able to count it. Two minutes. He would meet people, later, who assumed it must have been longer, and he never corrected them, because correcting them meant explaining that he had been counting.

Two minutes is a long time.

Garrick Voss went into the bend with a brush axe, which is a farm tool with a hooked head on a four-foot haft, made for cutting saplings off at the root, and he cut. He did not do anything fancy. He put his back against the rock on the eastern side so nothing could get behind him, and he worked the axe in short flat arcs at the height of a man's knee, and the husks came at him one and two at a time because that was all the width the bend allowed, and they went down at the knee and he stepped on them.

Toren stood eight feet behind him and to the left with the crooked spike out, and killed the ones that got past, and there were not many, and he was so frightened that his hearing had gone strange — everything arriving a half second late and slightly too loud.

"Wheel!" his father shouted, without turning. "*Wheel!*"

Wyck and Milo had the cart moving again. Dessa had Pitch. Senna was flat in the bed with the blankets over her and one hand knotted in the side rail.

"Toren, get on the far side of the cart — *far* side —"

He got on the far side. He didn't want to. He did it because his father's voice had a thing in it that made your legs obey before your opinion arrived.

Which is why he was on the western side, with the whole width of the cart between him and the guardrail, when the husk came over the rail into the cart bed on top of his grandmother.

Senna did not scream. She got her arm up. She was seventy-three years old and there was almost nothing left of her and she got her arm up between it and her throat, and it opened her forearm and her shoulder and then Wyck hit it off her with a length of rebar he had been carrying since Sallow Creek for exactly no reason at all, and it went over the side, and Wyck hit it again on the ground, and then twice more after it had stopped moving.

Marta came up the side of the cart. She had one hand out behind her holding the wall and she put the other flat on Senna's shoulder and the blood slowed. It did not stop. It slowed, the way water slows when someone upstream shuts a gate part of the way.

"That's all I have," she said. "Milo. Milo, look at me. Press here. Both hands, all your weight, and don't lift them to look."

"Mum —"

"Both hands."

She said it perfectly calmly. Then she turned around to face back down the cut, because her wall had gone thin enough to see through, and she was the only one who could hold it, and she was standing on the road with her boots planted and her back to the guardrail.

Nothing came around the wall. They came over the rail.

Toren saw it happen and it was three steps away and he could not get there. He would go over those three steps for the rest of his life and there was never any version of them where he got there.

Marta Faust went down under it and the wall went out like a lamp, and Milo made a sound that Toren had no name for and did not let go of his grandmother's shoulder, because his mother had told him not to.

---

Then the noise from the north changed.

It came down the cut ahead of them: a series of hard flat cracks, spaced, like a stone skipping — but they were not on the road, they were coming down the *wall*, high up, and Toren looked up and saw a man come off the eastern rock face forty feet above the road.

He did not fall. That was the first wrong thing.

He came down the face in three long strides, and each one hit the rock and pushed off it, and the pushing off made the flat cracking sound. There was something on his legs — struts, a frame, thin bright lines of it running down the outside of each thigh and shin like a splint made of light. He crossed the last thirty feet of open air in a single flat leap and landed in the middle of the road with his knees bent, square in the middle of the ones that had spilled past Garrick out of the bend and were coming down on the cart.

And there was a blade in his right hand.

Toren stood there with a crooked grey spike sticking out of his own fist and the whole bottom fell out of his understanding of the world.

*One at a time.* His father had said it that morning, and Sallow Creek had said it for sixty years, and it was not a rule anybody argued about because it was not a rule, it was a fact, like water being wet: an Ember made a weapon or it made a stride, and it made one, and while it was making one it was not making the other. Toren had watched his father do the change-over ten thousand times. It took a breath. It took a *breath*, and in the breath you were a man standing in a field with a grey stone in his hand.

The man on the road was doing both, at the same time, and he was doing them the way a person breathes with both lungs.

Nobody said his name because nobody knew it. He put the blade through the nearest husk and stepped forward onto the next one and the struts on his legs fired and he was eleven feet up the road, and the blade came round, and the shape of it was different — longer, thinner — and Toren thought, *he changed it while it was moving*, and could not make that thought go anywhere.

The husks did what husks do. They went at him, all of them, straight and fast and without any interest in what had happened to the one in front.

He killed eleven in about nine seconds and it was not impressive. That was the strangest part. There was nothing showy in it at all. He killed them the way Bram Sark had split them, economically, at the knee and the hip, only faster and without ever quite stopping moving, and his face while he did it was the face of a man clearing a blocked drain.

Then he went past them the other way — south, back down the road, against every instinct Toren had about which direction safety was in — to the place forty paces behind the cart where a slab of the eastern wall had come down some winter long ago and spilled across the road until only half its width was left. The pinch. Every one of the thirty coming up from the south had to come through it.

He stopped in it, and put his left hand flat down onto the road bed, and said something under his breath.

The pinch shut.

Toren felt it go. Not saw — *felt*, in his back teeth and in the new open door at the bottom of his own head that had been there since morning. It was the same wrongness Marta's wall had been, the same place where the road stopped being available, except that Marta's had been a wagon-width and this one filled the whole pinch from rock to rail, and it did not thin, and it did not flicker.

Everything still coming up from the south hit it and stacked against it and stayed there, shoving at nothing, three and four deep, as stupid as water.

*He did Marta's thing,* Toren thought. *He did Marta's thing and he did my father's thing and he did them in the same minute and he isn't even looking at us.*

"You," said the man. He had not turned around. "Boy with the Ember. What's ahead of you? North. In the bend."

Toren's mouth didn't work.

"The bend. How many. Answer me."

"I don't — it was full. My father's in there."

"Alone?"

"Yes."

The man said nothing for a second, and his left hand stayed flat on the road, and his arm had begun, very slightly, to shake.

"Then get the cart north and go through the bend and don't stop in it," he said. "I can hold one end. Do you understand what I'm telling you? *One end.*"

Toren understood it perfectly and instantly and he would think about it for years.

"*Dessa!*" he shouted. "North — get him moving north, *go!*"

---

His father came out of the bend.

He came out at a run with the axe down at his side and half the haft slick and dark, and he was moving badly, and Toren saw the reason before he understood he was seeing it: the whole left side of Garrick's coat was wrong, hanging heavy and open, and there was too much of it.

"Move them," Garrick said, going past. "Move them, Tor."

"Dad —"

"*Move them.*"

The bend was open. That was what his father had spent two minutes buying, and Toren did not understand until much later that his father had been standing in it after it was already won, on legs that had stopped being able to carry him anywhere else, waiting for somebody to come and take the road off him.

The cart went for it. Dessa had Pitch's head down and was hauling him bodily and Wyck was at the wheel and Milo was still in the bed with both hands on Senna's shoulder and his face turned away from where his mother had been, and Toren ran alongside with the spike out and killed one more, badly, on the third try.

Garrick did not go with them.

He turned around in the road and set his feet, and Toren, looking back, saw why: eleven or twelve husks had come through the pinch before the stranger shut it, and they were between the wall and the cart, and there was nothing to hold them but the man with his hand pinned flat to a road bed forty paces the wrong side of them.

The cart was still forty paces short of the bend. That was the whole thing. Forty paces of cracked road bed.

"Hold —" the man on the road said, to no one, to himself, to his own shaking arm. "Hold, hold —"

Then he said, sharply: "Boy. Look up."

Toren looked up.

They had started climbing.

Not all of them — most of them were still stacked against the shut place in the pinch, shoving at nothing, as stupid as water. But six or seven had gone at the eastern rock face where it was broken and stepped, and they were going up it in that terrible unhurried way they had, falling and not minding, and the top of the shut place was only the top of the pinch, and above the pinch there was open rock.

"They'll come over," said Rook — except he was not Rook yet, he was a man with his hand flat on a road. "Two, maybe three. They'll drop onto this side. I can't reach them."

"Then let go and—"

"If I let go of this, everything behind it comes through in one go and the cart is still on the wrong side of me." He did not turn his head. His left arm was shaking properly now, all the way to the shoulder. "I have a blade and I have this and I have two hands, and I have already spent them both. So it's yours. Two of them. On this side. Say yes."

Toren stared at the back of the man's head.

"Say *yes*, or say no and I'll drop it now and we'll all find out together."

"Yes," said Toren.

The first one came off the rock about fifteen feet up and landed badly, on the road, eight paces behind the stranger's braced boots — past the shut place, past the narrow place where Garrick was, past everything, with nothing between it and the cart but Toren.

Toren went at it before it finished getting up, and did not think about the shape, and put the crooked spike through it under the arm the way he had the first one, and it came apart, and he had one enormous and entirely unearned second of believing he was the sort of person this happened easily for.

The second one landed on him from behind.

He went down hard on his front with the whole weight of it across his shoulders and the spike went out — *out*, gone, a grey stone in his fist under his own chest — and he could not get his arm free and could not roll and he had time to understand, with awful precision, that this was going to be how it happened.

Then the road cracked.

Rook let go of the wall.

Toren felt the shut place open above him the way you feel a window go up in another room, and there was one flat report of the struts and the man crossed nine feet without seeming to travel through any of them, and the weight came off Toren's back, and Rook's hand was already going back down toward the road bed before the thing had finished falling.

It was a second and a half. Perhaps two.

Four husks came through the open place in that second and a half. They went up the road on the rail side at a dead sprint, past the stranger, past Garrick's shoulder — because Garrick could only stand in one place and had chosen the place that mattered — and they did not stop for any of it, because the cart was the nearest living thing and the cart was what they wanted, and Toren rolled over on the road and screamed a warning that was too late to be a warning.

Wyck Tanner met the first one at the tailgate with a length of rebar and did not step back.

The second one he took across the knuckles. The third one Dessa turned Pitch into, deliberately, a nine-hundred-pound mule pivoted on a cheek strap by a fourteen-year-old girl who had spent twelve days learning exactly where his feet went, and the husk went under and did not come out.

The fourth one reached the cart bed and got one arm over the side rail before Milo Faust — who had not lifted his hands off Senna's shoulder, who did not lift them then — kicked it in the head twice with both heels and screamed at it until Wyck got there.

And the pinch shut again behind all of it, wall to rail, and stayed shut.

"*Now*," the stranger said, through his teeth. "Cart. Bend. *Now.*"

The cart cleared the bend.

Toren came through behind it and turned, and forty paces back down the road his father was still standing in the narrow place with the brush axe up, and forty paces beyond him the shut place held from wall to rail with the whole south side of the cut stacked against it, and between the two of them there was a stretch of cracked road bed with eleven or twelve husks in it and one man.

He did not look back. Toren wanted that later and never got it. Garrick Voss stood facing the wrong way with his shoulders set and swung the axe at the knee, and again, and again, and the fourth time he was slow, and after that the bend came between them and Toren could not see him.

---

It ended the way those things end, which is to say it ran out.

They came at the man's line until there were no more of them coming, and he took his hand off the road and let it drop and killed the last nine on the near side without appearing to hurry, and then the cut was quiet in a way that had nothing in common with the quiet at the top of it.

The bodies went to dust.

Toren had heard about that. Everybody had heard about that — it was the one thing every story about Riftspawn agreed on, that they did not leave bodies. Seeing it was different: the husks sagged where they lay and lost their edges and came apart into a fine drifting grey that did not blow away, that pooled and ran down the camber of the road like water finding a low place. Where enough of it collected it thickened into something with a shine on it, a slow slick of it in the seams of the old road bed, and it made Toren's back teeth hum the same way the wall had.

The man walked back down the line of it with a flat tin flask and crouched at each of the good seams and drew it up into the flask with two fingers, unhurried, the way you'd skim cream.

He did four before he said anything.

"Anyone still bleeding?"

"My grandmother," Toren said. "She — she's —"

"Show me."

He came to the cart. Up close he was not old, but he had the look of somebody who had been outdoors continuously for a decade: burnt across the cheekbones, greyed at the temples, a coat that had been repaired by whoever was nearest at the time. He put two fingers on Senna's throat, and then lifted Milo's hands off her shoulder without asking, looked, and put them back.

"Good," he said to Milo. "You did that right. Keep doing it exactly like that."

Milo nodded. Milo did not appear to be entirely present.

The man took a grey slab out of his coat pocket — an Ember, ordinary, worn smooth on the corners, and it looked a shade small in his hand — and set it flat against Senna's collarbone, and put his other palm over the wound, and shut his eyes.

Nothing happened that Toren could see. Senna's breathing changed, that was all. It went from the shallow snatching thing it had been to something slower and deeper, and some of the grey came off her face, and after perhaps a minute the man opened his eyes and looked considerably worse than he had a minute earlier.

"That's not fixed," he said. "That's borrowed. She needs a bed and she needs it in days, not weeks." He put the Ember away. "And that's not the thing that's actually killing her. Was she sick before today?"

"Reactor," Toren said. "Years ago. We're taking her to Meridian."

"Of course you are," the man said, and something in his voice closed.

He straightened up and looked at them properly for the first time, and Toren watched him count.

One. Two. Three. Four. And an old woman in a cart.

He looked down the cut to the south, at the two shapes on the road and the third one back around the bend that none of them had gone to yet. He looked north up the empty road. He looked at the sky, which had maybe two hours in it.

"Where are the rest of your people?" he said.

Nobody answered him.

"Your other adults. Your — there's a settlement behind you, there's somebody. Who's coming for you?"

"That was them," Wyck said. He was leaning on the cart with the rebar still in his fist and his knuckles were split and he sounded absolutely level, which was worse than shouting. "That's all of them. That's all there was."

The man stood in the road with the light going and rubbed his hand hard down over his mouth.

"No," he said.

He said it quietly, and not to any of them. He said it the way a man says it to a door he has just heard open somewhere behind him in a house he was sure was empty.

Then he turned around and walked south down the cut, past them, past the wheel ruts, to where Marta Faust was, and Toren watched him crouch down and straighten her arms and put her coat over her face, and go on to the next one, and do the same.

Milo made a small noise. Dessa put her arm around him without letting go of the mule.

Toren stood in the middle of the road with his father's Ember gone cold and slack in his fist and the disk hanging under his shirt where nobody could see it, and did not go with the man, and did not go around the bend, and could not make his legs do either thing.
