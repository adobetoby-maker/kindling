# Chapter Thirteen

Wyck did not ask in the morning.

Toren woke on the twenty-third day with the whole of the night still in him and lay under the blanket for a while doing nothing but waiting for it, the way you wait for a tooth to be pulled once you have sat down in the chair. He had it planned out. He knew where he was going to stand. He had decided, somewhere around the fourth hour of not sleeping, that whatever happened he was going to stay in it and not walk away and not say anything clever, because there was going to be a moment where somebody said something clever and made it worse, and he had a strong suspicion about who that somebody was going to be.

Wyck got up, rolled his blanket, put a pan on, did the thing with his left hand where he flexed the fingers four hundred times, ate, and said nothing at all except "there's tea in that if you're quick."

By the third hour of the march, Toren understood that he had been an idiot, and that he was going to have to wait, and that waiting was worse.

---

They found it at midday, and they found it because Rook turned off the road for it.

He had been walking out ahead all morning — thirty or forty yards, which he did when the country opened out — and a little after noon, he stopped in the middle of a perfectly ordinary stretch of hedged road and stood there with his head turned to the left, and then went through a gap in the thorn and did not come back for four minutes.

"What's he doing?" said Milo.

"Standing still," said Dessa, who had the mule's head.

"He's been standing still for—"

"I can count, Milo."

When Rook came back through the gap, he did it without hurrying, and he came up to the cart and put his hand flat on the shaft, and said, "Bring it in. Mule off the road, behind the thorn, and Senna stays in the cart."

"Is it—" Milo started.

"It's nothing that's going to move," said Rook. "All four of you. Come and look at this properly, because I'm not going to get a better one to show you."

---

It was a camp.

It lay in the field behind the hedge, in the corner where two hedges met, which meant two walls and two open sides, and the moment Toren came through the gap, he understood that he was looking at something somebody had chosen rather than something somebody had settled for.

Twelve sleeping places. He counted them twice. They were pressed into the grass in two rows of six, all of them the same length, all of them the same distance apart, with a clear lane down the middle — and the grass had come up in them, a little, but only a little, and it had come up bent.

There was a fire. One fire, in a pit, with the turf lifted off in a neat square and stacked beside it to go back on afterward, which somebody had not got round to doing.

There was a latrine trench, cut straight, forty paces downwind, with the spoil in a ridge along the side of it.

There was a picket line strung between two thorn stumps with the rope gone and the wear still on the bark.

And at the far corner, where the ground was flattest, there was a patch of field about the size of a barn floor where the grass had been trodden out of existence entirely, down to bare earth in places, and beaten into a pattern — two long parallel lanes and a wide worn arc at one end of them — that Toren looked at for about six seconds before his stomach went over.

He had been standing in that shape every morning for fifteen days.

"Yes," said Rook, watching him. "Go on."

"It's a line."

"It's a line. Say what else."

"They drilled." Toren's mouth was dry. "They drilled here. That's — that's a line and that's where the man walked up and down in front of it."

"That's where the man walked up and down in front of it," Rook agreed.

---

He took them round it in a slow circle, and he did not touch anything, and he made them not touch anything, and he talked the whole way in the flat handing-you-something voice.

"Four days," he said. "Maybe five. The turf's dried at the cut edge, but it hasn't curled, and the trench hasn't fallen in at the lip, and there's been rain in the last week that the trench has had and the fire hasn't, because the fire's got a crust on it that a shower would have broken. Four days. If anybody tells you they can do closer than that off a cold camp, they're selling you something.

"Twelve down. Not twelve men — twelve *lying down*. There'll have been two up at any hour, and they'll have slept in shifts, so call it fourteen or fifteen. Nobody's dug a second trench, which means they weren't here a week. Nobody's built a second fire, which means nobody was allowed to.

"And they went that way." He nodded at the gate in the far hedge, at the top of the field, and the country beyond it that went up and away from the road in long dun folds. "North and a bit east. Off the road on purpose. They came out of the road, and they went back to it eight miles on, is my guess, and they did that so nobody would count them at a gate."

Dessa said, "How do you know they went and didn't come?"

Rook stopped.

He looked at her the way he had been looking at her since a fire twelve nights ago, which was one part longer than he looked at anybody.

"Say why you asked."

"Because everything else you've said is about how many and how long," said Dessa, "and that one's about which way, and it's the only one you've said a guess about, and you don't guess out loud." She had both hands in her coat pockets and her weight off the bad leg. "So either you don't know, or it's the one you care about."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Out in the field a pigeon went up out of the thorn and clattered away.

"Gate's rutted going out and not coming in," said Rook. "Two carts, out. And the grass at the field gate is trodden flat on the inside and standing on the outside, because fifteen people leaving a field in the morning walk out of it in a bunch and fifteen people coming into a field at dusk come in one at a time, looking about them."

"So they're in front of us," said Wyck.

"Yes."

"We've been watching the road behind us for six days."

"Yes," said Rook, and that was all he said about that.

---

The straw coat was at the top of the drill ground, and Toren did not see it until the end, because it had fallen over.

It was on a post that had been driven into the earth and then worked loose by whatever had been done to it, and it had gone down sideways in the grass: a coat, an ordinary coat, stuffed with straw and old sacking, with a rolled bundle of the same tied above the collar to make a head, and a length of split ash lashed across the shoulders for arms.

There were cuts in it.

Toren crouched down in front of it before he knew he was going to. The cuts were not everywhere. That was the thing he could not stop looking at. There was almost nothing in the straw arms and almost nothing in the belly of the thing. Everything that had been done to it had been done in three places: a band across the front of the thighs, a band around the outside of the upper arm and the shoulder, and the throat, and the throat had been opened so many times that the sacking had come away from itself entirely, and the straw was out.

"Don't touch it," said Rook.

"I wasn't."

"You were going to put it back up."

Toren took his hand back. He had been going to put it back up.

Milo said, in a voice that had gone thin, "Why those bits? Why not the — " and then stopped, and went slightly grey, and said, "Oh."

"Say it," said Rook.

"Because that's where you are," said Milo. "That's where — the arm's got the bar in it, and the leg's got you standing on it, and the — "

He did not finish.

"It's where a man keeps what he's using," said Rook. "That's all of it. Nobody in that coat's been hit in the chest because hitting a man in the chest is what you do in a story. They've been at the legs, the arm that holds the thing, and the neck, and they've been at them for as long as it took to wear a post loose in the ground, and there were fifteen of them taking turns.

"Now listen to me, because I'm going to say something I've been putting off for about a fortnight.

"Everything I have taught the four of you since the road cut is about meeting a thing that is not a person. Every bit of it. Husks come at you; hounds pick a side and can't take it back; the long-legged ones go in straight lines and have to stop to choose another. Those are all the same lesson wearing three coats, and the lesson is: *it cannot change its mind about you.* That's the whole of it. That's why it's teachable in a fortnight, and that's why you're alive.

"A person can change his mind about you. He can change it four times while he's crossing the ground, and he can decide halfway that he doesn't want you at all, he wants the mule, and he can turn round and start again tomorrow, and he can have friends whose entire job is to be somewhere you have stopped looking."

He stood up.

"So from tonight I'm going to teach you the other thing," he said. "And it's going to be unpleasant, and I want you to understand in advance that being unhappy about it is correct."

---

They made camp early, three miles on, in a fold of ground with a bank on two sides and no long clear sightline anywhere, and Rook ran the drill before the light went instead of after.

It started ordinarily. He put them on the line. He walked up and down in front of it.

Then he said, "Wyck. Come out here and be the man."

"Be the what?"

"Come and stand here."

Wyck came out and stood where he was put, six feet in front of Toren, with his bar in his hand, and Rook stepped back out of it and said, "Now take it off him."

There was a pause of about a second.

"Take what off him?"

"Anything. His stone, his coat, the spike, whichever one of those you can get. He's going to stop you. You're going to try. Nobody puts anything in anybody's face, and nobody goes for a head, and if either of you does it, we stop for the night, and it'll be a poor night." Rook folded his arms. "Go on."

It was the single strangest ninety seconds of Toren's life, and it went in three distinct stages.

For the first twenty seconds, it was nothing. It was two boys shuffling at each other in a field with everybody watching, and Milo laughed once and then stopped laughing on his own, and Toren got the spike out and held it low and pointed away, because he was not going to put a length of lit anything near Wyck Tanner, and Wyck knew that, and both of them knew that both of them knew it.

For the next thirty seconds, it was a game. Wyck went left, and Toren went with him, and Wyck came back and got a fistful of Toren's coat, and Toren twisted out of it, and it was — and Toren hated this afterward more than any other part of it — it was *fun*, for about eight seconds, the two of them grinning at each other in the grass like a pair of dogs.

Then Wyck said, "Sorry," quite quietly, and hooked his boot behind Toren's ankle and put him on his back in the wet grass and knelt on his chest and took the Ember out of his coat pocket.

He did it in about a second and a half. There was no part of it Toren could remember afterward as a decision.

The spike went out. Toren lay in the grass with the whole weight of another person on his sternum and his own stone in somebody else's fist above him, and the sky behind Wyck's head, and something happened in his chest that was not fear and was not anger and was in fact both of those things trying to occupy the same space, and he heard himself make a sound he had never made before.

"Off," said Rook.

Wyck was already off. He was already three feet away and up and holding the Ember out, flat on his palm, the way you offer food to a horse, and his face had gone a colour.

"I didn't—"

"Sit down, both of you."

"I *said* sorry, I said it before I—"

"I heard you say it," said Rook. "Sit down."

---

They sat down. Nobody else said anything. Milo had not made a sound since the laugh.

Rook crouched on his heels in front of the pair of them and put his forearms on his knees.

"Right," he said. "Toren. You had a lit Ember in your hand, and you were on your back in nine seconds. Why."

"Because he's twice my—"

"No. Try again."

Toren sat in the wet grass with his stone back in his hand and his heart still going and thought about it properly, because that was the thing this man did to you, he made you do the arithmetic while your hands were still shaking.

"Because I was waiting to see what he'd do," he said.

"Go on."

"It's — you taught us to watch. Watch the lines, watch the stillness, watch where the loud side isn't. You watch it and then it comes and then you meet it." His voice was unsteady. "And he came and I was still watching, because I was waiting for the bit where I find out which one it is. And there isn't one. He just — he did it. He did it while he was still deciding to do it."

"Yes."

"Everything you've taught us is *wait and read*."

"Yes," said Rook. "And against anything that comes out of a rift, it is the whole of the craft and it will keep you alive for the rest of your life and I would teach it to you again tomorrow in exactly the same order. And against a man, it will get you killed on your back in a field, because a man is not showing you a tell. A man is showing you whatever he thinks will make you stand where he needs you to stand.

"A hound is honest. Hold on to that. Every single thing in this country that isn't a person is honest with you — it wants you, it comes, it has one idea and it can't have a second one. That's why I could teach you it in eight days.

"A man will say *sorry* first." He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "And he'll mean it. That's the part you won't believe until you've had it done to you twice. He'll mean it, and he'll do it anyway, and the meaning it is not the lie, the meaning it is how he gets close enough."

Wyck said, from beside Toren, "That's a horrible thing to say."

"Yes."

"You made me do that."

"I did," said Rook. "And I picked you because you're the only one here who'd have been sorry about it, and I wanted him on his back with somebody sorry on top of him rather than the alternative, which is that the first time it happens to him, the man kneeling on his chest is fourteen stone and isn't."

---

He worked them for another hour, and it did not get better.

He taught them to stand where a person had to come past something to reach them, which was the same lesson as the trunks in the orchard and was not the same lesson at all, because a man would go *round* the tree and use it, and would put his friend on the other side of it. He taught them that four people standing in a line were four people a man could count, and that the first thing a man did was count you. He taught Milo — and this was the worst part, and Toren watched Milo's whole face go through it — that the light in his hands made him the most important person in any dark yard in the world and the most obvious, and that a person, unlike everything else they had ever met, would come for the light on purpose and knowing why.

"Do I stop?" said Milo. "Do I stop doing it?"

"No."

"Then what do I—"

"You put it down," said Rook. "On a stone, on a stump, on the ground, ten feet from where you're standing, and you stand in the dark next to it. That's all. That's the whole answer, and it took me three years to learn it, and a man showed me in about four words."

"Which man?"

It was out of Milo's mouth before he heard himself say it, and Toren felt the entire camp go still around it, the way a room goes still.

Rook picked up his coat.

"That's enough for tonight," he said.

---

They ate late and badly. Dessa had the fire small, and Senna went to sleep early, and afterward the four of them sat round it in a shape that had got wrong somehow, and Toren could not work out what had moved until he looked at the ground and saw it.

They had all sat down facing outward. Not one of them had done it on purpose. Milo was turned a quarter to the hedge, and Dessa had her back to the bank, and Wyck was sitting where he could see the gap they had come in by, and Toren realised that he himself had chosen his stone because it was the one you could see the top of the field from, and that he had chosen it without any thought at all.

Fifteen days ago they had sat in a ring looking at each other's faces.

Wyck said, to the fire, "He knew that camp."

"He's seen camps," said Dessa.

"He didn't read that camp, Dessa. He *recognised* it. He came through the hedge and stood in it for four minutes on his own before he called us in, and I want to know what he was doing for four minutes."

"Counting sleeping places," said Milo.

"It takes four seconds to count twelve of anything."

Nobody answered that.

Toren looked across the fire at Wyck and found, to his considerable alarm, that Wyck was already looking back at him, and that the look was not angry, and was not asking permission either.

"Tomorrow," Wyck said.

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." He put another stick on the fire and did not take his eyes off it. "Your gran's asleep."

Up on the bank, in the dark, the man who had spent the afternoon teaching four children how to be hurt by people sat with his back to the camp and his face to the north, which was new, and which nobody mentioned, and which every single one of them had noticed.
