# Chapter Sixteen

They waited two hours and eleven minutes, and it was the longest two hours and eleven minutes of Toren Voss's life, and afterward he could have drawn a map of that fire from memory: every stone in the ring, the exact place where the turf had scorched, the piece of unburnt sacking at the edge that nobody had moved for three days.

Nobody lay down. That was the first mistake, and they made it immediately, and Dessa caught it about twenty minutes in.

"Milo, get under your blanket."

"I'm not going to—"

"You don't have to sleep. Get under it. He's going to come down that bank, and if all four of us are sitting bolt upright at the third hour like a row of owls, he'll turn round and go back up it, and we'll have spent our one go on nothing."

So they lay down, which was worse, because lying down in a blanket with your eyes open and your heart going is a thing that has no end to it.

Senna, from the cart, said, "Are you going to tell me what this is, or shall I go on guessing?"

"Gran—"

"Because I've got to about my fourth guess," said Senna, "and they're getting steadily better, and if any of you would like to head me off, you've got about an hour."

Nobody headed her off.

"Mm," said Senna, and settled, and did not sleep either.

---

He came down at the third hour, as Dessa had said he would, because the fire needed feeding.

He did it the way he did it every night: came down the bank at the side where the grass was short and came into the ring of light from behind the cart, so that he arrived at the fire from the direction no one was watching, and crouched, and reached for the stack of sticks with his left hand.

And stopped.

He did not straighten up, and he did not turn his head. He stayed exactly where he was, crouched on his heels at the edge of the firelight with his hand out over the woodpile, and Toren, lying with his cheek on his rolled coat eleven feet away, watched the man's eyes go round the camp in four movements, like somebody counting coins.

Milo, sitting up, blanket round his shoulders, on the near side.

Dessa, on the low stone, with her bad leg out, on the fire's other side.

Wyck, standing — he had got up about a minute before, without being told to, and he was standing by the cart wheel with the bar in his left hand and his weight even, and he was standing in the gap between the fire and the road.

Senna, up against the boards with the blanket round her, awake, watching.

Rook put the stick on the fire.

"Who set this," he said.

Nobody answered.

"That's not a complaint. It's a good line." He fed the fire a second stick, deliberately, and pushed the ash in with the side of his boot. "Four of you, an old woman at my back, the one who won't be moved standing where the road is, and the one who can't run sitting down where I'd have to step over her. Nobody's blocking anything. Nobody's raised a voice. If I walk out of here, I have to do it on purpose and in front of everybody, and afterward it'll have been a thing I did."

He stood up.

"Dessa," he said.

"It was all of us."

"It was all of you. It was your shape." He put his hands in his coat pockets. "Right. Go on, then."

---

Milo Faust got to his feet with the blanket still round his shoulders, which ruined about half of the effect and possibly saved the whole evening.

"We're not going to ask you anything," he said.

"That's a novelty."

"Wyck said he'd had his go and he's keeping to it, and Dessa said if we ask you a question, you'll answer the question and then the answer will be a door shutting, and she's right, because you're extremely good at that, and everybody here knows it." Milo's voice was going up and down like a boy carrying something heavy across uneven ground. "So we're not asking. We're going to tell you a thing. That's all this is. It'll take about a minute and then you can do whatever you want."

"Go on."

"You said a name this afternoon."

The fire ticked.

"Twice, sort of," said Milo. "The first bit was — you said something about a gate, and you said get up, and that bit was bad, and I'm not going to say any more about that bit. And then you said a name. And you said it the way you say something to somebody who's standing right there."

Rook's face did nothing at all.

Toren, watching it from eleven feet away with his cheek on his coat, had seen that face do nothing twice before: on a gravel bar at midnight, four feet from a disk on a cord, and across a low fire from a girl with a warm stone in her hands. Both times, afterward, something had changed that could not be put back.

"You listened to a sleeping man," said Rook.

"Yes."

"You stood eight feet from somebody who couldn't stop you, and you took something off him."

"*Yes*," said Milo, and his voice cracked straight down the middle of it, "and I've felt sick about it since about one o'clock this afternoon, and I'd do it again, and that's the worst part, so you can say the rest of it if you want because I've already said all of it to myself."

Rook did not say the rest of it.

---

"I'm going to tell you my thing now," said Milo. "That's the whole of what this is. Nobody asked me to, they don't know I'm going to.

"My mother's name was Marta Faust."

Toren heard Dessa's breath go out.

"She died in the cut on the second of the month holding a wall up so that a woman she'd known for six weeks could keep breathing, and you came and put her arms straight and put her coat over her face, and you were the only person in the world who saw her do it and lived, and eight days later at the cairn, you told me what she did, and you said it like reading a weight off a scale.

"And I want you to know that for eleven days after that, I could not say her name out loud." He was crying now, in the entirely unembarrassed way he had, and he did not stop talking. "Eleven days. I said *her*. I said *my mum* twice to Dessa in the dark, and then I couldn't do it again for a week. And it wasn't because I'd forgotten her. It was because the second I said *Marta* out loud in a field with people listening, she'd be a thing that had happened, instead of a thing that was still — "

He stopped and wiped his face with the blanket.

"And then one night Toren's gran asked me what she was like when she was young, and I said *Marta was*, and it came out, and it was awful, and it was the first minute since the cut that I wasn't carrying her on my own."

Rook was absolutely still.

"That's it," said Milo. "That's the thing. I'm not asking you who she is, and I'm not asking what the gate was. We're not owed it, and I know we're not.

"I'm telling you that we've got room. That's all. Whatever it is, and however bad, there's four of us and an old woman, and we've all got a hole in us the size of somebody, and there is *room here*, and you've been sitting on a bank sixty feet away every night for a month like a man who thinks there isn't."

---

Rook turned round and walked out of the camp.

He did it without a word. He went past Wyck, who did not move and did not try to stop him and was standing in exactly the place where a man leaving would have to acknowledge him, and Rook acknowledged him — he looked at him, one clear look, and then went on out past the mule into the dark, sixty paces, eighty, until the firelight gave up.

Milo sat down where he had been standing, straight down, as though his legs had been taken away, and put his forehead on his knees.

"That's it, then," he said.

"That's not it," said Dessa.

"He *left*—"

"He walked eighty paces. He's been walking eighty paces since the twelfth night. If he wanted to leave, he'd have gone up the bank for his kit first." She was already getting herself up off the stone, awkwardly, the bad leg last. "Tor. Come with me."

"Dessa—"

"I'm not going to ask him about himself," said Dessa. "I'm going to ask him about me. And you're going to come and stand ten feet behind me where he can see you, and you are not going to say one single word, and if he tells you to go, you're going to stay, and I want you to understand before we walk out there that I'm using you as a witness on purpose, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise afterward."

"Why do you need a witness?"

"Because he's about to answer something," said Dessa, "and I'd like there to be two of us who heard it."

---

They found him standing in stubble with his back to the camp.

There was a quarter moon and enough of it to see the shape of him by. He did not turn round when they came up, and he did not tell them to go away, and Dessa stopped about six feet short of him and stood there with her hands in her coat pockets, and Toren stopped where he had been told to stop, and stood there, and did not say one single word for eleven minutes.

"I've got a question," said Dessa. "It isn't about you."

"Everything's about me tonight."

"This one's been about me for fifteen days, and I've asked it once, and you said *go to sleep*." She let that sit. "The night at the wall. When you put your stone in my hands and it went warm and I sat there for four seconds that felt like half the night. I asked you what it was, and you said it wasn't the stone, and I said *then what was it*, and you went out into the dark and stood at the top of a field wall with your hand over your mouth for about an hour.

"So. What was it."

Rook did not answer.

"I'm going to tell you what it's been like," said Dessa, "and then I'll stop.

"I sat on a bank every night for two years in a kitchen where nobody could tell me why my door went nowhere, and then for another twenty-six on the road. I did four seconds once. Since then I have sat down every single night, in the wet, with my leg out, and got nothing, and I have been perfectly cheerful about it in front of everyone, and I want you to know that I have not been cheerful about it for one minute of one evening.

"And I don't know what it was. That's the whole of it. I don't know if I did something or if I was a bit of ground that lightning happened to go through. And you're the only person alive who saw it and knows the answer, and for fifteen days you've said *don't chase it*, which is good advice, and *it wasn't the stone*, which is four words, and nothing else — and you answer everything now. You answer Toren about roads. You answered Wyck about the water end of a towpath in about nine sentences.

"You answer everything except that."

She took one hand out of her pocket.

"Why is my four seconds the one thing you won't talk about?"

---

Rook turned round.

Toren was ten feet behind, and the light was bad, and he could not see the man's face properly, and he was, for the rest of his life, grateful about that.

"Because you weren't the first one," said Rook.

Dessa did not move.

"Say that again."

"You weren't the first one I've watched do it." His voice had gone somewhere Toren had never heard it go. "I have seen it happen to exactly three people in my life, including me, and the other two are dead, and one of them was — "

He stopped.

He stood in the stubble in the quarter moon with a sentence unfinished in the air, and Toren, standing ten feet back with his hands at his sides, thought with a horrible lurch: *he's done that before. He did it in the lamplight at the windbreak and he did it in the lane and he's going to do it now.*

"Go on," said Dessa.

"No."

"You've started—"

"I know what I've started," said Rook.

And then, after a long moment, in a much smaller voice:

"You're asking me a fair question. I want you to know I've heard that it's fair. It's the fairest question anybody's put to me since I came down that rock face, and you've waited fifteen days to ask it twice, and you're owed it, and I'm not going to give it to you in a field in the dark because — "

He made a gesture with one hand, a short one, the kind a man makes when the words are not going to be there.

"Because I can't," he said. "Not the way you're asking. I've tried it in my head about forty times since the Drum, and it doesn't come apart into pieces. If I tell you what your four seconds was, I have to tell you how I know, and if I tell you how I know, I have to tell you where I learned it, and who from, and what happened to her, and what happened to the place, and who did it, and what I've been doing every day for the eleven years since, and why there's a piece of brass in my coat that cost me half a flask.

"It's one thing. It's all one thing. There's no version of it that's just the answer to your question, and I've been standing on a bank every night for a fortnight trying to find one, and there isn't one, and I've known there isn't one since the field with the twelve sleeping places in it."

Dessa said, very quietly, "Then say the whole thing."

"Yes," said Rook.

---

Toren did not, at first, believe that he had heard it.

He stood in the stubble with his hands at his sides and his ears going and thought, *he said yes, that was a yes, that was the word yes,* and had a wild lurching moment of wanting to run back to the fire and shout it.

Rook walked past both of them.

He got about four paces and stopped and stood with his back to them.

"Not because you set a line," he said. "I want that said, and I want it said to all of them, because if the four of you go to sleep tonight thinking that the way to get a thing out of me is to arrange the ground, you'll try it again one day on somebody who'll oblige you, and it'll be the last thing you ever arrange.

"You didn't get it out of me. It's been coming out of me since a woman at a burnt fuel relay said a name over a bag of apples, and it's been getting worse every day for ten days, and this afternoon I went over sitting up against a cart wheel in the middle of the day like a drunk, which has not happened to me since I was twenty. I have been going to have to say it since the Drum.

"What the four of you have done is decide I don't have to do it on my own. That's different, and it's not nothing, and I'd rather you were proud of the right thing."

He went on toward the fire.

"Tonight?" said Dessa, behind him.

"Tonight," said Rook. "Build it up. It's going to be a long one and I'm not doing it in the cold."

---

They came back into the light, and the other three were exactly where they had been left, and not one of them had said a word, and Milo's face when he saw all three of them coming was a thing Toren thought about for years.

Rook went to the woodpile and began, without any comment at all, to build the fire properly — the way none of them had built it in twenty-seven days, because wood was time and time was miles: the ring opened out, the big stuff broken down, a proper flat bed laid and the sticks stood up round it in a cone the way you do it when you intend the fire to still be there in four hours' time.

Wyck came and crouched down and started handing him sticks, and neither of them said anything about it.

"Milo," said Rook, without looking up.

"Yes."

"Your mother had a flask in her kit, and I told you to take it, and I never once asked you whether you had."

"I did take it," said Milo. "It's in the bottom of the bag under the blankets. I've never opened it."

"Go and get it and put it by the fire," said Rook. "You won't need it. I'd like it there."

He stood up and looked round at the five of them: the boy with the blanket and the wet face; the girl on the stone with her leg out; the one standing by the wheel who had not once sat down; the old woman up against the boards in the cart with her eyes bright and completely awake; and Toren Voss, thirteen years old, with a cold disk on a cord under his shirt and both hands empty.

"All five at once," he said. "That was the term. That's what you're getting."

He sat down on the ground with his back to the cart wheel, which nobody had ever seen him do at a fire, and put his forearms across his knees.

"Sit down," said Rook. "And don't start with a question, because I'll answer it, and then it'll come out in the wrong order, and this only comes out in one order, or it doesn't come out at all."
