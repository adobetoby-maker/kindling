# Chapter Twenty-Eight

Doctor Sowerby said the worst thing first, on purpose, and Toren decided inside four minutes that he liked him better than anybody else he had met behind that wall.

He was small and bald and in a hurry, and he came into the ward at the ninth hour on the third day with a folder under his arm and four chairs already put out by somebody else, which meant the number of chairs had been decided in advance by a person who knew how many of them there were, and Toren noticed that and did not like it.

"Right," said Doctor Sowerby. "I'll do this in the order I always do it in, which is the bad thing, then the good thing, then the bill, and I've been told I do it too fast, so stop me.

"The bad thing is that we cannot fix her. I want that said in this room with her sitting up in it, because in about a fortnight somebody in a corridor is going to say the word *treated* to you and you'll hear *cured*, and then you'll be angry with me in six months and I'd rather be got out of the way now. There is damage in this woman that was done in eleven days in 2130 and it has had twenty years to go on being done, and it is not coming out. Her marrow is failing. It has been failing for about nine years and whatever was keeping her upright on that road was not medicine."

"It was a flask of road-ash and a man with a hole in his back," said Senna, from the bed.

"It was, and it should not have worked, and there's a paper in it for somebody who isn't me.

"The good thing. We can take out what's still in her that's still doing harm — that's a course, it takes about twenty weeks, it's unpleasant, and it works; we've run it two hundred and eleven times and I've got the numbers in this folder and you can read them. And we can hold the marrow up from outside. Not replace it. Hold it up. That's a transfusion and a course of something we make here, on a cycle, and the cycle is eleven days, and it doesn't stop.

"And with those two things, this woman is not going to die this winter, and without them she was going to die within about five weeks, and I'd have given you six."

Nobody in that room said anything at all.

"And then the bill," said Doctor Sowerby, "and I'm going to let her ask for it, because it's hers."

"Say it," said Senna Voss.

He said it. It took him about ninety seconds and he did not soften one word of it, and when he had finished he asked her whether she wanted him to stay in the room, and she said no, thank you, and he went, and the door shut, and the four of them sat on four chairs that somebody had counted out.

---

"Right," said Senna. "I'm going to say the whole of it once, properly, with all of you here, because that is the rule in this family and I did not make it and I'm not going to be the first one to get out of it.

"And then I should like nobody ever to say it for me again, in any room, to anybody, for the rest of my life. If somebody asks, you send them to me. I have a great deal of time now and very little to do with it.

"First. I'm going to live." She let that sit. "That's the biggest one and it's first and nobody in this room is going to take it off me by being sad in front of me before I've finished. Three days ago I was five weeks from dying in a cart, and I am not, and four children and a man carried me three hundred miles to make that true, and if any one of you looks at your boots while I'm saying this I shall be extremely rude.

"Second. I'm going to live *here*.

"Not for the winter. Not until I'm stronger. Here. There is a machine in a shed at the north end of this station and it is the only one of its kind that anybody in this room has ever heard of or is ever likely to, and I must be inside a mile of it every eleven days for the rest of my life. Eleven days. Not a fortnight, not a month, not if I'm careful. Eleven.

"Which means I will not see Sallow Creek again. I will not see Hobb's Wall, or that dreadful weighmaster I was extremely fond of, or the Level, or that boy's stones on the spur, ever, and none of you are to build me a chair on wheels and get clever about it, because it isn't the distance, it's the eleven days, and I've done the sums four times and so will you and we will all get the same answer.

"And I have been in a cart since the second of the month. I've crossed three hundred miles of the worst road in this country lying on my back being no use to anybody, and it's the only thing in my life I was ever actually any good at, and I have done it for the last time, and I should like that noticed by somebody, because it will not be in any of that man's folders."

Dessa said, "It's noticed."

"Thank you.

"Third, and this is the one I'd rather not, so I'm going to do it quickly." She put her right hand out of the blankets and laid it on top of them, palm up. "The course takes the nerves. Not all at once and not everywhere — it takes the hands and the feet, and it does not give them back, and it is not a small percentage of people, it is nearly everybody, and he showed me the number and then he made me say it back to him.

"So in about four months I will not be able to feel anything with these." She turned the hand over. "I'll be able to move them. I'll be able to pick things up, if I look at them while I'm doing it. I won't know I'm holding them.

"And I won't walk. I can walk now, badly, as far as that door. In the spring I shall not, and that's the feet, and that one's simple and there's nothing to discuss about it."

Toren said, "Gran—"

"Don't do that face," said Senna. "Just say it."

"Is it worth it?"

"Yes," said Senna Voss, immediately, without any weighing at all. "Yes, it is, and I want you to hear how fast I said that, because you'll want it in about ten years when somebody you love is in a bed and you're doing the same arithmetic.

"I am seventy-three. I have had twenty years of something that was always going to finish me, and a man in a corridor has just offered me eight more and told me to my face that he's seen twelve and he's seen three, which he did not have to do. Eight years. I shall watch that one — " the hand moved a little toward Toren " — stop being thirteen. I shall watch that one — " toward Wyck " — be told what to do by somebody and not do it. I shall watch Dessa Cole run a room. That is not a consolation prize. That is the entire thing I have wanted since the morning we left Sallow Creek, and they are going to sell it to me for my hands and my feet and the rest of the road, and I'd have paid more, and I told him so, and he said everybody says that in the first week."

She stopped and got her breath.

"I've finished," she said. "Somebody go and find out about the tea in this place, because it is not good and I refuse to believe that's the best they can do."

---

Hut eleven had four beds in it and a stove and a window with a blanket over it and a floor of poured concrete with a drain in the corner that Dessa filled with a rag on the second night because she said she could hear the station's insides through it.

That was the clan now. Four beds and a stove and a woman in a long building four hundred yards away, and no cart, and no mule, and no road.

Wyck slept eleven hours the first night and woke up at the eighth hour in a rage about it and had to be talked down by Dessa, who pointed out, in her flattest voice, that there was nothing whatever to keep watch against inside a berm with a gate on it and a man paid to stand at the gate, and that he had not been on watch for four nights and the world had continued.

"That's not the point."

"No," Dessa agreed. "It isn't."

He got a new bar on the fourth day. He went to the yard office and asked whether there was work and was told there was always work, and came back with eighteen inches of square-section steel out of a scrap bin that a man had cut down for him for nothing, and he spent the next four evenings sitting on the step of hut eleven turning it over in his left hand, badly, dropping it, and picking it up, and dropping it.

"Say it," said Toren, on the third evening.

"Say what."

"You've been about to say something for an hour."

Wyck turned the bar over. He dropped it. He picked it up.

"He said he'd go," he said.

Toren did not say anything.

"At the fire on the lane. He said *I'll walk you to Meridian, and when you're through the gate and she's in front of somebody with proper instruments, I'll go.* He said it to all of us and I wrote it down in my head at the time because I write down everything that man says and so does everybody in this hut, and we are through the gate, and she is in front of instruments, and he's had nine days and he hasn't said a word about it, and I'm not going to sit here for a month waiting for it to happen at four in the morning."

"Then ask him."

"I'm not asking him," said Wyck Tanner. "If I ask him he answers, and then I'll never know whether he'd have said it on his own."

---

He said it on his own on the sixth day, in the hut, at the eleventh hour, with the stove going and all of them in the room — which meant that Dessa had gone and got Senna in a chair with wheels on it from the ward, over four hundred yards of wet path, with Wyck pushing and complaining, on the basis of a message that said *hut eleven, tonight, all of us* and nothing else.

Rook stood with his back to the stove because he could not sit down and get up again in one evening.

"I'm going to do this badly," he said, "and I'm going to do it in one go, because there's a rule in this room about all of us at once and nobody finding out second, and it was set by somebody in a lane four months ago and it is the best rule anybody has ever given me.

"On the lane I said I'd walk you to Meridian, and I said that when you were through the gate and she was in front of instruments, I'd go. I meant it entirely. I had it worked out. There was a version of this week in my head where I stood at that gate on the day she went in, and shook four hands, and went east, and I have been carrying that version about with me since before any of you were hurt and it has been a great comfort to me and I'd like to put it down now."

He stopped.

"I'm not going," he said.

Nobody moved.

"That's all of it. There's no second part. I'm not going, and there isn't a reason I can give you that would stand up in front of the man I was in that lane, and I've stopped needing one." He looked at the stove rather than at any of them. "I've taken work in the north yard. It's ash-lifting on the Sag line and it's the worst money in this station and nobody else will take it because you have to be able to hold a road shut, and I've signed for four seasons, which in this place is a thing you sign.

"And I'll say the other half of that out loud now, so that nobody has to work it out on their own at four in the morning. Her course is clinical. It went through at the gate and it is not conditional on one single thing any of the rest of us does, and the man at that window told me so to my face and made a promise about it he didn't have to make, and I believe him.

"But she has to be inside a mile of that shed every eleven days for the rest of her life. Which means she has to be inside this berm. And being inside this berm is residency, and residency in this station is *reviewed*, and a household gets reviewed on what the station reckons it's worth. So that's what I've signed for. Not for her medicine. For her address.

"And if anybody in this room wants to say *you said you'd go*, now's when, and I'll hear the whole of it standing up."

There was a long silence.

Then Wyck said, "Right."

And that was the whole of it, and the stove ticked, and Dessa put her head back against the wall and shut her eyes, and Senna Voss said, "Well," in a voice that had a great deal in it, "I shall need somebody to push this chair on Tuesdays."

---

Toren went to the record office on the seventh day.

Rook told him where it was without being asked and then made a point of not coming.

"It's a long room at the top of the stair in the yard office and it's open to residents on Tuesdays and Fridays and the woman who runs it is called Annice Fell and she's been in that room longer than I've been alive. That's all I've got and it's all I'm giving you." He was sitting on the step with his coat over his knees. "I'm not going to tell you to do it. I'm not going to tell you not to. Your father gave you a rule in a road on the day he died and it was a good rule and I've told you to keep it twice, and I'm not going to be the man who talks you out of your father's last instruction in a yard in the rain. It's yours. You'll do what you do."

"What would you do?"

"I'd have done it eleven years ago if I'd had one," said Rook.

---

The record office was ninety feet long and smelled of dust and warm metal and had a single window at the far end, and the whole of one wall was paper and the whole of the other was steel cabinets with handles, and in the middle there was a desk with a screen on it that glowed a flat green and made a low noise that Toren could feel in his teeth.

Annice Fell was about sixty and had a cardigan and half-glasses on a chain and did not look up for some time.

"Resident?"

"Toren Voss. Yard C, hut eleven. Conditional."

"Conditional's fine. What is it you want?"

"I want to know if there's anything in here about an object."

"Everything in here is about an object," said Annice Fell. "Try again, and try again with a shape and a material and a measurement, because that's how the depot lists went in and it's how they come out. The catalogue doesn't know stories. It knows grammes."

Toren stood at the desk.

He thought about a cut in a road four months ago and a man with his hands in his coat pockets walking at a pace the road had agreed to beforehand, and *under the shirt, always, you don't show it, not at a gate, not at a wall, not to a friend*, and *because I don't know why, and neither did any of them, and we all did it anyway, and we're all still here, so we keep doing it*.

And he thought: *we're all still here* was said by a man who had nine days left.

He put his hand up under his collar and lifted the cord over his head and put the disk down on the desk.

It lay on the wood between them, dark, near-black, about the width of two thumbs, with nine fine grooves running from the centre out to the rim on the face that was showing, and nine on the other, on a cord of braided boot-lace gone soft with four months of being worn against a boy's chest.

Annice Fell looked at it for a moment. Then she got up and fetched a pair of callipers and a small scale, and she did not ask him one question about where it had come from, and Toren understood, watching her, that he had walked into the one room in Meridian where not being asked was the whole service.

---

"Thirty-one across," she said. "Thirty-one and a bit. Four millimetres. Forty grammes, which is wrong for that size in anything I can name. Non-ferrous — the magnet's doing nothing. Nine radial channels, both faces. Is that all nine? Count them for me, I don't trust my eyes on fine work."

"Nine."

"Nine," she said, and typed, and the green screen did nothing for a while, and then did something.

"Right," said Annice Fell. "Sit down."

Toren sat down.

"I'm going to tell you three things and then I'm going to tell you what I don't know, and I'd like you not to interrupt during the third one, because it's the one people interrupt during.

"First. There's one entry. Hardened Materials Depot, this station, receiving list, sixty-one years back, before the Fall's tenth anniversary and before there was any such thing as a Kindling. Item nineteen of forty-one, in a lot of returned survey stores with no manifest attached, which is itself unusual and which the clerk noted at the time in three words and then let go." She turned the screen a little, which was a courtesy, and which Toren could make nothing of. "Dimensions as I've just read them off your desk there. The clerk wrote them down sixty-one years ago and I wrote them down four minutes ago and they are the same to a tenth of a millimetre."

"So it's—"

"Second," said Annice Fell. "There's a note in the margin in a different hand and a different ink, pencil over ink, and it was written after the list was made, and I'm going to read it to you as it is, and I'd ask you to let me get to the end.

"*Not an instrument. Do not requisition. It came here in a gift's coat and it is not a gift; log it, shelf it, and do not let anybody wear it.*"

Toren sat absolutely still.

"There's more," said Annice Fell. "*It has already saved a world and it has already ended one. It will not do either again. Whoever is frightened of what this is going to do has not understood what it is.*"

She took her glasses off.

"That's the end of the note," she said. "There's no name on it. There's a mark where a name would go, and it isn't initials, it's a mark, and there are four of them in that ledger, all on the same two pages, and in thirty-one years I have never found a name attached to one and I have looked, because I am that sort of person."

---

Toren could hear the screen humming. It was the only thing in the room that was making any noise at all.

*A curse in a gift's coat. That's what my grandfather called it. He wore it forty years, and he never once let anyone see it.*

He heard it in his father's voice, exactly, the way you carry a hot thing across a room. And under it the other one, the warning, the thing that had survived when everything else in that family got repeated wrong for sixty years — *because warnings survive* — *it's going to save the world or it's going to end it, that's the whole thing, that's all anybody ever knew, and nobody who ever carried it lived long enough to find out which.*

His grandfather had not made up that phrase.

It had come with it. It had come in the box.

And whatever had actually been said, at the start, by whoever made that mark, had not been a warning about something that was going to happen. It had been a *report*. Somebody sixty-one years ago had written down that it was finished, and the Voss family — who had had it, Tam and Tam's mother and the sixty wrong years and Garrick's grandfather and Garrick and now a boy sitting in a chair in a long room — had spent five generations being frightened of the wrong tense.

"You're very pale," said Annice Fell.

"What's the third thing?"

"The third thing is the disposal line, and it's the reason I told you to sit down." She put her glasses back on. "That depot did not release material. That was the entire point of it — things went in and they were logged and they stayed in, and there is a column for release authorisations, and in sixty-one years of that ledger, across four thousand items, that column is empty. Empty. There is no such thing as a thing that came out of the Meridian hardened depot, and the reason I can say that with my whole chest is that I have read every page of it twice.

"Item nineteen is struck through. And in the authorisation column, in the same pencil as the note, it says: *released to bearer.*

"Not released to a name. Not released to a station, a company, a survey, a family. *To bearer.* No date. No signature. No countersign, no docket, no receipt, and there is no second entry anywhere in this room, from that year or any year, that ever mentions it again until a boy of thirteen put it down on my desk on a Tuesday."

---

Toren walked back across the station with the cord back round his neck and the disk cold under his shirt against his breastbone, exactly as cold as it had always been, and found Rook on the step of hut eleven with his coat over his knees, in the same place, which Toren was reasonably certain meant the man had been there for three hours.

He told him the whole of it. It took about four minutes and he got every word of the note right, because he had made Annice Fell read it to him twice and had then said it back to her until she stopped correcting him.

Rook listened to all of it with his head down.

Then he sat for a long time without saying anything, and the light on the main street came on, and a man went past the end of the row with a bucket.

"Say it," said Toren.

"No."

"You said ask you in about a year. It's not a year."

"It isn't," said Rook.

"Then say something, because I've been carrying it up that path for twenty minutes and it's got heavier every step and I don't know what to do with it."

Rook lifted his head.

"All right," he said. "One thing, and then I'm done tonight, and I'd like you to take it away and not open it in front of anybody.

"Your father told you a thing was going to happen. That paper says it already has." He was not looking at Toren. He was looking at the lit circle of wet ground under the lamp at the end of the row. "There is no version of that where what you've got round your neck is an object, Toren. Objects don't have a tense."

He got himself up off the step in three movements, with his hand on the door frame.

"Ask me again in a year," he said. "And I'll tell you what I believe, and it will be worse than tonight, and you'll be able to hold it by then. That's not me being careful with you. That's me not being ready, and I'd rather say that out loud than dress it up as looking after you, because a boy on a wall four months ago told me to say things out loud even when they're awful, and I have been extremely slow about learning it."

---

The card came on the ninth day.

It was buff, and it was the size of a hand, and a boy of about ten brought it to the door of hut eleven at the fourth hour and waited to be told there was no reply.

There were two lines on it.

*E. MARSH and T. VOSS are requested at the Director's office, second floor, yard office, on the twenty-ninth, at the second hour, concerning (i) the south approach incident of the nineteenth and (ii) a records consultation of the twenty-fifth. Attendance is not compulsory. Please bring your bands.*

Dessa read it over Toren's shoulder and then took it out of his hand and read it again.

"They've put them on the same card," she said.

"It's a card, Dessa, they've probably got one—"

"They have got more than one card," said Dessa Cole. "I have been in this station nine days and I have counted eleven kinds of paper. They put those two things on one card because somebody has sat at a desk and joined them up, and I'd like everybody to notice that the two things are *you killed something nobody can kill* and *you put a thing on a table in the record office*, and that until seven days ago the second one didn't exist."

Nobody said anything.

"*Attendance is not compulsory*," said Wyck, from the step, without looking round.

"Yes."

"They wrote that down." He turned the bar over in his left hand, and did not drop it. "That's the bit I'd want a column for."

---

They did the bread that night, because Dessa said the day had to have a halt in it, and because Senna was in the hut in the chair with the wheels on it and would have to go back at the tenth hour.

It was Meridian bread, which was grey and even and came in a wrapper with a number printed on it, and Dessa cut it badly, in slabs, on the top of the stove-box, with a lot of concentration.

"I know the order," she said.

"I know you do," said Senna.

She picked up the end crust.

"Senna."

She put it in her grandmother's hand, and then, without being asked, she folded the fingers over it, because the course had started on the fourth day and by the ninth there was something wrong with the hand already, four months ahead of everything Doctor Sowerby had drawn on his folder — which happened sometimes, and which he had told them plainly happened sometimes, in the same voice he used for everything else.

Senna looked down at her own hand.

"It's there," said Dessa.

"I know it's there. I can feel the *weight* of it. I can't feel the bread." She turned the hand over slowly, watching it, and the crust stayed where it was. "It's like being handed things through a glove. A thick one. That's the whole of it and it isn't dramatic and I'd be obliged if nobody made a face."

"Do you want—"

"I do not," said Senna Voss, "want anybody in this room to hold my bread for me. Not tonight, not in the spring, not in eight years, and I'd like that minuted." She lifted it, the way you lift a thing you are doing with a tool. "I can see it perfectly well. I shall simply have to look at it, and I have looked at worse."

She ate the crust, looking at it.

"Me," said Dessa, and put a slab down beside her own hand and did not eat it.

"Wyck."

Wyck took it, left-handed.

"Rook."

Rook took it.

"Tor."

Toren took it.

"Milo," said Dessa, and cut the last slab, and put it on the cloth at the end of the row, and folded the cloth over it.

Then she did the thing she had not done on the bank at the Level, and had not done on any night since, and had plainly decided on the path over from the ward with Wyck complaining behind her.

"He'd have hated this place," she said.

Wyck made a noise.

"He would. He'd have hated it for about four days and then he'd have known everybody's name in the whole north yard and he'd have been telling that Hallet man his business, and he'd have got a lamp — he'd have got one of those lamps off the pole somehow, he'd have found out who owns them —" She stopped, and put the knife down, and carried on anyway, in the manner of somebody walking over a bad patch of road without slowing down. "He'd have stood in the middle of that street with three lights on it and said something completely idiotic about how it wasn't as good as his."

"It wasn't as good as his," said Wyck.

"It wasn't," Rook agreed.

"It was *terrible*," said Dessa. "It was a jar of fireflies. It was never once enough to see by in four months and it is the only thing that has ever got any of us anywhere."

Senna Voss put her right hand flat on the top of the stove-box, which was warm, and which she could only just tell was warm, and would not be able to tell by the spring, and left it there anyway.

"Milo Faust," she said, to the room, in a voice like a dry stick breaking. "There. Now it's been said tonight, and it'll be said tomorrow, and I shall say it every single night in this station until they carry me out of it, and none of you have to."

---

Toren went out and sat on the step at about the twelfth hour, after Wyck had pushed the chair back across to the long building and come back, after the stove had been banked, after the last of them had stopped talking.

The lamp at the end of the row went out at the first hour, the way it did.

He sat in the dark with his back against the door of a hut in a place with a written reason for everything, and put his hand in his coat pocket, the way he had done about every forty steps for four months without ever quite deciding to.

He had stopped checking after the fourth day. That was the honest truth of it and he had told nobody: he had checked it nine times a day for four days and found a stone, and on the fifth day he had made himself stop, because a man on a towpath in the spring had told him what checking on it did, and because he had been told there was one girl's worth of evidence in the whole world and no more.

So he had not gone looking. He had gone eleven days and had not gone looking once.

It was warm.

Not hot, and not much, and not steady — it came and went under his fingers like something breathing, exactly the way it had on four hundred yards of canal towpath with a sack of corn across his knees and a boy calling imaginary husks at his back. It was the least impressive thing anybody had ever felt.

Toren Voss sat on a concrete step at the first hour with his broken arm in a sling and a summons on the table behind him and his grandmother in a building four hundred yards away with a pair of hands that were already going, in a station that had written every one of them down in a book, and held a warm stone in his coat pocket, and did not take it out, and did not look at it, and did not ask it for anything at all.
