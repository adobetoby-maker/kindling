# Chapter Eight

Dessa lasted nine minutes.

She came down off the bank at a furious hobble while the rest of them were still on the first drill of the morning, and stood at the edge of the field with the stick in one hand and the other hand out sideways in a gesture of enormous appeal, and said, "Nine minutes."

"Nine," Rook agreed.

"It was the worst nine minutes of my life."

"Go back up."

"I am *not* — do you know what happens? Nothing happens. You sit in the grass and you think, right, nothing, and then thirty seconds go past and you think, that was thirty seconds, and then you think, why am I counting, and then you count how long you went without counting." She was genuinely red in the face. "It's not *hard*. That's the insulting part. It's not hard and I can't do it."

"Nine minutes," said Rook. "Go back up."

She went back up. Toren, holding a stupid crooked spike out at arm's length in the middle of a mown field, watched her go and thought, with a certainty that would look extremely funny to him later, *that's not going to come to anything.*

---

The steading was a mile off the road at the top of the second hill, and Rook took them to it the way he took them anywhere, which was by walking there and not saying where they were going.

It had been a farmhouse and three sheds around a yard. The house was down to two gables and a chimney. One shed had a roof.

"Three in the shed," said Rook, from forty feet out. "Been there a while. Nothing's been at that midden in weeks and something's been sleeping in the straw."

"Husks?" said Milo.

"Husks."

"Are we going to —"

"You're going to. The doorway's four foot wide, and there's no other way out of that shed, and there's nothing behind you but the yard, and I'll be standing in the yard." He looked at Toren. "Set it."

Toren looked at the shed.

Nine days ago he would have looked at it and seen a shed. What he saw now — and he was pleased with himself about it, in a way he would want back within the hour — was a four-foot doorway, a lintel low enough to take your teeth out, straw on a beaten-earth floor, a wall of loose stone on the left that would go if you put a shoulder into it, and no light past about six feet in.

"Milo, light, and on my shoulder, not behind Wyck. Wyck left of the door, I'm right of the door. We don't go in."

"We don't go in?" said Wyck.

"They come out. It's four foot wide. If we go in, it's four foot wide *behind* us and dark."

Rook said nothing, which meant it was right.

It was right. That was what made the rest of it so bad.

They kicked the door frame and made noise and the first husk came out into the yard light at a flat run and Wyck took it across the shins with the bar and Toren put the spike through it under the arm and it came apart into grey and it took about four seconds. The second one came out three seconds behind the first and did the same thing and lasted about as long.

Toren had a full second and a half in which he understood that he was extremely good at this.

Then the third one came out of the dark of the shed, low and fast and silent, and did not come at Wyck and did not come at Toren. It went straight for the light.

Milo screamed, which was correct, and did not drop the light, which was remarkable, and went over backwards with the thing on top of him.

The spike went out.

It went out the way a candle goes out in a draught — completely, instantly, between one heartbeat and the next — and Toren was standing in a farmyard with a grey stone in his fist and a husk on top of Milo four feet away, and he did the only thing available to a person in that situation, which was to fall on it bodily with a rock in his hand.

He hit it with his own fist. He hit it with the Ember. He hit it four times before Wyck got the bar under its chin and hauled it off and stood on it, and it came apart, and the grey ran out across the beaten earth of the yard and went into the seams.

Milo sat up. He had a long red scrape from his ear to his collarbone, and he was going to have a magnificent bruise, and he was otherwise entirely fine, and he held up both hands and said, in a cracked voice, "I kept it lit."

"You did," said Rook, from the gate, where he had not moved.

---

"Why does it go out?"

They were sitting on the yard wall. Rook had let them collect the ash themselves for the first time — three seams' worth, which in the flat tin flask came to about two fingers, and Rook had looked at it and said "a Handful, maybe, if you sold it at a wall, and don't ever let them tell you it's less."

"Why does it go out," Toren said again.

"Tell me your answer first."

"I don't have an answer."

"You have four," said Rook. "You've had one a day since the bridge. Say the current one."

Toren looked at the stone.

"Because I get frightened," he said. "It goes out when I'm frightened, and I've got to stop being frightened, or — no. All right. Not stop. I've got to hold it harder when I'm frightened. It's a thing you hold. So when the rest of me goes, I have to hold it harder, and that's the whole skill, and that's what I haven't got."

"Good," said Rook. "Test it."

"How?"

"I'm going to frighten you," said Rook, "and you're going to hold it harder."

He did that for forty minutes.

It was not cruel, and it was not a game. He walked Toren up and down the yard with the spike out and did things to him: threw a stone past his ear without warning; kicked the loose wall down behind him; came at him out of the shed doorway at a dead run and stopped four feet away; and twice, without announcing it, put a hand on his shoulder from behind when Toren did not know he was there.

The spike went out every single time.

By the end of it Toren's jaw hurt from the top of his skull to the base of his throat, and his hand was cramped shut around the stone so hard that Rook had to work two of the fingers open, and he was almost crying with rage.

"It's worse," he said. "It's *worse* now than it was this morning."

"Yes."

"I held it as hard as I —"

"Yes," said Rook. "Good. Now you've got evidence. Your answer's wrong, and you know it's wrong because you tested it properly and it got worse, and that's worth more than four days of me telling you." He let go of Toren's hand. "Holding it harder is the thing that puts it out. Every time. That's real, and you found it, and it's going to be a month before you can do anything with it."

"Then what do I —"

"I don't know how to say the next part to you yet," said Rook.

And he stopped, and looked at Toren for a second longer than he needed to, as though something about that sentence had surprised the man who said it.

"Go and see to Milo's face," he said.

---

They camped that night in the lee of the field wall about half a mile short of the steading, and Dessa sat on the bank in the grass for fifty-one minutes.

She came down and reported it like a weight off a scale. "Fifty-one."

"What changed?"

"I stopped trying to do it right."

Rook was quiet.

Then he said, "Tomorrow, come here in the evening instead. When they're tired and there's nobody watching you do it."

---

So it was the twelfth night, at the wall, with the fire low and Pitch hobbled twenty feet off and Senna asleep in the cart, and Toren three-quarters of the way through repairing the same strap he had been failing to repair for four days.

Dessa sat down across the fire from Rook and put the stick beside her.

"Hands out," said Rook.

She put her hands out. Rook looked at Toren without turning his head, which was a trick he had, and said, "Give her yours."

Toren dug the Ember out of his coat and handed it over, and Dessa took it in both hands and held it flat across her palms exactly the way Garrick Voss had set it across Toren's on the morning of his Kindling, and Toren felt something turn over in his chest that had nothing to do with what happened next.

"Now nothing," said Rook. "Same as the bank."

She sat with it for a long time. Twenty minutes, by the fire. The wood settled twice.

Nothing happened, because nothing was going to happen. The Ember lay in her hands and stayed the flat water-grey it had been in the coals at Sallow Creek, in Nell Quill's weigh-yard, in Rook's own fist in a mown field. After twenty minutes Dessa opened her eyes, looked down at it, and said, without any particular heat, "It's a rock."

"It is."

"It was always going to be a rock."

"Yes."

"Then why —"

"Because now you've held one," said Rook, "and you know exactly how much of you wanted it to work, and next time you'll be able to put that down first." He held his hand out. "Give it back to him."

She gave it back.

And then Rook took his own Ember out of his coat pocket and put it into her hands.

Toren, afterward, would be able to account for every second of the next four minutes, and would never be able to account for that one. There was no reason for it. There was no lesson in it that was not already in the first one. Rook did it quickly — took it out, leaned across, set it flat in her palms, sat back. The whole thing took less than three seconds, and he did not say anything while he did it. Toren, who had watched the man put that stone against his grandmother's collarbone and against Dessa's shin and had never once seen it leave his hand otherwise, sat in the grass with an awl in his fist and could not have explained why his mouth had gone dry.

It was smaller than Toren's. He had noticed that in the cut and had never thought about it. It sat in Dessa's hands as though it had been cut for them.

"Same as the bank," said Rook.

"It's not going to—"

"I didn't ask you what it's going to do," said Rook. "Sit there and don't want anything."

---

She sat.

It went on for a long while. Long enough that Toren finished the strap. Long enough that the fire needed feeding, and nobody fed it. Wyck came back from the latrine trench and stopped at the edge of the firelight and looked, and then went and sat down without saying anything, which from Wyck was the same as clearing a room.

Dessa's face did not do anything interesting. That was what Toren kept telling people afterward, when he tried to describe it, and nobody ever believed him: she just sat there with her bad leg out straight and her hands in her lap and her chin slightly down, breathing, the way you sit when you have been sitting for a while. Once she scratched her nose.

Then her breathing changed.

It did not catch. It went longer — two breaths, three — the way Senna's had gone longer under Rook's hand at the cut, out and out and out, as though somebody upstream had opened a gate rather than shut one.

And the stone in her hands went warm.

Toren felt it before he saw it, in his back teeth, in the door at the bottom of his own head that had been standing open since a road cut thirteen days ago: a small, near, *domestic* wrongness, like the feeling of someone lighting a lamp behind you in a room you thought you were alone in.

Then he saw it, because everybody saw it.

Light came up out of the grey slab in Dessa Cole's hands. Not much of it. It was the colour of the inside of a coal at the bottom of a fire that has been banked properly and left, and it did not flare, and it did not flicker, and it did not come off the stone and go anywhere. It stayed exactly where it was, a little steady warmth held in two hands, about as bright as Milo's light and about a hundred times more solid.

Pitch went sideways on the hobble and made a noise.

Dessa opened her eyes.

She looked at her own hands with an expression of absolutely blank, undefended astonishment — the face of somebody who has said a word in a language she does not speak and been understood — and the light went on sitting in her palms for a count of four, and then it went out.

The fire cracked. Out in the dark the mule stamped and settled.

"Oh," said Dessa.

Nobody said anything.

"It's not in the stone," she said.

She was not talking to any of them. She had her hands still cupped, and she was staring into them, and her voice had gone small and fast, the way it went when she was working something and the working was ahead of the words. "It's not — I thought it was a *place*, I thought you go and you — it's not in the stone, the stone's just somewhere to put your hands, it's like. It's like counting on your fingers." She looked up. "Why is it like counting on your fingers?"

Toren looked at Rook.

Rook was sitting exactly as he had been. His hands were on his knees. His face was doing nothing whatsoever, which was a thing Toren had seen that face do exactly once before, at midnight, on gravel, four feet from a disk on a cord.

"How long?" said Rook.

"What?"

"How long did it feel like."

Dessa thought about it. "A long time," she said. "Ages. Half the night."

"It was four seconds."

"That's not —" She stopped. "Oh."

Rook stood up.

"Do it again," he said.

---

She could not do it again.

She tried for an hour. She tried it the way she had done it, and then she tried it seven other ways, and about the fourth time she started swearing under her breath in a steady unbroken murmur, and by the ninth she had gone white around the mouth, and Milo had woken up and come and sat next to her without saying anything, which was becoming the thing Milo did.

"Enough," Rook said, eventually.

"One more."

"No."

"I nearly had it that time, I could feel where it—"

"You could feel where it was two hours ago," said Rook. "That's not the same thing, and it's the exact thing that will keep you from it for a month. Enough."

She handed him back his Ember. Her hands were shaking, and she was angry about that too.

"Was that it?" she said.

"Was that what?"

"Was that the thing." She wiped her face with her sleeve, roughly, once. "Two years. Two years of people in my kitchen saying *never mind, love*. Was that it, or was that a — was that an accident, or a, I don't know, was that *your* stone doing it and not me —"

"It wasn't the stone," said Rook.

"Then what was it?"

And Rook — who had answered no question anyone had put to him in thirteen days, who had said *it's called none of your business* and *you'll find that out* and *when I decide to teach it to you* — stood on the other side of a low fire with his own Ember in his hand and looked at a fourteen-year-old girl who had been told there was nothing behind her door, and Toren watched him come within about one breath of saying something.

"Go to sleep," he said.

He walked out past the mule into the dark, fifty paces, sixty, until the firelight stopped being any use.

Toren, lying down, saw him out there against the last of the sky at the top of the field wall, standing with his back to the camp and one hand up over his mouth, and the hand did not come down for a long time.

---

In the morning Rook ran the wall drill again, and Wyck came across it poorly and got fouled on his own bar for the fourth day running.

"Again," said Rook.

Wyck did it again. It was worse.

"Stop."

Wyck stopped, with his jaw set, waiting for the *no* — because that was the shape of it, that had been the shape of every morning since Hobb's Wall: you did it, and he said no, and you did it again, and he said no, and somewhere around the ninth time you found out what no had meant.

Rook walked over and put two fingers on Wyck's left forearm.

"That's right," he said. "That arm. Don't change that, whatever else you change — you've been putting that arm across the top of the wall for four days, and every one of you has been doing it wrong except you, and it's the only reason you haven't gone over head first. Keep it." He stepped back. "Now the bar's in the wrong hand, and it's in the wrong hand because you're strong enough to get away with it on a two-foot wall and you won't be on a five-foot one. Swap it and go again and it'll be terrible, and it'll be terrible for three days, and then it won't."

Wyck stood there.

"Go on."

He swapped the bar and went at the wall again, and it was terrible.

Toren, waiting his turn, found that he had stopped breathing for a moment, and could not have told anybody why, except that he had been counting the man's sentences for thirteen days the way you count coins, and that was more words about how to do a thing than Rook had spent on any of them since the road cut.
