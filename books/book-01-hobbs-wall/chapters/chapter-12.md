# Chapter Twelve

The road north of the Drum went up out of the watercourse and ran for three days through country that had nothing in it, and those were, Toren thought afterward, the best three days of the whole first year.

Nothing happened. That was the whole of it. The rain went off, and the light came back thin and yellow, and the hedges were full of haws. Pitch found a stretch of good verge and ate his way along it for eleven miles in a state of visible contentment, and Milo taught them all a counting song from Sallow Creek that had four verses — and then made up a fifth that was about Rook, and got two lines into performing it before losing his nerve, and was tormented about it for the rest of the week.

Nobody bled. Nothing came out of a ditch.

And every morning and every evening, on any patch of flat ground wide enough for it, the man made them work, and the work had changed shape so completely that Toren could no longer remember what it had been like before.

---

He could remember, actually. He had the tally on the back of his father's paper.

*Because the hedge doesn't stop anything.* Four marks in four days, and then a page of them, and then nothing, because it had become the weather.

What he could not remember was the feeling of the first eight days — the flat *no*, and the doing it again, and the second flat *no*, and the sensation of being a tool somebody was sharpening on a stone and not talking to. It had been eleven days, and it was already hard to believe it had been like that. He knew it had. He could produce the evidence. He simply could not get his body to remember the taste of it any more, the way you cannot remember toothache.

Wyck could. Wyck brought it up twice.

"He was going to let her turn into a cripple to make a point," he said, the first time, out of nothing, on the road, apropos of a perfectly good morning.

"He said he'd make the trade again."

"He said that eleven days ago." Wyck flexed the fingers of his left hand, which he did about four hundred times a day now. "And then at the orchard he told us everything before, sat us down and told us the whole thing with a stick in his hand, and my arm's a mess instead of Milo being dead. So either he was lying on the bridge or something happened to him in between, and I've thought about it a lot, and I don't like either one."

"Why not the second one?"

"Because nobody's said what it was," said Wyck.

---

On the nineteenth day Toren finally answered the question.

It had been sitting in him for twelve days like a stone in a pocket — *where does it go while I'm holding it, does it wait in the stone, does it follow you around the field* — and he had been turning it over for twelve days without saying one word about it to anybody. Partly because he had been told not to, and mostly because every answer he came up with sounded stupid the moment he tried it out in his own head.

They were on a bare hillside above the road, and Rook had him doing the thing he now did most evenings, which was standing about with the spike out while other people threw things at him.

"It doesn't go anywhere," Toren said.

Rook did not stop what he was doing. "Go on."

"When you were holding it. It didn't go anywhere, because it was never in there to start with." He was talking too fast and could not stop. "You held it up, and it was a rock, and everybody said, *see, it's his, it's dead in another hand* — and they've got it backwards. It wasn't dead because the stone wasn't working. It was dead because *you* weren't in it. It's — " he cast about — "it's not a lamp. It's a window. There's nothing in a window."

"Where's the light coming through it, then."

"Me," said Toren.

"Say the rest."

"There isn't any rest, that's all I've—"

"There is," said Rook, "and you've had it for a week, and you keep flinching off it. Say it."

Toren stood on a bare hillside with a warm crooked spike in his hand, and said, slowly, the thing he had not let himself think all the way to the end of:

"Then anybody could."

"Yes."

"Anybody. Anybody at all, whether they'd ever been given a — " He stopped. "Dessa."

"Yes."

"Four seconds, at a fire, with a stone she'd never touched in her life."

"Yes," said Rook.

He came over and stood in front of Toren and took the spike between his thumb and forefinger, right at the ugly crooked end of it, and held it there — which was a thing Toren had not known anybody could do, and which he never forgot, because the stone in his own fist did not go out, and there were suddenly two people holding it.

"Now here's the part you'll refuse for about a year," said Rook, "so I'm going to say it plainly, and then I'm not going to argue with you about it, and in a year you can come back and tell me I was right.

"That stone is a door. What your healer did at the cut with her hands and no stone at all was a door. What the boy does with the light in his hands is a door, and it's a poor one, and he's been laughed at for it since he was nine. Every settlement between here and the coast has picked one of them and built a whole life around believing it's the only one, and they're all of them standing in the same house arguing about which wall the house is on.

"There's one thing behind all three. It comes from inside you. It always did. The door you got handed at thirteen was an accident of where you were born and who was in the room, and it decides absolutely nothing about what you are.

"And a person who gets that all the way down — not agrees with it, *gets* it, down where they don't have to think about it — stops being stuck with the one door."

He let go of the spike.

Toren stood there.

"Like you," he said.

Rook picked up his coat.

"I said I wasn't going to argue with you about it," he said, and walked back down to the fire.

---

Toren did the arithmetic on the twentieth day, because it was that or think about the other thing.

He got out his father's paper at the midday halt and sat on the milestone that was not a milestone — it was a concrete post with the numbers weathered off it — and looked at the column of days in Garrick's blocky hand, ending at eighteen and then at eighteen crossed out and nothing after it, and the five-bar gate underneath in his own.

Then he did what his father had done, which was sums on the back of a piece of paper about whether an old woman was going to live.

A hundred and ten miles. Nell Quill had said it standing at her own table with a book open in front of her, and Nell Quill did not say numbers she had not checked.

They made nine miles on a bad day and thirteen on a good one and had not had a day under seven since the creek bridge. Eighteen days north of Hobb's Wall.

He did it three times, because the first time he assumed he had made a mistake.

Then he folded the paper up and put it away and went and walked beside Rook for a quarter of a mile without saying anything, which was a technique he had learned from Dessa.

"Say it," said Rook.

"A hundred and ten miles."

"About that."

"We've walked a hundred and eighty."

Rook walked on for perhaps ten paces.

"About that," he said.

"So we're not on the Meridian road."

"No."

Toren waited. The cart creaked. Somewhere behind them Milo was singing the fourth verse.

"Then what road are we on?" he said.

"A longer one," said Rook.

And that was all of it, and Toren put it on the pile, which was getting to be an interesting pile: a man who knew what a disk was and would not say; a name off a piece of brass that was worth half a flask; a road a hundred and eighty miles long that was supposed to be a hundred and ten; and a lesson on a bare hillside about doors that Toren had not been able to sleep properly since.

---

He found Dessa on the bank on the twenty-first evening, because he went looking.

She was sitting in the dry grass above the camp with her bad leg out straight and her hands open on her knees, and he stood at the bottom of the bank for a while and then came up it and sat down six feet away, and she did not tell him to go away, so he stayed.

After a long time she said, "Nothing."

"How long have you been up here?"

"Since he put the fire on." She let her hands fall. "It's fifteen days. I've done it every night except the night at the orchard and the night in the rain. Fifteen days and one four-second — " she made a small disgusted noise " — *thing*."

"He said not to chase it."

"I know what he said. He says it about twice a week now, which is new, because he used to say it once and then let me be wrong for a fortnight." She pulled her knees up, carefully, the bad one last. "Tor. Can I say a thing and you don't repeat it?"

"Yes."

"I don't know what it was."

She said it to the grass in front of her.

"Everybody's decided. Milo thinks I'm going to go off like a flare one of these days, and he's *pleased*, he's genuinely pleased for me, which makes it worse. Wyck thinks I'm his — " she stopped. "Wyck thinks about it. And you've been looking at me for a fortnight like I'm a bridge you're waiting to cross.

"And I sat there and I held a warm stone and I said a stupid thing about counting on my fingers, and I don't *know*, Tor. I don't know if it was a door opening or if it was one loose brick and I'll never get another one. I don't know if it was mine or if it was his stone being a good stone in a bad hand. He says it wasn't the stone. He's never said anything else about it. Not once in fifteen days, and I've given him about eleven openings."

"He answers things now," Toren said. "He answers everything now. He answered me about the road this afternoon, and it was four words, but he—"

"He answers everything except that," said Dessa.

---

On the twenty-second night, the fire got low, and Milo went to sleep in the middle of the fifth verse, and Dessa went and lay down alongside the cart where she could hear Senna's chest, and Rook did what he had done every night since the Drum, which was take the first watch and the second watch and most of the third.

Toren lay with the disk on his breastbone under his shirt and did not sleep, and across the ashes Wyck lay on his back with his bad arm on his chest and did not sleep either, and eventually, without either of them acknowledging the other one was awake, Wyck said:

"How long do you reckon he's been carrying it?"

"Carrying what?"

"Whatever it is." Wyck's voice was low and perfectly ordinary. "The name. Whatever's under the name. Ten years? Twenty? He's not old. He looks old because he's been outside since he was about our age, but he's not old."

"I don't know."

"No."

The fire ticked.

"I'm not going to make a scene about it," said Wyck. "Before you start."

"I didn't say anything."

"You did the face."

"I haven't got a face."

"You've got an enormous face," said Wyck, "and everybody in this camp can read it, including the mule."

Toren laughed before he could stop himself, and it came out poorly, and after it there was a silence that was almost comfortable, and that was somehow the most alarming thing that had happened in three days.

"Here's the thing, though," Wyck said.

"Go on."

"He's teaching us something. Not survival. He's stopped teaching survival — he taught us survival in eight days, and it was all shoving and shouting, and he'd have finished in eight more. This is something else. He's building something in four people, and he hasn't asked one of us whether we want it built, and he won't say what it's for, and today he stood on a hill and told you the *world's wrong about how power works* and then walked off to make the corn."

"You were listening."

"I was thirty feet away, I've got ears." Wyck turned his head. "And he's not on the road to Meridian. I heard that too."

Toren said nothing.

"So I just want to know what I'm standing in a line for," said Wyck Tanner. "That's all. That's the whole of it. I've stood in that line every day since the cut and I'll stand in it tomorrow and I'll probably stand in it the day I die, and I don't think it's a lot to ask, and I've stopped being able to come up with a single reason why I shouldn't ask it out loud."

He rolled over.

"Not tonight," he said. "Your gran's asleep."

Toren lay on his back with the cold disk against his breastbone and looked up at the sky and thought, with great clarity and no comfort at all: *he means it, and he's right, and it isn't going to be me.*

Up on the bank the man who would not tell any of them anything sat facing back down the road they had come by, the way he had sat every night for six nights, turning a piece of brass over and over in his fingers, keeping the third watch nobody had asked him to keep.
